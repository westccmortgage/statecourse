# Washington Real Estate Fundamentals — Module WA-F-03

## Legal descriptions, encumbrances of record and land use control

## Learning objectives

1. Read the three legal description methods and identify which Washington uses.
2. Compute area and acreage from a description.
3. Distinguish a legal description from an address, a tax parcel number and a plat reference.
4. Distinguish zoning, variances, conditional uses and nonconforming uses.
5. Identify the land use questions a broker routes rather than answers.
6. Apply the material fact definition in RCW 18.86.010(11) to a land use problem.

## A. Legal descriptions

An **address** identifies a mailbox. A **tax parcel number** identifies an assessment record. **Neither
describes land.**

### A.1 The rectangular (government) survey — Washington's primary system

Washington is a **public land survey state**, and most of its land is described by **township, range and
section** referenced to the **Willamette Meridian**.

- A **township** is six miles square and contains **36 sections**.
- A **section** is **one square mile** and contains **640 acres**.
- Sections are numbered **1 through 36**, beginning in the **north-east** corner and running **boustrophedon**
  — west across the top row, east across the second, and so on.

**Worked example.** *The NW ¼ of the SE ¼ of Section 14* contains 640 × ¼ × ¼ = **40 acres.** Read the
description **backwards** — start at the section, take the SE quarter, then the NW quarter of that. Each
"of" divides.

*The E ½ of the NW ¼ of the SW ¼* contains 640 × ¼ × ¼ × ½ = **20 acres.**

### A.2 Metes and bounds

Used in Washington for parcels the rectangular system does not neatly describe — irregular tracts, water
boundaries, and older conveyances. A metes and bounds description begins at a **point of beginning**, runs
**courses** and **distances**, calls **monuments**, and **returns to the point of beginning**.

**A description that does not close describes no parcel**, and that is a surveyor's problem, not a broker's.
**Monuments generally control over distances** where they conflict, because a called monument is physical
evidence of what the parties meant.

**A fence is not a boundary.** It is evidence of where somebody once thought the boundary was. Where a
fence, hedge, driveway or outbuilding sits near a line, the answer is a **current survey**.

### A.3 Reference to a recorded plat

*Lot 7, Block 3, [named] Addition, according to the plat recorded in Volume __ of Plats, page __, records of
__ County, Washington.* The recorded plat supplies the description by reference, and **the plat is only as
good as its recording.**

### A.4 What a broker actually does

Read the description in the listing **against the seller's own deed** and against a **survey**. If the seller
says the property "includes the strip down to the creek" and the deed does not describe it, you have found a
question — and RCW 18.86.040(1)(c) tells you what to do with it.

**Area conversions worth memorising:** **1 acre = 43,560 square feet.** **1 section = 640 acres = 1 square
mile.** **1 township = 36 sections.**

## B. Encumbrances of record, and how they are found

A **title search** discloses what the public record holds against the parcel: **deeds of trust and other
liens**, **easements**, **restrictive covenants**, **plat notes and dedications**, **judgments**, and
**assessments**.

**A preliminary commitment for title insurance lists exceptions** — the matters the policy will **not**
cover. **Reading the exceptions is the point of receiving it**, and a broker who hands a buyer a commitment
without saying so has handed over a document the buyer will not open.

**No Washington title policy form, exception schedule or rate manual was read for this project.** This course
states no policy term, exception or premium. **Coverage questions go to the title company and to counsel.**

## C. Land use control

### C.1 The instruments

- **Comprehensive plan** — the jurisdiction's long-range policy. In Washington this sits inside a statutory
  planning framework; **the Growth Management Act and Washington's planning statutes were not read for this
  project**, and this course states no requirement of any of them.
- **Zoning ordinance** — the enforceable rule: **use districts**, plus **bulk and dimensional controls** —
  setbacks, height, lot coverage, minimum lot area, frontage.
- **Subdivision ordinance** — governs dividing land, short plats and full plats.
- **Building codes** — with permits and a **certificate of occupancy**.
- **Critical areas and shoreline regulation** — a genuinely Washington-specific layer. Wetlands, steep
  slopes, fish and wildlife habitat, aquifer recharge areas and marine and freshwater shorelines carry
  regulation that can materially limit what may be built. **The governing statutes and every local ordinance
  were not read for this project. No buffer width, slope threshold, shoreline designation or permit
  requirement is stated in this course.**

### C.2 The four relief mechanisms

| Mechanism | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Variance** | Permission to depart from a dimensional or use requirement, generally on a hardship showing not created by the owner. |
| **Conditional use permit** | A use the ordinance **already contemplates** in that district, allowed once stated conditions are met. |
| **Nonconforming use** | A use that **lawfully predates** the ordinance. Its permitted scope, expansion limits and continuation conditions are set by the ordinance. |
| **Rezone** | Amendment of the ordinance itself — a legislative act. |

**Students merge the first three constantly.** A **variance** asks for an exception; a **conditional use**
asks for something the rule already allows on conditions; a **nonconforming use** asks for nothing, because
it was there first.

**"Grandfathered" is not a legal term and it is not a broker's conclusion.** When an owner uses it, what they
mean is a legal nonconforming use whose scope is defined by an ordinance the broker has not read. **Say what
is known — the owner states the use predates the current zoning — and route the rest.**

## D. Where land use meets the material fact duty

**RCW 18.86.010(11)** defines a material fact:

> *"information that **substantially adversely affects the value of the property or a party's ability to
> perform its obligations** in a real estate transaction, or **operates to materially impair or defeat the
> purpose of the transaction**."*

**RCW 18.86.030(1)(d)** makes disclosing them a duty owed to **all parties**, unwaivable:

> *"To **disclose all existing material facts known by the broker and not apparent or readily ascertainable
> to a party**; **provided that this subsection shall not be construed to imply any duty to investigate
> matters that the broker has not agreed to investigate**."*

Apply the two together to land use, because the analysis is not obvious.

**A known critical-area designation that prevents building on the buildable portion of a lot** substantially
adversely affects value and may defeat the purpose of the transaction. If the broker **knows** it and it is
**not apparent or readily ascertainable**, it is disclosed.

**But the proviso is real.** *"This subsection shall not be construed to imply any duty to investigate
matters that the broker has not agreed to investigate."* A broker is not required to research the wetland
inventory. The duty attaches to **what the broker knows.**

**And RCW 18.86.030(2)** removes any duty *"to conduct an independent inspection"* or *"to independently
verify the accuracy or completeness of any statement made by either party or by any source reasonably
believed by the broker to be reliable."*

**So the shape is: no duty to dig, an absolute duty to disclose what you know, and a statutory duty to tell
the party to get expert advice.** Those three sentences answer most Washington land use questions a broker
faces.
