## A. Four figures that are not the same thing

**Value** is worth. **Cost** is what was spent to create it. **Price** is what someone actually paid.
**Assessed value** is what a taxing authority places on it, and it need not equal market value.

A house can cost $520,000 to build, be priced at $479,000, have a market value of $495,000 and be assessed
at $410,000, all at the same moment, with no contradiction. **A broker who tells a seller "the county says
it's worth $410,000" has confused two of the four.**

### A.1 The four elements of value

**Demand. Utility. Scarcity. Transferability.** All four must be present. A thing everyone wants, that is
useful and rare, has no market value if it cannot be transferred.

### A.2 Economic principles

**Substitution** — a buyer will pay no more than the cost of an equally desirable substitute. It is the
basis of the sales comparison approach.
**Contribution** — an improvement adds what it **returns**, not what it **cost.** This is the principle a
broker uses every time a seller asks whether to renovate before listing.
**Highest and best use** — one at a time, and it changes.
**Conformity. Anticipation. Change. Progression and regression. Supply and demand.**
**Plottage** — the increment of value from merging adjacent parcels, produced by the process of
**assemblage.**

## B. The sales comparison approach

The dominant approach for residential property, and the one a broker actually performs.

### B.1 Selecting comparables

**Closed sales only.** A **current listing is what a seller hopes for**; a **pending sale has not yet
survived appraisal, financing or inspection.** Neither is evidence of what a buyer paid.

Select for **similarity** in: **location**; **physical characteristics and condition**; **terms and
conditions of sale**; and **date of sale.** Three to four comparables of the **same property type** is the
usual working minimum.

### B.2 The two rules that govern adjustment

**Rule one: adjust the comparables, never the subject.** The subject is the unknown. Adjusting it would be
adjusting the answer.

**Rule two: superior comparables adjust DOWN; inferior comparables adjust UP.** The logic follows from rule
one — you are making each comparable into the subject on paper. If the comparable has something the subject
lacks, subtract, because the subject would not have brought that extra.

**Worked example.** *Comparable A sold for $612,000 and has a finished basement the subject lacks; the market
contribution of that basement, derived from paired sales, is $28,000. Comparable B sold for $574,000 and has
one fewer bathroom than the subject; the market contribution of that bathroom is $12,000.*

Comparable A adjusted: 612,000 − 28,000 = **$584,000** *(superior, so down).*
Comparable B adjusted: 574,000 + 12,000 = **$586,000** *(inferior, so up).*

**Two adjusted figures $2,000 apart is a tight range and good evidence.** If they were $60,000 apart, the
comparables were poorly chosen — go back to selection rather than averaging the spread away.

### B.3 Deriving the adjustment — paired sales analysis

Find two sales alike in everything but the one feature. **The difference between them is what the market
paid for that feature.** That is how an adjustment is derived honestly, and it is **contribution** applied to
one feature: **market value, which is usually less than actual cost.**

### B.4 Reconciliation

**Weighted consideration, not simple averaging.** A comparable that needed $4,000 of adjustment is better
evidence than one that needed $50,000, and averaging treats them as equal.

## C. The cost approach

Best where there are no comparables: **unique or special-use property**, and **new construction.**

**The method:**

1. **Estimate the land value separately**, by sales comparison. **Land is never depreciated.**
2. **Estimate the replacement or reproduction cost** of the improvements.
   - **Reproduction** rebuilds it as it was, same materials and design — used for historic property.
   - **Replacement** builds something of **equal utility** with current materials and methods.
   - Costing methods, most to least detailed: **quantity survey**, **unit-in-place**, **square foot**.
3. **Subtract accrued depreciation.**
4. **Add the depreciated improvement value to the land value.**

### C.1 Depreciation, and the one that cannot be cured

- **Physical deterioration** — wear, damage, deferred maintenance. **Curable or incurable.**
- **Functional obsolescence** — a defect in the design or utility. **Curable or incurable.**
- **External (economic) obsolescence** — a cause **outside the property line**. ***Incurable, always.***

**The reason external obsolescence is incurable is structural, not financial.** The other two are defects
**in** the property; the owner can in principle fix them. External obsolescence comes from a new industrial
use across the road, a highway realignment, a change in the neighbourhood — **the owner does not own the
cause and cannot cure it at any price.**

**Effective age is not chronological age.** A forty-year-old house maintained impeccably may have an
effective age of fifteen. It is the age the condition suggests.

## D. The income approach

### D.1 Direct capitalization

**Value = Net operating income ÷ capitalization rate.**

Deriving NOI:

- **Potential gross income**
- less **vacancy and collection loss** → **effective gross income**
- less **operating expenses** → **net operating income**

**Debt service is NOT an operating expense and is excluded.** NOI describes **the property's** earning power;
two buyers could buy the same building with entirely different financing and the NOI is identical. **NOI less
debt service is before-tax cash flow — a different figure answering a different question.**

**Worked example, rate given.** *Potential gross income $268,000; vacancy and collection loss 7 percent;
operating expenses $91,000; annual debt service $104,000; market capitalization rate 6.5 percent.*

Effective gross income = 268,000 × 0.93 = **$249,240.**
NOI = 249,240 − 91,000 = **$158,240.**
Value = 158,240 ÷ 0.065 = **$2,434,461.54** *(≈ $2,434,000).*
*(Before-tax cash flow = 158,240 − 104,000 = $54,240 — not the value.)*

**The cap rate measures risk, and it is inversely related to value.** At the same income a **higher** cap
rate gives a **lower** value, because it is the divisor.

### D.2 Gross rent multiplier

Used for small residential income property. **GRM = sale price ÷ gross rent**, derived from comparables;
then **Value = subject's gross rent × GRM.**

**Be consistent about the period.** A GRM derived from **monthly** rent is applied to **monthly** rent.
Mixing monthly and annual figures produces an answer wrong by a factor of twelve.

**Worked example.** *Comparables sell at $780,000 on $5,200 monthly gross rent, giving GRM = 780,000 ÷ 5,200
= **150**. A subject renting for $5,500 monthly is indicated at 5,500 × 150 = **$825,000**.*

## E. Appraisal, CMA and broker's price opinion — three different things

| | Who produces it | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| **Appraisal** | A **licensed or certified appraiser** | An opinion of value produced under professional standards |
| **Comparative market analysis** | A **broker** | An opinion of probable sales price, for a pricing or offering decision |
| **Broker's price opinion** | A **broker** | Defined in **RCW 18.85.011**, which this project did not read |

**A broker is not an appraiser, and a CMA is not an appraisal.** Presenting one as the other misdescribes
what the client is holding.

### E.1 The one thing RCW 18.86 says about a broker's price opinion

**RCW 18.86.080(9):**

> *"A firm **may receive compensation without a services agreement** for the provision of a **broker's price
> opinion**, as defined in RCW 18.85.011, **or a referral by one firm to another firm if the referring firm
> provided no real estate brokerage services in the transaction**."*

**That is an exception to a rule you will meet in Module WA-P-04.** RCW 18.86.080(7) provides that **to
receive compensation for rendering real estate brokerage services, a firm MUST HAVE a services agreement.**
Subsection (9) names **two** things that fall outside that requirement: **a broker's price opinion**, and **a
pure referral where the referring firm did no brokerage services.**

**RCW 18.85.011's definition of a broker's price opinion was NOT read for this project.** **This course
states no requirement about what a BPO must contain, who may prepare one, what it may be used for, or what
disclaimer it must carry.** Those are questions for RCW 18.85 and WAC 308-124 in their own text.

### E.2 What a broker should actually say about value

Say what the analysis is and what it is not. *"This is my opinion of probable sales price, based on these
four closed sales and these adjustments. It is not an appraisal. Your lender will order one, and it may come
in differently."*

**That sentence is defensible.** *"It'll appraise for six-fifty"* is not, and
**RCW 18.86.030(1)(b)** — deal honestly and in good faith, owed to all parties, unwaivable — is what it will
be measured against.
