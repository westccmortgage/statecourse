## A. Principles, types and estimates of value

### A.1 Value, price and cost are three different numbers

| Term | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Market value** | An estimate of what a property **should** bring under stated conditions, between a willing buyer and a willing seller, neither under compulsion, each reasonably informed, with the property exposed for a reasonable time |
| **Price** | What was **actually paid** in a particular transaction, which may have been affected by circumstances peculiar to those parties |
| **Cost** | What it took, or would take, **to build or improve** |

None of the three need equal the others, and a client who uses them interchangeably will misread every
figure a broker gives them.

**Other types of value the literature names:** investment value (to a particular investor), insurable
value, assessed value (for property taxation), going concern value, and liquidation value. **Assessed value
is not market value**, and a Colorado assessor's figure is produced under statutes **this project did not
read.**

### A.2 The elements of value

Value is ordinarily said to require four elements together — **demand**, **utility**, **scarcity**, and
**transferability**. Remove any one and there is no market value: a thing everyone wants but nobody may
transfer has none, and a thing freely transferable that nobody wants has none either.

### A.3 The economic principles

| Principle | What it says |
|---|---|
| **Substitution** | A buyer will pay no more than the cost of acquiring an equally desirable substitute |
| **Supply and demand** | Value moves with the relation between available properties and buyers for them |
| **Highest and best use** | The legally permissible, physically possible, financially feasible and maximally productive use |
| **Conformity** | Value is supported where a property conforms reasonably to those around it |
| **Progression and regression** | A lesser property benefits from better neighbours; a better property is dragged by lesser ones |
| **Contribution** | An improvement adds what it contributes to value, not what it cost |
| **Anticipation** | Value reflects the expectation of future benefits |
| **Change** | No condition is permanent; every estimate has a date |
| **Increasing and diminishing returns** | Added investment increases value up to a point, and beyond it does not |
| **Plottage** | Combining parcels under one ownership may produce more value than the sum |
| **Externalities** | Influences outside the property affect it |

**Substitution is the one that matters most in daily practice**, because it is why recent sales of
comparable properties indicate value at all — and why a "comparable" that is not genuinely comparable is
worse than none.

**Contribution is the one clients most often get wrong.** The question is never what a renovation cost; it
is what it contributes.

## B. Influences on property value

Four categories, and a broker who can name them can explain almost any client question about why a figure
moved:

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Physical and environmental** | Size, shape, topography, soils, view, exposure, condition, hazards, climate |
| **Economic** | Employment, income levels, interest rates, rents, vacancy, construction costs, tax burden |
| **Governmental and legal** | Zoning, building codes, land use plans, taxation, public services, environmental regulation |
| **Social** | Population change, household composition, and the pattern of demand for property types |

**Location is not a category; it is the intersection of all four at a point.** That is why the same
structure is worth different sums a mile apart, and it is why the Commission's outline puts
**neighborhood** into the investment analysis sub-topic at D.3 rather than treating it as a physical
attribute.

**A fair housing boundary that runs through this whole topic.** A broker may discuss **the property and
the physical, economic, governmental and legal characteristics of an area** using sources a client can
check. A broker may **not** characterise an area by the protected characteristics of the people in it,
whatever the client asks and however accurate the characterisation would be. Module 03 of this course
delivers the federal fair housing content under the Commission's Topic III.F.1; the discipline belongs
here too, because valuation conversations are where the question usually arises.

## C. The three approaches to value

The Commission's outline names them in this order: **Cost**, **Income**, **Market data.**

### C.1 The cost approach

**Estimated cost to reproduce or replace the improvements, less accrued depreciation, plus the site value
as if vacant.**

**Reproduction cost** is the cost of an exact replica, including its obsolete features. **Replacement
cost** is the cost of a structure of equal utility using current materials and standards. Replacement cost
is the more common basis.

**Accrued depreciation** — loss in value from any cause — comes in three forms, and distinguishing them is
the substance of the approach:

| Form | What it is | Ordinarily curable? |
|---|---|---|
| **Physical deterioration** | Wear, tear, and the action of the elements | Often, at least in part |
| **Functional obsolescence** | A defect in the structure itself — poor layout, obsolete systems, a superadequacy | Sometimes |
| **External (economic) obsolescence** | Loss caused by something **outside** the property — the neighbourhood, the market, a nuisance nearby | **No** — the cause is not on the site |

**Where it is most reliable:** **new construction** (little depreciation to estimate) and **special-purpose
properties** (too few comparable sales for the market data approach, and no income stream).

**Where it is weakest:** older buildings, where estimating accrued depreciation in all three forms becomes
the largest and least certain part of the calculation.

**Note the vocabulary trap.** Depreciation in the **appraisal** sense — loss in value from any cause — and
depreciation in the **tax** sense — a deduction taken by an owner of investment property under a schedule —
are different concepts sharing a word. **A broker does not use one to explain the other**, and the tax
question belongs to the client's accountant. **§ 12-10-404(1)(c)(V)**, **§ 12-10-405(1)(c)(V)** and
**§ 12-10-407(2)(b)(II)** each require the broker to advise the party to obtain expert advice on material
matters beyond the broker's expertise.

### C.2 The income approach

**Value is the present worth of the future income the property will produce.**

The direct capitalisation form is a single relationship, rearranged three ways:

> **Value = Net Operating Income ÷ Capitalisation Rate**
> **Net Operating Income = Value × Rate**
> **Rate = Net Operating Income ÷ Value**

**Building net operating income, in order:**

| Step | Line |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Potential gross income** — full occupancy, full collection |
| 2 | less **vacancy and collection loss** |
| 3 | plus **other income** |
| 4 | = **Effective gross income** |
| 5 | less **operating expenses** |
| 6 | = **Net operating income** |
| 7 | less **debt service** → cash flow before tax |

**Debt service comes off AFTER net operating income, not before it.** That is what makes NOI a measure of
the **property** rather than of a particular owner's financing, and it is why two buyers with different
loans can value the same building identically at the property level.

**Operating expenses** include fixed expenses, variable expenses, and reserves for replacement. They do
**not** include debt service, depreciation, or capital improvements.

**A higher capitalisation rate produces a lower value at the same income.** The rate carries the market's
view of risk: riskier income, higher rate, lower value.

### C.3 The market data (sales comparison) approach

**Comparable properties that have sold are adjusted to the subject.** It rests directly on **substitution**.

**The rule that decides every adjustment:** **adjust the COMPARABLE, never the subject.**

| If the comparable is | Adjust its sale price |
|---|---|
| **Superior** to the subject | **Downward** — it sold for more than the subject would |
| **Inferior** to the subject | **Upward** — it sold for less than the subject would |

Getting the direction backwards is the single most common error in student work, and the way to keep it
straight is to say the sentence: *the comparable had a better kitchen, so it sold for more than my subject
would have; take that difference off.*

**Elements ordinarily adjusted for:** property rights conveyed; financing terms; conditions of sale;
market conditions (time); location; physical characteristics; and use.

**Where it is most reliable:** residential property in an active market with genuine comparables — which is
most of what a Colorado broker does.

### C.4 Reconciliation

An appraiser **reconciles** the indications the approaches give — weighing each by the reliability of its
data and its applicability to the assignment — and does **not average them.** An average would give equal
weight to the least reliable indication.

## D. Investment analysis

### D.1 Gross rent multiplier

> **GRM = Sale Price ÷ Gross Rent** for the same period, and then
> **Indicated Value = Subject's Gross Rent × GRM**

**A screening tool, not the income approach.** It uses **gross** rent, so it takes no account of operating
expenses, vacancy, or the differences between two properties' operating positions. Two buildings with the
same gross rent and very different expense loads produce the same GRM indication and are not worth the
same.

**Use the same period throughout.** A multiplier built on monthly rent and applied to annual rent is out by
a factor of twelve, and that is the usual student error.

### D.2 Principles of capitalization

Direct capitalisation is stated at C.2 above. Three further ideas the Commission's outline reaches:

**The rate can be derived from the market.** If comparable sales show what investors paid for given
incomes, dividing NOI by price for each gives a rate the market itself is using.

**The overall rate covers return ON and return OF capital.** An investor requires both a return on the
money invested and its eventual recovery.

**Cash-on-cash return is a different measure from the capitalisation rate.** Cash flow before tax divided
by cash invested measures the **equity position**; the capitalisation rate measures **the property.** A
client who conflates them will misjudge leverage in both directions.

### D.3 Neighborhood

The outline puts **neighborhood** inside investment analysis, and the reason is that an income property's
future depends on the area's trajectory more than on its own condition. The analysis looks at the stage of
the neighbourhood's life cycle, the direction of rents and vacancy, the pattern of new construction, the
condition of public services and infrastructure, and access.

**The fair housing discipline from Part B applies here without qualification.** Neighbourhood analysis in
an investment context is analysis of **economic and physical** conditions, and every prospect is given the
same information and the same sources.

## E. What a Colorado broker may call their own valuation work — Rule 6.12

This is the one part of this module that states a **Colorado** requirement, and it was read in full.

> **4 CCR 725-1, Rule 6.12 — Notice Required on Competitive Market Analysis (CMA) or Broker's Price Opinion
> (BPO) for Purposes Other Than Marketing**
>
> *"When a Broker prepares a CMA or BPO **for any reason other than the anticipated sale or purchase of the
> property**, the Broker **must include a notice stating**:*
>
> ***'This evaluation was prepared by a licensed real estate broker and is not an appraisal. This evaluation
> cannot be used for the purposes of obtaining financing.'***
>
> *"Pursuant to section 12-10-602(9)(b)(II), C.R.S, **Brokers are prohibited from completing CMAs or BPOs
> that are used for the purpose of obtaining financing.** Preparation of CMAs or BPOs for reasons other than
> anticipated sale, purchase, or lease **is not considered Real Estate Brokerage Services.** As such, any
> compensation received for such preparation **is not required to be paid to the Broker's Brokerage Firm
> unless stated otherwise in Brokerage Firm's Office Policy Manual.**"*

**Four separate things in one rule:**

1. **The trigger.** Any CMA or BPO prepared for a reason **other than an anticipated sale or purchase.**
2. **The notice.** The rule prescribes **the words**, and this module states them exactly as the rule does.
3. **The prohibition.** A broker **may not** complete a CMA or BPO **used to obtain financing.** That is
   not a disclosure requirement — it is a prohibition, and the rule cites § 12-10-602(9)(b)(II) for it.
4. **The compensation consequence.** Because such preparation is **not Real Estate Brokerage Services**,
   compensation for it **need not** go to the brokerage firm — **unless the firm's office policy manual
   says otherwise.** A broker checks the manual before assuming.

**A CMA is not an appraisal, and the rule makes a broker say so in writing in the situations it covers.**
The general point stands everywhere: describing a CMA as an appraisal describes it as something it is not,
and **Rule 6.2** requires a broker to possess the necessary experience, training and knowledge — or to
decline, obtain them, obtain assistance, or co-list.

**§ 12-10-602 itself was NOT read by this project.** This module states only what **Rule 6.12** says about
it, in the rule's own words, and states **no Colorado appraisal licensing requirement, exemption, standard
or penalty.**

**Rule 6.11's square footage requirements bear on valuation work too**, and Module 03 of the Practical
Applications course delivers them; they are transcribed in
`colorado/regulatory/primary-text-readings.md` § 5.3.
