## Hour 41 — Prorations, closing calculations, and reconciliation

## 41.1 Debit and credit, once and properly

A **debit** is a charge against a party. A **credit** is an amount in that party's favour.

The whole of proration follows from one question: **has the expense been paid, and for whose period
of ownership?**

**Paid in arrears** — the bill has not yet been paid and the buyer will pay it after closing for a
period that includes the seller's ownership. So the **seller owes the buyer** for the seller's days:
**debit seller, credit buyer.** Real property taxes in most Virginia localities work this way.

**Paid in advance** — the seller has already paid through a period extending past closing. So the
**buyer owes the seller** for the buyer's days: **debit buyer, credit seller.** Association dues,
prepaid insurance where a policy is assigned, and prepaid fuel work this way.

**Rent** is a third case. Rent is collected in advance, and on the settlement date the seller holds
rent covering days the buyer will own the property: **debit seller, credit buyer** for the unearned
portion. **Security deposits** are not prorated at all — they are the tenant's money, and the entire
deposit transfers: debit seller, credit buyer, in full.

## 41.2 The conventions come from the contract

Before any arithmetic, three conventions must be settled, and **all three come from the contract, not
from habit**:

**The day count.** A 365-day year and actual days in each month, or a 360-day banker's year of twelve
30-day months. The two give different answers.

**The settlement day.** Charged to the seller or to the buyer.

**The period.** Calendar year, fiscal year, or the locality's tax year, which may not be a calendar
year.

A licensee who computes an estimate without stating the conventions used has produced a number
nobody can check.

## 41.3 Worked: taxes paid in arrears

*Annual tax $4,380. Calendar-year period, paid in arrears. Settlement 12 September. 365-day year.
Settlement day charged to the buyer.*

Daily rate: 4,380 ÷ 365 = **$12.00** per day.
Seller's days: 1 January through 11 September = 31+28+31+30+31+30+31+31+11 = **254 days**.
Seller's share: 254 × 12.00 = **$3,048.00**.

**Debit seller $3,048.00; credit buyer $3,048.00.** The buyer will pay the whole year's bill when it
falls due and has been put in funds for the seller's portion.

## 41.4 Worked: association dues paid in advance

*Quarterly assessment $465, covering 1 July to 30 September, paid by the seller on 1 July. Settlement
12 September. Actual days. Settlement day to the buyer.*

Days in the quarter: 31 + 31 + 30 = **92**.
Daily rate: 465 ÷ 92 = **$5.0543**.
Buyer's days: 12–30 September = **19 days**.
Buyer's share: 19 × 5.0543 = **$96.03**.

**Debit buyer $96.03; credit seller $96.03.**

## 41.5 Worked: rent and deposits on an occupied property

*Monthly rent $1,750, due the first, collected by the seller on 1 September. Settlement 12 September.
Security deposit held $1,750. Actual days; settlement day to the buyer.*

Days in September: **30**. Daily rent: 1,750 ÷ 30 = **$58.3333**.
Buyer's days: 12–30 = **19**. Unearned rent: 19 × 58.3333 = **$1,108.33**.

**Debit seller $1,108.33; credit buyer $1,108.33** for the rent.
**Debit seller $1,750.00; credit buyer $1,750.00** for the deposit — the whole of it, not prorated.

Note the Board dimension: a rental security deposit received by a firm is escrow money under
**18VAC135-20-181 A 2**, and its transfer must appear in the records **18VAC135-20-185 A** requires.

## 41.6 Assembling the buyer's cash and the seller's proceeds

**Buyer's cash to close** = purchase price + closing costs + prepaids and reserves + prorated items
charged to the buyer − loan amount − earnest money − seller and lender credits − prorated items
credited to the buyer.

**Seller's net proceeds** = purchase price − loan payoffs and accrued interest − brokerage fee −
seller-allocated charges − prorated items charged to the seller + prorated items credited to the
seller.

*Worked seller's side.* Price $520,000; first deed of trust payoff $311,480 including accrued
interest; brokerage fee 5% = $26,000; grantor's tax and seller-allocated settlement charges $1,940;
tax proration debit $3,048; rent and deposit debits $2,858.33; association credit $96.03.

520,000 − 311,480 − 26,000 − 1,940 − 3,048 − 2,858.33 + 96.03 = **$174,769.70**.

## 41.7 Reconciliation, and what a licensee's estimate is

Both statements must balance, and the same prorated amount must appear on both, in opposite
directions and to the penny. A prorated figure that appears as a credit on one side and a different
number as a debit on the other is an error, and finding it is what reconciliation is for.

**A licensee's net-proceeds sheet or cash-to-close estimate is an estimate.** The controlling figures
come from the lender's Closing Disclosure and the settlement agent's statement. Under
**§ 54.1-2131 A 4** the duty is ordinary care, and an estimate presented as a final figure is not
ordinary care. Every estimate should state its assumptions — the payoff figure used and its good-
through date, the tax convention, the fee, and what has been excluded.

### Hour 41 engagement check

**Question.** Settlement 20 April. Annual taxes $3,650, calendar year, paid in arrears. The seller
prepaid an annual insurance policy of $1,460 on 1 January which the buyer will not assume. A tenant
paid $2,100 rent on 1 April and holds a $2,100 deposit with the seller. Use a 365-day year and charge
the settlement day to the buyer. Produce every entry with its side.

**Expected answer.**
**Taxes:** daily 3,650 ÷ 365 = $10.00. Seller's days 1 January–19 April = 31+28+31+19 = **109**.
109 × 10.00 = **$1,090.00 — debit seller, credit buyer** (arrears).
**Insurance:** the buyer is **not assuming the policy**, so there is **no proration**; the seller
cancels and deals with the insurer. A common error is to prorate it anyway.
**Rent:** daily 2,100 ÷ 30 = $70.00. Buyer's days 20–30 April = **11**. 11 × 70.00 = **$770.00 —
debit seller, credit buyer.**
**Deposit:** **$2,100.00 in full — debit seller, credit buyer.** Deposits are never prorated.

## Hour 42 — Appraisal foundations

## 42.1 What an appraisal is

An **appraisal** is an opinion of value developed by an appraiser for an **intended user** and an
**intended use**, as of an **effective date**, with a **scope of work** the appraiser must document.
Every one of those elements changes the answer, which is why an appraisal produced for one purpose
cannot simply be reused for another.

Appraisers in Virginia are licensed or certified under a **separate board** from the Real Estate
Board, and appraisal practice is governed by professional standards. A real estate licensee's
comparative market analysis is not an appraisal, and Hour 3 explained why the distinction matters.

## 42.2 Value, price, and cost

**Value** is an opinion of what a property should bring under stated conditions. **Price** is what was
actually paid in a particular transaction, which may reflect unusual motivation or terms. **Cost** is
the expenditure to create the improvements, which may be more or less than what they add to value.

**Market value** is the classic definition and its conditions are the point: the most probable price
in a competitive and open market, under all conditions requisite to a fair sale, with buyer and
seller each acting prudently and knowledgeably, **neither under duress**, allowing a reasonable
exposure time, with payment in cash or its equivalent and financing terms typical of the market.

Strip out any one of those conditions — a distressed seller, a related-party sale, no market
exposure, unusual financing — and the price observed is not evidence of market value without
adjustment. That is precisely the screen Hour 3 applied to comparable selection.

## 42.3 The principles of value

**Supply and demand** — value moves with the relationship between them, as Hour 2 set out.

**Substitution** — a buyer will pay no more for a property than the cost of acquiring an equally
desirable substitute. This is the foundation of all three approaches to value: sales comparison,
cost, and income each ask what a substitute would cost.

**Highest and best use** — the reasonably probable use that is **legally permissible, physically
possible, financially feasible, and maximally productive**. All four tests, in that order. Land is
valued at its highest and best use as though vacant, and this is why an old house on a commercially
zoned corner may sell for land value.

**Anticipation** — value reflects expected future benefits, not past cost.

**Contribution** — a component adds value equal to what it contributes to the whole, not what it
cost. A $90,000 kitchen in a $300,000 neighbourhood does not add $90,000, and this is the principle a
seller most often needs explained.

**Conformity** — value is maximised where a property conforms reasonably to its surroundings.
**Progression** — a lesser property gains from better neighbours. **Regression** — a superior
property is dragged down by lesser ones.

**Increasing and diminishing returns** — additional investment adds value up to a point, after which
it does not.

**Competition** — excess profit invites competition, which erodes it.

**Change** — no condition is permanent; every value opinion has an effective date for that reason.

**Plottage** is the increment of value from combining adjacent parcels under one ownership;
**assemblage** is the act of combining them.

**Externalities** — influences outside the property affect its value.

## 42.4 The four forces

Value is influenced by **physical and environmental** factors (location, topography, climate,
improvements), **economic** factors (employment, income, credit, rents, construction costs),
**governmental** factors (zoning, codes, taxation, public services), and **social** factors
(population, household formation, tastes). Examination questions frequently ask which force a given
fact belongs to.

## 42.5 The appraisal process

The sequence, which is examinable:

1. **Define the problem** — client, intended user, intended use, type of value, effective date,
   property rights being appraised, and any assumptions or limiting conditions.
2. **Determine the scope of work.**
3. **Collect data** — general data about the market and specific data about the subject and
   comparables.
4. **Analyse highest and best use**, as vacant and as improved.
5. **Estimate land value** separately, which the cost approach requires.
6. **Apply the approaches to value** — sales comparison, cost, income.
7. **Reconcile** the indications into a single opinion or a range. Reconciliation is judgment, not
   averaging.
8. **Report**, in the form the assignment requires.

### Hour 42 engagement check

**Question.** A seller says, "I spent $110,000 on the addition, so the house is worth $110,000 more."
Name the principle that answers this, and name the principle that explains why an addition making the
house the largest on the street may add even less.

**Expected answer.** **Contribution** — a component adds value equal to what it contributes to the
whole, which is measured in the market and is frequently less than its cost. And **regression**,
together with **conformity**: a property that substantially exceeds the standard of its neighbourhood
is dragged toward the level of the surrounding properties, so the excess improvement contributes less
still. **Increasing and diminishing returns** describes the same effect from the investment side.

## Hour 43 — Residential sales comparison and cost approaches

## 43.1 Sales comparison, in one rule

**Adjust the comparable, never the subject.** The subject is the unknown; the comparables are the
evidence. Every adjustment answers: *what would this comparable have sold for if it had been like the
subject?*

From that follow the two directions, and they are the most-missed point in the hour:

**Comparable superior to the subject → adjust the comparable DOWN.**
**Comparable inferior to the subject → adjust the comparable UP.**

A memory hook: **CBS — Comparable Better, Subtract.**

## 43.2 The elements of comparison, in order

Appraisal practice applies adjustments in a sequence, because some are made to the whole price and
some to the already-adjusted price:

1. **Real property rights conveyed** — fee simple, leased fee, leasehold.
2. **Financing terms** — seller concessions and non-market financing.
3. **Conditions of sale** — arm's length or not.
4. **Expenditures made immediately after purchase** — a buyer who had to spend $30,000 on a roof paid
   more than the recorded price suggests.
5. **Market conditions** — the time adjustment, for the movement of the market between the comparable's
   sale date and the subject's effective date.
6. **Location.**
7. **Physical characteristics** — size, age, condition, room count, garage, quality.
8. **Economic characteristics, use, and non-realty components** — the appliances and furniture
   included in the price.

## 43.3 Worked adjustment grid

*Subject: 1,900 sq ft, 3 bed, 2 bath, 2-car garage, no fireplace, no basement.*

| | Comp A | Comp B | Comp C |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Sale price | $412,000 | $398,000 | $425,000 |
| Sold | 2 months ago | 5 months ago | 1 month ago |
| Market conditions +0.4%/month | +$3,296 | +$7,960 | +$1,700 |
| Living area | 2,000 sq ft (+100) | 1,850 (−50) | 1,900 (same) |
| Area adj. at $60/sq ft | −$6,000 | +$3,000 | $0 |
| Bathrooms | 2 | 2 | 3 (+1) |
| Bath adj. at $8,000 | $0 | $0 | −$8,000 |
| Garage | 2-car | 1-car | 2-car |
| Garage adj. | $0 | +$7,000 | $0 |
| Fireplace | none | none | one |
| Fireplace adj. | $0 | $0 | −$3,500 |
| **Net adjustment** | **−$2,704** | **+$17,960** | **−$9,800** |
| **Adjusted price** | **$409,296** | **$415,960** | **$415,200** |

Note the directions. Comp A is **larger** — superior — so the area adjustment is **negative**. Comp B
is **smaller** and has a **smaller garage** — inferior — so both adjustments are **positive**. Comp C
has an **extra bathroom and a fireplace** — superior — so both are **negative**.

## 43.4 Reconciling the grid

Indicated range: **$409,296 to $415,960.**

Reconciliation is **not averaging**. It weighs the comparables by their reliability: how few
adjustments each needed, how large the gross adjustment was, how recent the sale, and how close the
location. Comp A needed the smallest net adjustment but a moderate gross adjustment; Comp C is the
most recent and needed only physical adjustments; Comp B required a large gross adjustment and is the
weakest evidence.

A defensible conclusion on these facts is an indication near **$413,000–$415,000**, weighted toward
Comps A and C — and the reasoning, not the number, is what makes it defensible.

**Gross adjustment** — the sum of the absolute values — measures comparability. A comparable with a
small net adjustment made up of large offsetting adjustments is not a good comparable; it is a lucky
one.

## 43.5 The cost approach

**Value = land value + cost new of improvements − accrued depreciation.**

**Land value** is estimated separately, normally by sales comparison of vacant sites.

**Cost new** is either **reproduction cost** — an exact replica including its obsolete features — or
**replacement cost** — a substitute of equivalent utility using current materials and standards.
Replacement cost is used more often, because nobody would rebuild the obsolete features.

**Depreciation** is loss in value from any cause, and it has three sources:

**Physical deterioration** — wear, tear, and age. **Curable** where the cost to fix is recovered in
value; **incurable** where it is not.

**Functional obsolescence** — a defect in the design or utility of the improvement itself. A
three-bedroom house with one bathroom; a bedroom reachable only through another bedroom; a floor plan
nobody now wants. Also curable or incurable.

**External obsolescence** — loss caused by something **outside** the property: a new highway, a
closed employer, a change in the neighbourhood. **Always incurable**, because the owner cannot fix it
from inside the boundary.

*Worked:* land value $95,000; replacement cost new $310,000; physical deterioration $46,500;
functional obsolescence $12,000; external obsolescence $8,000.
95,000 + 310,000 − 46,500 − 12,000 − 8,000 = **$338,500**.

The cost approach is most reliable for **new construction** and for **special-purpose properties**
with few comparable sales, and least reliable for old improvements, where depreciation estimates
carry most of the answer.

## 43.6 Where a licensee stands

Everything in this hour is a method a licensee uses to build a **comparative market analysis**, which
is legitimate brokerage. It is not an appraisal, it is not prepared to a professional appraisal
standard, and it must never be represented as either. Presenting a CMA as an appraisal risks both a
misrepresentation finding under **18VAC135-20-300** and a problem under the separate statute
governing appraisers.

### Hour 43 engagement check

**Question.** The subject has a finished basement. Comp D, otherwise identical, has none and sold for
$389,000. Finished basements in this market contribute about $22,000. What is Comp D's adjusted
price, and state the rule you applied.

**Expected answer.** Comp D is **inferior** to the subject — it lacks a feature the subject has — so
the comparable is adjusted **upward**: 389,000 + 22,000 = **$411,000**. The rule is that adjustments
are made to the comparable, never to the subject, and a comparable inferior to the subject is adjusted
up while a comparable superior to the subject is adjusted down.

## Hour 44 — Income-producing property appraisal

## 44.1 The income statement, line by line

**Potential Gross Income (PGI)** — total income at 100% occupancy at market rent, plus other income
such as parking, laundry, and storage.

**less Vacancy and Collection Loss** — a percentage supported by market evidence, not by hope.

**= Effective Gross Income (EGI).**

**less Operating Expenses** — fixed (taxes, insurance), variable (utilities, maintenance, management,
turnover), and **replacement reserves**.

**= Net Operating Income (NOI).**

Three items are **excluded from operating expenses** and their exclusion is the single most
examinable point in the hour:

**Debt service** — the mortgage payment. NOI measures the **property's** performance, independent of
how any particular owner financed it. Including debt service would make the same building have a
different NOI for every buyer.

**Depreciation** — an accounting and tax entry, not a cash expense.

**Capital expenditures** — a new roof is capital. Replacement reserves, which spread the anticipated
cost, **are** included; the capital outlay itself is not.

*Worked:* 24 units at $1,450 per month, plus $8,400 annual laundry and parking income.
PGI = (24 × 1,450 × 12) + 8,400 = 417,600 + 8,400 = **$426,000**.
Vacancy and collection loss at 6% = 25,560. EGI = **$400,440**.
Operating expenses $168,000, including a $9,600 replacement reserve.
**NOI = 400,440 − 168,000 = $232,440.**

## 44.2 Direct capitalization

**Value = NOI ÷ Capitalization Rate**, usually written **V = I ÷ R**.

The relationships are worth stating because examinations test them: **I = V × R** and **R = I ÷ V**.

*Continuing the example, market cap rate 6.75%:*
V = 232,440 ÷ 0.0675 = **$3,443,555.56**, say **$3,440,000**.

**The cap rate is derived from the market**, by dividing the NOI of comparable sold properties by
their sale prices — not chosen. Two consequences a licensee must be able to state:

**A higher cap rate produces a lower value** at the same NOI. Cap rates rise with perceived risk and
with the cost of capital, so a market where rates rise is a market where values fall even if rents do
not change.

**Small changes in the rate move value a great deal.** At the same $232,440 NOI, a rate of 7.25%
gives $3,206,069 — a drop of about $237,000 for half a percentage point. This is why the derivation
of the rate matters more than the arithmetic.

## 44.3 Gross rent and gross income multipliers

A **gross rent multiplier (GRM)** relates sale price to **monthly gross rent**; a **gross income
multiplier (GIM)** relates it to **annual gross income**.

**GRM = Sale Price ÷ Monthly Gross Rent.** Then **Value = GRM × subject's monthly gross rent.**

*Worked:* three comparable sales give GRMs of 14.2, 14.8, and 14.5; the subject's monthly gross rent
is $29,000. Using 14.5: **Value = 14.5 × 29,000 = $420,500.**

**What the multiplier omits is the whole point.** It uses **gross** rent, so it ignores vacancy,
ignores operating expenses, and ignores differences in expense structure between properties. Two
buildings with identical gross rents and very different expense ratios have very different NOIs and
very different values, and the GRM cannot see it. It is a screening tool for small residential income
property, not a valuation method for anything serious.

## 44.4 Reserves, and the honest number

The most common way an income statement is manipulated is by omitting the **replacement reserve**.
Leave it out and NOI rises; capitalise the inflated NOI and value rises with it. A pro forma with no
reserve line is describing a building that never needs a roof.

The second most common is presenting **market rent** as though it were **contract rent** — the rent
actually being collected under existing leases. A building with below-market leases is worth what its
leases produce until they roll, and the difference is a timing and risk question, not a free upgrade.

## 44.5 The licensee's limits, one more time

A licensee may compute these figures, may present an owner's actual operating statements, and may
show how a value indication follows from them. A licensee may **not** appraise, may not warrant
income and expense figures supplied by an owner, and may not tell an investor what return they will
achieve. Under **§ 54.1-2131 A 4** the standard is ordinary care, and passing on unverified figures as
fact fails it. State the source of every number, and say what has not been verified.

### Hour 44 engagement check

**Question.** A 16-unit building rents at $1,275 per unit per month with $4,800 other annual income.
Vacancy and collection loss is 7%. Operating expenses are $101,000 including an $8,000 replacement
reserve, and the owner also pays $146,000 in annual debt service. Market cap rate 6.5%. Compute NOI
and value, and state what you did with the debt service and why.

**Expected answer.**
PGI = (16 × 1,275 × 12) + 4,800 = 244,800 + 4,800 = **$249,600**.
Vacancy and collection loss at 7% = 17,472. **EGI = $232,128.**
**NOI = 232,128 − 101,000 = $131,128.**
**Value = 131,128 ÷ 0.065 = $2,017,353.85**, say **$2,017,000**.
**Debt service is excluded** from operating expenses. NOI measures the property's own performance
independent of any particular owner's financing; including debt service would give the same building a
different NOI for every buyer. The **$8,000 replacement reserve is correctly included** as an
operating expense, unlike a capital outlay itself.

## Instructor source note for Module 11

Virginia statements rest on text read directly from law.lis.virginia.gov on 2026-08-20:
**§ 54.1-2131 A 4** (ordinary care), **18VAC135-20-181 A 2** (rental security deposits are escrow
funds), **18VAC135-20-185 A** (records of receipt and disposition of funds), and **18VAC135-20-300**
(misrepresentation).

**Appraisal is regulated in Virginia by a different board than the Real Estate Board**, and this
course states no appraiser qualification, standard, or requirement. Hours 42 through 44 teach
appraisal **method** because 18VAC135-20-400(20) requires appraisal of residential and income
producing property to be included in the course, and because a licensee uses the method to build a
comparative market analysis. Hours 42.1, 43.6 and 44.5 each state the boundary expressly.

All arithmetic is worked in full so a reviewer can verify each figure. The prices, rates, adjustment
amounts, cap rates and multipliers are **illustrative and are not market data for any Virginia
locality.** The adjustment values in Hour 43.3 in particular are teaching figures; real adjustments
are derived from the market by paired sales analysis.

Proration conventions in Hour 41 — the 365-day year and charging the settlement day to the buyer —
are stated as **conventions taken from the contract**, and Hour 41.2 says so before any arithmetic is
performed.
