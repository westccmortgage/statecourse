## Learning objectives

By the end of this module the student will be able to:

- Classify an interest in land as freehold or leasehold and name the present and future interests
  in a life-estate arrangement.
- Distinguish severalty, tenancy in common, joint tenancy, and tenancy by the entirety, and state
  what happens on death and on transfer under each.
- Identify an easement by type and by how it was created, and distinguish it from a licence, an
  encroachment, and a restrictive covenant.
- State the four powers of government over land and match a fact pattern to the correct one.
- Explain how zoning, subdivision control, and the comprehensive plan relate to one another, and
  what a variance, a special use permit, and a nonconforming use each are.

# Hour 5 — Estates in land and future interests

## 5.1 What an estate is

An **estate** is the degree, quantity, nature, and extent of a person's interest in land. Two
questions define it: *what may the holder do?* and *for how long?* Everything in this hour is an
answer to the second question.

The primary division is between **freehold** estates, which are of indefinite duration and involve
ownership, and **leasehold** estates, which are of fixed or determinable duration and involve
possession without ownership. A tenant has an estate in land. It is simply not a freehold.

## 5.2 Fee simple

**Fee simple absolute** is the largest estate the law recognises. It is of potentially infinite
duration, freely transferable during life and at death, and subject only to the four government
powers and to any private restrictions the owner or a predecessor accepted. Unless a deed says
otherwise, a conveyance of land is presumed to convey a fee simple absolute.

**Defeasible fees** are fee estates that may end if a stated condition occurs. Two forms matter:

A **fee simple determinable** ends automatically when the stated event occurs. The classic
language is durational — "so long as", "while", "during", "until". The grantor keeps a
**possibility of reverter**, and title returns without any action being taken.

A **fee simple subject to a condition subsequent** does not end automatically; it gives the
grantor the **right of re-entry**, which must be exercised. The classic language is conditional —
"but if", "provided that", "on condition that".

The practical significance for a licensee is that a defeasible fee is a title matter, it appears
in the record, and it belongs to the title examiner and the buyer's attorney. Discrimination-based
conditions in old instruments are unenforceable, and in Virginia § 36-96.3 A 6 makes it an
unlawful discriminatory housing practice to include such a covenant in a transfer or to honour or
attempt to honour one.

## 5.3 Life estates

A **life estate** is a freehold measured by someone's life. The holder — the **life tenant** — has
full use and possession, may lease or sell what they have, and takes the income. What they cannot
do is convey more than they own, and they may not commit **waste**: they must not damage the
property or allow it to deteriorate, and they must ordinarily pay the taxes and the interest on
existing debt.

When the measuring life ends, the estate ends and someone takes possession:

- If the property returns to the grantor or the grantor's heirs, the grantor's retained interest
  is a **reversion**.
- If it passes to a named third party, that party holds a **remainder**.

A **life estate pur autre vie** is measured by the life of someone other than the holder. It is the
one life estate that can be inherited — if the holder dies while the measuring life continues, the
remaining estate passes to the holder's heirs until the measuring life ends.

**Legal life estates** arise by operation of law rather than by grant. Virginia's marital property
regime has changed over time and the treatment of dower and curtesy is a matter of statute and
transitional law; a course document should not state a rule here without reading Title 64.2, and
this course does not. What a licensee needs to know is that a spouse may have an interest that
must be released at closing, and that determining it is a title and legal question, not a
brokerage one.

## 5.4 Leasehold estates named

Four, in decreasing order of definiteness:

**Estate for years** — a fixed term with a definite beginning and end. It expires on its own; no
notice is required. The name is misleading: a two-week lease is an estate for years.

**Periodic estate** — a term that renews automatically for successive periods until one party
gives notice. Month-to-month tenancies are the common case.

**Estate at will** — possession with the landlord's consent, for no fixed period, terminable by
either party. Statutes generally impose notice requirements.

**Estate at sufferance** — a tenant who came in lawfully and stayed past the term without consent.
This is the weakest interest recognised; it is distinguished from trespass only by the lawful
entry.

Virginia's residential landlord-tenant treatment is Hour 32. Leases as contracts are Hour 31.

## Hour 5 engagement check

**Question.** A grants Blackacre "to B for life, then to C." A dies. Then B dies. Who owns
Blackacre at each stage, and what is C's interest called before B's death?

**Expected answer.** On the grant, B holds a life estate and C holds a vested remainder in fee
simple. A retains nothing, because the grant disposed of the entire estate; A's death changes
nothing. On B's death the life estate ends and C's remainder becomes possessory — C holds fee
simple. Had the grant said only "to B for life", A would have retained a reversion and A's heirs
would take on B's death.

# Hour 6 — Forms of ownership and home ownership

## 6.1 Ownership in severalty

**Severalty** means ownership by one person or one legal entity alone. The word misleads students
constantly — it comes from "severed", not "several". A sole owner may deal with the property
freely, and on death it passes by will or by intestate succession.

## 6.2 Concurrent ownership

**Tenancy in common** is the default when two or more people take title without specifying
otherwise. Each holds an **undivided fractional interest** — undivided meaning each has the right
to possess the whole, not a marked-off portion. Shares need not be equal. There is **no right of
survivorship**: on death an owner's share passes to that owner's heirs or devisees, who become
tenants in common with the survivors. Any co-tenant may transfer their share without the others'
consent, and any co-tenant may seek **partition**.

**Joint tenancy** adds the **right of survivorship**: on the death of a joint tenant, that
tenant's interest passes to the surviving joint tenants outside probate. The common-law
requirements are the four unities — **time, title, interest, possession** — acquired at the same
time, by the same instrument, in equal shares, with equal rights of possession. Survivorship is
not presumed; in Virginia it must be expressed in the instrument. A joint tenant who conveys their
interest destroys the joint tenancy as to that share, and the transferee takes as a tenant in
common.

**Tenancy by the entirety** is available only to spouses and treats them as a single owner. It
carries survivorship, and neither spouse may convey or encumber the property alone. Its practical
significance is creditor protection: a creditor of one spouse alone generally cannot reach
property held by the entirety. Virginia recognises this form, and it is a common way for married
couples to hold a home.

**Community property** is a marital-property system used in a minority of states. **Virginia is
not a community property state.** A student who learned the term from a national text should know
it does not describe Virginia title.

## 6.3 Entity and trust ownership

Property may be owned by a **corporation**, a **partnership**, a **limited liability company**, or
a **trust**. Two consequences for a licensee: first, the entity's authority to sign is a legal
question, evidenced by organisational documents and resolutions, and it is verified by the
settlement agent and counsel, not by the licensee. Second, an entity in a residential transaction
does not change the licensee's duties.

**Trusts** separate legal and beneficial ownership: the trustee holds legal title and manages,
the beneficiary takes the benefit. A **living trust** is a common estate-planning vehicle for a
residence, and the trustee is the party who signs.

## 6.4 Common-interest ownership, introduced

Two forms will recur throughout the course and are covered fully in Hours 29 and 30.

A **condominium** divides a project into individually owned **units** and **common elements**
owned in undivided shares by all unit owners. **Limited common elements** — a balcony, an assigned
parking space — serve fewer than all units.

A **cooperative** is a corporation that owns the building; the resident owns shares in the
corporation and holds a proprietary lease of an apartment. The resident owns personal property,
not real property, which changes financing and title work entirely.

A property owners' association governs a subdivision of separately owned lots and enforces
covenants; it is not a condominium, and Virginia treats the two under separate statutory schemes.

## 6.5 Home ownership as a decision

Required subject 10 is **home ownership**, and it deserves to be taught honestly rather than as
advocacy.

The financial case for owning includes forced saving through amortisation, a hedge against rent
inflation, the possibility of appreciation, potential tax treatment, and control over the property.
The costs include the transaction costs of buying and selling, which are large and are paid twice;
property taxes and insurance; maintenance and capital replacement, which do not stop; the
illiquidity of the asset; the concentration of household wealth into one undiversified holding;
and the loss of mobility.

**Appreciation is not guaranteed.** A licensee who tells a client that a house is a sure
investment is making a representation about future value that no one can support, and 18VAC135-20-300
makes misrepresentation a disciplinary matter. The competent formulation is to identify the
factors and let the client, with their own advisers, decide.

Tax treatment is the clearest example of the boundary of the licence. Mortgage interest and
property-tax deductions, the exclusion of gain on a principal residence, and the interaction with
the standard deduction all depend on the individual's circumstances and on law that changes.
**Refer the client to a tax professional and say so in writing.**

## Hour 6 engagement check

**Question.** Two unmarried siblings take title as joint tenants with right of survivorship. One
sibling sells their interest to a third party. Describe the resulting ownership, and describe it
again if instead the selling sibling had died still owning the interest.

**Expected answer.** On sale, the unity of time and title is broken as to that share: the buyer
takes as a **tenant in common** with the remaining sibling, and there is no survivorship between
them. Had the sibling died still owning, the right of survivorship would have operated and the
surviving sibling would hold the whole in severalty, outside probate.

# Hour 7 — Private rights, easements, and encumbrances

## 7.1 Encumbrances

An **encumbrance** is any claim, right, or interest held by someone other than the owner that
affects title or limits use. Encumbrances do not prevent transfer; they travel with the land.
They fall into two families: those that affect **use** — easements, restrictive covenants,
encroachments — and those that affect **title as security for money**, which are **liens** and are
Hour 13.

## 7.2 Easements

An **easement** is a non-possessory right to use another's land for a stated purpose. It is an
interest in land, and it is created and transferred by written instrument or by operation of law.

An **easement appurtenant** benefits a parcel. The benefited parcel is the **dominant tenement**
and the burdened parcel is the **servient tenement**. It runs with the land: it passes to the next
owner automatically, whether or not anyone mentions it. This is the easement in most residential
transactions — shared driveways, access to a back lot, drainage.

An **easement in gross** benefits a *person or entity* rather than a parcel. Utility easements are
the everyday case, and commercial easements in gross are assignable.

**Creation.** By **express grant or reservation** in a deed. By **implication**, where a use was
apparent, continuous, and necessary at the time a parcel was divided. By **necessity**, the
landlocked-parcel case, which requires common ownership at the time of severance. By
**prescription**, from open, notorious, continuous, hostile use for the statutory period — this is
a legal determination, and the Virginia period must be read from statute before any document
states it, which this course does not do. By **condemnation**, when a public body takes an
easement rather than the fee.

**Termination.** By merger, when one person acquires both tenements. By release from the dominant
owner. By abandonment, which requires conduct showing intent and not mere non-use. By the end of
the necessity, for an easement by necessity. By express terms.

## 7.3 Licence, encroachment, party wall

A **licence** is permission to do something on another's land that would otherwise be a trespass.
It is *personal, revocable, and not an interest in land*. Permission to park in a neighbour's
drive is a licence; if it were written, permanent, and running with the land it would be an
easement. The distinction decides whether the right survives a sale.

An **encroachment** is an unauthorised physical intrusion — a fence, a roof overhang, a driveway —
onto adjoining land. It is discovered by a survey, not by looking. It is both a title problem and
a potential dispute, and long-standing encroachments can mature into prescriptive rights. When one
is suspected, the professional answer is a survey and, if it exists, legal advice — not a licensee's
opinion about where the line is.

A **party wall** is a shared wall on a boundary, with each owner typically owning to the centre
and holding an easement of support in the other half.

## 7.4 Restrictive covenants

**Restrictive covenants** are private limits on use, imposed by a developer or by agreement, and
recorded so they bind successors. They regulate setbacks, minimum sizes, materials, outbuildings,
signage, use, and much else, and in a common-interest community they are enforced by the
association.

Three points a licensee must hold:

**They are private law, and they can be stricter than zoning.** Where zoning permits and a covenant
forbids, the covenant governs the owner as a matter of private obligation.

**They are enforced privately** — by an association or a neighbour, by injunction or damages — not
by the locality.

**Discriminatory covenants are void and must not be honoured.** § 36-96.3 A 6 makes it an unlawful
discriminatory housing practice to include such a covenant in a transfer, sale, rental or lease, or
for any person to honour or exercise, or attempt to honour or exercise, such a covenant. Historic
racial covenants remain physically present in many recorded chains of title in Virginia; they are
unenforceable, and repeating them as though they operated would itself be a violation.

## 7.5 What the licensee actually does

The licensee is not the person who determines the existence, scope, or validity of an easement.
The licensee is the person who **notices** that a question exists and routes it correctly: to the
title examiner for what is recorded, to a surveyor for where things physically are, and to counsel
for what the instrument means. Under § 54.1-2131 A 6 the duty is to disclose material facts of
which the licensee has **actual knowledge** — a shared driveway the licensee knows about is a
material fact; the legal effect of the instrument creating it is not the licensee's to opine on.

## Hour 7 engagement check

**Question.** A buyer is told by a neighbour, "your seller has always let me cut across the back
corner to reach my dock." The arrangement is not in any recorded instrument. What is it most
likely to be, what is the risk to the buyer, and what should the licensee do?

**Expected answer.** On these facts it is most likely a **licence** — personal and revocable —
because nothing was written and nothing was recorded. The risk is that long, open, continuous and
hostile use could have ripened into a **prescriptive easement**, which would bind the buyer, and
that is a legal determination on facts nobody in the conversation has established. The licensee
should disclose the conversation as a known material fact, and direct the buyer to the title
examiner and to counsel, and to a survey. The licensee must not tell the buyer whether the
neighbour has a right.

# Hour 8 — Public controls, land use, and development

## 8.1 The four government powers

Every limitation government places on land traces to one of four powers. The mnemonic is **PETE**.

**Police power** — the power to regulate for public health, safety, morals, and general welfare.
It is the source of zoning, building codes, subdivision regulation, and environmental controls.
No compensation is paid, because nothing is taken; use is regulated. In Virginia the power belongs
to the Commonwealth and is delegated to localities by enabling legislation in Title 15.2.

**Eminent domain** — the power to take private property for public use, with **just compensation**.
The taking is executed through a **condemnation** proceeding. A partial taking is common — a strip
for road widening — and compensation covers damage to the remainder.

**Taxation** — the power to levy against property to fund government, backed by a lien and
ultimately by sale. Hour 14.

**Escheat** — the reversion of property to the Commonwealth when an owner dies with no will and no
heirs. Rare, and the reason the list is four rather than three.

The line between police power and eminent domain is where regulatory takings litigation lives.
That is a constitutional question and a matter for counsel.

## 8.2 The comprehensive plan and zoning

A **comprehensive plan** is a locality's long-range statement of intended growth, land use,
transportation, and public facilities. It is a policy document. **Zoning** is the ordinance that
implements it and has legal force.

Zoning divides a locality into districts and prescribes, for each, the permitted **uses** and the
**bulk** controls — height, lot area, lot coverage, setbacks, density, parking, and frequently
signage. Districts are conventionally residential, commercial, industrial, agricultural, and mixed,
with numbered subdistricts of differing intensity.

Three release valves exist, and students confuse them:

A **variance** relieves a property owner from a **bulk or dimensional** requirement where strict
application would cause an unnecessary hardship arising from the property itself — an unusual
shape, topography, or size. A variance is granted by a board of zoning appeals. **A variance does
not authorise a use the district forbids**, and self-created hardship and mere financial
disadvantage are not grounds.

A **special use permit** — also called a conditional use permit — authorises a use the ordinance
lists as permissible in the district *subject to review and conditions*. It is not an exception to
the ordinance; it is the ordinance operating as written.

A **nonconforming use** is a use that was lawful when established and has since been made
non-compliant by a change in the ordinance. It is generally allowed to continue — "grandfathered"
— but typically may not be expanded, and may be lost if abandoned or destroyed. A licensee
marketing such a property should never assume the right survives a change of ownership or a
period of vacancy; that is a question for the zoning administrator in writing.

A **rezoning** is an amendment of the ordinance itself, granted by the governing body, and is a
legislative act.

## 8.3 Subdivision and development

Creating new lots from a parcel is **subdivision**, and it is regulated separately from zoning. A
typical sequence: concept plan; preliminary plat, reviewed against the subdivision ordinance;
engineering and public-improvement plans for streets, water, sewer, and stormwater; construction
of improvements, usually secured by a bond or letter of credit; final plat, which is recorded and
which legally creates the lots; and dedication of streets and utilities to public control.

**A lot does not exist until the plat is recorded.** Selling "lot 14" from an unrecorded
preliminary plan is selling something that has no legal description.

Development also passes through environmental review. In Virginia, the **Chesapeake Bay
Preservation Act** creates resource protection areas in localities that adopt implementing
ordinances, and § 55.1-703 expressly tells buyers to investigate whether such an ordinance affects
the property. Wetlands, floodplains, and stormwater requirements can each make a physically
buildable lot unbuildable.

## 8.4 The licensee's boundary, again

Everything in this hour is determined by a public body and recorded in a public office. The
licensee's job is to know that the questions exist, to know **who** answers each one — zoning
administrator, planning department, building official, health department for a well or septic,
VDOT for road access — and to send the client there in writing. Answering a zoning question from
memory is how licensees create liability, and under 18VAC135-20-300 an inaccurate statement of a
material fact is a disciplinary matter regardless of good intentions.

Note also the boundary § 54.1-2131 B draws: a listing licensee's disclosure duty covers material
adverse facts about the **physical condition of the property** actually known, and the statute
says that term does **not** include matters relating to governmental land use regulations, matters
outside the boundaries of the land, or matters relating to highways or public streets. That is not
a licence to conceal — it is a statement of what the physical-condition duty covers. Other duties,
including the duty of ordinary care and the client-side duty to disclose material facts under
§ 54.1-2131 A 6, operate independently.

## Hour 8 engagement check

**Question.** A buyer wants to run a small dog-grooming business from a house in a district that
permits only single-family dwellings and home occupations of a listed kind not including animal
care. The lot is unusually narrow. Which relief mechanism, if any, fits — variance, special use
permit, or rezoning — and why?

**Expected answer.** Not a variance: the narrow lot is a dimensional problem, and a variance
relieves bulk requirements, not use prohibitions. Not a special use permit either, on these facts,
because the ordinance does not list this use as permissible in the district subject to conditions.
The only route that reaches the desired use is a **rezoning or a text amendment to the ordinance**,
which is a legislative act of the governing body with no guarantee of success. The licensee's
correct action is to refer the buyer to the zoning administrator in writing before the buyer relies
on the intended use, and never to advise that the use "should be fine".

## Instructor source note for Module 02

Virginia-specific statements in this module rest on: **§ 36-96.3 A 6** (discriminatory covenants
may not be included, honoured, or exercised); **§ 54.1-2131 A 6 and B** (client disclosure of
material facts of actual knowledge; the physical-condition duty and the three matters the statute
excludes from it); **§ 55.1-703** (buyer due diligence, including Chesapeake Bay Preservation Act
resource protection areas); and **18VAC135-20-300** (misrepresentation or omission). All were read
from law.lis.virginia.gov on 2026-08-20 and are recorded in
`virginia/regulatory/primary-text-readings.md`.

Two rules are deliberately **not** stated in this module because the primary text has not been
read: the **prescriptive-easement period** in Virginia, and the current treatment of **legal life
estates and marital interests** under Title 64.2. Both are identified to the student as questions
for counsel rather than answered. They must be read before any instructor supplements this text
with a figure.

Zoning and subdivision procedure varies by locality under the enabling legislation in **Title
15.2**; the sequence in Hour 8.3 is the general pattern and is taught as such, not as any
particular locality's ordinance.
