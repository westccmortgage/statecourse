## Hour 29 — Home ownership, associations, and common-interest communities

## 29.1 What a common interest community is

A **common interest community** is a development in which ownership of a unit or lot carries with it
mandatory membership in an association and an obligation to pay assessments for commonly held or
commonly maintained property. The obligation runs with the land: it is not a club a buyer joins, and
it cannot be resigned from.

Virginia governs the forms separately, in Title 55.1:

- **Chapter 18 — Property Owners' Association Act.** Subdivisions of separately owned lots.
- **Chapter 19 — Virginia Condominium Act.** Condominiums.
- **Chapter 20 — Horizontal Property Act.** The older condominium statute, still governing some
  older projects.
- **Chapter 21 — Virginia Real Estate Cooperative Act.** Cooperatives.
- **Chapter 22 — Virginia Real Estate Time-Share Act.** Time-shares.
- **Chapter 23.1 — Resale Disclosure Act.** The resale disclosure regime for all of them, and the
  subject of Hour 30.

There is also a separate regulator. The **Common Interest Community Board** licenses common interest
community managers and registers associations, under Title 54.1; § 55.1-2314 D gives it power to
assess monetary penalties and issue cease and desist orders for resale-certificate failures. A
licensee should know that the association side of a transaction has its own regulator.

## 29.2 Condominium

A condominium divides a project into:

**Units** — the individually owned volumes of space, described by reference to a recorded
condominium plat and, because a unit is a volume, by elevation as well as footprint.

**Common elements** — everything else, owned by all unit owners as undivided interests: the land,
the structure, the roof, the corridors, the mechanical systems.

**Limited common elements** — common elements reserved for the use of fewer than all units. A
balcony serving one unit, an assigned parking space, a storage locker.

The unit owner's percentage interest in the common elements is fixed by the declaration and usually
determines both the voting power and the share of assessments.

The governing instruments are the **declaration**, which creates the condominium and defines the
units and common elements; the **bylaws**, which govern the association's operation; the recorded
**plats and plans**; and the **rules and regulations** adopted by the board.

## 29.3 Property owners' association

A POA governs a subdivision of **separately owned lots**. Each owner owns their lot in fee, and the
association owns or maintains the common areas — the entrance, the private streets if any, the
stormwater pond, the pool. The instruments are the **declaration of covenants, conditions and
restrictions**, the **articles and bylaws**, and the **rules**.

The critical difference from a condominium: in a POA the owner owns land and the structure on it,
and the association's authority comes from the recorded covenants rather than from a division of the
building itself.

## 29.4 Cooperative

In a cooperative, a **corporation owns the real property**. The resident buys **shares** in the
corporation and receives a **proprietary lease** of an apartment.

The consequence that changes everything: the resident owns **personal property**, not real property.
There is no deed. Financing is a share loan rather than a deed of trust. Title work, transfer taxes,
and the whole settlement mechanic differ. And the corporation's board generally has approval rights
over a transfer — subject, always, to fair housing law, which applies to a cooperative board's
decisions as it does to anyone else's.

## 29.5 Assessments, budgets, and reserves

**Regular assessments** fund the annual budget: insurance for what the association insures,
utilities for common areas, landscaping and maintenance, management, and a contribution to reserves.

**Special assessments** fund something the budget did not — a roof, a private road, litigation.
They are the single largest financial risk in common-interest ownership, and a buyer who has not
read the minutes and the reserve study has not investigated it.

**Reserves and the reserve study.** A reserve study estimates the remaining life and replacement
cost of major components and derives the annual funding required. A community with a large deferred
liability and a thin reserve is a community heading toward a special assessment, whatever the
current dues are.

**Assessment liens.** Unpaid assessments become a lien against the unit or lot under the governing
statute, and unpaid assessments are precisely what a resale certificate exists to reveal.

**Insurance.** The association's master policy covers what the declaration says it covers, and the
owner's policy — an HO-6 in a condominium — covers the rest. The boundary between them is set by
the declaration, and neither the licensee nor the buyer should assume it. Hour 49 develops this.

## 29.6 What the buyer must be told to investigate

A buyer due-diligence list a licensee should be able to produce from memory: the declaration and
bylaws; the rules, including any restrictions on leasing, pets, parking, vehicles, signs and
exterior alterations; the current budget and dues; the reserve study and reserve balance; the last
two years of board and member minutes, where special assessments are first discussed; the status of
any litigation; any pending or threatened special assessment; the insurance summary and what the
owner must insure separately; and the association's approval or right-of-first-refusal provisions.

Leasing restrictions deserve their own mention. A buyer who intends to rent the unit and who
discovers after closing that the community caps rentals or imposes a waiting period has lost the
entire basis of their purchase. **Ask the question before the offer, and get the answer in writing.**

### Hour 29 engagement check

**Question.** A buyer is comparing a condominium unit and a house in a POA, and says "the dues are
$310 a month in one and $95 a quarter in the other, so the second is cheaper." What is wrong with
the comparison, and what would you tell the buyer to obtain?

**Expected answer.** The comparison is between two different things. In a **condominium** the
association typically maintains and insures the building itself, so a large part of what an owner
would otherwise pay directly — roof, structure, exterior, often water and master insurance — is
inside the dues. In a **POA** the owner owns the lot and the structure and pays for its maintenance,
insurance and replacement personally, so a low assessment reflects a narrower scope, not a lower
cost of ownership. The buyer should obtain, for each: the declaration and bylaws, the current
budget, the **reserve study and reserve balance**, two years of minutes, any pending or threatened
**special assessment**, the insurance summary showing the boundary between master and owner
coverage, and the leasing, pet and alteration restrictions.

## Hour 30 — Subdivisions, condominiums, and the Resale Disclosure Act

## 30.1 Creating the community

Hour 8 covered subdivision procedure: concept plan, preliminary plat, public improvement plans,
construction secured by bond or letter of credit, and a **recorded final plat** that legally creates
the lots. A condominium is created by recording a **declaration** together with the plats and plans.

For a **new** unit or lot sold by the declarant, a **public offering statement** regime applies under
the Condominium Act and the Property Owners' Association Act, with its own delivery and cancellation
rules. **Resales** — every subsequent sale — are governed by Chapter 23.1, and that is what a
salesperson meets in practice.

## 30.2 The Resale Disclosure Act — Chapter 23.1

This chapter is recent — enacted 2023 and amended in 2024 and 2025 — and it **replaced** the older,
separate resale-certificate provisions that sat inside the Condominium Act and the Property Owners'
Association Act. National course material and older Virginia material frequently still describe the
old regime. Read the chapter.

**§ 55.1-2308 — what the contract itself must disclose.** Unless exempt under § 55.1-2317, any
contract for the resale of a unit in a common interest community shall disclose:

**(i)** that the unit is **located in a common interest community**;
**(ii)** that the **seller or the seller's agent is required to obtain from the association a resale
certificate and provide it** to the purchaser or the purchaser's agent;
**(iii)** the **purchaser's right to cancel** the contract under § 55.1-2312;
**(iv)** that the purchaser or purchaser's agent **may request an updated resale certificate** under
§ 55.1-2311; and
**(v)** that the purchaser's right to receive the resale certificate and the right to cancel are
**waived conclusively if not exercised before settlement.**

And the remedy for a contract that omits them: **the purchaser's sole remedy is to cancel the
contract prior to settlement.**

**§ 55.1-2309 — delivery.** The **seller or the seller's agent shall obtain the resale certificate
from the association and provide it** to the purchaser or the purchaser's agent, and — the words
matter — **this requirement shall not be waived or changed by agreement.**

The association, its managing agent, or a third party preparing the certificate **shall deliver it
within 14 days after a written request** by the seller or the seller's agent. **If no certificate is
delivered within 14 days, the resale certificate is deemed unavailable.** The association **may not
require the purchaser's name** before preparing it. Delivery to the seller may be printed or in a
generally accepted electronic format as the seller requests. The information must be **current as of
a date specified on the certificate**.

**§ 55.1-2311** provides for an **updated resale certificate** and a financial update.
**§ 55.1-2310** prescribes the form and contents. **§ 55.1-2316** governs fees.

## 30.3 The cancellation right — § 55.1-2312

This is the section a licensee will use most, and its structure is a set of three timing rules.

**A — certificate or unavailability notice delivered *before* ratification.** The purchaser may
cancel within the period **agreed by all parties in the ratified contract**; if the contract
specifies no period, **three days from ratification**.

**B — delivered *after* ratification.** Cancel within the agreed period; if none is specified,
**three days from receipt** of the certificate or of the notice of unavailability.

**C — never delivered at all.** The purchaser **may cancel at any time prior to settlement.**

**D — the notice.** Written notice of cancellation must be given to the seller in accordance with the
contract's terms, and **the purchaser bears the burden of demonstrating delivery** of the notice.
That single sentence is why a purchaser's licensee sends cancellation by a method that produces
proof and retains it.

**E — more than one association.** Where a unit is governed by more than one association, the
cancellation period runs **from the date of delivery of the last resale certificate.**
§ 55.1-2315 addresses properties subject to more than one declaration.

**F — the consequence.** Cancellation is **without penalty**, and the seller shall cause any deposit
or escrowed funds to be **returned promptly** to the purchaser.

## 30.4 When the association gets it wrong — § 55.1-2314

**A.** If the association, its managing agent, or a third party preparing the certificate fails to
comply with § 55.1-2310 or § 55.1-2311, the **purchaser is not required to pay delinquent
assessments or to remedy violations of the governing documents or rules existing as of the date of
the certificate or updated certificate.** The association may enforce a **previous owner's**
violation against the purchaser only if (i) the violation was **properly noted** in the certificate
or (ii) the **seller failed to provide** the certificate as § 55.1-2309 requires.

**B.** The purchaser must nonetheless abide by the governing documents and rules **as to matters
arising after acquiring the unit**, certificate or no certificate.

**C.** The **preparer is liable to the seller** for actual damages **not exceeding $1,000**.

**D.** The **Common Interest Community Board** may assess a monetary penalty against an association
under § 54.1-2351 or a common interest community manager under § 54.1-2349, and may issue a cease
and desist order under § 54.1-2349 or § 54.1-2352.

## 30.5 What this means for the licensee's practice

Four operating rules follow directly:

**Request the certificate in writing, immediately, and keep the request.** The 14-day clock in
§ 55.1-2309 B runs from a **written** request, and the deemed-unavailable consequence depends on it.

**Confirm the contract carries the § 55.1-2308 disclosures.** Their absence gives the purchaser a
right to cancel before settlement and gives the seller a problem.

**Diary the cancellation period the moment of delivery is known** — three days from ratification,
three days from receipt, or open until settlement, depending on which of § 55.1-2312 A, B or C
applies, and adjusted by any period the parties agreed.

**Never agree to waive the seller's obligation.** § 55.1-2309 A says it **shall not be waived or
changed by agreement**, and a licensee who drafts around it has drafted something ineffective.

### Hour 30 engagement check

**Question.** A contract for a condominium resale is ratified 3 June and specifies no cancellation
period. The seller's agent requests the resale certificate in writing on 5 June. Nothing arrives.
On 24 June the buyer, having heard about a pending roof assessment, wants out. What is the position?

**Expected answer.** The written request was made 5 June, so under **§ 55.1-2309 B** the 14 days ran
to 19 June; nothing having been delivered, the certificate is **deemed unavailable** — but the buyer
was never given the certificate *or* a notice of unavailability. That puts the buyer within
**§ 55.1-2312 C**: where neither the certificate nor a notice of unavailability has been delivered,
the purchaser **may cancel at any time prior to settlement**. Cancellation is **without penalty**
under subsection F and the deposit must be returned promptly. The buyer must give **written notice
in accordance with the contract's terms**, and under subsection D **bears the burden of proving
delivery** — so the notice goes by a method that produces proof, and the proof is retained.

## Hour 31 — Leasehold estates and lease agreements

## 31.1 What a lease is

A **lease** is simultaneously a **conveyance** — it transfers a leasehold estate and the right of
exclusive possession — and a **contract** — it creates mutual promises. Both characters matter:
the conveyance explains why a tenant may exclude even the landlord except as the lease and the law
allow; the contract explains why breach analysis works as it does.

The parties are the **lessor** (landlord), who retains a **reversion**, and the **lessee** (tenant),
who holds the leasehold.

## 31.2 The four leasehold estates, applied

From Hour 5: **estate for years** (fixed term, expires without notice), **periodic estate**
(automatically renewing until notice), **estate at will** (no fixed period, terminable by either),
and **estate at sufferance** (holdover after a lawful entry).

The everyday classification errors: calling a one-year lease a "periodic" tenancy because rent is
paid monthly — it is an estate for years, and the payment interval is not the term; and assuming a
holdover tenant is a trespasser — they are a tenant at sufferance, and removing them is a legal
process, not a self-help matter.

## 31.3 Essential terms

A residential lease should identify the parties; the premises; the term with definite start and end;
the rent, its amount, due date, place and method of payment, and any late fee; the security deposit
and its treatment; who pays which utilities; the permitted use and occupancy limits; maintenance
responsibilities on each side; the landlord's right of entry and the notice required; rules on pets,
smoking, parking and alterations; assignment and subletting; default and remedies; renewal or
termination; and the required disclosures.

The **statute of frauds** reaches leases: a lease for a term longer than the statutory period must
be in writing. The Virginia period is a matter of Title 55.1 that this course has not read from
primary text and therefore does not state; write every lease down regardless, which is the answer in
practice.

## 31.4 Security deposits

A security deposit secures the tenant's performance. Statutes typically regulate the maximum amount,
how it is held, the deadline for returning it, the itemisation required for deductions, and whether
interest is payable. Virginia regulates all of this in the **Virginia Residential Landlord and Tenant
Act**, Chapter 12 of Title 55.1 — and **this course states no figure or deadline**, because Chapter
12 has not been read from primary text for this project. Hour 32 teaches the navigation.

What is certain from the Board side: a deposit held by a firm is **escrow money**.
**18VAC135-20-181 A 2** lists rental security deposits among the funds that must go into the escrow
account, and § 54.1-2135 makes accounting a statutory duty for a licensee engaged to manage.

## 31.5 Assignment and sublease

**Assignment** transfers the tenant's **entire remaining interest** to another. The assignee comes
into direct relationship with the landlord. **Sublease** transfers **less than the entire remaining
interest** — a shorter period, or part of the premises — and the original tenant becomes a landlord
to the subtenant while remaining a tenant to the landlord.

The rule students must retain: **in neither case is the original tenant automatically released.**
Release requires the landlord's agreement, and that agreement is a **novation**. A tenant who
"assigned the lease and moved out" is still liable unless the landlord released them in writing.

Most leases restrict both, requiring the landlord's consent.

## 31.6 Termination, breach, and what a licensee must not do

A lease ends by expiration of its term, by mutual agreement, by proper notice in a periodic tenancy,
by surrender and acceptance, or by operation of law. It is not ended by a tenant leaving.

Landlord remedies for non-payment and other breaches run through a **statutory judicial process**.
**Self-help is prohibited**: changing the locks, removing the tenant's possessions, or shutting off
utilities to force a tenant out is unlawful. A licensee who advises a landlord client to do any of
those has advised a violation, and under § 54.1-2133 the licensee's duties include compliance with
applicable law.

Where a landlord asks what remedy to pursue, the answer is counsel and the statutory process — and
the licensee's contribution is documentation: the ledger, the notices actually given and how, the
condition record, and the correspondence.

### Hour 31 engagement check

**Question.** A tenant with eight months remaining transfers the apartment to a friend for the
remaining eight months, with the landlord's written consent to the transfer but nothing more. Six
weeks later the friend stops paying. Who is liable, and what would have changed the answer?

**Expected answer.** This is an **assignment** — the entire remaining interest was transferred. The
assignee is in direct relationship with the landlord and is liable. But **the original tenant is not
released**: consent to the assignment is not a release, and the original tenant remains liable to
the landlord on the lease covenants. What would have changed the answer is a **novation** — a written
agreement by the landlord substituting the new tenant and expressly releasing the original.

## Hour 32 — The Virginia residential landlord-tenant framework

## 32.1 Which statute governs

Virginia's residential tenancy law is in Title 55.1, and the first professional skill is knowing
which chapter a question belongs to:

- **Chapter 12 — Virginia Residential Landlord and Tenant Act (VRLTA).** The principal residential
  regime.
- **Chapter 13 — Manufactured Home Lot Rental Act.** Renting the lot beneath a manufactured home.
- **Chapter 14 — Nonresidential Tenancies.** Commercial.
- **Chapter 15 — Residential Ground Rent Act.**
- **Chapter 16 — Leases.** General leasehold provisions.
- **Chapter 17 — Emblements.**
- **Chapter 29 — Virginia Self-Service Storage Act.**

A question about a commercial tenant's obligations answered from the VRLTA is answered from the
wrong chapter.

## 32.2 What the VRLTA does, in outline

The Act sets a floor of rights and obligations for residential tenancies within its scope. In broad
terms it addresses: the scope of its own application and the tenancies excluded from it; the form
and required contents of rental agreements and prohibited provisions; **security deposits** —
amount, handling, deductions, itemisation, and the deadline for return; the landlord's obligation to
**maintain fit premises** and comply with building and housing codes; the tenant's obligations to
maintain the dwelling unit; the **landlord's right of access** and the notice required; remedies for
non-compliance on each side; the process for **termination and for possession**; **retaliatory
conduct**, which is prohibited; and required **disclosures**, including those relating to mould and
to defective drywall.

**This course does not state the Act's figures, deadlines, or notice periods.** Chapter 12 has not
been read from primary text for this project, and stating a number without reading it is exactly the
failure the project's source rule exists to prevent. The figures a licensee needs — the security
deposit maximum, the deadline for its return, the notice period for entry, the notice periods for
non-payment and for lease violations, and the periodic-tenancy termination notice — must be read
from Chapter 12 directly.

## 32.3 How to answer a tenancy question properly

The method, which is examinable and which a licensee will use weekly:

**One — classify the tenancy.** Residential or commercial? Dwelling unit or manufactured home lot?
Within the VRLTA's scope or excluded from it?

**Two — go to the chapter**, not to a summary. law.lis.virginia.gov publishes the current text of
every section, free.

**Three — read the section that names the situation**, and read the section around it. Virginia's
landlord-tenant provisions cross-reference heavily and a single subsection read alone frequently
misleads.

**Four — check the rental agreement**, which may give the tenant more than the statute requires but
cannot give less where the Act sets a floor.

**Five — refer.** Whether a notice was validly given, whether a breach is material, and what remedy
is available are legal conclusions. A property manager who guesses at a notice period has
frequently destroyed the landlord's case before it starts.

## 32.4 Where the licensing law bears on tenancy work

**§ 54.1-2133** sets the duties of a licensee engaged by a **landlord to lease property**;
**§ 54.1-2134** those of a licensee engaged by a **tenant**; **§ 54.1-2135** those of a licensee
engaged to **manage** real estate. All three carry the architecture of Hour 21 — performance per the
agreement, promotion of the client's interests, confidentiality, ordinary care, timely accounting,
disclosure of material facts of actual knowledge, and compliance with the article and fair housing.

**§ 54.1-2138 B** requires the brokerage-relationship disclosure to a landlord or tenant who is
neither the client nor represented, in writing, **in the lease application or in the lease, whichever
occurs first** — and it **does not apply to lease terms of less than two months.**

**18VAC135-20-181 A 2** puts **rental payments and rental security deposits** into the escrow
account. **18VAC135-20-190** governs advertising a rental exactly as it governs advertising a sale.
And **§ 36-96.3** applies to rental in the same terms as to sale — with **source of funds** the
class most often violated in leasing.

## 32.5 Fair housing in leasing, specifically

Leasing is where fair housing complaints concentrate, because screening is where discretion lives.

The defensible practice is **written, objective, uniform criteria applied identically to every
applicant**: income relative to rent, credit standard, rental history, and whatever else the landlord
lawfully requires, stated in advance and documented for each application. Deviating from published
criteria for one applicant is the fact pattern that produces liability, whatever the reason.

**Source of funds** requires a specific change of habit: a housing choice voucher is a lawful source
of funds in Virginia, "no Section 8" is unlawful in an advertisement under § 36-96.3 A 3 and in
practice under A 1 and A 2, and income criteria must be applied to the **tenant's portion** of the
rent rather than to the whole where a subsidy pays the balance.

**Reasonable accommodation and modification requests** — Hour 27 — arrive in leasing more than
anywhere else, and the assistance-animal request is the most common of all. A no-pets policy is not
an answer to it.

### Hour 32 engagement check

**Question.** A property manager asks you how many days' notice they must give a tenant before
entering to show the unit, and how long they have to return a security deposit after move-out. What
is the correct professional response?

**Expected answer.** Both answers are in the **Virginia Residential Landlord and Tenant Act, Chapter
12 of Title 55.1**, and both must be read from the current text rather than recalled — the notice
period for landlord access and the deadline and itemisation requirements for the security deposit
are statutory and have been amended over time. The professional response is to go to the chapter at
law.lis.virginia.gov, read the operative section and the sections around it, check the rental
agreement, which may give the tenant more than the statute requires but not less, and — where the
consequence of getting it wrong is the loss of a remedy — confirm with counsel. Giving a remembered
number is how a manager destroys a landlord's case.
