## Hour 31 — Leasehold Estates and Lease Agreements

**Required subject mapping:** T08 — Leases

### Learning objectives

- Distinguish tenancy for years, periodic tenancy, tenancy at will, and tenancy at sufferance.
- Identify essential lease business terms and risk allocations.
- Compare assignment and sublease concepts.

### Instructional content

A lease transfers a right of possession for a term while ownership remains with the landlord. A tenancy for years has a definite beginning and ending; a periodic tenancy renews by periods until properly terminated; a tenancy at will has no fixed duration and depends on applicable law; and a tenancy at sufferance arises when a lawful tenant remains without consent after the right of possession ends.

Lease analysis begins with parties, premises, term, rent, deposit, possession, permitted use, utilities, maintenance, repairs, alterations, insurance, entry, assignment/subletting, renewal, default, notices, and remedies. Residential statutes can override or supplement contract terms. Commercial leases often allocate more duties by negotiation but remain subject to law.

An assignment generally transfers the tenant’s remaining leasehold interest; a sublease transfers less than the full remaining interest and creates an intermediate landlord-tenant relationship. Consent clauses and liability consequences matter. A salesperson explains transaction vocabulary but sends legal interpretation and custom drafting to the broker/attorney.

### 50-minute instructional sequence

- **0–5:** Retrieval prompt, objectives, and vocabulary activation.
- **5–20:** Direct instruction segment A with worked examples.
- **20–35:** Direct instruction segment B, misconceptions, and Virginia/current-source boundary.
- **35–45:** Applied exercise — Students classify four tenancies and determine whether three transfers are assignments, subleases, or require document review.
- **45–50:** Scored engagement check, feedback, and completion gate.

### Scored engagement check

**Question:** What distinguishes an assignment from a sublease at a principles level?

**Expected answer and rationale:** An assignment generally transfers the tenant’s entire remaining leasehold interest; a sublease transfers less than the full remaining interest.

## Hour 32 — Virginia Residential Landlord-Tenant Framework

**Required subject mapping:** T08, T22, T23 — Leases; regulatory statutes; housing legislation

### Learning objectives

- Navigate the current Virginia Residential Landlord and Tenant Act.
- Identify source sections for duties, deposits, entry, notices, and remedies.
- Avoid giving unsupported eviction or self-help advice.

### Instructional content

The Virginia Residential Landlord and Tenant Act in Title 55.1, Chapter 12 is the controlling starting point for covered residential tenancies. Students learn to locate definitions, scope/exclusions, landlord and tenant duties, rental agreements, security deposits, access, maintenance, notices, remedies, and termination procedures in the current text.

Timing and content of notices depend on the event, lease, statute, service method, and current law. The course does not substitute generic ‘five-day’ or ‘thirty-day’ slogans for source review. Property managers and agents use approved notices and broker/legal procedures and preserve proof of delivery.

Self-help actions—lockouts, utility interruption, property removal, or retaliation—create serious risk. When possession, habitability, domestic violence protections, military status, assistance animals, or fair housing is involved, the licensee escalates promptly. A court order and statutory process may be required.

## Hour 34 — Promissory Notes, Mortgages, and Deeds of Trust

**Required subject mapping:** T06, T14 — Mortgages/deeds of trust; financing

### Learning objectives

- Distinguish the debt instrument from the security instrument.
- Identify parties in a deed-of-trust structure.
- Explain recording, lien, assumption, and subject-to concepts.

### Instructional content

The promissory note is the borrower’s promise to repay and states principal, interest, payment, maturity, default, and related terms. A mortgage or deed of trust secures performance with an interest in real property. Paying or transferring the debt and releasing the lien are related but distinct legal/document steps.

In a deed of trust, the borrower/trustor grants security to a trustee for the beneficiary/lender under the instrument. Virginia transactions commonly use deeds of trust, but students examine the actual documents rather than assume. Recordation gives notice and establishes priority subject to law.

An assumption can make the transferee personally responsible for the debt if the lender and documents permit. A subject-to transfer means title transfers with the lien remaining but does not by itself make the transferee personally liable to the lender. Due-on-sale provisions, qualification, insurance, and disclosure require lender/legal review.

## Hour 35 — Default, Foreclosure, and Lien Enforcement

**Required subject mapping:** T06, T09, T14 — Mortgages/deeds of trust; liens; financing

### Learning objectives

- Describe default, acceleration, reinstatement, and foreclosure concepts.
- Distinguish judicial and nonjudicial process vocabulary.
- Recognize consumer referral and anti-misrepresentation duties.

### Instructional content

Default occurs when a borrower fails to perform an obligation, such as payment or another covenant. Loan documents and law govern notice, cure, acceleration, fees, loss mitigation, and enforcement. Reinstatement cures defined defaults under applicable terms; payoff satisfies the full obligation. Neither should be promised without a current written figure.

Foreclosure enforces the security interest. Judicial foreclosure uses court process; nonjudicial foreclosure relies on a power of sale and statutory/instrument procedures. Virginia deed-of-trust practice may involve trustee sale, but exact notices, timing, publication, accounting, and borrower protections must be taught from current law and documents.

Licensees handling distressed property avoid legal conclusions, foreclosure-rescue promises, fee schemes, pressure, and concealment. They provide accurate transaction information, notify the broker, coordinate with lender/servicer and closing professionals, and refer the owner to qualified legal and HUD-approved housing counseling resources.

## Hour 36 — Conventional, FHA, VA, and USDA Mortgage Programs

**Required subject mapping:** T07, T14 — Types of mortgages; financing

### Learning objectives

- Distinguish conventional from government-insured or guaranteed programs.
- Recognize broad program purposes and changing guidelines.
- Avoid eligibility or approval promises.

### Instructional content

Conventional loans are not insured or guaranteed by a federal housing agency. Conforming loans meet current purchaser/agency parameters; nonconforming loans do not. Private mortgage insurance may apply depending on risk and structure. Guidelines and loan limits change and must be verified with current lender sources.

FHA programs are federally insured; VA programs provide a guaranty for eligible veterans and other qualified borrowers; USDA programs support eligible rural housing and borrowers under current rules. Each has property, occupancy, appraisal, insurance/fee, underwriting, and documentation requirements that cannot be reduced to a single down-payment slogan.

A real estate licensee may explain categories and transaction effects but does not act as the loan originator unless separately licensed and authorized. The licensee avoids steering, fair-lending violations, or statements that a borrower or property ‘will qualify.’ Multiple lender/program paths should be discussed objectively.

## Hour 37 — Alternative Financing and Consumer Protection

**Required subject mapping:** T07, T14, T23 — Types of mortgages; financing; housing legislation

### Learning objectives

- Describe ARM, balloon, buydown, construction, bridge, reverse, seller-financing, and land-contract concepts.
- Identify TILA, RESPA, ECOA, and fair-lending issues.
- Recognize high-risk terms requiring specialist review.

### Instructional content

Alternative structures redistribute payment, rate, maturity, collateral, construction, or underwriting risk. Adjustable-rate loans change under index/margin/cap terms. Balloon loans mature before full amortization. Buydowns alter scheduled payment through funds or rate structure. Construction and bridge loans address timing. Reverse mortgages convert equity under specialized rules. Seller financing and land contracts create creditor, title, servicing, disclosure, and default questions.

Truth in Lending/Regulation Z addresses cost and credit disclosures and other mortgage rules; RESPA/Regulation X addresses settlement practices and servicing; ECOA/Regulation B prohibits credit discrimination and governs application treatment. The current CFPB regulations and lender compliance process control.

Students identify red flags: undisclosed balloon, payment shock, negative amortization, prepayment consequences, unlicensed origination, fee splitting, required provider use, discriminatory pricing, document mismatch, or pressure to misstate occupancy/income. The response is pause, document, escalate, and obtain qualified advice.

## Hour 38 — Finance Mathematics

**Required subject mapping:** T14 — Real estate financing

### Learning objectives

- Calculate LTV, down payment, points, and simple interest.
- Explain amortization and payment-factor use.
- Organize estimated cash-to-close components.

### Instructional content

Loan-to-value equals loan amount divided by value under the lender’s applicable definition, usually expressed as a percentage. Down payment equals price minus base loan when no other structure changes the equation. One discount point equals one percent of the relevant loan amount; points are a cost, not automatically a one-percent rate reduction.

Simple interest is principal × rate × time. Mortgage payments usually reflect amortization, in which periodic payments cover interest and reduce principal over time. Early payments in a level-payment fully amortizing loan typically contain more interest because outstanding principal is higher. Payment factors are tools and must match rate, term, and payment frequency.

Cash to close can include down payment, closing costs, prepaids, reserves, credits, deposits, and lender adjustments. A licensee can prepare an estimate from disclosed assumptions but should not replace the official lender/settlement disclosure or promise exact funds.

- **0–5:** Retrieval prompt, objectives, and vocabulary activation.

- **45–50:** Scored engagement check, feedback, and completion gate.

## Hour 39 — The Loan Process and Financing a Transaction

**Required subject mapping:** T14 — Real estate financing

### Learning objectives

- Sequence application through closing and servicing.
- Distinguish prequalification, preapproval, conditional approval, and clear-to-close language.
- Track financing contingency evidence and communication.

### Instructional content

The process commonly includes application, required disclosures, borrower documentation, verification, processing, underwriting, appraisal, title review, insurance, conditions, final approval, closing disclosure, signing, funding, recording, and servicing. Actual sequence varies, and ‘approved’ may still be conditional.

Prequalification is generally an early estimate based on limited information; preapproval usually involves more review but is not a guarantee. Conditional approval lists conditions. Clear-to-close is lender-specific and can still depend on final verification. Only the lender communicates credit decisions and official conditions.

The licensee coordinates property access, appraisal timing, association/title documents, repair evidence, contingency dates, and closing communications. The licensee protects borrower privacy, avoids asking for unnecessary financial data, and never alters lender documents or coaches misrepresentation.

## Hour 41 — Prorations, Closing Calculations, and Reconciliation

**Required subject mapping:** T13, T19 — Taxes; title closing

### Learning objectives

- Apply debit/credit logic to a settlement worksheet.
- Calculate prepaid and accrued prorations using a stated convention.
- Reconcile buyer cash and seller proceeds.

### Instructional content

A debit is an amount charged to a party; a credit is an amount allowed to a party. The same item can produce opposite entries. Students first identify who paid, who benefits after closing, the covered period, ownership/possession date, and the contract’s day-count convention.

An accrued item is owed but not yet paid; a prepaid item was paid in advance. Taxes, rent, association charges, utilities, fuel, and similar items may be prorated. The calculation must state annual/monthly amount, daily rate, number of days, who owns the closing day, and which party receives the debit/credit.

Seller proceeds start with sale-price credits and subtract liens, expenses, credits, and other debits. Buyer cash begins with price/cost debits, subtracts loan/deposit/credits, and incorporates adjustments. The official settlement figure controls; the classroom worksheet teaches reconciliation, not a guaranteed quote.

## Hour 42 — Appraisal Foundations

**Required subject mapping:** T20 — Appraisal of residential and income-producing property

### Learning objectives

- Distinguish value, price, and cost.
- Explain market value and highest and best use concepts.
- Apply core valuation principles.

### Instructional content

Price is the amount asked, offered, or paid; cost is the expenditure to create or acquire; value is an opinion or relationship under a defined type and date. Market value requires the definition used by the assignment and assumes specified market conditions. A sale price can differ from market value because of motivation, financing, relationship, condition, information, or timing.

Highest and best use is the reasonably probable use that is legally permissible, physically possible, financially feasible, and maximally productive, analyzed as vacant and as improved when relevant. A current use is not automatically the highest and best use, and a theoretical use fails if zoning, site, or economics do not support it.

Substitution holds that a buyer will not pay more than the cost of an equally desirable alternative absent delay or other factors. Contribution measures how a component changes total value, not its cost. Conformity, competition, anticipation, change, balance, and external influences help explain market behavior.

- **5–20:** Direct instruction segment A with worked examples.

- **45–50:** Scored engagement check, feedback, and completion gate.

### Scored engagement check

**Expected answer and rationale:** Value contribution depends on market demand and the property as a whole, not simply the owner’s expenditure.

- Reconcile indications without simple averaging.

### Instructional content

The sales comparison approach compares recent, relevant transactions. The appraiser researches and verifies sales, selects units of comparison, and adjusts the comparable—not the subject—for material differences. If a comparable is superior, its sale price is adjusted downward; if inferior, upward. Adjustments require market support.

The cost approach estimates land value plus current cost of improvements minus depreciation. Replacement cost uses equivalent utility; reproduction cost replicates the improvement. Depreciation can be physical deterioration, functional obsolescence, or external obsolescence. Curable/incurable analysis compares cost to cure with value benefit where appropriate.

Reconciliation weighs the quality, quantity, and relevance of evidence. It is not an automatic average. A CMA may use comparison principles, but a salesperson must not claim to perform an appraisal or present an unsupported value opinion.

- **35–45:** Applied exercise — Students adjust three comparables for condition and garage differences and reconcile a supported range; then identify depreciation in a subject.
- **45–50:** Scored engagement check, feedback, and completion gate.

### Scored engagement check

**Question:** If a comparable is superior to the subject, in which direction is its price adjusted?

**Expected answer and rationale:** Downward, to model what the comparable might have sold for if it were similar to the inferior subject.

## Hour 44 — Income-Producing Property Appraisal

**Required subject mapping:** T12, T20 — Investment; appraisal

### Learning objectives

- Build an income statement through NOI.
- Calculate direct-capitalization value and GRM.
- Distinguish property operating expenses from financing and income-tax items.

### Instructional content

Potential gross income assumes full occupancy and collection. Subtract vacancy and collection loss and add applicable other property income to reach effective gross income. Subtract operating expenses and reserves used by the analysis to derive net operating income. Debt service, depreciation for income tax, and owner income taxes are not property operating expenses in the standard NOI calculation.

Direct capitalization converts one stabilized year’s NOI to value: Value = NOI ÷ capitalization rate. The cap rate reflects market evidence and risk; it is not the mortgage interest rate. Small changes in NOI or rate can materially change value, so assumptions and consistency matter.

Gross rent multiplier relates sale price to gross periodic rent and omits expenses. It can be a quick market tool among comparable properties but is weaker when expense ratios differ. Discounted cash flow and yield capitalization are introduced conceptually for multi-period analysis.

## Hour 45 — Real Estate Investment Fundamentals

**Required subject mapping:** T12 — Investment

### Learning objectives

- Identify investment objectives, risk, return, liquidity, leverage, and holding-period factors.
- Perform a structured due-diligence review.
- Avoid suitability and return guarantees.

### Instructional content

Investment objectives may emphasize current income, long-term appreciation, capital preservation, tax characteristics, control, diversification, or development potential. Real estate is generally less liquid than publicly traded securities and carries market, tenant, operating, physical, legal, financing, environmental, and concentration risk.

Leverage can increase equity returns when performance exceeds financing cost, but it also magnifies losses, default risk, and cash-flow pressure. Appreciation is not guaranteed. A sound analysis separates property operations from financing, states assumptions, uses reserves, and tests adverse scenarios.

Due diligence reviews title, survey, zoning/use, leases, rent roll, payment history, expenses, contracts, physical condition, environmental matters, insurance, taxes, association, permits, legal compliance, financing, market evidence, and exit assumptions. A licensee supplies factual analysis within competence and does not guarantee suitability or tax results.
