## Course Day 1 · Hours 1–4 · The market, the product, and what is being sold

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module the student will be able to:

- Separate the three words *land*, *real estate*, and *real property*, and say what each adds.
- Explain why the physical characteristics of land produce local markets, and predict what a
  demand shock does to price and vacancy when supply cannot move in the short run.
- Describe the four phases of a real estate cycle and identify which phase a set of indicators
  describes.
- Define a market area, select comparable properties, and state in plain words what a comparative
  market analysis is not.
- Apply a fixture test to a disputed item and explain which of the three factors decides it.

## Hour 01 — The real estate profession and its economic role

## 1.1 Three words that are not synonyms

Almost every misunderstanding in the first week of a real estate course comes from treating three
words as interchangeable. They are not.

**Land** is the surface of the earth, the space above it, and the ground beneath it, to the extent
the law recognises rights in each. The classical formulation is that land runs from the centre of
the earth to the heavens; the modern formulation is narrower, because aviation law and mineral law
each cut into it. What matters at this stage is that land is a *physical* thing with a vertical
dimension, not a flat shape on a map.

**Real estate** is land plus everything permanently attached to it by nature or by people. A
stand of timber, a building, a well, a driveway, a fence — attach them and they stop being
separate objects and become part of the real estate. Detach them and they become something else
again. Hour 4 is entirely about the moment of attachment and detachment, because that is where
money is lost.

**Real property** is real estate plus the legal interests a person may hold in it. This is the
concept the rest of the course actually operates on. When a licensee lists "a house", what is
being marketed is not the bricks; it is a set of legal rights in the bricks — the right to
possess, to use, to exclude others, to encumber, to transfer, and to do nothing at all. Those
rights can be divided among several people, limited by neighbours, limited by government, and
sold separately from one another.

The traditional shorthand for the set is the **bundle of rights**. It is a teaching image rather
than a legal doctrine, but it is a good one, because it makes visible the fact that a seller can
convey some sticks and keep others. A seller who conveys the surface and reserves the minerals has
sold real property and kept real property.

## 1.2 Why land behaves unlike other goods

Four physical characteristics explain most of what follows in the next 59 hours.

**Immobility.** A parcel cannot be moved. If demand rises in Richmond it cannot be met by shipping
in surplus houses from Roanoke. This single fact is why real estate markets are local, why
location dominates value, and why national statistics are close to useless for advising an
individual seller.

**Indestructibility, or more accurately durability.** The land itself survives. Improvements decay,
but slowly, and they are expensive to remove. The consequence is that the stock of housing in any
market at any moment is overwhelmingly made up of buildings built in the past, under past codes,
for past tastes.

**Uniqueness, sometimes called heterogeneity or non-homogeneity.** No two parcels occupy the same
space. Two identical houses on the same street are still not the same property, because one is
closer to the corner. This is why real estate cannot be traded like a commodity, why every
valuation is an estimate rather than a lookup, and why the doctrine of specific performance
applies to land contracts in a way it does not to contracts for ordinary goods.

**Scarcity.** Not "there is no land left" — there is a great deal — but that land in a particular
useful location is finite.

To these the economics texts add three economic characteristics: **improvements** change value in
ways that ripple to neighbours; **permanence of investment** means capital sunk into land
(sewers, roads, foundations) is not recoverable; and **area preference**, or situs, is the
observed human preference for a particular location, which is what people actually pay for.

## 1.3 The sectors and where a salesperson sits

Real estate as an industry is not one occupation. A student should be able to name the sectors
because a large part of a licensee's working life is knowing whom to hand a question to.

**Brokerage** connects the parties to a transaction. It is the sector this licence regulates.
**Lending** supplies capital and is regulated separately, largely federally. **Appraisal**
develops supported opinions of value and in Virginia is a separately licensed profession under a
different board. **Title and settlement** examine and insure title and conduct closings.
**Property management** operates income property. **Development and construction** create new
supply. **Land use planning** determines what may be built and where, through local government.

A Virginia salesperson works inside brokerage and, critically, does not work alone. The licence
is not a licence to practise independently. Every salesperson is affiliated with a firm and
supervised by a supervising broker whose duties are set out at § 54.1-2110.1 of the Code of
Virginia. Hour 23 covers the structure in detail; the point to carry from Hour 1 is that a
salesperson's authority is derived, not original.

The other point to carry is the boundary of competence. The licence permits a person to provide
real estate brokerage services. It does not make that person a lawyer, an inspector, a surveyor,
an appraiser, a lender, or an accountant. A great deal of licensee discipline in every state
consists of people who forgot that.

## 1.4 What licensure is actually for

Licensing is a consumer-protection mechanism. Its logic is that a transaction which is infrequent,
expensive, legally complex, and information-asymmetric is a transaction in which an unqualified or
dishonest intermediary can do very great harm. Setting a minimum standard of competence and a
published standard of conduct, and attaching a disciplinary process to both, reduces that harm.

Notice what licensure does not do. It does not guarantee that any given licensee is good at the
job. It does not guarantee an investment result. It does not make the licensee an insurer of the
property's condition. And it does not displace the buyer's own responsibility to investigate —
which, as Hour 19 will show, Virginia takes further than most states.

### Hour 01 engagement check

**Question.** A seller conveys the surface of a parcel and expressly reserves the mineral rights.
Has the seller conveyed real estate, real property, or neither? Explain.

**Expected answer.** Real property. The seller conveyed land and its improvements — real estate —
together with a defined subset of the legal interests in it, which is what makes the conveyance a
conveyance of real property. The reserved mineral rights are themselves real property that the
seller has retained. The bundle-of-rights image is the point: rights are separable, and separating
them does not turn either share into personal property.

## Hour 02 — Supply, demand, and real estate cycles

## 2.1 Demand, supply, and the lag that makes real estate different

In an ordinary market, a rise in demand raises price, the higher price draws out more supply, and
the price settles back. Real estate does the first two steps very slowly on the supply side.

**Demand** for housing in a market is driven by population and household formation, employment and
wages, the cost and availability of credit, expectations about future prices, and the price and
quality of substitutes such as rentals. A change in any of them shows up in the market within
weeks.

**Supply** is the existing stock plus whatever new construction can deliver. New construction
requires land assembly, entitlement, financing, permitting, and building. In most Virginia
localities that is a matter of years, not weeks. Meanwhile the existing stock changes only at the
margin, as owners decide whether to list.

The consequence is the single most useful prediction in residential real estate: **in the short
run, supply is close to fixed, so a demand increase shows up almost entirely as price.** Days on
market fall, inventory falls, list-to-sale price ratios rise, and multiple-offer situations
appear. It is only later — sometimes years later — that new supply arrives, and by then demand may
have moved again. That mismatch is not a market failure. It is the structural reason real estate
cycles.

The same logic runs in reverse. When demand falls, supply cannot be withdrawn quickly. Sellers who
must sell still sell; builders finish what they started. Price falls further than an
instantaneous-adjustment model would predict, and vacancy rises.

## 2.2 The four phases

The conventional description of the cycle has four phases. They are descriptive, not predictive:
knowing the names does not tell anyone when the next turn comes.

**Expansion.** Demand is growing faster than supply. Vacancy falls below its long-run average,
rents and prices rise, days on market shorten, and construction begins to respond.

**Peak, then contraction — often called oversupply.** New construction started during expansion
completes. Supply catches up with, and then exceeds, demand. Vacancy rises, price growth
decelerates and then stops, concessions appear, and days on market lengthen.

**Recession.** Demand is below supply. Vacancy is above the long-run average, rents and prices
fall, construction stops, and distressed sales enter the market.

**Recovery.** Demand begins to absorb the surplus. Vacancy falls back toward its average, but
prices lag, because sellers and lenders remain cautious. Construction has not yet resumed.

The most common student error is to equate the cycle with interest rates. Rates are one input to
demand, not the cycle itself. Local employment is usually a stronger driver.

## 2.3 Reading the indicators

A licensee is expected to be able to look at a handful of local numbers and describe the market
honestly. The core set:

**Months of inventory** — active listings divided by the average monthly closed sales. It answers
"how long would it take to sell everything now on the market at the current pace?" A low figure
describes a seller's market and a high figure a buyer's market. The commonly quoted balance point
of around six months is a rule of thumb from national data, not a law of nature, and it varies by
market and price band.

**Absorption rate** — the pace at which available units are taken up, expressed in units per
period. Months of inventory is its reciprocal, scaled.

**Days on market** — median rather than mean, because a few very stale listings distort a mean
badly.

**List-to-sale price ratio** — closed price divided by the last list price, which measures
negotiating strength.

**Vacancy rate** — for rental property, the share of units unoccupied.

**New listings versus closed sales** — the flow into and out of inventory, which turns before the
stock does.

## 2.4 Honesty about what these numbers can do

Two constraints belong in the same hour as the indicators.

First, **market statistics are historical**. Closed sales report decisions made 30 to 60 days
earlier. Nothing in the data set describes the future, and a licensee who presents an extrapolation
as a forecast is making a representation that may prove false. Under § 54.1-2131 A 4 the duty is
to exercise **ordinary care**; under 18VAC135-20-300 misrepresentation is a disciplinary matter.
The safe and accurate formulation is what the evidence shows, not what it predicts.

Second, **aggregates hide the property**. A median sale price for a county describes a mix of
properties, and a change in the median can be entirely a change in the mix. If more large houses
sold this quarter, the median rose without any individual property becoming more valuable.

### Hour 02 engagement check

**Question.** A large employer announces 3,000 new jobs in a locality where residential
construction takes about two years from entitlement to occupancy. Describe what happens to price
and to vacancy over the first six months, and explain why.

**Expected answer.** Demand rises quickly; supply is effectively fixed over six months, because
neither the existing stock nor new construction can respond in that time. Prices rise and vacancy
falls, and the adjustment is almost entirely in price rather than quantity. Days on market shorten
and the list-to-sale ratio rises. New supply arrives only after the construction lag, by which
time the price signal has already been given — which is the mechanism that produces the cycle.

## Hour 03 — Market data and comparative analysis

## 3.1 Defining the market area

Before any comparison is possible, the analyst has to say what market the property is in. A market
area is the geographic area within which properties compete for the same buyers. It is bounded by
whatever actually changes buyer behaviour: school attendance zones, a river with few crossings,
an interstate, a jurisdictional line that changes the tax rate, the edge of a subdivision with its
own association.

The boundary is an evidentiary question, not an administrative one. A ZIP code is not a market
area; it is a mail-delivery convenience that frequently spans two school zones and two price
bands. A licensee who defines a market area by ZIP code because that is what the software offers
is starting the analysis with an error.

## 3.2 Comparability

A comparable property is one a buyer would have seriously considered instead of the subject. The
usual dimensions:

**Location** within the market area. **Physical characteristics** — living area, lot size, bedroom
and bath count, age, condition, garage, and functional layout. **Time** — how recently it sold,
because a sale from a different phase of the cycle describes a different market. **Terms of
sale** — cash, conventional, seller concessions, and whether the price reflects them. **Conditions
of sale** — an arm's-length transaction between unrelated parties acting without unusual pressure,
as distinct from a sale between family members, an estate sale, a foreclosure, or a relocation
company sale.

**Closed sales are evidence. Active listings are not.** An active listing shows what a seller
hopes for; it is an offer to the market that has not been accepted. Pending sales are better
evidence than actives because a price has been agreed, but the price is usually not yet public and
the sale may not close. Expired and withdrawn listings are useful in a specific way: they show
prices the market **refused**, which brackets the top of the range.

## 3.3 What a comparative market analysis is, and what it is not

A comparative market analysis is a licensee's presentation of market evidence to help a client set
a list price or an offer price. It is a legitimate and expected part of brokerage.

It is **not an appraisal**. An appraisal is an opinion of value developed by a licensed or
certified appraiser under a professional standard, for an intended user and an intended use, with
a scope of work the appraiser must document. Virginia regulates appraisers under a separate board.
A licensee who presents a CMA as an appraisal, or who prepares something styled as an appraisal
for a lender, is on the wrong side of a line that exists for good reasons.

It is also **not an automated valuation**. An automated valuation model applies a statistical
model to recorded characteristics. It cannot see condition, it usually cannot see a renovation
that did not go through permitting, and it does not know that the subject backs onto a rail line.
Automated figures are a starting hypothesis at best.

The honest framing of a CMA to a client is: *here is what comparable properties in this market
area actually sold for, here is how the subject differs from each, and here is the range that
evidence supports.* Not: *your house is worth X.*

## 3.4 Building the analysis

A workable sequence: define the market area and defend it; pull closed sales within a defensible
period, usually the most recent three to six months, extended only if data is thin; screen for
arm's-length conditions; select the three to five closest in physical characteristics; note every
material difference between each comparable and the subject; adjust in the direction that makes
the comparable resemble the subject; reconcile to a range rather than a point; and disclose the
limits — the number of sales, the age of the data, and anything the licensee could not verify.

Adjustment mechanics belong to Hour 43. The habit to build now is the discipline of writing down
*why* each comparable was included and each near-miss excluded. That written record is what makes
the analysis reviewable, and it is what protects the licensee if the price is later disputed.

### Hour 03 engagement check

**Question.** A subject property is a 1,900-square-foot three-bedroom house. Which is stronger
evidence of its value: a 1,950-square-foot house two streets away that sold four months ago
between unrelated parties, or an identical 1,900-square-foot house next door currently listed at a
price the seller chose last week? Explain.

**Expected answer.** The closed sale. It records a price a buyer actually paid and a seller
actually accepted, which is evidence of market behaviour. The active listing records only what one
seller is asking, which is an unaccepted offer and may be far from what the market will pay. The
active listing is still useful as competitive supply and as a bracket, but it is not a value
indicator.

## Hour 04 — Real property, personal property, and fixtures

## 4.1 The distinction and why it decides disputes

**Real property** is land, improvements, and the legal interests in them. **Personal property** —
also called chattels or personalty — is everything else: movable things, and intangible rights not
attached to land.

The distinction matters because the two transfer by different instruments. Real property is
conveyed by **deed**. Personal property is transferred by **bill of sale**, or simply by delivery.
A sales contract that conveys the house says nothing at all about the refrigerator unless someone
writes it in. Most fixture disputes are not legal puzzles; they are contracts that failed to say.

## 4.2 Fixtures

A **fixture** is an item that was personal property and has become real property by being attached
to the land or to an improvement. Once it is a fixture it passes with the deed automatically.

The classical test has three parts, and they are usually taught with the mnemonic **MARIA** —
Method of attachment, Adaptability, Relationship of the parties, Intention, and Agreement — or in
the shorter three-factor form:

**Method of annexation.** How is it attached, and what happens if it is removed? An item bolted,
wired, plumbed, or built in is more likely a fixture. An item that would leave a hole, a scar, or
a non-functioning system is more likely a fixture. A picture hanging on a nail is not.

**Adaptation to the real estate.** Has it been fitted to this particular property? Custom-cut
shutters, a built-in bookcase sized to the alcove, a key to the front door, and a
custom-fabricated storm window are adapted, and adaptation makes an item a fixture even when the
physical attachment is slight. A key is the classic example: physically unattached, unquestionably
a fixture.

**Intention of the party who attached it.** Courts treat this as the controlling factor, and the
first two are largely evidence of it. Intention is judged objectively — by what a reasonable
person would infer from the circumstances, not by what the seller says afterwards.

The two supporting factors: **relationship of the parties** — the same item is treated differently
between landlord and tenant than between seller and buyer, and doubt is generally resolved in
favour of the party with the weaker position, historically the tenant and the buyer — and
**agreement**, which is decisive when it exists.

## 4.3 Trade fixtures

A **trade fixture** is an article installed by a *tenant* for use in the tenant's trade or business.
Trade fixtures remain the tenant's personal property and may be removed before the lease ends,
with the tenant responsible for repairing damage caused by removal. A restaurant tenant's walk-in
cooler, a salon's stations, a shop's display cases: attached, sometimes heavily, and still the
tenant's.

The trap is timing. Trade fixtures left behind after the lease term generally become the
landlord's by **accession**. The right to remove is real, and it expires.

## 4.4 In the other direction

Attachment is not one-way. **Severance** turns real property into personal property: standing
timber is real property, cut timber is personal property; a crop growing in a field is real
property, harvested grain is personal property; a hot-water heater in a box at the supply house is
personal property, and installed it is a fixture.

**Emblements** are a distinct case worth knowing. Annual crops produced by a tenant's labour —
*fructus industriales* — are treated as the tenant's personal property, and a tenant whose tenancy
ends through no fault of their own generally retains the right to re-enter and harvest the crop
that was planted during the tenancy.

## 4.5 How a competent licensee prevents the dispute

The professional answer to fixture questions is almost never litigation. It is drafting.

At listing, walk the property and identify every item whose status a reasonable buyer might read
differently: mounted televisions and their brackets, mirrors, chandeliers and other light
fittings, appliances, window treatments and their hardware, shelving, playground equipment,
above-ground pools, generators, water-treatment equipment, security cameras, and anything the
seller intends to take. Record the seller's intention **before** marketing, and reflect it in the
listing information and in the contract. If a seller wants the dining-room chandelier, the time to
remove and replace it is before the first photograph — not after a buyer has seen it.

This is also a disclosure question. Under § 54.1-2131 A 6 the licensee owes the client disclosure
of material facts of which the licensee has actual knowledge; under 18VAC135-20-190 B 3 property
information in advertising must be consistent with the property's condition and contract status.
A photograph showing a chandelier the seller intends to remove is inconsistent property
information, and fixing it is not optional courtesy.

### Hour 04 engagement check

**Question.** A seller installed a custom-fitted bookcase into a wall alcove, screwed to studs and
cut to the alcove's exact dimensions. The contract is silent. The seller wants to take it. What is
the outcome under the fixture test, and which factor decides it?

**Expected answer.** It is a fixture and passes to the buyer. Method of annexation points that way
— screwed to structure, removal would damage the wall — but the decisive factor is adaptation
combined with objective intention: an item cut to fit this specific alcove evidences an intention
to make it part of the property permanently. The seller's after-the-fact wish is not the test.
The correct professional response is that this should have been agreed in writing before listing.
