## Hour 45 — Real estate investment fundamentals

## 45.1 What an investor is buying

Four sources of return, and an investor's objective is usually a weighting among them:

**Cash flow** — the money left after operating expenses and debt service.
**Appreciation** — an increase in value over the holding period, which is expected rather than
promised.
**Equity buildup** — the principal retired by each payment, which converts borrowed money into
ownership without any further outlay.
**Tax treatment** — deductions, depreciation, and deferral, all of which depend on the investor's own
circumstances.

To which add **control**: unlike a share in a fund, a building can be managed, improved, refinanced,
and repositioned by the person who owns it.

## 45.2 The costs, stated honestly

**Illiquidity.** Selling takes months and costs a substantial percentage of the price.
**Indivisibility.** An investor cannot sell one bedroom to raise cash.
**Concentration.** A single property is an undiversified holding in one location and one asset class.
**Management burden.** Income property is a business, not a security.
**Operating leverage.** Fixed costs continue when income stops.
**Physical risk.** Fire, storm, flood, and failure of major components.
**Market risk.** Employment leaves; the cycle turns; cap rates move.
**Regulatory risk.** Zoning, codes, and rules change.

## 45.3 Leverage

**Leverage** is the use of borrowed money to control an asset larger than the investor's own capital.

**Positive leverage** occurs where the return on the total investment exceeds the cost of the
borrowed money, so borrowing increases the return on the investor's own cash. **Negative leverage**
occurs where the cost of debt exceeds the return on the asset, and borrowing then *reduces* the
return on equity — and magnifies the loss.

*Worked, positive.* A property produces $60,000 NOI and costs $800,000. Unleveraged return: 60,000 ÷
800,000 = **7.5%**. With $200,000 down and $600,000 borrowed at 6% interest-only ($36,000 a year):
cash flow = 60,000 − 36,000 = $24,000; cash-on-cash = 24,000 ÷ 200,000 = **12%**.

*The same deal at 9% interest ($54,000):* cash flow = $6,000; cash-on-cash = 6,000 ÷ 200,000 =
**3%** — below the 7.5% unleveraged return. **Negative leverage.**

The lesson is not that leverage is good or bad. It is that leverage **amplifies whatever is
happening**, in both directions, and that the direction is decided by the relationship between the
asset's return and the cost of debt.

## 45.4 Property types and their risk

**Residential income** — single-family rentals, small multifamily, apartments. Shorter leases,
frequent turnover, and demand tied to household formation.
**Commercial** — office and retail, with longer leases, credit tenants, and tenant-improvement costs.
**Industrial** — warehouse and flex, driven by logistics.
**Land** — no income, carrying costs, and a return entirely dependent on entitlement and timing. The
highest-variance category and the one most often sold to people who do not understand it.
**Special purpose** — self-storage, hospitality, senior housing; operating businesses attached to
real estate.

## 45.5 Holding period, exit, and due diligence

An investment analysis needs an **exit assumption**, because a large share of total return is
realised on sale. The **terminal capitalization rate** — the rate assumed on resale — is an
assumption, and assuming a lower exit cap than the entry cap is assuming the market improves.

**Due diligence** for income property: verified rent roll and leases; two to three years of operating
statements with supporting invoices; the tax bill and any assessment appeal history; utility
histories; service contracts and their termination terms; insurance loss runs; a physical property
condition assessment; environmental screening where the use warrants it; a survey; zoning
verification in writing; title; and — for common-interest property — the association documents from
Hour 29.

## 45.6 Suitability and the licensee's limit

A licensee may assemble facts, compute the standard measures, and present them with their sources. A
licensee may **not** tell a client that an investment is suitable for them, forecast appreciation, or
promise a return. Suitability depends on the investor's finances, tax position, risk tolerance, and
time horizon, and none of those is the licensee's to assess.

Under **§ 54.1-2131 A 4** and **§ 54.1-2132 A 5** the duty is ordinary care, and under
**18VAC135-20-300** an unsupported representation about value or performance is a disciplinary
matter. The professional sentence is: *here are the figures and where each came from; here is what I
could not verify; take this to your accountant and your attorney.*

### Hour 45 engagement check

**Question.** An investor is considering a property producing $88,000 NOI at a price of $1,200,000,
with $300,000 down and $900,000 borrowed at 7% interest-only. Compute the unleveraged return and the
cash-on-cash return, and say whether the leverage is positive or negative.

**Expected answer.** Unleveraged return = 88,000 ÷ 1,200,000 = **7.33%**. Interest = 900,000 × 0.07 =
$63,000. Cash flow before tax = 88,000 − 63,000 = **$25,000**. Cash-on-cash = 25,000 ÷ 300,000 =
**8.33%**. Because the cash-on-cash return **exceeds** the unleveraged return, the leverage is
**positive** — the cost of debt at 7% is below the property's 7.33% return. The margin is thin: a
rate of 7.33% would make it neutral and anything above would make it negative.

## Hour 46 — Investment and tax calculations

## 46.1 Before-tax cash flow

**NOI − annual debt service = Before-Tax Cash Flow (BTCF).**

*Worked.* NOI $131,128 (from Hour 44). Annual debt service $96,400.
BTCF = 131,128 − 96,400 = **$34,728**.

## 46.2 Cash-on-cash return

**Cash-on-cash = BTCF ÷ total cash invested.**

Total cash invested is the down payment **plus** closing costs, loan fees, and any immediate capital
outlay — not the down payment alone, which is the most common error.

*Worked.* Down payment $500,000; closing costs and loan fees $18,500; immediate repairs $26,000.
Total cash invested = **$544,500**. Cash-on-cash = 34,728 ÷ 544,500 = **6.38%**.

**What it omits:** appreciation, equity buildup, and every tax effect. It measures one year's cash
against cash, and it is useful precisely because it is narrow.

## 46.3 Return on investment and equity buildup

**ROI** is used loosely in practice; state the definition being used before quoting a number. A common
formulation is total return over the period divided by the investment.

**Equity buildup** is the principal retired by the year's payments.

*Worked.* Loan $2,000,000 at 6.25%, monthly payment $12,311, so annual debt service $147,732. First
year's interest is approximately $124,300, so equity buildup ≈ 147,732 − 124,300 = **$23,432**.

**Total return before tax** for the year = BTCF + equity buildup + appreciation. If the same property
appreciated 2%, on $2,600,000 that is $52,000, and the year's total return before tax is
34,728 + 23,432 + 52,000 = **$110,160** on $544,500 invested, or **20.2%** — of which only 6.38% was
cash. Appreciation is an assumption, and saying so is part of the calculation.

## 46.4 Capitalization, in reverse

From Hour 44: **V = I ÷ R**, **I = V × R**, **R = I ÷ V**.

Investors use the reverse forms constantly. *An investor requires a 7% return and the property
produces $131,128 NOI:* the most they can pay is 131,128 ÷ 0.07 = **$1,873,257**. *A property is
offered at $2,100,000 with $131,128 NOI:* the implied rate is 131,128 ÷ 2,100,000 = **6.24%**, and
the question becomes whether the market supports 6.24% for this asset.

## 46.5 Tax concepts, and where they stop

**Basis** is broadly the purchase price plus capital improvements. **Adjusted basis** subtracts
depreciation taken. **Gain** on sale is broadly the amount realised — price less selling costs —
minus adjusted basis.

**Depreciation** is a deduction allowed on the improvements of income-producing property over a
statutory recovery period. **Land is not depreciable.** Depreciation reduces adjusted basis, so it
increases gain on sale, and part of that gain may be recaptured at a different rate.

*Illustration only.* Purchase $1,200,000, of which $250,000 is allocated to land. Depreciable basis
= **$950,000**. Over a 27.5-year residential recovery period the annual deduction is 950,000 ÷ 27.5 =
**$34,545**. **The recovery period is a matter of federal tax law and depends on the property's
classification and on law that changes; this figure is used as an arithmetic illustration and is not
tax advice.**

**Deferred exchange.** Section 1031 permits deferral of gain on an exchange of real property held for
productive use or investment, subject to identification and completion periods and to the use of a
qualified intermediary. Virginia regulates exchange facilitators separately — **Chapter 8 of Title
55.1, the Exchange Facilitators Act** — and this course states no requirement from it, because the
chapter has not been read from primary text for this project.

**Principal residence.** An individual may be eligible to exclude gain on the sale of a principal
residence subject to ownership and use requirements and dollar limits set by federal law.

**Passive activity rules, at-risk rules, and the treatment of losses** all bear on whether a paper
loss is usable, and all depend on the taxpayer's circumstances.

## 46.6 The referral, stated as a rule

Every figure in 46.5 depends on facts the licensee does not have and on law that changes. A licensee
may **describe the concepts** so a client knows what to ask. A licensee may **not** compute a client's
tax, advise on an allocation between land and improvements, advise whether an exchange will qualify,
or state what a client will owe.

The rule to carry: **compute the property; refer the person.**

### Hour 46 engagement check

**Question.** An investor buys for $1,450,000 with $362,500 down plus $21,000 of closing costs. NOI
is $94,000 and annual debt service is $71,600, of which $66,900 is interest in year one. Compute
before-tax cash flow, cash-on-cash return, and equity buildup. Then say what you may and may not tell
the investor about depreciation.

**Expected answer.**
BTCF = 94,000 − 71,600 = **$22,400**.
Total cash invested = 362,500 + 21,000 = **$383,500**. Cash-on-cash = 22,400 ÷ 383,500 = **5.84%**.
Equity buildup = 71,600 − 66,900 = **$4,700**.
On depreciation: I may explain **the concept** — that a deduction is allowed on the improvements of
income-producing property over a statutory recovery period, that **land is not depreciable**, that
depreciation reduces adjusted basis and therefore increases gain on sale, and that part of that gain
may be recaptured. I may **not** allocate the price between land and improvements, compute the
deduction as advice, state the recovery period as settled for this property, or tell the investor what
they will owe. That is the accountant's work, and I will put the referral in writing.

## Hour 47 — Feasibility, market analysis, and the investment decision

## 47.1 What feasibility asks

A **feasibility study** asks whether a specific project, in a specific market, at a specific time,
can meet the investor's objectives. It differs from an appraisal: an appraisal asks what a property
is worth, feasibility asks whether a proposed course of action works.

Four questions, in order:

**Is there demand?** Who are the users, how many are there, and what are they paying now?
**Is there competing supply?** What exists, what is under construction, and what is entitled?
**Can it be built and operated at a cost that works?** Land, construction, entitlement, carrying,
operating.
**Does the return meet the objective at an acceptable risk?**

## 47.2 Defining demand

Demand analysis is built from evidence about people, not from enthusiasm.

**Population and household formation** in the market area, and their direction.
**Employment** — total, by sector, and the concentration risk of a dominant employer.
**Income** — household income distribution, because it sets what can be paid.
**Existing occupancy and rents** in comparable properties, from actual leases where obtainable.
**Absorption** — the historical pace at which the market has taken up comparable space.

The market area is defined as Hour 3 defined it: the area within which properties compete for the
same users, bounded by what actually changes user behaviour.

## 47.3 Competitive supply

Count what exists, what is under construction, and — the item most often missed — **what is entitled
but not yet built**. An entitled site is supply that can appear on short notice, and a project
underwritten against current occupancy in a market with a large entitled pipeline is underwritten
against a number that will not survive.

For each competitor: unit mix, rents, concessions actually being given, occupancy, age and condition,
and amenities.

## 47.4 Operating assumptions

Every line of the Hour 44 statement is an assumption and each needs a source:

**Market rent** from comparable leases, not from asking rents.
**Vacancy and collection loss** from the market's history, not from the property's best year.
**Operating expenses** from the property's own statements, checked against per-unit or per-square-foot
benchmarks for the type.
**Replacement reserve** from the age and condition of the major components.
**Growth rates** for rent and expenses, stated explicitly. Assuming rent grows faster than expenses
for ten years is a substantial assumption dressed as arithmetic.

## 47.5 Sensitivity analysis

The single most useful discipline in the hour. Take the base case and move one variable at a time.

*Base case:* 30 units at $1,600, vacancy 6%, operating expenses $205,000, cap rate 6.5%.
PGI = 30 × 1,600 × 12 = **$576,000**. Vacancy 6% = 34,560. EGI = **$541,440**.
NOI = 541,440 − 205,000 = **$336,440**. Value = 336,440 ÷ 0.065 = **$5,175,000**.

**Rent down 5%** → PGI 547,200; vacancy 32,832; EGI 514,368; NOI **$309,368**; value **$4,759,508**.
A 5% rent decline costs about **$415,000 of value**.

**Vacancy to 10%** → vacancy 57,600; EGI 518,400; NOI **$313,400**; value **$4,821,538**.

**Expenses up 10%** → expenses 225,500; NOI **$315,940**; value **$4,860,615**.

**Cap rate to 7.25%** → value = 336,440 ÷ 0.0725 = **$4,640,552**. The largest single effect, and
the one entirely outside the owner's control.

**All four together** → PGI 547,200; vacancy 10% = 54,720; EGI 492,480; expenses 225,500; NOI
**$266,980**; value at 7.25% = **$3,682,483** — roughly **29% below the base case.**

That last line is the point of the exercise. Individually survivable assumptions are not individually
independent, and a downturn moves all of them at once.

## 47.6 Go, no-go, and how a licensee presents it

A decision needs a stated **threshold** set in advance — a minimum cash-on-cash return, a minimum
debt service coverage ratio, a maximum price per unit, a maximum acceptable downside in the stress
case. Setting the threshold after seeing the numbers is not analysis.

The licensee's presentation should show: the base case with every assumption sourced; the stress
case; what could not be verified; and the questions that remain open. It should not show a
recommendation to invest, a forecast of appreciation, or a projected return described as expected.

### Hour 47 engagement check

**Question.** A client is looking at a 20-unit building at $1,375 per unit, 5% vacancy, $138,000
operating expenses, offered at $3,600,000. Compute NOI, the implied cap rate, and the value if the
market cap rate is 6.75%. Then test rent 5% lower and say what you would tell the client.

**Expected answer.**
PGI = 20 × 1,375 × 12 = **$330,000**. Vacancy 5% = 16,500. EGI = **$313,500**.
NOI = 313,500 − 138,000 = **$175,500**.
Implied cap rate at the asking price = 175,500 ÷ 3,600,000 = **4.88%**.
Value at a 6.75% market rate = 175,500 ÷ 0.0675 = **$2,600,000**.
Rent 5% lower: PGI 313,500; vacancy 15,675; EGI 297,825; NOI **$159,825**; value at 6.75% =
**$2,367,778**.
What I would tell the client: the asking price implies a **4.88%** return where the market appears to
support **6.75%**, a gap of roughly **$1,000,000** on these figures — and the figures rest on the
owner's rent and expense statements, which I have not verified. I would ask for the rent roll, the
leases, and two years of operating statements with invoices, and I would say plainly that the
conclusion is theirs and their accountant's, not mine.

## Hour 48 — Residential construction, property condition, and development risk

## 48.1 Why a licensee needs construction vocabulary

Not to inspect. To **describe accurately, recognise a question, and route it**. A licensee who calls
a truss a rafter, or a service panel a meter, will be misunderstood by the professionals they are
trying to involve.

## 48.2 Site and structure

**Site work** — clearing, grading, drainage away from the structure, utility connections, and the
driveway. Poor site drainage is behind a very large share of residential water problems.

**Foundation** — slab on grade, crawl space, or full basement; footings below the local frost depth;
foundation walls of poured concrete or block; damp-proofing or waterproofing; a perimeter drain in
many designs.

**Framing** — sills, floor joists or engineered floor trusses, subfloor, stud walls, headers over
openings, ceiling joists, and roof rafters or **trusses**. A truss is an engineered assembly and
**must not be cut or modified**; a homeowner who cut a truss chord for storage has created a
structural problem.

**Envelope** — sheathing, a weather-resistive barrier, flashing at every penetration and
intersection, cladding, windows and doors, roof covering, underlayment, gutters and downspouts.
**Flashing is where roofs and walls actually leak**, and its absence is invisible from the ground.

**Insulation and air sealing**, measured by R-value; ventilation of attics and crawl spaces; and
vapour control appropriate to the climate.

## 48.3 Systems

**Electrical** — service entrance and its amperage; the panel and its breakers; branch circuits;
grounding and bonding; ground-fault and arc-fault protection in the locations current codes require.
Aluminium branch wiring from a period of the 1960s and 70s, and older fuse panels, are conditions a
buyer should have evaluated.

**Plumbing** — supply piping material, drain-waste-vent, water heater with its temperature and
pressure relief valve and discharge, shutoffs, and — where present — a well and septic system, which
in Virginia are matters for the local health department.

**HVAC** — the heat source and its fuel, distribution, the cooling system and its refrigerant, the
condensate path, the flue or venting, and the age of each component against its expected life.

**Envelope penetrations and appliances** — the dryer vent, the range hood, the bath fans, and whether
each terminates outside rather than into an attic.

## 48.4 New construction

Milestones: **permits**; foundation and its inspection; framing, mechanical rough-ins and their
inspections; insulation inspection; drywall; finishes; final inspection and the **certificate of
occupancy**. The certificate of occupancy is the document that says the building may lawfully be
occupied, and no closing on new construction should occur without confirming it.

**Warranties** — the builder's express warranty, any statutory warranty, and manufacturers'
warranties on components — and the process for making a claim, which has deadlines.

**Change orders** must be in writing and priced before work proceeds, and a builder's contract is
usually the builder's form. A buyer signing one needs counsel, not a licensee's reassurance.

## 48.5 Depreciation, from the physical side

Hour 43's three categories, seen on the property:

**Physical deterioration** — the twenty-year-old roof, the failing water heater, the settled walk.
**Functional obsolescence** — one bathroom for four bedrooms, no closet in a bedroom, a bedroom
reached only through another bedroom, a kitchen that cannot fit a dishwasher.
**External obsolescence** — the new road, the closed plant, the changed neighbourhood. Always
incurable from inside the boundary.

## 48.6 Triage: disclose, investigate, inspect, or refer

The professional skill of the hour. For any observed condition, decide which of four things it is:

**Disclose.** A **material adverse fact pertaining to the physical condition of the property that the
licensee actually knows** must be disclosed in writing under **§ 54.1-2131 B** by a listing licensee
to a prospective buyer, and a material fact related to the property or the transaction of which the
licensee has actual knowledge must be disclosed to the **client** under **§ 54.1-2131 A 6** or
**§ 54.1-2132 A 7**. **Actual knowledge of defective drywall** as defined in § 36-156.1 must be
disclosed under both § 54.1-2131 B and § 54.1-2132 B.

**Investigate.** A documentary question the licensee can properly ask: permit history, the age of a
component, whether a repair was performed by a licensed contractor, whether the certificate of
occupancy issued.

**Inspect.** Anything requiring the opinion of a **home inspector** — defined for Virginia purposes in
**§ 54.1-500** and named in **§ 55.1-703** as an example of the diligence the purchaser is advised to
exercise.

**Refer to a specialist.** A structural engineer for movement, a licensed electrician for a panel, a
roofer, a plumber, a septic evaluator or the local health department, a mould assessor following EPA
guidance — which § 55.1-703 also names — an environmental consultant, or a surveyor for anything at a
boundary.

**What a licensee never does is diagnose.** "It's just settling" and "that stain is old" are opinions
outside the licence, they are frequently wrong, and if wrong they are a misrepresentation under
18VAC135-20-300. The correct sentence is: *I can see it, I have written it down, and here is who can
tell you what it is.*

## 48.7 Development risk

For a buyer of land or a lot in an unfinished development, the questions that decide feasibility:
zoning and permitted use in writing from the zoning administrator; whether the **final plat is
recorded**, because a lot does not exist until it is; the status of public improvements and any bond
or letter of credit; utility availability and capacity, including whether a connection is merely
available or actually permitted; soil and percolation for a septic system; floodplain and wetlands;
the **Chesapeake Bay Preservation Act** resource protection areas that § 55.1-703 expressly tells
purchasers to investigate; access and any VDOT entrance permit; and the association documents and
the developer's remaining control.

### Hour 48 engagement check

**Question.** At a showing you observe a diagonal crack in a basement wall about four feet long, fresh
water staining below it, and a downspout discharging directly against the foundation at that corner.
The house was built in 1974. Triage each observation and state your written communication.

**Expected answer.** All three are **observations to disclose** in writing if they are material
adverse facts about the physical condition actually known — the listing licensee's duty under
**§ 54.1-2131 B**, and a material fact to the client under § 54.1-2131 A 6 or § 54.1-2132 A 7. The
crack with fresh staining is a **specialist referral** — a structural engineer, not a general
inspector's final word — and the downspout is an **investigation** item that may explain it. The
1974 construction date makes this **target housing** for the **federal lead-based paint disclosure**,
which is a separate and mandatory requirement including the pamphlet and the ten-day assessment
opportunity. My written communication states **what I observed**, in plain descriptive terms, names
the **home inspector** and the **structural engineer** as the people who can say what it means, notes
the contingency deadline, and contains **no opinion about the cause or seriousness** — diagnosis is
outside my licence and a wrong one is a misrepresentation under 18VAC135-20-300.
