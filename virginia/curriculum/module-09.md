# Hour 33 — Real property management

## 33.1 What a property manager is engaged to do

A property manager is a licensee engaged by an owner to operate real property: to produce the income
the owner wants, to preserve the asset, and to do both within the law. **§ 54.1-2135** governs
licensees engaged to manage real estate, and the duty architecture is the one from Hour 21 —
performance per the brokerage agreement, promotion of the client's interests, confidentiality,
ordinary care, **timely accounting**, disclosure of material facts of actual knowledge, and
compliance with the article and with fair housing.

Management is the branch of brokerage where the accounting duty does the most work, because the
licensee holds other people's money continuously rather than once.

## 33.2 The management agreement

It is a **brokerage agreement**, so **§ 54.1-2137 C** applies in full: in writing; **a definite
termination date, failing which it terminates 90 days after its date**; the amount of the fees and
how and when they are paid; the services to be rendered; and the other agreed terms. Under
**§ 54.1-2110.1 B 6** it must carry the **supervising broker's name and contact information**.

Beyond the statutory minimum, a workable agreement fixes: the scope of authority — what the manager
may spend without approval, and the threshold above which the owner decides; leasing authority and
the criteria to be applied; rent collection and enforcement authority; maintenance and emergency
authority; the reporting package and its frequency; insurance requirements on both sides;
indemnification; and the handling of the owner's funds and of tenant deposits.

## 33.3 Trust accounting

This is where managers lose licences.

**18VAC135-20-181 A 2** requires **rental payments** and **rental security deposits** to be
deposited into the firm's escrow account, along with the other categories named there. The account
requirements of **A 1** apply in full: federally insured, in the firm's licensed name, labelled
escrow at the institution, with the principal broker responsible and holding signatory authority,
and **the balance sufficient at all times** to account for all funds held.

**18VAC135-20-185 A** requires records showing, for each transaction, from whom money was received,
the date of receipt, the place of deposit, the date of deposit, and the final disposition of the
funds, **readily accessible from the broker's place of business**.

Three rules a manager follows without exception:

**One owner's money is never used for another owner's property.** A pooled escrow account is
permitted; a pooled *ledger* is not. Every owner and every deposit has its own running balance.

**Security deposits are the tenant's money held in trust**, not the owner's income, and they are not
available to fund operations.

**Reconcile monthly**, bank statement to book balance to the sum of the individual ledgers, and
retain the reconciliation. **18VAC135-20-225** provides for audits, and a reconciliation that has
never been performed is what an audit finds.

## 33.4 Budgets and operating statements

The manager builds an annual operating budget: **potential gross income** at full occupancy, less
**vacancy and collection loss**, giving **effective gross income**; less **operating expenses**,
giving **net operating income**. Debt service and capital replacement reserves are handled outside
NOI, and Hour 44 explains why that convention matters to value.

Operating expenses divide into **fixed** — taxes, insurance — and **variable** — utilities,
maintenance, management fee, turnover costs. **Capital expenditures** — a roof, a boiler — are not
operating expenses, and treating a capital item as an operating expense understates NOI and
misstates value.

Reporting to the owner should show, monthly: rent roll and occupancy; collections and delinquency;
income and expense against budget; the trust account balance and the owner's ledger; maintenance
completed and outstanding; and lease expirations coming up.

## 33.5 Leasing, maintenance, and risk

**Leasing** applies Hours 31 and 32: classify the tenancy, use the right chapter of Title 55.1, apply
written objective screening criteria uniformly, and observe § 54.1-2138 B's disclosure requirement
in the lease application or the lease, whichever comes first.

**Maintenance** requires a documented request-to-completion path, because the record of what was
reported and what was done is the whole defence in a habitability dispute. Emergency versus routine
must be defined in advance. Vendors need current insurance certificates on file, and the
worker-classification question — employee or contractor — is a legal and tax question for the owner's
advisers.

**Risk management** in management work means: written policies, which § 54.1-2110.1 B 2 requires the
firm to have in eight areas including fair housing and escrow; consistent documentation; adequate
insurance, including the manager's own errors and omissions coverage; and an escalation path to the
principal broker that people actually use.

**Unlicensed assistants** are common in management offices, and **18VAC135-20-335** sets what they
may and may not do. **18VAC135-20-165 3** requires the supervising broker to provide adequate
supervision over unlicensed individuals. An unlicensed person who negotiates a lease term has
performed licensed activity, and the broker's exposure is decided by **18VAC135-20-330**.

## Hour 33 engagement check

**Question.** A manager holds deposits and rents for 40 owners in one escrow account. In March, a
roof emergency at Owner A's property costs $9,400 and Owner A's ledger holds $3,100. The manager
pays the invoice from the escrow account and asks Owner A to reimburse. Identify the violations.

**Expected answer.** The manager has spent **$6,300 of other owners' and tenants' money**. That is
**conversion**, and it breaches **18VAC135-20-181 A 1 e**, which requires the balance to be
sufficient **at all times** to account for all funds designated to be held — the account is now short
against the sum of the individual ledgers even if the bank balance is positive. It breaches the
timely accounting duty in **§ 54.1-2135**, and the record requirements in **18VAC135-20-185 A**
cannot show a proper disposition of funds. The **principal broker is responsible** for the account
under **18VAC135-20-181 A 1 b**. The correct action was to obtain funds from Owner A, or the owner's
authority to advance, before paying — and the management agreement should have fixed the spending
threshold and the emergency procedure in advance.

# Hour 34 — Promissory notes, mortgages, and deeds of trust

## 34.1 Two documents, two jobs

Every secured real estate loan has **two instruments**, and confusing them is the most common error
in the finance half of a pre-licence course.

**The promissory note** is the **debt**. It is the borrower's personal promise to repay: principal,
interest rate, payment amount and schedule, maturity, late charges, prepayment terms, default, and
acceleration. It is a negotiable instrument and it can be sold; that is what happens when a loan is
sold on the secondary market.

**The security instrument** is the **collateral**. It pledges the real property as security for the
note and creates the lien. It is recorded; the note is not.

Pay off the note and the security instrument must be released of record. The note is the obligation;
the security instrument is the remedy.

## 34.2 Mortgage versus deed of trust — and what Virginia uses

A **mortgage** has **two parties**: the **mortgagor** (borrower, who gives the mortgage) and the
**mortgagee** (lender, who receives it). Enforcement is typically judicial.

A **deed of trust** has **three parties**: the **grantor** or **trustor** (borrower), the
**trustee** (a neutral third party who holds legal or bare title for the purpose of the security),
and the **beneficiary** (lender). On default the trustee, rather than a court, conducts the sale
under the power of sale in the instrument.

**Virginia is a deed-of-trust state.** Residential lending in Virginia is secured by a deed of trust
with a trustee and a power of sale. A student who learns "mortgagor and mortgagee" from a national
text must translate: in Virginia, the everyday parties are **grantor, trustee, and beneficiary** —
though "mortgage" is universally used in ordinary speech, and the Code itself uses both words.

The distinction is not academic. It determines who conducts a foreclosure, whether a court is
involved, and how quickly it happens.

**Lien theory and title theory** are the traditional labels for the two ways of describing what the
borrower keeps. Under **lien theory** the borrower holds title and the lender holds a lien. Under
**title theory** the lender holds title until the debt is paid. Deed-of-trust states are often
described as intermediate, because the trustee holds bare legal title for a limited purpose. The
practical point is the same everywhere: the borrower has possession and the benefits of ownership,
and the lender's interest is security.

## 34.3 The clauses that decide outcomes

**Acceleration.** On default, the lender may declare the entire unpaid balance immediately due.
Without it, a lender could only sue for missed payments. Every foreclosure begins here.

**Alienation, or due-on-sale.** The lender may accelerate if the property is transferred without
consent. This is what makes most modern loans **non-assumable**, and it is why "taking over
payments" is usually not available.

**Prepayment.** Whether the borrower may pay early and on what terms. Prepayment penalties are
restricted on many consumer loans.

**Defeasance.** On full payment the security instrument is defeated and must be released — in
Virginia, by a **certificate of satisfaction** recorded in the land records.

**Subordination.** An agreement to take a lower lien position than recording order would give.
Hour 13 explained why refinancing a first while a home equity line remains requires it.

**Escrow or impound.** The lender collects taxes and insurance monthly and pays them when due,
protecting its own collateral against a tax sale or an uninsured loss.

## 34.4 Assumption, subject-to, and seller financing

**Assumption** — the buyer takes over the existing loan and becomes personally liable. Whether the
seller is **released** depends on the lender: without a written release, the seller remains liable,
and the parallel with assignment in Hour 31 is exact.

**Subject to** — the buyer takes title with the loan still in place but does **not** assume personal
liability. The seller remains liable on the note, the loan remains in the seller's name, and the
due-on-sale clause is very likely triggered. This is a structure clients hear about and should not
enter without counsel.

**Seller financing** — the seller takes back a note secured by a deed of trust, in whole or in
part. A **wraparound** wraps an existing loan inside a new larger one, and it is only viable where
the underlying loan permits it. Federal consumer-protection rules constrain seller financing of
owner-occupied residential property. Every one of these is a legal and tax structure, and the
licensee's role is to say so.

**Land contract** — also called an installment contract or contract for deed — leaves legal title
with the seller until payment is complete while the buyer takes possession and equitable title.
Virginia has a **Residential Executory Real Estate Contracts Act** at Chapter 30 of Title 55.1
governing this arrangement; its provisions have not been read from primary text for this project and
this course states none of them. A licensee who encounters an installment contract routes it to
counsel and to that chapter.

## 34.5 Recording, priority, and release

The deed of trust is recorded in the land records of the circuit court of the city or county, and
its priority ordinarily runs from the date and time of recording, subject to the exceptions in Hour
13 — statutory tax priority, the relation-back of mechanics' liens, and subordination.

On payoff, a **certificate of satisfaction** is recorded. Payment without release leaves the lien of
record, and an unreleased paid-off deed of trust is one of the most common clouds a title
examination reveals.

## Hour 34 engagement check

**Question.** In a Virginia residential purchase, the buyer borrows $340,000 from a bank. Name every
document created, name each party to the security instrument by its Virginia name, and say which
document is recorded and which is not.

**Expected answer.** Two instruments: a **promissory note**, which is the buyer's promise to repay
and is **not recorded**, and a **deed of trust**, which secures the note against the property and
**is recorded** in the land records of the circuit court. The parties to the deed of trust are the
**grantor** (the buyer-borrower), the **trustee** (a neutral third party holding bare legal title
for the purpose of the security, with the power of sale), and the **beneficiary** (the bank). The
**deed** conveying the property from seller to buyer is a third, separate instrument and is also
recorded. On payoff, a **certificate of satisfaction** must be recorded to release the deed of trust.

# Hour 35 — Default, foreclosure, and lien enforcement

## 35.1 The sequence

**Default** is failure to perform: missed payments most often, but also failure to pay taxes, to
maintain insurance, or to maintain the property.

**Notice and demand.** The servicer notifies the borrower and demands cure. Federal servicing rules
impose requirements on the servicer before it may proceed, including timing rules and loss-
mitigation obligations.

**Acceleration.** The lender declares the whole balance due, under the acceleration clause.

**Reinstatement** is the borrower's opportunity to cure by paying arrears plus costs and having the
loan restored; **redemption** is paying the entire accelerated balance. Whether, and for how long,
each is available in Virginia is governed by the deed of trust and by statute, and **this course
states no period**, because Virginia's foreclosure provisions have not been read from primary text
for this project.

**Sale.** In a deed-of-trust state, the **trustee** conducts a sale under the power of sale in the
instrument, after the advertisement and notice the instrument and the statute require.

**Distribution of proceeds.** Costs of sale first, then the foreclosing lien, then junior liens in
priority order, then any surplus to the borrower.

**Deficiency.** Where the sale proceeds are less than the debt, the lender may in some circumstances
pursue the borrower for the shortfall.

## 35.2 What survives the sale, and what does not

The rule from Hour 13, restated because it is the one buyers at foreclosure sales get wrong:
**liens junior to the foreclosing lien are extinguished as to the property; senior liens survive and
the purchaser takes subject to them.** Real property tax liens hold statutory priority and survive.

A purchaser at a trustee's sale is buying whatever the trustee can convey, usually with no
warranties, no inspection, and no vacant possession. It is a transaction for people with counsel and
a title search, and a licensee's contribution is to say so plainly.

## 35.3 Alternatives to foreclosure

**Reinstatement** — cure the arrears.
**Loan modification** — the lender changes the terms.
**Forbearance** — payments are reduced or suspended for a period and made up later.
**Repayment plan** — arrears are spread over subsequent payments.
**Short sale** — the property is sold for less than the balance with the lender's agreement to
release the lien; the lender must approve, deficiency treatment must be addressed in writing, and
there are tax consequences.
**Deed in lieu of foreclosure** — the borrower conveys to the lender voluntarily; junior liens
generally survive, which is why lenders often refuse it.

Each has consequences for credit and for tax that the licensee is not qualified to explain. The
professional response is to identify the options as options and route the borrower to a **HUD-approved
housing counselling agency**, to the servicer's loss-mitigation department, and to counsel.

## 35.4 Where licensees get into trouble

Distressed-property work attracts problems, and three are worth naming.

**Foreclosure rescue and equity-skimming schemes.** Arrangements in which a distressed owner deeds
the property to someone who promises to save it, then loses everything. A licensee approached to
participate should decline and report.

**Advance-fee arrangements** for loan modification. Restricted by federal rule and by state law.

**Advising a client to stop paying** in order to qualify for something. This is advice a licensee is
not qualified to give and whose consequences fall entirely on the client.

Note also **§ 54.1-2108.1**, which protects escrow funds held by a broker in the event of
**foreclosure of the real property** — a Virginia-specific protection that exists because deposits
were being lost in exactly that situation.

## 35.5 Other enforcement

**Tax sale.** Unpaid real property taxes are enforced by a statutory process ending in sale.

**Mechanic's lien enforcement.** Title 43, with strict deadlines, and — per Hour 13 —
**relation back** that can put a later-filed lien ahead of an earlier-recorded deed of trust.

**Judgment execution.** A docketed judgment is a general lien on the debtor's real property in the
jurisdiction, including property acquired later, and it must be satisfied at closing.

**Association assessment liens** are enforced under the Property Owners' Association Act or the
Condominium Act.

## Hour 35 engagement check

**Question.** A buyer says, "I want to buy at the trustee's sale — the opening bid is far below
market and I'll wipe out the other liens." Correct the three assumptions embedded in that sentence.

**Expected answer.** First, **not all liens are wiped out**: only liens **junior** to the foreclosing
deed of trust are extinguished as to the property. Senior liens survive and the purchaser takes
subject to them, and **real property tax liens hold statutory priority** and survive regardless of
date. Second, an opening bid is not evidence of value; it reflects the debt and costs, and the
purchaser needs an independent basis for value. Third, the purchaser is buying **whatever the trustee
can convey**, generally without warranties, without inspection, and without assured vacant
possession, so a **title search and counsel are prerequisites**, not refinements. The licensee's role
is to state those three things and refer the buyer to counsel — not to advise on bidding.

# Hour 36 — Conventional, FHA, VA, and USDA mortgage programs

## 36.1 The one rule for this hour

Program guidelines change, sometimes several times a year, and eligibility depends on facts the
licensee does not have. **A licensee describes program categories; a lender determines eligibility.**
Under **§ 54.1-2131 A 4** and **§ 54.1-2132 A 5** the duty is ordinary care, and under
**18VAC135-20-300** a material misstatement is a disciplinary matter. Telling a buyer "you'll
qualify" is both.

## 36.2 Conventional loans

A **conventional** loan is one not insured or guaranteed by a government agency.

**Conforming** conventional loans meet the purchase criteria of the government-sponsored
enterprises — Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac — including a maximum loan amount that is set annually and
varies by county. **Non-conforming** loans do not: **jumbo** loans exceed the limit, and other loans
fail a different criterion.

**Private mortgage insurance** is generally required on a conventional loan where the borrower puts
down less than 20 percent. It protects the **lender**, not the borrower — the most persistent
consumer misunderstanding in residential lending. Federal law provides for cancellation and
automatic termination of borrower-paid PMI on the basis of the loan's amortisation, and the specific
thresholds are the lender's and the statute's to state, not the licensee's.

**The secondary market** explains why guidelines exist at all. Lenders sell loans to Fannie Mae,
Freddie Mac, and private investors, and Ginnie Mae guarantees securities backed by government loans.
A loan that does not meet the buyer's criteria cannot be sold, so the criteria govern origination.

## 36.3 FHA

The **Federal Housing Administration**, part of HUD, **insures** loans made by approved lenders. It
does not lend.

Characteristic features: a low minimum down payment; more flexible credit and ratio criteria than
conventional underwriting; **mortgage insurance premiums** — an upfront premium and an annual
premium — whose duration depends on the loan; **maximum loan amounts that vary by county**; and a
requirement that the property meet **minimum property standards** and be appraised by an
FHA-approved appraiser. FHA loans are for **owner-occupants**, not investors.

The point a buyer's licensee must convey without overstating it: an FHA appraisal serves the
insurer's interest in the property's condition as well as its value, and a property with certain
deficiencies may need repair before the loan can close. That is a real scheduling consequence for a
seller.

## 36.4 VA

The **Department of Veterans Affairs** **guarantees** a portion of a loan made by an approved lender
to an eligible veteran, service member, or surviving spouse.

Characteristic features: **no down payment** in many cases; **no monthly mortgage insurance**; a
**funding fee** that varies and is waived in defined circumstances; an entitlement determined by a
**certificate of eligibility**; a **VA appraisal** producing a notice of value; and **occupancy**
requirements — this is not an investor programme.

Eligibility is determined by the VA and the lender. A licensee should never tell a veteran whether
they are eligible, how much entitlement they have, or whether the funding fee is waived.

## 36.5 USDA

The **US Department of Agriculture** operates rural housing programmes, guaranteeing loans made by
approved lenders and, in some programmes, lending directly.

Characteristic features: **no down payment**; **geographic eligibility** limited to designated rural
areas, which are defined by USDA maps that change; **household income limits** by area and household
size; **owner-occupancy**; and guarantee and annual fees.

The two eligibility gates — the **property's location** and the **household's income** — are both
checked against USDA's own tools, and both change. A property that qualified last year may not now.

## 36.6 Talking about financing without misrepresenting it

Three habits keep a licensee on the right side of this.

**Describe categories, never outcomes.** "That is generally an FHA question; here is a lender who can
tell you" rather than "FHA will take you at that credit score."

**Send the buyer to a lender before the search, not after.** A **pre-qualification** is an informal
estimate from unverified information. A **pre-approval** rests on a submitted application, verified
documentation, and usually an underwriting decision subject to a property. They are not the same
thing, and a seller evaluating offers is entitled to know which one is in front of them.

**Do not let a preference become steering.** A seller may lawfully weigh the financing attached to an
offer on the basis of its likelihood of closing. What a licensee must never do is convert that into
a rule of thumb that excludes buyers using a particular programme without reference to the offer in
front of them — and a general refusal to consider VA or FHA offers can shade into practices that
implicate **military status**, a Virginia protected class under § 36-96.3, and into disparate
treatment on other protected bases.

## Hour 36 engagement check

**Question.** A buyer says, "my credit is around 600, I have $6,000 saved, I'm an Army veteran, and
I want a house 20 minutes outside town." Identify which programme categories are worth exploring and
what you may and may not say.

**Expected answer.** Worth exploring: **VA**, because the buyer is a veteran and VA loans commonly
require no down payment and no monthly mortgage insurance, with entitlement determined by a
certificate of eligibility; **FHA**, which is designed for lower down payments and more flexible
credit criteria; and possibly **USDA**, depending on whether the property is in a designated rural
area and whether household income is within the area limit. What may be said is that these are the
**categories** to raise with a lender. What may **not** be said is that the buyer qualifies for any
of them, what rate or funding fee applies, or that $6,000 is enough — eligibility and cost are the
lender's and the agency's determinations, and stating them would breach the ordinary-care duty in
§ 54.1-2132 A 5 and risk a misrepresentation under 18VAC135-20-300.

**Deliberately not stated, for want of a primary reading:** every **Virginia foreclosure period,

own schedules and are deliberately given without figures. The hour is written so that the correct
student answer is a referral to a lender.
