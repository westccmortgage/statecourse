# Hour 13 — Liens, priority, and release

## 13.1 What a lien is

A **lien** is a charge against property that secures payment of a debt or performance of an
obligation. It is an encumbrance on title, and it gives the lienholder the right to force a sale of
the property if the obligation is not met.

Liens do not affect possession. An owner with three liens still lives in the house. What liens
affect is **title**, and therefore transferability: a lien must be satisfied or otherwise dealt
with before clear title passes, which is why they surface at closing rather than in daily life.

## 13.2 Two classifications, used together

**Voluntary or involuntary.** A **voluntary** lien is one the owner creates deliberately — in
Virginia, most commonly a **deed of trust** given to secure a loan. An **involuntary** lien arises
by operation of law without the owner's agreement: tax liens, judgment liens, mechanics' liens.

**Statutory or equitable.** A **statutory** lien is created by statute — the tax lien, the
mechanic's lien. An **equitable** lien arises from a court's application of fairness or from the
conduct of the parties.

**General or specific.** A **general** lien attaches to **all** of the debtor's property, real and
personal, within the jurisdiction: judgment liens, federal and state tax liens, estate liens for
death taxes. A **specific** lien attaches to **one identified parcel**: the deed of trust, the real
property tax lien, the mechanic's lien, the special assessment.

Every lien is describable in both dimensions. A judgment lien is involuntary, statutory, and
general. A deed of trust is voluntary and specific.

## 13.3 The particular liens

**Real property tax liens** secure ad valorem taxes. They are specific, involuntary, and — in most
states, and generally in Virginia — **superior to all other liens regardless of when other liens
were recorded**. That priority is not a matter of recording date; it is conferred by statute.

**Special assessment liens** secure the cost of a public improvement that benefits particular
properties — a sewer extension, sidewalks, street lighting. Specific, involuntary, usually payable
in instalments, and typically carrying a high priority.

**Deed of trust liens.** Virginia is a deed-of-trust jurisdiction rather than a mortgage
jurisdiction; the mechanics are Hour 34. As a lien, a deed of trust is voluntary and specific, and
its priority ordinarily runs from the date and time of recording.

**Judgment liens** arise when a court judgment is docketed and attach to the debtor's real property
in that jurisdiction. General, involuntary, and — importantly — they attach to property the debtor
acquires later as well as property owned at the time. A judgment against a seller shows up in the
title search and must be paid at closing.

**Mechanics' and materialmen's liens** secure payment for labour and materials furnished to improve
real property. They are the most dangerous lien for a buyer because of **relation back**: the
priority of a mechanic's lien is generally measured not from the date of filing but from an earlier
date tied to the commencement of work or the furnishing of materials, so a lien filed after closing
can outrank a deed of trust recorded before it. Virginia's mechanics' lien scheme is in Title 43,
with strict deadlines and formalities. **This course does not state those deadlines**, because
Title 43 has not been read from primary text for this project. What a licensee must know is the
warning sign: recent or ongoing construction on a property being sold is a question for the
settlement agent and counsel, every time, without exception.

**Federal tax liens** — from unpaid federal taxes — are general and involuntary. **Estate and
inheritance tax liens** attach on death.

**Association assessment liens** are created by the governing documents and by statute for
condominiums and property owners' associations, and are covered in Hours 29 and 30.

**Vendor's and vendee's liens** are equitable liens securing, respectively, an unpaid seller and a
buyer's deposit.

## 13.4 Priority

**Priority determines who is paid first from a forced sale, and whose lien survives it.** Liens
junior to the foreclosing lien are generally extinguished as to the property; senior liens survive
and the buyer takes subject to them.

The general rule is **first to record, first in right**. Three sets of exceptions:

**Statutory superpriority.** Real property tax liens and, in most schemes, special assessments come
first whenever recorded.

**Relation back.** Mechanics' liens, as above.

**Subordination.** A lienholder may agree in a recorded **subordination agreement** to take a lower
position than recording order would give. This is routine when an owner refinances a first deed of
trust while a home equity line remains: without subordination, the equity line would advance to
first position and the new lender would refuse to close.

## 13.5 Release and the licensee's role

A lien ends when the obligation is satisfied and a **release** — for a deed of trust, a
**certificate of satisfaction** — is recorded. Payment alone does not clear the record. Unreleased
paid-off deeds of trust are among the most common clouds a title search turns up, and clearing one
requires locating a lender that may have been acquired twice since.

Liens are found by title search, cleared by the settlement agent, and interpreted by counsel. The
licensee's contribution is timing and disclosure: get the title work ordered early, read Schedule
B-I when it arrives, escalate anything involving construction or a judgment immediately, and
disclose material facts actually known under § 54.1-2131 A 6 or § 54.1-2132 A 7.

## Hour 13 engagement check

**Question.** A property is sold at a foreclosure sale under a deed of trust recorded in 2019.
Also of record: unpaid real property taxes for the current year, a judgment docketed in 2021, and a
second deed of trust recorded in 2022. Rank the claims on the sale proceeds and say which liens
survive the sale.

**Expected answer.** The **real property tax lien** is paid first by statutory priority regardless
of date. Then the **2019 first deed of trust**, the foreclosing lien. Then, from any surplus, the
**2021 judgment**, then the **2022 second deed of trust**, in recording order. The 2021 judgment and
the 2022 deed of trust are **junior** to the foreclosing lien and are extinguished as to the
property, whether or not they are paid; the debts themselves may survive against the debtor
personally. Nothing here is advice a licensee gives — this is why a buyer at a foreclosure sale
needs counsel.

# Hour 14 — Taxes in real estate

## 14.1 Ad valorem taxation

**Ad valorem** means "according to value". Real property tax is levied annually on the assessed
value of the property, by the locality — in Virginia, the city or county — and it funds schools,
public safety, roads, and local services.

The arithmetic has three moving parts:

**Assessed value** — the value the locality's assessor places on the property. It may equal market
value or a stated fraction of it, depending on the jurisdiction's practice, and it is set on an
assessment cycle rather than continuously.

**The rate** — set annually by the governing body. In Virginia it is conventionally expressed in
dollars per $100 of assessed value. A rate of $1.05 means $1.05 of tax for every $100 of assessed
value. Elsewhere rates are expressed in **mills** — thousandths of a dollar, so 10.5 mills is the
same as $10.50 per $1,000, or $1.05 per $100. Students should be able to move between the two.

**Exemptions and relief** — reductions available to particular owners or uses: religious and
charitable ownership, government property, land-use assessment for agricultural and forest land,
and relief programmes for the elderly, for people with disabilities, and for certain disabled
veterans. Eligibility is determined by the locality, not by the licensee.

**Worked example.** Assessed value $340,000; rate $1.05 per $100.
$340,000 ÷ 100 = 3,400 hundreds. 3,400 × $1.05 = **$3,570 annual tax.**

If instead the rate is expressed as 10.5 mills: $340,000 × 0.0105 = **$3,570.** Same answer.

## 14.2 Assessment, appeal, and what a licensee may say

An owner who believes an assessment is wrong has an administrative route — an appeal to the
assessor, then to a board of equalisation, then to court. The grounds are that the assessment
exceeds fair market value or is not uniform with comparable properties.

Two cautions belong here. First, **assessed value is not market value and must never be presented
to a client as evidence of it.** A buyer told that "the assessment is $340,000, so it's worth at
least that" has been misinformed. Second, an assessment can and often does change **after a sale**,
and a licensee who tells a buyer their taxes will stay at the seller's level is making a prediction
that will frequently prove false. The accurate statement is that the current tax is X, that
assessment practice is set by the locality, and that the buyer should confirm with the
commissioner of the revenue or assessor's office.

## 14.3 Other taxes touching real estate

**Recordation tax** is charged on recording deeds and deeds of trust; **grantor's tax** is charged
to the seller on conveyance. Rates and the allocation between the parties are set by statute and by
contract, and the settlement agent computes them. This course states no figure, because the
statutory rates in Title 58.1 have not been read from primary text for this project.

**Transfer of a principal residence and capital gain.** A homeowner selling a residence may be
eligible to exclude gain from federal income tax subject to ownership and use requirements and
dollar limits set by federal law. **Basis** is broadly the purchase price plus capital improvements
minus depreciation taken. Every element of this depends on the taxpayer's circumstances and on law
that changes.

**Income-property taxation** — depreciation, passive-activity treatment, deferred exchanges —
belongs to Hour 46 and to the client's tax professional.

**Property tax deduction and mortgage interest deduction** are federal matters subject to
limitations that change.

## 14.4 The referral rule, stated plainly

A real estate licensee is not licensed to give tax advice. The client's tax outcome depends on
facts the licensee does not have and on rules the licensee is not qualified to apply. Under
§ 54.1-2131 A 4 the duty is **ordinary care**, and telling a client something outside the
licensee's competence is not ordinary care. Under 18VAC135-20-300 a material misstatement is a
disciplinary matter.

The professional formulation: *here is the current annual tax on this property from the
locality's record; here is what the assessment cycle is; questions about what you will owe, and
about the tax consequences of buying or selling, go to your tax adviser, and I am putting that in
writing.*

## 14.5 Proration, introduced

**Proration** divides a shared expense between seller and buyer at closing according to the period
each owns the property. Taxes, association dues, rent, and prepaid fuel are commonly prorated. Full
mechanics are Hour 41; the concept is introduced here because it is a tax topic.

**Worked example.** Annual tax $3,570, paid in arrears, settlement on 15 September, using a
365-day year and charging the day of settlement to the buyer.
Daily rate: $3,570 ÷ 365 = **$9.7808** per day.
Seller's period: 1 January through 14 September = 257 days.
Seller's share: 257 × $9.7808 = **$2,513.67**, which appears as a **credit to the buyer and a debit
to the seller**, because the buyer will pay the whole bill when it comes due.

Change any convention — a 360-day year, a different settlement-day rule, taxes paid in advance —
and the number changes. **The convention comes from the contract, not from a habit.**

## Hour 14 engagement check

**Question.** A property is assessed at $412,000 in a locality with a rate of $0.89 per $100.
Compute the annual tax. Then state, in one sentence each, why the licensee may not tell the buyer
(a) that the property is worth at least $412,000 and (b) that the tax will remain at this level.

**Expected answer.** $412,000 ÷ 100 = 4,120; 4,120 × $0.89 = **$3,666.80**. (a) Assessed value is
an administrative figure produced on an assessment cycle for taxation and is not evidence of market
value; presenting it as such is a misrepresentation. (b) Assessment practice and rates are set by
the locality and commonly change after a sale, so a statement that the tax will stay level is a
prediction the licensee cannot support and the buyer must confirm with the locality.

# Hour 15 — Contract foundations

## 15.1 The four requirements

A **contract** is a legally enforceable agreement. Four elements are required:

**Mutual assent** — an offer and an acceptance, producing a meeting of the minds on the essential
terms. Assent must be genuine: fraud, misrepresentation, mistake, duress, and undue influence each
undermine it.

**Competent parties** — parties with legal capacity. Minors, persons adjudicated incompetent, and
persons lacking capacity at the moment of contracting are treated specially; a minor's contract is
generally **voidable by the minor**.

**Lawful objective** — the purpose must be legal. A contract to do something illegal is void and no
court will enforce it.

**Consideration** — something of legal value exchanged by each side. Courts examine whether
consideration exists, not whether it was adequate.

For contracts concerning land there is a fifth practical requirement: the **statute of frauds**
requires a contract for the sale of an interest in real property to be **in writing and signed by
the party to be charged**. An oral agreement to sell land is not enforceable, however clear the
evidence of it.

## 15.2 The four states of a contract

Students confuse these constantly, and examination questions exploit it.

**Valid** — meets all requirements and is binding on both parties.

**Void** — has no legal effect at all and never did. Nothing to enforce. An agreement with an
illegal objective, or a deed from a person adjudicated incompetent.

**Voidable** — valid and enforceable **unless** the party with the power to avoid it chooses to
disaffirm. A minor's contract; a contract induced by fraud, at the defrauded party's option. Until
disaffirmed, it operates.

**Unenforceable** — valid on its face but not enforceable in court. An oral land contract is the
standard example; so is a claim barred by the statute of limitations. The parties may perform it
voluntarily; no court will make them.

## 15.3 Other classifications

**Bilateral or unilateral.** In a **bilateral** contract each side makes a promise — a residential
sales contract is bilateral. In a **unilateral** contract only one party promises, and the other
accepts by performing; an open listing and a reward offer are the classic examples.

**Executory or executed.** **Executory** means something remains to be done — a ratified sales
contract before settlement is executory. **Executed** means fully performed. Note the trap: a
signed contract is often loosely called "executed" in the industry, meaning signed, while the legal
meaning is *performed*. Examinations use the legal meaning.

**Express or implied.** **Express** contracts are stated in words, written or spoken. **Implied**
contracts are inferred from conduct.

## 15.4 Discharge, breach, and remedies

A contract is discharged by **performance**, by **mutual agreement to rescind**, by **novation** —
substituting a new contract or a new party with the consent of all — by **operation of law**, or by
**breach**.

Remedies for breach in a real estate contract include:

**Damages** — money to put the injured party where performance would have. **Liquidated damages** —
a sum the parties fixed in advance, commonly the earnest money deposit. **Rescission** — unwinding
the contract and restoring the parties. **Specific performance** — a court order to convey, which
is available in land contracts precisely because every parcel is unique and money is treated as an
inadequate substitute.

**Assignment** transfers a party's rights under a contract to another; **novation** replaces a
party entirely and, unlike assignment, releases the original party.

## 15.5 The licensee's boundary

**§ 54.1-2101.1** of the Code of Virginia governs preparation of real estate contracts by
licensees, and §§ 54.1-2131 A 2 b and 54.1-2132 A 3 b describe the duty as *assisting in the
drafting and negotiating of offers and counteroffers, amendments, and addenda to the real estate
contract pursuant to § 54.1-2101.1*.

Read those together and the shape is clear: filling in and assisting with contract forms in the
manner the statute permits is licensed activity. **Deciding what a clause means, drafting bespoke
language to solve a legal problem, or advising a client whether to declare a breach is not.** The
question "am I in default?" goes to an attorney every time.

## Hour 15 engagement check

**Question.** Classify each and give the one-line reason. (a) An oral agreement to buy a house,
fully agreed on all terms. (b) A sales contract signed by a 16-year-old buyer. (c) A contract to
buy a property for use as an unlicensed gambling operation. (d) A ratified sales contract awaiting
settlement in three weeks.

**Expected answer.** (a) **Unenforceable** — the statute of frauds requires a signed writing for a
contract concerning an interest in land. (b) **Voidable** at the minor's option — the minor lacks
full capacity, and the contract operates until disaffirmed. (c) **Void** — unlawful objective, no
legal effect from the outset. (d) **Valid and executory** — all elements are present and
performance remains outstanding.

# Hour 16 — Offer, acceptance, and contract formation

## 16.1 The offer

An **offer** is a proposal that, if accepted, forms a contract. It must show present intent to be
bound, be communicated to the offeree, and state the essential terms with enough definiteness that
a court could enforce them — parties, property, price, and the material terms of the transaction.

An advertisement is generally **not** an offer; it is an invitation to make one. A listing is not an
offer to sell to whoever appears.

## 16.2 How an offer ends

An offer terminates on:

**Acceptance**, which forms the contract.

**Rejection.** Once rejected, an offer is dead. The offeree cannot change their mind and accept it.

**Counteroffer**, which is in law a rejection plus a new offer. This is the mechanic students most
often get wrong, and it has a practical consequence worth stating: **the moment a seller
counteroffers, the buyer's original offer no longer exists**, and if the buyer walks away the seller
cannot then accept the original.

**Revocation by the offeror**, effective when communicated to the offeree, at any time before
acceptance — unless the offeree has paid for an **option**, which is a separate contract holding the
offer open for a stated period.

**Lapse of time**, on the expiration stated in the offer, or after a reasonable time if none is
stated.

**Death or incapacity** of either party before acceptance.

**Destruction of the subject property** before acceptance.

## 16.3 Acceptance

Acceptance must be **unqualified** — the mirror image rule — and must be **communicated** in the
manner the offer requires or permits. Any change to terms is a counteroffer.

The moment of formation matters because it starts every deadline in the contract. Two rules
compete. The traditional **mailbox rule** treats a mailed acceptance as effective on dispatch. Most
modern residential contracts displace it by requiring **delivery** of the signed acceptance, and
defining what delivery means. **Read the contract; do not assume the common-law rule.**

## 16.4 Multiple offers

The rules above resolve most multiple-offer confusion.

A seller may accept one offer, counter one, counter several, or reject all. Countering several
simultaneously creates a real risk: if two counteroffers are accepted, the seller may have two
contracts on one property. Sellers who wish to negotiate with several buyers at once need language
making acceptance subject to the seller's further written approval — and drafting that language is
a legal question.

The licensee's own duties in a multiple-offer situation are statutory and specific. Under
§ 54.1-2131 A 2 c a licensee engaged by a seller must **receive and present in a timely manner
written offers and counteroffers to and from the seller and purchasers, even when the property is
already subject to a contract of sale.** Under 18VAC135-20-300 3 it is misrepresentation or
omission to fail **to tender promptly to the client or unrepresented party every written offer,
every written counteroffer, and every written rejection**.

Two consequences follow that licensees get wrong: **every** written offer goes to the seller,
including one the licensee thinks is insulting; and an existing ratified contract does not stop the
obligation to present a later offer, though it does not entitle the seller to accept it either.

Whether to disclose the existence or terms of competing offers is a different question, controlled
by the client's instructions and by the confidentiality duty in § 54.1-2131 A 3. The client decides
what is disclosed about their own transaction; the licensee follows lawful instructions and does
not volunteer another client's confidences.

## 16.5 Amendments, addenda, assignment, and electronic signature

An **addendum** is attached at the time of contracting and forms part of the original agreement. An
**amendment** changes an existing contract and requires the assent of all parties. A **contingency**
is a condition that must be satisfied or waived for a duty to become absolute, and Hour 18 covers
their operation.

**Assignment** of a real estate contract transfers the assignor's rights to an assignee; whether it
is permitted depends on the contract's own terms, and many residential forms restrict it.

**Electronic records and signatures** are effective for real estate contracts under federal
E-SIGN and state adoptions of the Uniform Electronic Transactions Act, subject to the parties'
consent to transact electronically. The professional issues are practical rather than doctrinal:
verifying identity, preserving the record, controlling version drift when several counter-signed
copies circulate, and — the risk that actually causes losses — **wire fraud**, where an intercepted
email carries changed settlement instructions. Hour 50 is devoted to it.

## Hour 16 engagement check

**Question.** Buyer A offers $400,000. Seller counters at $415,000. While the counteroffer is
outstanding, Buyer B offers $412,000. Buyer A then says "we accept your counter" but has not yet
delivered a signed copy. The seller now wants Buyer B's offer. What is the position, and what must
the licensee do with Buyer B's offer?

**Expected answer.** Whether a contract with Buyer A has formed depends on the contract's own
delivery requirement: under most modern residential forms acceptance is effective on **delivery**
of the signed acceptance, not on an oral statement of intent, so on these facts a contract has
probably not yet formed and the seller may still revoke the counteroffer before delivery. That is
close enough to a legal question that the correct professional step is counsel, not a licensee's
ruling. What is not in doubt is the licensee's own duty: **Buyer B's written offer must be
presented to the seller promptly** under § 54.1-2131 A 2 c and 18VAC135-20-300 3, and it must be
presented even if the licensee believes a contract with Buyer A already exists.

`virginia/regulatory/primary-text-readings.md`.

Three matters are deliberately **left unstated** for want of a primary reading: the deadlines and
formalities of the **Virginia mechanics' lien** scheme in **Title 43**; the **recordation and
grantor's tax rates** in **Title 58.1**; and the statutory formulation of **tax-lien priority**.
Each is identified to the student as a question for the settlement agent or counsel rather than
answered with a figure. An instructor supplementing this text must read the title first.

The proration in Hour 14.5 uses a 365-day year and charges the settlement day to the buyer. Both
are **conventions taken from the contract**, not rules of law, and the hour says so.
