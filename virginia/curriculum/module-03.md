# Virginia Principles and Practices of Real Estate — Module 03

## Course Day 3 · Hours 9–12 · Describing land, conveying it, and proving title

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module the student will be able to:

- Read a metes-and-bounds description well enough to detect an ambiguity, and explain why a street
  address is not a legal description.
- List the elements a deed must contain to be valid, and explain what delivery and acceptance add
  that signature does not.
- Distinguish a general warranty deed, a special warranty deed, and a quitclaim deed by what each
  promises.
- Explain actual, constructive, and inquiry notice, and why recording protects a buyer.
- Read a title commitment well enough to separate requirements, exceptions, and insured risks.

## Hour 09 — Legal descriptions, plats, and surveys

## 9.1 Why the address will not do

A street address identifies a mailbox. It is assigned administratively, it can be changed by a
locality, it does not describe boundaries, and two different parcels can share one. A **legal
description** identifies a unique parcel of land in a way a surveyor could reproduce on the ground.
Deeds, deeds of trust, plats, and judgments use legal descriptions. Contracts should.

Three systems are in use in the United States. Virginia uses two of them.

## 9.2 Metes and bounds

**Metes and bounds** describes a boundary by walking it. *Metes* are distances and *bounds* are
directions and limits. A description begins at a **point of beginning** — a fixed, identifiable
starting point — and proceeds by a sequence of calls, each giving a direction and a distance,
until it **closes** back at the point of beginning. A description that does not close is defective.

A call reads, for example, *N 45° 30′ 00″ E, 210.50 feet to an iron pin*. The bearing is read as a
deviation from north or south, toward east or west: north forty-five degrees thirty minutes east.
Degrees, minutes, seconds — sixty minutes to a degree, sixty seconds to a minute.

**Monuments** are physical markers referenced in the calls: an iron pin, a concrete monument, a
stone, a tree, a road centreline, a watercourse. The traditional rule of construction is that
where a description conflicts internally, **monuments control over courses and distances**,
because a monument is what a person on the ground can actually find. A description calling for
"the centreline of Mill Creek" governs even if the stated distance no longer reaches it.

Metes and bounds is the dominant system in Virginia, as in the original thirteen colonies. It is
also the system with the most ways to go wrong: natural monuments that move or disappear, calls
that reference "the old Johnson fence", adjoining-owner references that changed hands a century
ago, and descriptions carried forward unchanged through generations of deeds while the physical
world moved.

## 9.3 Lot and block

The **lot and block** system — also called the recorded plat or subdivision system — describes a
parcel by reference to a **recorded plat**: *Lot 14, Block C, Fairview Estates, as recorded in Plat
Book 22, page 118, among the land records of the Circuit Court of ___ County, Virginia.*

The plat itself carries the metes and bounds of every lot, the streets, the easements, and the
surveyor's certification. Once recorded, the plat is incorporated into every deed that references
it. This is the system for essentially all modern subdivision property, and it is why Hour 8
insisted that a lot does not exist until the plat is recorded.

## 9.4 The rectangular survey system, and why Virginia does not use it

The **rectangular** or **government survey system** — principal meridians, base lines, townships
six miles square, thirty-six sections of one square mile each, sections of 640 acres divided by
quarters — governs most land west and north of the original colonies. **Virginia is not a public
land survey state.** A student who has studied a national text will meet township-and-range
questions on a national examination and should understand the arithmetic — a quarter of a quarter
section is 40 acres, a half of a quarter is 80 — but should not expect to see it in a Virginia
deed.

## 9.5 Surveys, and what they are for

A **survey** is a licensed surveyor's determination of where the boundaries are on the ground.
A **plat of survey** shows the parcel, the improvements, and anything crossing a line.

A survey answers questions a title search cannot. The title record shows what *should* be true;
the survey shows what *is*. Encroachments, structures over setback lines, fences that do not follow
the boundary, driveways serving the neighbour, and a septic field on the wrong lot are found by
survey and by nothing else.

The licensee's role is to know when to recommend one — irregular parcels, older metes-and-bounds
descriptions, visible fences and structures near lines, planned construction, unimproved land, any
mention of a boundary dispute — and to route the question to the surveyor. Reading a plat and
opining on where the line is falls outside the licence.

Vertical descriptions matter too. Condominium units are described by reference to a recorded
condominium plat and by elevation, because the unit is a volume of space, not a footprint.

### Hour 09 engagement check

**Question.** A deed's description calls for a course of "S 12° 00′ 00″ W, 320 feet to a large oak
tree at the edge of the branch". A modern survey finds the oak stump 344 feet from the prior point.
Which controls, and why?

**Expected answer.** The **monument** — the oak at the edge of the branch — controls over the
stated distance, under the ordinary rule that monuments prevail over courses and distances. The
distance is treated as an approximation of a boundary the parties fixed by reference to a physical
object. The practical answer is nevertheless that the discrepancy is a title and survey matter, and
the licensee's job is to get it in front of the surveyor and the title examiner, not to resolve it.

## Hour 10 — Deeds and voluntary transfer

## 10.1 What a deed does

A **deed** is the written instrument by which an interest in real property is voluntarily
transferred from a **grantor** to a **grantee**. The deed is the operative act of transfer. It is
not the contract — the contract creates the obligation to convey, and the deed performs it.

A deed transfers title. **Recording** does not transfer title; it gives notice to the world. Title
passes on delivery and acceptance, whether or not the deed is ever recorded. Recording protects
the grantee against subsequent claims, which is why it is done immediately and why a gap of even
hours can matter.

## 10.2 The elements

For a deed to be valid it must have:

**A competent grantor.** Of legal age and legal capacity. A deed from a person adjudicated
incompetent is void; a deed from a person lacking capacity at the moment of signing is voidable.
Entities act through authorised signatories.

**A named grantee, identifiable with certainty.** A deed to a non-existent person or entity fails.

**A statement of consideration.** Something of value, though the deed need only recite it — the
common "for ten dollars and other good and valuable consideration" is sufficient recital, and deeds
of gift recite love and affection.

**Words of conveyance**, called the **granting clause** — "does grant and convey", or "grants,
bargains and sells". These words show present intent to transfer.

**An adequate legal description**, as Hour 9 required.

**The grantor's signature.** The grantee does not sign a deed. Where the property is held by the
entirety or a spouse has an interest, the spouse signs to release it.

**Delivery and acceptance.** The most misunderstood element. A signed deed sitting in a drawer
transfers nothing. Delivery requires the grantor's present intent to pass title; acceptance is
presumed where the conveyance benefits the grantee. This is why a deed handed over at closing with
instructions not to record until funds clear is a matter the settlement agent controls carefully.

**Acknowledgment** — the grantor's declaration before a notary or other authorised officer that the
signature is genuine and voluntary. Acknowledgment is generally not required for validity between
the parties, but it **is required to record**, and an unrecordable deed is a practical disaster.

Two things a deed does not require: the grantee's signature, and, at common law, witnesses in most
circumstances.

## 10.3 Types of deed, ranked by what the grantor promises

**General warranty deed.** The grantor warrants title against defects arising at any time,
including before the grantor owned the property. The traditional covenants are seisin, right to
convey, against encumbrances, quiet enjoyment, further assurance, and warranty forever. This is the
strongest deed and the norm in Virginia residential resale transactions.

**Special warranty deed.** The grantor warrants only against defects arising **during the
grantor's own ownership**. Anything from before is not warranted. Fiduciaries, corporations,
lenders selling foreclosed property, and relocation companies commonly use this form, and it is
usual in new construction from a builder.

**Quitclaim deed.** The grantor conveys whatever interest the grantor has — which may be nothing —
and warrants nothing at all. Quitclaims are curative instruments: clearing a possible interest,
correcting a name, releasing a spouse's interest, resolving a boundary. **A quitclaim is not a
lesser way to buy a house.** A buyer who accepts one is accepting the risk that the grantor owned
nothing.

**Special-purpose deeds** include the deed of trust — Virginia's security instrument, Hour 34 —
executor's, administrator's, trustee's, and commissioner's deeds, each carrying the authority
under which the fiduciary acts, and a deed in lieu of foreclosure.

Virginia deeds normally also carry a **deed of bargain and sale with English covenants of title**,
a formulation with a long local history. What matters for a student is to read what the covenants
say rather than to rely on the deed's label.

## 10.4 Involuntary and testamentary transfer, briefly

Not every transfer is by deed from a willing owner. Title passes by **will** — the testator is the
devisor and the recipient the devisee — or by **intestate succession** under Title 64.2 when there
is no will. It passes by **foreclosure sale**, by **tax sale**, by **eminent domain**, by
**adverse possession**, by **escheat**, and by **partition**. Each is a chapter of its own; the
point here is that the chain of title a searcher builds in Hour 11 will contain instruments that
are not ordinary deeds, and each carries its own questions about authority.

## 10.5 What the licensee does and does not do

**§ 54.1-2101.1** of the Code of Virginia governs the preparation of real estate contracts by
licensees, and §§ 54.1-2131 A 2 b and 54.1-2132 A 3 b describe the licensee's role as *assisting in
the drafting and negotiating of offers and counteroffers, amendments, and addenda to the real
estate contract pursuant to § 54.1-2101.1*. That is the boundary. Assisting with contract forms in
the manner the statute permits is licensed activity. **Drafting or selecting a deed is not.** The
deed is prepared by an attorney, and choosing between a general warranty deed and a special
warranty deed is legal advice.

A licensee who is asked "which deed should we use?" has one correct answer: *that is a question for
your attorney or the settlement agent, and here is how to reach them.*

### Hour 10 engagement check

**Question.** A grantor signs and acknowledges a deed, puts it in a safe deposit box, and tells no
one. The grantor dies two years later and the deed is found. Did title pass? What element decides?

**Expected answer.** No. **Delivery** is missing. A deed takes effect on delivery with the present
intent to pass title, and a signed, acknowledged deed retained by the grantor and never handed over
evidences no such intent. Signature and acknowledgment make the instrument formally complete and
recordable; they do not transfer anything. The property passes instead under the grantor's will or
by intestate succession.

## Hour 11 — Title search, chain of title, and notice

## 11.1 The public records and what they hold

Land records in Virginia are maintained by the **clerk of the circuit court** of each city and
county. What is recorded there includes deeds, deeds of trust and their releases, plats,
easements, restrictive covenants, judgments, liens, wills and probate records, and lis pendens.

Recording is **constructive notice to the world**. That is the entire point of the system: it
allows a stranger to a transaction to discover, by searching a public office, what interests exist
in a parcel, without asking anyone.

## 11.2 The chain of title

A **chain of title** is the succession of recorded owners of a parcel over time, each conveyance
linking to the next. A searcher builds it by working **backward** from the current owner —
finding the deed by which the current owner took title, then the deed by which that grantor took
title, and so on — and then forward again, checking each owner's period for liens, judgments and
other adverse instruments.

Records are indexed by name, in **grantor and grantee indexes**. A search moves between the two:
the grantee index to find how someone acquired, the grantor index to find what they did with it.

Two defects a searcher looks for:

A **gap** is a break in the chain — a conveyance out from someone who never appears as a grantee,
or a period with no recorded transfer where one must have occurred. Gaps are often the result of
an unrecorded deed, a probate that was never recorded, or a name change.

A **cloud** is any recorded instrument or claim that casts doubt on the title without necessarily
defeating it: an unreleased deed of trust from 1988, an old judgment, an ambiguous easement, a
defective acknowledgment, or a deed from one of several heirs.

**Marketable title** is title a reasonable, well-informed purchaser would accept — not perfect
title, but title free from reasonable doubt and from the threat of litigation. A sales contract
ordinarily requires the seller to convey marketable title, and it is the buyer's remedy when a
cloud will not clear.

A **quiet title** action is the judicial proceeding used to resolve a cloud that cannot be cleared
by curative instrument.

## 11.3 The three kinds of notice

This is the most examinable concept in the hour and the most commonly muddled.

**Actual notice** is what a person in fact knows. A buyer told about an easement has actual notice
of it.

**Constructive notice** is what the law charges a person with knowing whether or not they know it,
because it is recorded. Everyone is charged with notice of every properly recorded instrument in the
chain of title. This is why recording protects: a subsequent purchaser cannot claim ignorance.

**Inquiry notice** is what a person would have discovered by making the investigation the visible
facts demanded. Someone else in possession of the property is the classic trigger. A buyer who sees
a tenant living in the house, or a neighbour's driveway crossing the lot, is on notice to ask, and
is charged with what a reasonable inquiry would have revealed — even if nothing is recorded.

The practical lesson for a licensee: **possession is notice.** A property occupied by someone other
than the seller raises a question that must be answered in writing before closing.

## 11.4 Recording acts

States use one of three rules to decide priority between competing claimants. **Race** — first to
record wins. **Notice** — a subsequent purchaser for value without notice wins. **Race-notice** —
a subsequent purchaser for value without notice who records first wins.

Which rule Virginia applies, and its exact statutory formulation, is a question of Title 55.1 that
this course has not read from primary text and therefore does not state. What the student must
carry is the operating principle that follows under any of the three: **record immediately**, and
treat any delay as risk.

## 11.5 The abstract and the opinion

An **abstract of title** is a condensed history of everything recorded affecting the parcel. An
attorney's **title opinion** is a legal opinion on the state of title based on the abstract.
Together they were the traditional method, and title insurance largely supplanted them because an
opinion compensates only if the attorney was negligent, whereas a policy pays on the risk.

### Hour 11 engagement check

**Question.** A buyer walks a property twice and both times sees a family living in the detached
guest cottage. The seller says they are "just friends staying for a while", and no lease is
recorded. What kind of notice does the buyer have, and what must the licensee do?

**Expected answer.** **Inquiry notice.** Visible possession by someone other than the seller
obliges a reasonable purchaser to investigate, and the buyer will be charged with whatever a proper
inquiry would have revealed — including an unrecorded lease or claimed interest. The licensee's
duty is to disclose the observed occupancy as a material fact actually known and to insist the
question be put in writing to the seller and to the settlement agent before closing. The licensee
must not accept or relay the seller's characterisation as though it settled the question.

## Hour 12 — Title defects and title insurance

## 12.1 What title insurance is

Title insurance is an indemnity contract that protects against **loss from defects in title that
existed at the moment the policy was issued** and were not excepted from coverage. It looks
backward. Every other insurance product a buyer holds — hazard, flood, liability — looks forward
at events that may happen. That single difference explains most of what follows.

A **one-time premium** is paid at closing. There are no renewals.

## 12.2 The two policies

An **owner's policy** insures the buyer's interest, in the amount of the purchase price, for as
long as the insured or the insured's heirs hold an interest.

A **lender's policy**, also called a loan policy or mortgagee policy, insures the lender's security
interest in the amount of the loan, and its coverage declines as the loan is paid down. Lenders
require it, and the buyer usually pays for it.

**A lender's policy does not protect the buyer.** This is the single most important consumer point
in the hour, and it is where buyers are most often left exposed. A buyer who pays for the lender's
policy and declines the owner's policy has purchased protection for someone else.

Virginia has a specific consumer protection here: the **Consumer Real Estate Settlement Protection
Act**, Chapter 10 of Title 55.1 (§ 55.1-1000 et seq.), governs settlement agents and is referenced
directly in the licensing statutes — § 54.1-2132 F and 18VAC135-20-155 A both point to it. The
detail belongs to Hour 40; the point here is that Virginia regulates the settlement side of this
transaction expressly.

## 12.3 Reading a commitment

Before the policy comes the **commitment**, sometimes called a binder or a preliminary report. It
is the insurer's statement of the terms on which it will insure, and it has parts a licensee should
be able to separate:

**Schedule A** — the facts: the proposed insured, the amount, the estate being insured, the current
record owner, and the legal description.

**Schedule B-I, Requirements** — what must be **done** before the policy issues. Pay off and
release the existing deed of trust. Obtain a death certificate. Obtain a signature from a spouse.
Record a corrective deed. These are action items with owners and deadlines, and they are the part
of the commitment most likely to derail a closing if nobody reads them early.

**Schedule B-II, Exceptions** — what the policy will **not** cover. Recorded easements, restrictive
covenants, mineral reservations, matters a survey would show, rights of parties in possession,
taxes not yet due. Exceptions are not defects; they are risks the insurer declines to carry, and
the buyer keeps.

**Exclusions** are different from exceptions: exclusions are printed in the policy form and remove
whole categories from coverage — governmental police power and zoning, eminent domain, defects the
insured created or agreed to, and matters first attaching after the policy date.

A **standard** owner's policy carries the general exceptions. **Extended coverage** removes some of
them, typically requiring a survey and an affidavit, and costs more. Recommending that a buyer
*ask the settlement agent about* extended coverage is proper; telling a buyer they do not need it
is not.

## 12.4 What title insurance covers, and what it does not

Covered, subject to the exceptions: forged deeds and signatures, undisclosed heirs, defective
acknowledgment, incapacity of a prior grantor, errors in the public records, improperly indexed
instruments, undisclosed liens, and clerical mistakes in the chain. Coverage includes the insurer's
duty to **defend** — which is frequently worth more than the indemnity.

Not covered: anything listed as an exception; the excluded categories; **and every problem with the
physical property.** A leaking roof, a failed septic system, radon, defective drywall, a boundary
the survey would have shown, and the neighbour's noise are not title defects. Buyers conflate the
two constantly, and a licensee who does not correct the confusion leaves a buyer believing they are
insured against something they are not.

## 12.5 Curative work

Most defects are cleared rather than claimed. A **release** or **certificate of satisfaction**
clears a paid-off deed of trust. A **corrective deed** or **scrivener's affidavit** fixes an error.
A **quitclaim** releases a possible interest. An **affidavit of heirship** or a probate proceeding
establishes succession. A **quiet title** action resolves what nothing else will. Each takes time,
and the deadline the contract sets for title objections is why Schedule B-I must be read the day it
arrives.

### Hour 12 engagement check

**Question.** A commitment lists, under Schedule B-II, "rights of parties in possession" and
"matters that an accurate survey would disclose". After closing the buyer discovers the neighbour's
garage sits three feet inside the boundary. Is the buyer covered? What should have happened?

**Expected answer.** Under a standard policy with those exceptions, **no** — an encroachment is
exactly the kind of matter an accurate survey would disclose, and it was excepted. The buyer should
have obtained a survey during the contract period and, on the strength of it, either resolved the
encroachment, negotiated, or sought extended coverage that removes the survey exception. The
licensee's part is to recognise the exception when the commitment arrives, tell the buyer in
writing what it means in plain terms, and route the buyer to the settlement agent and to counsel —
without opining on coverage, which is the insurer's contract to interpret.
