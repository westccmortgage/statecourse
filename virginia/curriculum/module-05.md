## Learning objectives

By the end of this module the student will be able to:

- Identify the standard components of a residential sales contract and say what risk each
  allocates.
- Distinguish satisfaction, waiver, and expiration of a contingency and build a deadline map from a
  ratification date.
- State what the Virginia Residential Property Disclosure Act actually requires of an owner, and
  correct the common belief that Virginia is a condition-disclosure state.
- Apply the federal lead-based paint disclosure requirement to a fact pattern.
- Distinguish client from customer, and state the two things § 54.1-2136 requires a licensee to say
  before any brokerage relationship begins.

## Hour 17 — Sales contract structure and risk allocation

## 17.1 A contract is a map of who bears which risk

A residential sales contract looks like a form to be filled in. It is better understood as a series
of decisions about **who carries which risk between now and settlement**. Every blank is a risk
allocation. A student who reads the form that way will never wonder why a clause exists.

## 17.2 The components

**Parties.** Full legal names, and the capacity in which each signs. A trust, an estate, or an LLC
signs through someone whose authority must be evidenced. Getting the party wrong is not a
typographical problem; it is a title problem discovered at closing.

**Property.** The legal description, and everything conveyed with it. This is where the fixture work
of Hour 4 lands: items included, items excluded, and personal property conveyed by bill of sale.

**Price and deposit.** The purchase price, and the **earnest money deposit** — its amount, who holds
it, when it is delivered, and what happens to it in each way the contract can end. In Virginia,
deposit money held by a firm is escrow money subject to **18VAC135-20-181**, and the statutory
protections in **§§ 54.1-2108, 54.1-2108.1 and 54.1-2108.2** apply. The deposit is not the seller's
money and it is not the firm's; it is held.

**Financing terms.** Whether the purchase is cash or financed, the loan type and amount, and — the
operative part — the **financing contingency**: the period in which the buyer must obtain a
commitment, and what happens if they do not.

**Settlement and possession.** The settlement date, who conducts settlement, and when possession
transfers. **Settlement and possession are separate**, and any gap between them needs its own
written agreement.

**Title.** The quality of title the seller must convey, the buyer's right to examine and object,
the period for objection, and the seller's right and obligation to cure.

**Inspections and investigations.** Which investigations the buyer may make, in what period, at
whose cost, and what remedies follow an unsatisfactory result.

**Risk of loss.** Who bears a casualty loss between ratification and settlement, and what happens if
the property is materially damaged.

**Default and remedies.** What each party may do if the other fails to perform, and how the deposit
is treated.

**Notices.** How notice is given and when it is effective. This clause decides disputes about
whether a deadline was met.

**Time provisions.** Whether time is of the essence, how days are counted, and whether weekends and
holidays count.

**Contingencies and addenda.** Everything conditional, and every attachment that forms part of the
agreement.

## 17.3 Where the money sits

A licensee should be able to explain the deposit's life cycle without hesitating: received;
delivered to the holder named in the contract within the time the contract and the regulations
require; deposited into a designated escrow account that is **in the firm's licensed name, labelled
escrow at the financial institution, and sufficient at all times to account for all funds held**
(18VAC135-20-181 A 1); disbursed only as the contract and the law permit; and accounted for in a
timely manner under § 54.1-2131 A 5.

The disputed-deposit situation is where licensees create liability. When buyer and seller both
claim the deposit, the firm may not simply pick a side. Virginia legislates this specifically at
**§ 54.1-2108.2**, which addresses protection of escrow funds on termination of a purchase
contract. The professional answer is to follow the contract, the statute, and the firm's written
policy — which § 54.1-2110.1 B 2 a requires the firm to have — and to escalate to the principal
broker immediately.

## 17.4 Issue-spotting without giving legal advice

A licensee reviewing a contract is looking for **incompleteness and inconsistency**, not for legal
meaning. Blanks not filled. Dates that contradict one another — a financing contingency expiring
after settlement, an inspection period ending before the contract was ratified. Parties named
inconsistently between page one and the signature block. Addenda referenced but not attached.
Initials missing where a change was made. A deposit whose delivery deadline has already passed.

Those are within the licensee's competence and are exactly what ordinary care under § 54.1-2131 A 4
requires. **What a clause means, and what a party should do about a breach, are not.**

### Hour 17 engagement check

**Question.** A ratified contract sets settlement for 30 October, a financing contingency expiring
15 November, and an inspection period of "ten days from ratification" where ratification occurred
on 2 October. Identify every problem a licensee should raise, and say what the licensee must not do.

**Expected answer.** The financing contingency expires **after** settlement, which is incoherent —
the buyer's protection would not resolve until after they were obliged to complete. The inspection
period ends 12 October, which is workable but tight and should be confirmed against the contract's
day-counting rule and its notice provision. The licensee should raise both, in writing, to their
client and to the other side immediately, and route them to the parties for correction by written
amendment. The licensee must **not** decide what the conflicting dates legally mean, must not tell
the client which date controls, and must not redraft the clause; that is counsel's work under the
limits of § 54.1-2101.1.

## Hour 18 — Performance, breach, remedies, and contingencies

## 18.1 What a contingency does

A **contingency** makes a party's duty to perform conditional on a stated event. Until the
condition is resolved, the contract exists and binds — but the conditioned duty is not yet absolute.

Three things can happen to a contingency:

**Satisfaction.** The event occurs. The buyer obtains the loan commitment; the inspection produces
an acceptable result. The condition drops away and the duty becomes absolute.

**Waiver.** The party the contingency protects gives it up voluntarily, in writing. A buyer who
waives the financing contingency is now obliged to complete whether or not the loan arrives, and
their deposit is exposed.

**Failure with a stated consequence.** The event does not occur within the period, and the contract
says what follows — usually a right to terminate on written notice within a stated time, with the
deposit returned.

The fourth possibility is the dangerous one: **the period expires and nobody does anything.** Under
many forms a contingency not exercised in time is deemed satisfied or waived. A buyer who lets an
inspection period lapse without giving notice may have lost the protection entirely. That is why
deadline management is a core professional skill and not an administrative chore.

## 18.2 The common contingencies

**Financing.** The buyer must obtain a written commitment for a loan of a stated type and amount
within a stated period. Note what it does not do: a commitment is not funding, and a commitment can
be withdrawn if the buyer's circumstances change. The licensee's instruction to a buyer during the
contract period — do not change jobs, do not open credit accounts, do not move large sums — is not
folklore; it protects the commitment.

**Appraisal.** In practice usually tied to financing, sometimes separate. If the appraised value
comes in below the contract price, the lender lends against the lower figure and the gap must be
closed by the buyer, by the seller, or by termination.

**Inspection.** The buyer investigates the condition. Depending on the form, the buyer may have a
right to request repairs, a right to terminate outright, or both. **A home inspection is performed
by a home inspector**, defined for Virginia purposes in § 54.1-500, and it is not the licensee's
inspection and not the licensee's opinion.

**Title.** The buyer examines and objects; the seller cures or does not.

**Sale of the buyer's current home.** The most fragile contingency, and the one most often paired
with a kick-out clause allowing the seller to continue marketing.

**Association documents.** In a common-interest community, the buyer's right to receive and review
the governing documents and a resale certificate, with a statutory right to cancel within a period
set by Virginia's condominium and property owners' association statutes. Hours 29 and 30 develop
this.

## 18.3 Breach and remedies

A **material breach** is a failure that defeats the purpose of the contract. A trivial failure is
not a breach that permits termination, and calling one a breach is itself a breach.

Remedies, from Hour 15, applied here: **damages**; **liquidated damages**, usually the deposit;
**rescission**; and **specific performance**, which a buyer may seek because land is unique and a
seller may sometimes seek as well. Contracts frequently require **mitigation** — the injured party
must not sit still and let the loss grow.

The single most important sentence in this hour: **whether a party is in default, and what remedy
follows, are legal conclusions.** A licensee who tells a client "they've breached, keep the
deposit" has given legal advice and, if wrong, has caused a loss. Under § 54.1-2131 A 4 the duty is
ordinary care; the ordinary-care answer is *this needs your attorney, today, and here is the
deadline we are working against.*

## 18.4 Building a deadline map

A working method, and one a student should be able to reproduce:

Start from the **ratification date** — the date the last party signed and delivered, as the contract
defines delivery. Read the day-counting rule: are these calendar days or business days, is day zero
the ratification date or the day after, do weekends and holidays extend a deadline that falls on
one. Then list every deadline in date order: deposit delivery, inspection period end, notice
deadline following inspection, association document delivery and cancellation period, loan
application, appraisal, title examination and objection, seller cure period, loan commitment,
walk-through, settlement.

Against each, record **who acts, what document is required, and what happens if the date passes**.
Then work backward and set internal reminders several days ahead of each. A deadline discovered on
the day it falls is a deadline already partly lost.

### Hour 18 engagement check

**Question.** The inspection report identifies a failing HVAC system. The buyer's licensee is asked,
"can we just cancel and get our deposit back?" What does the licensee do, and what does the licensee
not do?

**Expected answer.** The licensee reads the contract's inspection contingency with the buyer and
identifies **what the contingency actually permits** — request repairs, terminate, or both — the
**deadline** for acting, and the **form of notice** the contract requires, and then makes sure the
buyer acts within that period. The licensee does **not** advise whether the buyer is entitled to the
deposit, does not characterise the seller's position as a breach, and does not tell the buyer the
inspection result is or is not serious. Whether termination is available and whether the deposit is
returnable are legal conclusions for counsel; the licensee's job is to protect the deadline and put
the question in front of the right person in time.

## Hour 19 — Electronic transactions and property disclosures

## 19.1 Electronic records and signatures

Electronic records and signatures are valid for real estate transactions under the federal
**Electronic Signatures in Global and National Commerce Act** and under state adoption of the
**Uniform Electronic Transactions Act**, provided the parties have agreed to transact
electronically. A contract is not less enforceable because it was signed on a screen.

The professional issues are practical. **Consent** must be obtained and recorded. **Identity** must
be established by the signing platform. **Integrity** of the record must be preserved, including
the audit trail showing who signed what and when. **Version control** matters more than people
expect: when three counter-signed copies circulate, the question of which document is the contract
becomes real. And **delivery** — the act that usually forms the contract — must be executed the way
the contract defines it, not the way habit suggests.

## 19.2 The Virginia Residential Property Disclosure Act — what it actually says

This is the section of the course most likely to be taught wrongly, because most national material
describes a different model.

In many states the seller completes a **condition disclosure form**: a checklist of the roof, the
plumbing, the electrical system, known defects, and so on. Virginia does not work that way.

**§ 55.1-703** is headed *Required disclosures for buyer to beware; buyer to exercise necessary due
diligence.* The owner of residential real property furnishes the purchaser a **residential property
disclosure statement**, and that statement is **provided by the Real Estate Board on its website**.
What the statement contains is a series of statements that **the owner makes no representations** —
about the condition of the property or its improvements, about covenants and restrictions, about
mineral rights conveyances, about current lot lines or the ability to expand or add structures,
about matters pertaining to adjacent parcels including their zoning and permitted uses, about
whether a historic district ordinance affects the property, and about whether the property contains
resource protection areas under a locality's Chesapeake Bay Preservation Act ordinance — each
coupled with advice that the purchaser **exercise whatever due diligence that purchaser deems
necessary**.

The statute names examples of that diligence: a **home inspection** as defined in § 54.1-500, a
**mold assessment** by a business following EPA guidance, a **residential building energy analysis**
as defined in § 54.1-1144, a **property survey**, and **contacting the locality** about zoning, lot
coverage, height and setback requirements.

So the correct statement of Virginia law is: **the seller's statutory instrument disclaims
representations and directs the buyer to investigate.** A licensee who tells a buyer "the seller's
disclosure will tell you about the roof" has described another state's law.

## 19.3 What the licensee must disclose, which is a different question

The Act governs the **owner**. The licensee's duties are separate and come from Chapter 21.

A licensee engaged by a **seller** must, under **§ 54.1-2131 B**, treat all prospective buyers
honestly, not knowingly give false information, and **disclose all material adverse facts pertaining
to the physical condition of the property that are actually known by the licensee**, in writing.
The statute then draws the boundary of that duty: "physical condition of the property" refers to the
land and the improvements on it and **does not** refer to (i) matters outside the boundaries of the
land or relating to adjacent or nearby properties, (ii) matters relating to governmental land use
regulations, or (iii) matters relating to highways or public streets.

Two consequences. First, this is an **actual knowledge** standard, not a duty to investigate — a
licensee is not required to discover defects. Second, it is narrower than students expect: a known
plan to widen the road in front of the house is a matter relating to highways or public streets and
sits outside the physical-condition duty, though other duties may still bear on it and the honest
professional answer is usually to say what you know and point the buyer to the locality.

**Defective drywall** is named specifically in both § 54.1-2131 B and § 54.1-2132 B: a licensee with
**actual knowledge** of defective drywall in a residential property, as defined in § 36-156.1, must
disclose it — the seller's licensee to the prospective buyer, the buyer's licensee to the buyer.

To the **client**, the duty is broader in subject and identical in standard: under § 54.1-2131 A 6
and § 54.1-2132 A 7 the licensee discloses material facts **related to the property or concerning
the transaction** of which the licensee has actual knowledge.

## 19.4 Federal lead-based paint disclosure

This is federal law and it applies in Virginia regardless of anything in state law.

For **target housing** — most residential housing built **before 1978**, excluding housing for the
elderly or persons with disabilities unless a child under six resides there, and zero-bedroom
dwellings — a seller or landlord must:

- disclose known lead-based paint and lead-based paint hazards, and provide any available records
  and reports;
- give the purchaser or lessee the EPA-approved pamphlet;
- include the prescribed warning statement and acknowledgments in the contract or lease;
- and, **for sales**, give the purchaser a **ten-day** opportunity to conduct a risk assessment or
  inspection, unless the parties agree in writing to a different period.

The licensee's obligation is to ensure compliance and to retain the signed acknowledgment. This is
one of the few disclosure requirements where the licensee is personally exposed, and it is not
waivable by the seller's preference.

### Hour 19 engagement check

**Question.** A buyer asks, "the seller has to tell me about any problems with the house, right?"
Give the accurate Virginia answer in three sentences, and say what you would put in writing.

**Expected answer.** No — Virginia's Residential Property Disclosure Act is a **buyer-beware**
statute: the owner furnishes a Real Estate Board statement whose content is that the owner makes no
representations about the condition of the property, and which advises the purchaser to exercise
whatever due diligence the purchaser deems necessary. Separately, a **licensee** representing the
seller must disclose material adverse facts about the **physical condition** of the property that
the licensee **actually knows**, and any actual knowledge of defective drywall, in writing — but
that is an actual-knowledge duty, not a duty to investigate. In writing, I would confirm that the
buyer should obtain their own inspection, survey and any specialist assessments within the
contingency period, and name the deadline.

## Hour 20 — Brokerage agreements and agency fundamentals

## 20.1 The vocabulary, and why Virginia's differs

Most national material teaches the **common law of agency**: principal, agent, fiduciary duties,
and a body of case law. Virginia has displaced it in this field. **§ 54.1-2144** is titled *Common
law abrogated*, and it provides that the common law of agency relative to brokerage relationships in
real estate transactions is **expressly abrogated to the extent inconsistent with the article**.

The practical instruction to a student is exact: **a Virginia brokerage-relationship question is
answered from §§ 54.1-2130 through 54.1-2146, not from general agency doctrine.** Where a national
text and the Code differ, the Code governs in Virginia.

Virginia's own vocabulary, from § 54.1-2130 and the sections that follow, distinguishes a
**standard agent**, a **limited service agent**, and an **independent contractor** representative,
and speaks of a licensee **engaged by** a client. The word "client" is the person the licensee
represents; a **customer** is a party to the transaction whom the licensee does not represent.

## 20.2 Client and customer

The distinction decides what is owed.

To a **client**, the licensee owes the full set of statutory duties in § 54.1-2131 (seller),
§ 54.1-2132 (buyer), § 54.1-2133 (landlord), § 54.1-2134 (tenant), or § 54.1-2135 (management) —
performance per the agreement, promotion of the client's interests, confidentiality, ordinary care,
timely accounting, disclosure of material facts actually known, and compliance with the article and
fair housing law.

To a **customer**, the licensee owes honesty, the statutory disclosures, and no knowing false
information — and, for a seller's licensee dealing with a prospective buyer, the written disclosure
of material adverse facts about the physical condition actually known.

**Ministerial acts** are the bridge. Under § 54.1-2131 C and § 54.1-2132 C a licensee may provide
assistance to the other side by performing ministerial acts, and doing so does **not** breach the
brokerage agreement and does **not** create a brokerage relationship with that party — provided the
acts are not inconsistent with the licensee's duties and are not prohibited by the agreement.
Opening a door, providing a form, transmitting a document: ministerial. Advising the other side on
price or strategy: not.

## 20.3 What must be said before anything begins

**§ 54.1-2136, Preconditions to brokerage relationship**, is short and absolute. Before entering
into any brokerage relationship, the licensee shall advise the prospective client of:

**(i) the type of brokerage relationship proposed by the broker**, and
**(ii) the broker's compensation and whether the broker will share such salary or compensation with
another broker who may have a brokerage relationship with another party to the transaction.**

Both, before the relationship begins. Not at the listing appointment's end, not at the contract.

## 20.4 The brokerage agreement itself

**§ 54.1-2137** governs. The relationship commences when the client engages the licensee, and
continues until performance is complete or the earlier of an agreed expiration date, mutual
termination, a default, or a termination under § 54.1-2139 G.

**Except as otherwise provided, a licensee shall enter into a brokerage agreement with a prospective
client before providing brokerage services** — § 54.1-2137 B. Preparing property-specific materials
with the intent to obtain a relationship does not require an agreement first.

Brokerage agreements **shall be in writing** and shall:

1. **Have a definite termination date; if none is specified, the agreement terminates 90 days after
   its date.**
2. State the **amount of the brokerage fees and how and when they are to be paid**.
3. State the **services to be rendered**.
4. Include the other terms the client and licensee agreed.
5. Where entered in conjunction with consent to dual representation, carry the § 54.1-2139 A
   disclosures.

And under § 54.1-2110.1 B 6, brokerage agreements must **include the name and contact information
of the supervising broker**.

The buyer side carries an additional rule that surprises licensees from other states. **§ 54.1-2132
A 1: a licensee engaged by a buyer shall enter into a brokerage agreement with the prospective buyer
prior to showing property to such prospective buyer.** In Virginia, the agreement comes before the
showing.

## 20.5 After it ends

**§ 54.1-2137 D:** except as otherwise agreed in writing, after termination, expiration, or
completion the licensee owes **no further duties** except to (i) **account** for all money and
property relating to the relationship and (ii) **keep confidential** the client's personal and
financial information and anything else the client asked be kept confidential — unless the law
provides otherwise or the client consents in writing to release.

Confidentiality does not expire with the listing. That is the duty licensees most often forget, and
it is the one most likely to surface years later.

## 20.6 Compensation is not representation

**§ 54.1-2140:** the payment or promise of payment of compensation to a real estate broker **does
not create a brokerage relationship** between any broker, seller, landlord, buyer or tenant.

A seller paying a cooperating broker's fee does not thereby become that broker's client. Who pays
and who is represented are separate questions, and § 54.1-2136 (ii) requires the compensation
arrangement to be explained up front precisely so that nobody confuses them.

Similarly, **§ 54.1-2141**: a brokerage relationship is not created by using a common source
information company.

### Hour 20 engagement check

**Question.** A licensee meets a prospective buyer at an open house. The buyer asks to see three
other properties that afternoon. List, in order, what the licensee must do before showing any of
them, and cite the source for each step.

**Expected answer.** First, under **§ 54.1-2136**, advise the prospective client of (i) the type of
brokerage relationship proposed and (ii) the broker's compensation and whether it will be shared
with a broker representing another party. Second, under **§ 54.1-2132 A 1**, enter into a
**written brokerage agreement with the buyer before showing property** — Virginia requires the
agreement first. Third, that agreement must satisfy **§ 54.1-2137 C**: in writing, with a definite
termination date (or it ends in 90 days), stating the fees and how and when paid, stating the
services, and — under **§ 54.1-2110.1 B 6** — carrying the supervising broker's name and contact
information. Separately, if in the meantime the licensee has a substantive discussion about a
specific property with someone who is neither their client nor represented, **§ 54.1-2138 A**
requires written disclosure of any brokerage relationship with another party, no later than when
specific real estate assistance is first provided.
