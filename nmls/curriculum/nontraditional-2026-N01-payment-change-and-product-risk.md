## 1. Start with three separate questions

A label such as “alternative loan” gives an originator almost none of the information needed for a competent conversation. Ask three different questions:

1. **How does the obligation work?** Identify the interest-rate structure, required payments, principal deferral, possible balance growth, maturity, and any balloon. An adjustable rate describes how interest changes; it does not say whether the principal decreases.
2. **Which legal rules cover this transaction?** Consumer purpose, collateral, open-end versus closed-end structure, and any actual exemption matter. A consumer-purpose, dwelling-secured Non-QM loan is not excluded from the ability-to-repay rule merely because an investor calls it Non-QM. N02 develops the coverage analysis. [S3: §1026.43(a), comment 43(a)-1]
3. **Which approval or underwriting classification is being asserted?** Qualified Mortgage is a defined legal classification with multiple requirements. A lender's product name, a broker's preference, and NMLS approval of an education category are three different things.

An interest-only loan requires payments covering interest but no scheduled principal for a specified period. If all interest is paid, no fees are capitalized, and no new advances occur, the balance remains unchanged. It does **not** grow simply because principal is deferred. A payment-option ARM may permit a payment below accrued interest; if the unpaid interest is added to principal, the balance grows. A fully amortizing ARM can have changing payments while still paying principal down. A fixed-rate loan can nevertheless have a payment increase when an interest-only period ends. [S2: Appendix; S7: §1026.37(a)(10), comments 37(a)(10)-1 and -2]

Those distinctions prevent two opposite errors: describing every ARM as a negative-amortization product, and describing every fixed-rate product as having an unchanging total payment. Taxes and insurance can change even when scheduled principal and interest do not.

### What the older guidance does—and does not—establish

The 2006 interagency guidance addresses risks in institutions supervised by its issuing banking agencies. Its central examples are interest-only mortgages and payment-option ARMs. Its own footnote excludes fully amortizing residential products, reverse mortgages, and HELOCs except the simultaneous-second-lien discussion. NMLS's education-category list is broader; do not turn a difference in definitions into a contradiction. [S1; S2: introduction, footnote 1]

Supervisory guidance is not itself a statute or legislative regulation. The Federal Reserve expressly distinguishes it from law. The CSBS/AARMR framework is also listed by NMLS as an appropriate educational topic; this lesson does not establish whether or how a particular state adopted a binding requirement. Check the current state authority before asserting a state mandate. [S1; S8]

The older document is useful for understanding payment shock, risk layering, and balanced communication. It is **not** permission to apply its historical stated-income discussion instead of current verification requirements. Nor should its old qualifying-payment illustration replace the current Regulation Z interest-only rule: where §1026.43(c)(5)(ii)(B) applies, use the term **remaining at recast**, not a newly restarted original term. Current law, actual coverage, and the legal obligation control. [S2: Reduced Documentation and footnote 6; S3: §1026.43(c)(3)–(5)]

## 2. Interest-only does not remove the principal bill

### Original case: Lena's five-year interest-only period

Lena considers a $360,000 loan with a 30-year total term, a fixed 6.00% annual note rate, monthly interest calculated as annual rate divided by 12, and interest-only payments for the first 60 months. Beginning with payment 61, principal and interest amortize over the remaining 300 months. There is no negative amortization, balloon, additional advance, capitalized fee, or voluntary principal payment. Ignore taxes, insurance, escrow, and fees in this calculation; they are added separately when evaluating the household obligation.

The interest-only payment is:

`$360,000 × 0.06 ÷ 12 = $1,800.00 monthly interest.`

After 60 timely interest-only payments, the principal is still $360,000. The borrower has paid $108,000 of interest, not $108,000 of principal. The remaining time to repay principal is now 25 years, not 30.

For equal monthly principal-and-interest payments, use:

`Payment = Balance × r ÷ [1 − (1 + r)^(-n)]`

Here `r` is the annual note rate divided by 12, expressed as a decimal, and `n` is the number of remaining monthly payments. This is a simplified monthly-accrual mortgage calculation, not an APR calculation.

`Payment = $360,000 × 0.005 ÷ [1 − (1.005)^(-300)] = $2,319.49.`

The increase is approximately $519.49 a month, or 28.86%, despite **no interest-rate increase**. The loan has not become more expensive because the lender secretly changed the rate. The required payment now retires the unchanged principal within a shorter remaining period. [S3: §1026.43(c)(5)(ii)(B), comment 43(c)(5)(ii)(B)-1; original arithmetic]

### Compare structure, not just the first payment

These are separate hypothetical offers at the same 6.00% rate, solely to isolate structure. They are not market-rate quotes; actual products may have different rates and fees.

| Structure for $360,000 | Initial monthly P&I or interest | Scheduled principal treatment |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| 30-year fixed, amortizing from the beginning | $2,158.38 | Principal begins decreasing with payment 1 |
| 30-year fixed, first five years interest-only | $1,800.00 | No principal reduction for 60 payments; then approximately $2,319.49 for 300 payments |
| 15-year fixed, amortizing from the beginning | $3,037.88 | Higher required payment retires the original principal over 180 payments |

The 15-year option is not automatically the appropriate recommendation: a payment that forces a household to consume essential reserves can create a different risk. The interest-only option is not automatically an improper product: it has a defined principal-deferral structure that must be evaluated lawfully and explained accurately. A professional comparison includes the borrower's objectives, verified repayment resources, payment transitions, total costs, and actual lender criteria—not simply the smallest number in the first-payment column.

### Activity 1: separate structure shock from rate shock

For a **different hypothetical adjustable-rate version** of Lena's loan, assume the rate becomes 8.00% exactly when amortization starts after the first 60 months, remains 8.00% thereafter, and the contract permits that reset. All other balance and term assumptions remain unchanged. Compute the 300-month payment before looking below.

Worked result: $2,778.54 monthly P&I. This is about 54.36% above $1,800. The first scenario isolated principal deferral; the second adds a rate increase. Do not describe 8.00% as a forecast, the legal maximum, or the ATR qualifying rate; it is a deliberately stated sensitivity assumption. The contract and applicable regulation determine those other rates.

Now write two sentences for Lena: one explains why payment rises even at 6.00%; the other says what the 8.00% scenario does and does not predict. A sound answer does not promise that refinancing will prevent either transition.

## 3. A payment option can change the debt, not just the payment

### Original case: a minimum that leaves interest unpaid

This numerical extract illustrates 12 months of a hypothetical payment-option product. Opening balance: $180,000. During this entire illustration, the accrual rate remains 7.20% annually, or 0.60% monthly. The contractual minimum is $800 per month. All minimum payments are timely. Unpaid interest is added to principal monthly; no other fees, advances, or rounding adjustments affect the balance. Assume no balance cap or mandatory recast is reached during these 12 months. The extract is not a complete loan term sheet or a statement of product legality.

For each month:

`Interest = opening balance × 0.006`

`Closing balance = opening balance + interest − payment`

| Month | Opening balance | Accrued interest | Payment | Interest added to principal | Closing balance |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | $180,000.00 | $1,080.00 | $800.00 | $280.00 | $180,280.00 |
| 2 | $180,280.00 | $1,081.68 | $800.00 | $281.68 | $180,561.68 |

After 12 payments, the balance is approximately $183,473.13, using unrounded intermediate values. The consumer paid $9,600 during the year and owes about $3,473.13 more principal than at the beginning. Timely payment and growing principal are entirely compatible when the legal payment schedule permits negative amortization. [S2: Appendix; S3: §1026.43(b)(7); original arithmetic]

Compare three choices for **month 1 only**:

- $800 minimum: the balance increases to $180,280.
- $1,080 interest-only: all current interest is paid, and the balance remains $180,000.
- $1,100 payment, credited to interest and then principal: the balance declines to $179,980.

The third payment reduces principal that month. Without the full contract and amortization period, it should not be labeled a sufficient fully amortizing payment for the whole loan. Also, the second month's interest-only amount changes with the opening balance and accrual rate; “interest-only” is not a permanently fixed dollar amount.

### Activity 2: challenge the apparent saving

A draft explanation says: “Choose $800 instead of $1,080 and save $280 every month.” Identify both defects.

First, the $280 is deferred interest added to debt, not a reduction in the interest charge. Second, the difference is not necessarily $280 every subsequent month: month 2 already has $281.68 of unpaid interest under the specified assumptions. Rewrite the explanation using the actual first-month comparison and the consequences for principal.

### Three different caps

An **interest-rate cap** limits a change in the accrual rate, as specified in the note. A **payment cap** limits certain scheduled payment increases. A **negative-amortization cap** establishes a balance-related trigger or limit under the contract. None should be substituted for another.

Suppose a contract permits a minimum payment to increase by no more than 5% at its next annual minimum-payment adjustment. An $800 minimum would become no more than $840 under that provision. That does not prove the accrual interest will rise only 5%, does not prove $840 covers accrued interest, and does not establish that the cap controls a mandatory recast. Read the provisions governing recast and their interaction with payment limits. Some structures stop permitting the low minimum when a stated time expires or a balance condition is triggered. [S2: Introductory Interest Rates, Appendix; S3: §1026.43(b)(7), (b)(11)]

Do not multiply the initial principal by a stated balance-cap percentage and call that the exact regulatory maximum loan amount without modeling the contractual schedule. The permitted period, interest-rate path, payment adjustments, and cap trigger can produce a different amount. The current official commentary itself distinguishes the calculated maximum balance from a simple headline cap. [S3: comments 43(b)(7)-1 through -3]

The exit risk is not only a larger future payment. A larger debt can reduce equity, and selling involves transaction costs. A refinance requires a willing lender, a qualifying borrower and property, and acceptable terms at that later time. Those are not guaranteed by the current lender or the current appraisal.

## 4. A disclosed payment is not necessarily the underwriting payment

An originator should be able to show three separate outputs in a file:

| Output | What determines it | What it is not |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Contractual payment schedule | Executed legal obligation and actual rate/payment mechanics | Proof of affordability |
| Required disclosure | Applicable disclosure rules, including their stated projection assumptions | A prediction of future rates or automatically the ATR qualifying payment |
| ATR qualifying payment | Current applicable ability-to-repay rule and transaction facts | Necessarily the first advertised minimum or the largest payment disclosed anywhere |

For an ordinary covered transaction evaluated under §1026.43(c), the basic payment rule uses the **greater of the fully indexed rate and the introductory rate**, with substantially equal monthly fully amortizing payments. For interest-only and negative-amortization loans, apply their special rules rather than the general formula with the wrong balance or term. [S3: §1026.43(c)(5)]

The **fully indexed rate** uses the index or formula that will apply after recast, determined at consummation, and the maximum margin that can apply during the loan term. For an original simplified example, an applicable index of 3.25% plus an unchanging 2.50 percentage-point margin gives 5.75%. With an introductory rate of 4.50%, the greater-of-rate test uses 5.75%; if the introductory rate instead is 6.25%, it uses 6.25%. Assume no contractual lookback or lifetime-cap adjustment changes these example inputs. The result is not a prediction of the index several years later. [S3: §1026.43(b)(3); comments 43(b)(3)-1 through -2]

For an actual file, apply the commentary's rules rather than grabbing an arbitrary online index quote. A contractual lookback can affect the eligible index value; a periodic adjustment cap does not reduce the fully indexed rate merely because that rate cannot be reached at the first adjustment. The commentary separately permits taking the lifetime maximum into account. For fixed- or step-rate obligations without an index, it identifies the maximum rate that may apply over the term. These distinctions are why the note and applicable calculation method must travel with a qualifying-payment number. [S3: comments 43(b)(3)-2 through -5]

- **Interest-only:** calculate P&I to retire the loan amount over the term remaining at recast, using the greater of the fully indexed and introductory rates. Lena's fixed 6.00% example therefore uses $360,000, 6.00%, and 300 months—not the $1,800 interest-only payment and not a new 360-month period.
- **Negative amortization:** first determine the maximum loan amount and recast timing under §1026.43(b)(7). Assume minimum payments for as long as permitted and the maximum interest rate reached as early as permitted, including periodic caps. Then calculate substantially equal monthly P&I on that maximum balance over the remaining term using the greater of fully indexed and introductory rates. The rate used to project balance growth and the rate used in the qualifying-payment formula need not be identical. [S3: §1026.43(c)(5)(ii)(C), comments -1 through -3]

These are the §1026.43(c) calculations. Other ATR/QM routes have their own requirements. Do not borrow a convenient piece of the General QM or special-refinancing rules while ignoring their eligibility conditions. N02 addresses why a Non-QM label does not eliminate current verification.

### Legal availability is a separate gate

The arithmetic above does not authorize negative-amortization lending. For example, §1026.32(d)(2) prohibits a high-cost mortgage's regular payment schedule from increasing principal. The counseling requirement described next does not override that prohibition. State restrictions and other applicable federal requirements must also be checked before offering an actual product. [S5]

For covered negative-amortization counseling transactions, §1026.36(k) requires the creditor to receive documentation of qualifying HUD-certified or HUD-approved homeownership counseling before extending credit to a first-time borrower. The section applies to a closed-end, dwelling-secured transaction that may negatively amortize, with its stated reverse-mortgage and timeshare exceptions. “First-time borrower” here means a consumer who has not previously received a dwelling-secured closed-end loan **or open-end plan**—not simply someone who has never bought a home. The creditor may process the application before obtaining the documentation, but may not extend the covered credit without it; the creditor also may not steer the consumer to a particular counselor. A regular product explanation by the originator is not this counseling. [S6: §1026.36(k)(1)–(3), comments 36(k)(1)-1 through -4]

The required counseling must address the risks and consequences of negative amortization. Attendance at a generic homebuyer session without that content is not enough. [S6: comment 36(k)(1)-2]

## 5. Turn the product mechanics into an accurate conversation

For an applicable principal-dwelling variable-rate transaction with a term greater than one year, §1026.19(b) establishes early ARM booklet and program-disclosure requirements. Its timing depends on the application channel and the rule's exceptions. The basic timing is when the application form is provided or before a non-refundable fee, whichever occurs first, with a stated three-business-day mailing/delivery provision for telephone or intermediary/broker applications. Do not replace these requirements with “we will explain everything at closing.” This module is not an ARM notice-calendar exercise; the actual channel and full provision must be checked in the file. [S4: §1026.19(b), (b)(1)–(2)]

Where a Loan Estimate is required, the product description and Projected Payments table must reflect applicable features under §1026.37. An interest-only feature is not hidden by calling the rate fixed; where a payment feature permits negative amortization, the negative-amortization label has priority over interest-only under the prescribed hierarchy. Required disclosure projections follow their own assumptions, including rate caps and the special negative-amortization treatment. Do not populate a form by copying this lesson's one-rate illustration. [S7: §1026.37(a)(10)(ii)–(iv), (c); comments 37(a)(10)-2 and 37(c)(2)(i)-1 through -2]

The 2006 guidance's balanced-communication recommendation remains a useful practical discipline: describe the benefit and the material risk together, early enough to inform choice. It is a supervisory recommendation with its stated scope, not a newly invented universal disclosure form. Current applicable disclosure and consumer-protection law still controls. [S2: Consumer Protection Issues; S8]

### A usable explanation—not a waiver

For Lena's fixed-rate case, a precise explanation is:

“For the first five years your scheduled $1,800 monthly loan payment covers interest only. If you make only those payments, the $360,000 principal will still be due. With the same 6% rate, the following 25-year principal-and-interest payment is about $2,319.49. Taxes and insurance are separate from those figures. Refinancing before that change is not guaranteed.”

For the payment-option extract:

“At this month's balance and rate, $1,080 of interest accrues. Paying the permitted $800 minimum adds the unpaid $280 to your principal. Paying the minimum on time does not stop your debt from increasing. Later payment requirements depend on the contract's rate changes, balance trigger, and remaining repayment term.”

Ask the consumer to explain the consequence in their own words: “If you pay the minimum, what happens to principal?” and “What has to happen before the loan begins paying principal down?” A correct response is evidence of understanding, not a legal release of the creditor's responsibilities. If the explanation reveals confusion, resolve it; do not coach a consumer to repeat “I understand” solely to complete a checklist. [S3: comment 43(c)(1)-1.i; S2: Communications with Consumers]

### Activity 3: make the file handoff useful

Write a short handoff for a loan that combines five-year principal deferral, an adjustable rate, a simultaneous second loan, limited liquid reserves, and variable self-employment cash flow. Separate: (a) confirmed contractual facts; (b) missing verification; (c) payment sensitivity; and (d) the decision requiring an authorized underwriter. Do not conclude that a high credit score cancels the other risks. N02 will show how to assemble the evidence rather than relying on that score or expected appreciation.
