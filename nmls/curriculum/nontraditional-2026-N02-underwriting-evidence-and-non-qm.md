## 1. The first underwriting decision is which rules apply

“Non-QM” is not another way to say “unregulated.” A loan outside a Qualified Mortgage definition can still be a covered transaction subject to the creditor's reasonable, good-faith ability-to-repay determination under §1026.43(c). A lender can offer a lawful non-QM product, but the label does not excuse required consideration or verification. Conversely, the fact that a property contains a house does not by itself make every loan consumer credit. [S2: §1026.43(a), (c)(1), (e); S3: §1026.3(a)]

Before analyzing income, record the borrower, actual purpose, collateral, open- or closed-end structure, transaction type, and the exact coverage determination. “Broker says business purpose” is not a supported determination. Neither is “borrower is self-employed.” Someone who owns a business can obtain a consumer loan to buy their own home; someone employed on salary can borrow for a genuine business acquisition.

| Fictional transaction | Relevant initial scope conclusion | Boundary that remains |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Individual buys a one-unit principal residence with closed-end credit, for personal household use; no applicable exemption | Ordinarily covered by §1026.43; non-QM or bank-statement branding does not remove ATR | Apply the actual route's requirements and other applicable law |
| Individual borrows primarily to expand a business and pledges their residence | A qualifying business-purpose exemption can apply even though a residence secures it | Document actual purpose; this does not establish an exemption from every other federal or state law |
| Individual finances acquisition of a single-family rental and will not occupy it at all | Regulation Z's non-owner-occupied rental acquisition rule deems that credit business purpose | Evidence must support the facts; projected rent and lender eligibility still need assessment |
| Borrower seeks a HELOC | The HELOC is excluded from §1026.43 by (a)(1) | Other HELOC rules remain; a known simultaneous HELOC can still count in the first mortgage's ATR analysis |
| Consumer uses a trust for estate planning to hold their residence | Do not automatically treat the trust as exempt organizational credit | Comment 3(a)-10 treats specified consumer-purpose trusts as credit to a natural person |

The non-owner-occupied rental rule is specific. Under comment 3(a)-4, expected owner occupancy for more than 14 days in the coming year takes the property outside that special non-owner-occupied rule. Different owner-occupied rental rules and the actual primary-purpose analysis then matter; crossing that threshold does not automatically decide every transaction is consumer credit. Organizational credit also has a distinct exemption; genuine corporate borrowers and specified consumer trusts cannot be collapsed into one rule. [S3: comments 3(a)-1, -3 through -5, -9 through -10]

For this lesson's main repayment case, **assume a consumer-purpose, closed-end, dwelling-secured loan covered by §1026.43(c), with no special exemption or alternative QM compliance route**. It is not a reverse mortgage, HELOC, bridge loan, construction-phase loan, timeshare transaction, or special assistance-program loan. That assumption keeps the calculations tied to a defined rule rather than a shifting label.

## 2. Alternative documentation is still documentation

The general ATR framework asks the creditor to consider eight factors. It does not require every borrower to look like a full-time salaried employee, but it does require the creditor to support the information on which it relies. [S2: §1026.43(c)(2)]

| Factor | Evidence question for this module's file |
| --- | --- |
| Current or reasonably expected income or assets, excluding the dwelling securing the loan | What supported resources will actually be available for repayment? |
| Current employment status, if employment income is relied upon | Is the relied-on salaried or self-employment source current? |
| Payment on the covered loan | Was it calculated under the applicable §1026.43(c)(5) rule, not simply copied from the initial minimum? |
| Known or reasonably knowable simultaneous loan payment | Is another dwelling-secured loan financing part of this transaction? |
| Mortgage-related obligations | Have recurring property taxes, required insurance, association charges, and applicable other obligations been considered, whether or not escrowed? |
| Current debt obligations, alimony, and child support | What payment obligations must be included, including relevant obligations absent from the credit report? |
| Monthly DTI or residual income | Are the calculation and threshold supported under the applicable rule and underwriting method? |
| Credit history | Does the record support the assessment, rather than using the score as a substitute for the other factors? |

A complete matrix has an evidence location, an amount or conclusion, a calculation method, and unresolved questions. A box labeled “reviewed” without the actual source and reasoning is not a reproducible analysis.

### What the regulation actually requires

Under §1026.43(c)(3)–(4), information relied upon generally must be verified through reasonably reliable third-party records, with the section's specific exceptions. Financial-institution records can be used; a W-2 is not the only lawful form of evidence. Records can be obtained directly from the consumer if they remain reasonably reliable and specific to that consumer. “Third-party record” refers to the evidence, not necessarily a rule that the creditor must receive every record directly from its issuer. [S2: §1026.43(c)(3)–(4), comments 43(c)(3)-1 through -2 and 43(c)(4)-1 through -3]

A bank statement establishes that a deposit occurred. It does not establish that the deposit is personal income. The official commentary specifically rejects treating an unidentified inflow as income without measures or a basis to confirm it. A borrowing, an account transfer, a refunded deposit, and a customer's payment can all appear as credits to the same account while having different economic meanings. [S2: comment 43(c)(4)-4]

Avoid making the opposite overstatement: current law does not require a separate independent verification for every obligation under every circumstance. A credit report is generally a reasonably reliable record of the obligations it reports; there are rules for inaccurate or disputed information. If the creditor relies on the report and the application states an additional current debt absent from it, §1026.43(c)(3)(iii) does not require independently verifying that stated obligation. The creditor still considers it; absence from the report is not permission to omit it. Employment status can be verified orally if a record is prepared. These narrow provisions are not general permission to accept unverified income. [S2: §1026.43(c)(3)(ii)–(iii), comments 43(c)(3)-3 and -6]

### Joint applicants: income is not the same as obligations and history

When joint applicants have primary liability on the obligation, the creditor need not use income or assets it does not need for a reasonable repayment determination. If one applicant's supported income is sufficient, the other applicant's unused income need not be considered or verified for this purpose. But the creditor must still consider the debt obligations and credit histories of all such joint applicants. Unused income does not turn a joint obligor into an invisible borrower. [S2: comments 43(c)(2)(i)-5, 43(c)(2)(vi)-2, 43(c)(2)(viii)-2 and 43(c)(4)-2]

### Different evidence without discriminatory assumptions

Self-employment, part-time work, seasonal work, retirement benefits, and other documented sources may support repayment. The rule permits consideration of current or reasonably expected income and assets; expected income needs a reasonable, verified basis. A promise of a large future bonus without support is different from a documented expectation tied to reliable records. [S2: comments 43(c)(2)(i)-1 through -4 and 43(c)(2)(ii)-1]

Under Regulation B, a creditor may evaluate the amount and probable continuance of income but may not discount it because of a prohibited basis or merely because it is part-time income, an annuity, pension, or other retirement benefit. ATR does not authorize prohibited inquiries. A document request should address a material underwriting fact, not assumptions about age, family plans, national origin, or a borrower's preferred way of working. [S4: §1002.6(b)(5); S2: comment 43(c)(1)-3]

## 3. Original case: Mira's deposits are not her salary

Mira owns 100% of a small design business and is a joint applicant with salaried co-borrower Owen for a personal principal-residence loan. They are considering the interest-only Non-QM structure described in §4. For this educational exercise, the credit department has obtained reasonably reliable third-party financial records and corroborating business records supporting the following classification. This is a stipulated fact of the fictional exercise—not a claim that StateCourse verified a real business.

| Twelve-month business-account credits | Amount | Evidence-supported treatment |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Total incoming credits | $360,000 | Starting point, not personal income |
| Transfer from Mira's separate savings | $36,000 | Remove from operating revenue; moving existing funds is not new revenue |
| Business equipment-loan proceeds | $24,000 | Remove from revenue; financing inflow, with the associated debt addressed separately |
| Return of a refundable lease deposit | $12,000 | Remove from recurring operating revenue under this exercise's facts |
| Customer operating receipts after those adjustments | $288,000 | Supported business revenue |
| Supported recurring business expenses | $180,000 | Deduct once under the stipulated cash-flow method |
| Supported annual income available to Mira before personal income taxes | $108,000 | $288,000−$180,000 |
| Monthly equivalent used in this exercise | $9,000 | $108,000÷12 |

Assume the expense figure includes all relevant recurring business costs and business debt service for the exercise; there are no additional retained working-capital requirements, ownership distributions to others, duplicated transactions, or unavailable funds in that stipulated $108,000. Those assumptions would require support in a real file. The property-related and household obligations used later are separate, and are not already deducted here.

The point is not that every lender must use exactly 12 months or exactly this expense method. Section 1026.43(c) does not prescribe that universal method. It permits technical underwriting criteria consistent with a reasonable, good-faith determination; a particular product or investor may require a longer history, a different permitted calculation, or additional records. Such program conditions must be stated as program conditions, not as an invented federal formula. [S2: comment 43(c)(2)-1]

### Why plausible shortcuts produce different answers

Dividing all $360,000 of credits by 12 yields $30,000, more than three times the supported $9,000. Removing only transfers is insufficient because it still counts financing and other non-operating inflows, while ignoring expenses. Adding Mira's draws from the business to the $108,000 would also be wrong if the draws distribute the same earnings already counted. A payment from one account to another does not create another dollar of repayment capacity.

A blanket assumed expense factor is not made reliable merely by calling the product “bank statement.” If a lender's permitted method uses an expense factor, identify the actual policy, its conditions, and its reasonable application to the business facts. If the file already establishes materially higher expenses, ignoring that evidence to obtain a qualifying result is not justified by the label. [S2: §1026.43(c)(1), (c)(4); comments 43(c)(1)-1 and 43(c)(4)-4]

### Activity 1: identify what still needs reconciliation

The file receives three new items before the repayment decision:

1. A bank statement includes a $9,000 credit marked only “online transfer.”
2. Mira states that a customer responsible for one-third of annual revenue has ended its contract.
3. A reviewer suggests adding the business's $40,000 cash balance as a freely available personal asset, even though payroll and vendor bills are due from it.

Write the next evidence request for each item without accusing Mira of wrongdoing. The objective is to establish source, reasonable continuity, ownership, and availability. For item 1, obtain a basis to classify the credit; for item 2, assess current/reasonably expected income with corroborating evidence instead of relying mechanically on the old average; for item 3, establish whether funds can be withdrawn without undermining the business cash flow already supporting the loan. An unsupported answer remains unresolved; it is not converted to verified income by a checkbox.

The commentary distinguishes an unforeseeable post-consummation job loss from an income change evident before consummation. The creditor does not insure against every future event, but cannot disregard known evidence of a material repayment change in its determination. Do not manufacture a rule requiring prohibited personal inquiries in the name of predicting every change. [S2: comment 43(c)(1)-2 through -3]

## 4. Original case continued: use the payment the rule requires

For this step, return to the original supported $9,000 monthly Mira income; the three new uncertainties in Activity 1 are separate hypotheticals, not silently resolved facts. Owen has $3,200 of supported monthly gross salary. Assume both sources satisfy the fictional lender's documentation and continuity standards. Total monthly income used: **$12,200**.

The proposed covered loan has:

- $480,000 principal; 30-year total term.
- Fixed 6.50% annual rate; 60 interest-only monthly payments.
- No negative amortization, additional advance, capitalized fee, balloon, or extra principal payment.
- 300 monthly amortizing payments after the interest-only period.
- $950 of supported recurring monthly property taxes, insurance, and association charges in total; assume no other recurring mortgage-related obligations for this exercise.
- A simultaneous HELOC, secured by the same dwelling and known to the first creditor, with a required payment of $400 on the amount to be drawn at or before first-mortgage consummation. This figure has been calculated correctly from the plan's terms for the exercise.
- $700 in other current monthly debt obligations; no additional alimony or child-support obligation under the fictional facts.

Under §1026.43(c)(5)(ii)(B), the payment calculation uses $480,000 at 6.50% over the **remaining 300 months**, producing approximately **$3,240.99** of qualifying monthly P&I. The initial interest-only payment is $2,600, but that is not the qualifying P&I in this calculation. [S2: §1026.43(c)(5)(ii)(B), comment -1]

| Included monthly obligation | Amount |
| --- | ---: |
| Covered loan's qualifying P&I | $3,240.99 |
| Recurring mortgage-related obligations | $950.00 |
| Known simultaneous HELOC payment | $400.00 |
| Other current monthly debts | $700.00 |
| Total monthly debt used | $5,290.99 |

`DTI = $5,290.994374... ÷ $12,200 × 100 = 43.37% approximately.`

`Monthly residual income under §1026.43(c)(7) = $12,200 − $5,290.994374... = $6,909.01 approximately.`

That regulatory residual-income calculation is **not** a statement that $6,909 is free discretionary cash after income taxes and all living expenses. The defined subtraction uses the specified income and debt inputs. A reasonable assessment may require attention to known recurring non-debt living expenses; the figure should not be misrepresented to the consumer as disposable income. [S2: §1026.43(c)(7); comment 43(c)(1)-1.ii.B.5]

### The mistaken worksheet

An originator uses $2,600 interest-only, adds $950 and $700, and omits the HELOC because it is absent from the credit report. The worksheet yields:

`($2,600+$950+$700) ÷ $12,200 = 34.84% approximately.`

That result understates the calculation by about 8.53 percentage points. Its two defects are independent: the wrong first-loan payment and the missing known simultaneous debt. Fixing only one leaves the other wrong. The first creditor's obligation to consider the known HELOC does not disappear because the HELOC itself is outside §1026.43. [S2: §1026.43(c)(2)(iv), (c)(5)(ii)(B), (c)(6)(ii); comments 43(c)(2)(iv)-1 through -2 and 43(c)(3)-4]

For a simultaneous HELOC, the specific §1026.43(c)(6)(ii) calculation uses the plan's required periodic payment and the amount to be drawn at or before consummation. Do not say that this provision always requires the entire undrawn line limit, and do not assume zero when the transaction relies on a draw. A separate investor may impose a stricter stress test, but identify that as an additional program rule.

### Activity 2: can this loan be approved now?

Assume a fictional lender's written policy permits up to 45% DTI on this product, subject to all its other conditions and applicable law. At 43.37%, the example passes that **one policy screen**. It is not automatically approved. The authorized underwriter still needs the full current evidence, required considerations, documented exception decisions if any, and other applicable product/legal checks.

Now test a second version: Owen's supported qualifying salary is $2,400, not $3,200. Total income becomes $11,400. With unchanged obligations, DTI becomes approximately **46.41%**, and defined monthly residual becomes approximately **$6,109.01**. This version fails the fictional 45% policy screen absent a valid permitted policy exception. Federal law is not waived by a policy exception, and an originator should not edit income or omit obligations to get the ratio below 45%.

## 5. The obsolete 43% shortcut and the non-QM distinction

There is no universal maximum DTI in the general §1026.43(c) rule; the creditor determines an appropriate threshold in making its reasonable, good-faith decision. Nor should a 2026 course teach that the current General QM definition always caps DTI at 43%. The current official commentary explains that the General QM amendments removed that requirement and replaced it with pricing thresholds, among other revisions. The current General QM rule still requires consideration of income/assets, debts, and DTI or residual income, and verification under the specified provisions. [S2: comment 43(c)(7)-1; §1026.43(e)(2)(v)–(vi), comment 43(e)(2)-1]

General QM is not simply “DTI low enough” or “APR low enough.” Its requirements also concern regular payments and prohibited features, term, points and fees, qualifying-payment methodology, consideration and verification, and the applicable pricing test. Annual dollar thresholds and the relevant transaction characteristics must be checked in the operative rule. Special government, small-creditor, balloon, and other QM routes are distinct; this lesson does not qualify a file for them by implication. [S2: §1026.43(e)(2)–(7), (f)]

The main case's interest-only feature defers principal, so it does not satisfy the **General QM** payment-feature requirement in §1026.43(e)(2)(i). That is a feature-based conclusion, not a consequence of its DTI being 43.37%. A fully amortizing ARM is not disqualified from General QM solely because its rate adjusts; it still must meet the full applicable criteria. Non-QM is not a synonym for an illegal loan, bad credit, or no documentation. [S2: §1026.43(e)(2)(i), comment 43(e)(2)(i)-1 through -2]

Neither passing ATR nor qualifying as a QM is a universal statement that a product is the cheapest, the best personal choice, or free of every legal issue. Regulation Z's ATR standard should not be relabeled a universal “suitability” or fiduciary test with invented requirements. Applicable anti-steering, fair-lending, consumer-protection, state broker duties, and investor rules remain separate questions. A sound comparison explains options and costs without making a legal claim broader than the actual analysis supports.

## 6. Alternative repayment case: assets and access

Rafael has documented financial assets but limited current employment income. It is not inherently improper to evaluate supported assets other than the collateral dwelling. Section 1026.43(c)(2)(i) permits such assets; employment status needs verification under (c)(2)(ii) only if employment income is relied upon. The creditor still must make the full applicable repayment determination. [S2: comments 43(c)(2)(i)-1 and 43(c)(2)(ii)-1]

For this isolated worksheet, assume a **fictional educational policy** converts verified available financial assets into a monthly equivalent over 240 months. It excludes amounts earmarked for closing, pledged assets unavailable for withdrawal, and a separate protected reserve. This is not a federal asset-depletion formula or an actual StateCourse policy.

| Asset adjustment | Amount |
| --- | ---: |
| Verified account holdings before adjustments | $300,000 |
| Funds committed to closing | −$40,000 |
| Separate pledged portion unavailable for withdrawal | −$50,000 |
| Separate reserve excluded by this fictional policy | −$30,000 |
| Remaining amount permitted in this worksheet | $180,000 |
| Monthly equivalent under the stipulated 240-month method | $750 |

Assume the three deductions concern distinct portions and no further tax, withdrawal, valuation, or access adjustment is needed for this exercise. In an actual file, those issues require analysis. Dividing the entire $300,000 by 60 to claim $5,000 a month both ignores restrictions and invents a different depletion period. It is not an equivalent calculation.

A consumer's $700,000 collateral dwelling is different from the $180,000 available financial assets. The dwelling value cannot replace the income/assets considered under §1026.43(c)(2)(i). An appraisal may establish collateral value for a lender's separate credit decision, but it does not turn unavailable equity into monthly repayment resources under that factor. Do not count the same fund as both an asset being consumed and a perpetual income stream without a supported method addressing that interaction. [S2: §1026.43(c)(2)(i), (c)(4); comments 43(c)(2)(i)-1 and 43(c)(7)-3]

## 7. Residential investment comparison: DSCR is not a universal escape hatch

For a distinct case, Serena seeks credit solely to acquire a single-family rental property she will not occupy at all. Assume the actual purpose and use are supported and the special non-owner-occupied rental acquisition rule applies. Regulation Z treats that credit as business purpose. A property-cash-flow calculation can be relevant to this residential-investment product; it is not evidence that every loan called “DSCR” receives this exemption. [S3: comment 3(a)-4; S1]

This exercise defines a fictional lender's debt-service coverage ratio as annual net operating income divided by annual debt service. No federal rule cited here requires this exact formula, vacancy allowance, threshold, or approval result.

| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Scheduled annual rent | $36,000 | $33,000 |
| Vacancy/collection allowance | −$3,000 | −$6,000 |
| Operating expenses, excluding the debt service below | −$10,000 | −$11,000 |

If the fictional base-case policy screen is 1.10, the base case passes that screen. The stress case shows a $4,000 annual cash-flow shortfall before any other uncovered costs. Neither result alone authorizes the loan. The reviewer evaluates reliable lease/rent evidence, realistic operating costs, reserves, collateral, product changes, and the actual lender criteria. Vacancy and variable debt-service risk are specifically discussed in the older supervisory guidance for non-owner-occupied nontraditional loans; that discussion is an institution-focused risk framework, not a nationwide law mandating this worksheet. [S5: Non-Owner-Occupied Investor Loans]

If Serena instead intends to use the loan for her personal principal residence, writing “DSCR” on the form does not let the lender replace a covered consumer ATR analysis with property rent assumptions. Escalate the changed facts and determine coverage correctly. Do not advise borrowers to misstate occupancy or purpose to fit a product.

## 8. Write the decision another reviewer can reproduce

A defensible underwriting handoff is more than “strong borrower; compensating factors.” It tells the next person what the decision depends on. Use this original template as a practice activity, not an assertion that any regulator mandates this exact form:

1. **Scope:** actual purpose, collateral, borrower/structure, and the provision supporting the coverage conclusion.
2. **Product:** note terms, principal behavior, recast, and the applicable qualifying-payment calculation.
3. **Resources:** each relied-on income/asset amount, source, adjustments, ownership/availability, and unresolved continuity concerns.
4. **Obligations:** covered and simultaneous payments, recurring mortgage-related charges, other debts, and calculation method.
5. **Decision:** DTI/residual and relevant policy test; separately state any other required considerations and unresolved legal/product review.
6. **Exception, if requested:** exact policy clause, evidence-based reason, permitted approving role, and outcome. An exception can change a policy outcome only where authorized; it cannot waive a regulation.
7. **Consumer communication:** explain material payment and resource assumptions without making an approval, rate, refinance, or suitability guarantee unsupported by the file.

For Mira and Owen, an acceptable draft conclusion is limited: “The stipulated income and corrected obligations produce 43.37% DTI under §1026.43(c)'s payment method, below this fictional lender's 45% screen. This does not establish final approval; the authorized decision depends on the full verified record and all other conditions.” That is stronger professional work than calling a knowingly incomplete file “approved” and expecting later personnel to fix it.

The 2006 supervisory guidance's reduced-documentation language must not be used to justify unverified relied-on income for a covered 2026 consumer loan. Guidance on risk layering and institutional controls is useful; the current legal verification standard is separate and controlling where applicable. [S5: Reduced Documentation and Controls; S2: §1026.43(c)(3)–(4)]
