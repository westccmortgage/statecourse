## Purpose and learning outcomes

An experienced originator may know that an appraisal fee is usually subject to zero tolerance and still approve an improper increase. The mistake often occurs one level deeper: the fee worksheet calls the provider “third party” without checking affiliation, the system uses the latest LE for every comparison, or an employee treats a closing-cost increase as its own changed circumstance.

This lesson teaches a file-based decision process. For a transaction covered by the TRID LE/CD provisions, ask:

1. Which rule applies to this charge, based on the actual service and relationships?
2. What is the legally usable comparison amount?
3. Did a permitted event actually change this charge?
4. Was the relevant revised disclosure provided on time and in the correct form?
5. What must be corrected if the consumer is charged too much?

By the end, the learner should be able to classify a charge from evidence; calculate an aggregate tolerance using the correct services and unrounded amounts; distinguish a current estimate from a valid revised baseline; document a permitted revision; apply LE/CD timing rules without mixing their business-day definitions; and identify a consumer refund/disclosure correction that requires escalation.

Scope: ordinary closed-end consumer mortgage transactions subject to §1026.19(e) and (f). Do not extend this treatment to HELOCs, reverse mortgages, or exempt transactions without a separate applicability analysis. Each case assumes a validly established consummation date; do not substitute funding or recording for consummation.

The [NMLS 2026 required-topic list](https://mortgage.nationwidelicensingsystem.org/knowledge/Products/nmls/pubs/nmlsEdFuncSpec/education/funcSpec/maps/topics/approvedCourseTopics/nmlsFS_approvedCETopics2026.html) identifies LE tolerance, zero-tolerance cost increases, and revised-disclosure reasons/timing as required subjects. The operative rules and official interpretations below control the legal explanations.

## 1. Begin with the charge, not the column label

The general good-faith rule compares the consumer's actual charge with the originally disclosed estimate. Exceptions permit particular variations or a properly supported revised estimate. A tolerance is not a markup allowance, and a fee does not become lawful merely because a worksheet turns green. [§1026.19(e)(3)(i)–(iv)](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/19/#e-3).

### A practical classification map

| Rule | Typical scope, subject to the stated conditions | Evidence needed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| General/zero-tolerance rule | Creditor or mortgage-broker fees; affiliate fees; required third-party services the consumer cannot shop for; transfer taxes | Recipient/retention of the fee, affiliation, actual shopping permission, service identity |
| Ten-percent aggregate | Recording fees and qualifying unaffiliated required services for which the consumer could shop, including selection from the supplied list or no consumer selection | Valid shopping opportunity, written list, selected provider, affiliation, performed services |
| Permitted variations | Specified prepaid/escrow/insurance/tax charges and qualifying consumer-selected services outside the list or not required by the creditor | Best information reasonably available when estimated, bona fide charge, applicable selection and list facts |

Sources: [§1026.19(e)(3)(i), comments -1 through -4](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/19/#Interp-19-e-3-i); [§1026.19(e)(3)(ii), comments -1 through -6](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/19/#Interp-19-e-3-ii); [§1026.19(e)(3)(iii) and comments -1 through -4](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/19/#Interp-19-e-3-iii).

“Typical” matters. Paragraph (e)(3)(iii) expressly applies to its listed charges even when paid to the creditor or its affiliate, provided the charges are bona fide. Do not replace the actual rule with “anything involving an affiliate is always zero tolerance.” Conversely, a required settlement service does not escape the general rule just because an affiliate was one of several suggested providers.

“Paid to” also concerns who retains the fee, not simply whose account temporarily receives the money. A creditor collecting a county recording fee and passing it to the county does not turn that fee into creditor compensation. Investigate the service and ultimate retention, including reimbursement arrangements, before assigning the category. [Comment 19(e)(3)(i)-3](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/19/#Interp-19-e-3-i).

### Shopping is a real choice

The written provider list is not a device for requiring the consumer to choose only from the lender's favorites. A creditor may impose reasonable qualifications, such as appropriate licensing, but requiring a selection exclusively from its list is not permission to shop under this rule.

Where shopping is permitted, the separate list must identify at least one available provider for each required shoppable service and state that the consumer may select a different provider. A business that no longer serves the property's area is not an available provider simply because its name remains in a template. [§1026.19(e)(1)(vi)(A)–(C), comments 19(e)(1)(vi)-1 through -5](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/19/#e-1-vi).

Consider three versions of the same required title service:

- The consumer can shop and selects an unaffiliated provider from the properly supplied list: ordinarily the ten-percent aggregate test applies.
- The consumer can shop and selects an unaffiliated provider outside that list: the permitted-variation rule may apply, but the initial estimate must still be reasonable on the information available.
- The consumer actually can shop, but the creditor fails to give the required list: the creditor has a list-compliance problem. Under the commentary, the unaffiliated service is evaluated under the ten-percent rule, not automatically under permitted variations. If the service provider is the creditor or its affiliate, the general rule applies.

This last situation is a useful precision test. “Missing list means zero tolerance in every case” is not the current commentary's rule. Neither is “the customer chose it, so there is no tolerance issue.” [Comments 19(e)(3)(ii)-3 and -6; 19(e)(3)(iii)-2](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/19/#Interp-19-e-3-iii).

### A permitted variation is not permission to lowball

Linden Mortgage estimates prepaid interest using a closing date it knows is unrealistic, solely to make the initial cash-to-close estimate smaller. An employee says prepaid interest has no percentage cap, so any number is acceptable.

That is incorrect. The estimate must use the best information reasonably available and be made through good-faith diligence. The same discipline applies to the specified property-insurance, escrow, property-tax, and other paragraph (iii) charges. A reasonable estimate that later changes is different from an unreasonably low estimate on facts already known. [Comments 19(e)(3)(iii)-1 through -4](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/19/#Interp-19-e-3-iii).

## 2. Ten percent applies to the qualifying aggregate

Do not calculate a refund simply because one qualifying fee rose by 11%, 20%, or 50%. Add the applicable estimated charges, add the corresponding actual charges, and compare the totals. Use unrounded amounts in the actual compliance calculation even when the forms display rounded dollars. [Comments 19(e)(3)(ii)-2 and 19(e)(3)(i)-7](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/19/#Interp-19-e-3-ii).

For these examples, all displayed dollar figures are exact whole-dollar amounts. The baseline is valid, no permissible revision has occurred, all listed services are performed, and each service is correctly classified.

### Worked example A — a twenty-percent increase without an aggregate excess

| Qualifying charge | Valid estimate | Actual consumer charge |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Unaffiliated settlement service, provider selected from list | $600 | $720 |
| Unaffiliated lender's title service, provider selected from list | $400 | $380 |
| Recording fees | $100 | $100 |
| **Aggregate** | **$1,100** | **$1,200** |

The settlement charge rose by $120, or 20%. But the permitted aggregate maximum is $1,100 × 1.10 = $1,210. The actual $1,200 is below that maximum, so these charges do not produce a ten-percent aggregate excess. They still must satisfy other applicable rules, including the actual-charge rule.

The $20 decrease in one qualifying charge can offset an increase in another within this aggregate. Do not import that offset method into the separate zero-tolerance charges.

### Worked example B — a service never performed cannot pad the baseline

Change the facts: the original LE also listed a $150 pest inspection in this bucket, but no pest inspection was obtained. The actual settlement fee is now $760; the other actual charges remain $380 and $100.

The printed original subtotal might suggest $1,250. That is not the proper comparison subtotal for these facts. Remove the $150 estimate for the service not performed:

- Applicable baseline: $1,250 − $150 = $1,100.
- Permitted maximum: $1,100 × 1.10 = $1,210.
- Actual applicable charges: $760 + $380 + $100 = $1,240.
- Aggregate excess: $1,240 − $1,210 = $30.

Keeping the unused inspection in the estimate would create an artificial cushion and conceal the excess. This adjustment is specifically addressed in [comment 19(e)(3)(ii)-5](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/19/#Interp-19-e-3-ii).

The reverse mistake is treating an omitted individual qualifying charge as automatically subject to its own zero-dollar ceiling. The aggregate commentary accounts for an omitted individual charge when it genuinely belongs in this bucket. That does not excuse inaccurate disclosures generally or change a fee's correct category. [Comment 19(e)(3)(ii)-2.ii](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/19/#Interp-19-e-3-ii).

### Activity A — challenge the spreadsheet

A reviewer sees a $30 aggregate excess in example B. The processor proposes to:

1. Leave the unused pest-inspection estimate in the baseline.
2. Move $30 of the actual settlement charge into prepaid interest.
3. Preserve the true categories and amounts and escalate the excess for correction.

## 3. Zero tolerance is not “net all the closing costs”

Suppose the following required charges fall under the general rule and no valid revised baseline or other exception applies:

| Charge | Valid estimate | Actual consumer charge | Increase requiring attention |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Nonshoppable appraisal | $650 | $725 | $75 |
| Creditor processing charge | $800 | $780 | $0 |
| Transfer tax | $1,050 | $1,050 | $0 |

The appraisal has a $75 excess. The processing-charge reduction does not shrink that appraisal excess to $55. Compare each general-rule charge with its own valid estimate. The regulation's correction example adds the individual excesses; it does not pool all zero-tolerance costs into another aggregate. [§1026.19(e)(3)(i); comment 19(f)(2)(v)-1](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/19/#f-2-v).

The borrower paying the bill before closing does not make a nonshoppable appraisal immune from the LE good-faith analysis. The fee's timing of payment and its classification are different questions.

### Worked example C — the invoice is new, but the information was already available

Linden's current vendor schedule showed the $725 appraisal price before the initial LE. An employee accidentally used a superseded $650 schedule. The later invoice confirms $725, with no new service, property fact, consumer request, eligibility event, or other permitted revision reason.

Receiving the invoice is not a cure for the original estimating error. These facts do not establish a paragraph (e)(3)(iv)(A) changed circumstance. Calling the new invoice “new information” without considering what was already available would erase the good-faith requirement. The firm must deal with the $75 excess rather than reset the baseline merely by issuing another LE.

A genuine change can exist even when the appraisal is zero tolerance. But the file must show what changed, the information reasonably relied upon before the original estimate, and how the change actually affected this fee. [§1026.19(e)(3)(iv)(A), comments -1.i and -2; comment 19(e)(3)(iv)-5](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/19/#e-3-iv-A).

### Worked example D — a valid revision changes one charge, not the whole file

Before the original LE, the consumer's information reasonably indicated an ordinary one-unit property. The creditor used the then-applicable $650 appraisal estimate. Later verified information establishes a materially different property configuration that requires a more complex appraisal. The provider documents a $275 incremental cost caused by that fact. Assume due diligence, legal applicability, and all timing requirements are satisfied.

The revised appraisal baseline can be $925. The file contains:

- The original property description and basis for the $650 estimate.
- The later verified fact and the date sufficient information arrived.
- The provider's $275 explanation tied to the new complexity.
- The permitted reason for revision.
- The revised disclosure and evidence of timely provision/receipt.

On the same revised LE, a processing fee rises from $800 to $875 because the firm wants to recover unrelated overhead. No separate permissible reason supports that change. Updating the displayed processing amount does not make $875 its valid tolerance baseline. The relevant baseline remains $800.

Under [comments 19(e)(3)(iv)-2, -3, and -5](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/19/#Interp-19-e-3-iv), a revision can support only the increase actually attributable to its permitted reason. Updated disclosures still need current information, but an informational update is not a blanket baseline reset.

### Lender credits can create an increase too

Linden originally discloses a $900 lender credit. With no valid basis for revision, it provides only $750 at closing. The $150 reduction is an increased charge to the consumer for the good-faith analysis. A reviewer who checks only positive fee lines misses it.

Total lender credits include both general and specific credits as described in the commentary. Do not assume that shifting a credit from one display line to another changes the real amount provided, or that a lower fee automatically authorizes reducing a promised credit. [Comments 19(e)(3)(i)-5 and -6](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/19/#Interp-19-e-3-i).

## 4. Separate the displayed estimate from the comparison baseline

A file may need current information on a revised disclosure without qualifying to use every updated number in the good-faith calculation. Maintain a distinct audit trail for:

- The original estimate.
- Each current disclosed amount.
- Each legally supported baseline adjustment.
- The reason, affected amount, and timing supporting that adjustment.

This is a recommended control design, not a regulator-mandated database schema.

### Permitted revision paths

| Provision | What must be established |
| --- | --- |
| §1026.19(e)(3)(iv)(A) | A qualifying changed circumstance affects settlement charges; for the aggregate bucket, the applicable increase exceeds 10% |
| (B) | A qualifying change in creditworthiness or collateral value makes the consumer ineligible for the previously estimated charge or terms, and the resulting service-cost increase exceeds the applicable tolerance; see comment 19(e)(3)(iv)(B)-1 |
| (C) | The consumer requests a revision to terms or settlement that causes a charge to increase |
| (D) | Rate-dependent charges change because the rate was not locked when the original LE was provided |
| (E) | Intent to proceed occurs after the applicable estimate-expiration period: more than ten business days or a longer period the creditor established |
| (F) | The specified new-construction/delayed-settlement conditions and required original disclosure statement are satisfied |

Source: [§1026.19(e)(3)(iv)(A)–(F) and their interpretations](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/19/#e-3-iv). This table is not permission to use a generic “changed circumstance” code for all six provisions.

For path (A), qualifying changes include specified extraordinary/unexpected events, relied-upon consumer/transaction information that becomes inaccurate or changes, and qualifying newly discovered consumer/transaction information. Preserve evidence of what was reasonably available before the initial disclosure. Sending an LE before collecting one of TRID's six items does not let the creditor later treat receipt of that missing item as a qualifying changed circumstance. [Comment 19(e)(3)(iv)(A)-3](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/19/#Interp-19-e-3-iv-A).

For path (D), a subsequent rate lock ordinarily requires a revised LE within three business days with the rate-dependent terms. Once the CD has been provided, a corrected CD is required if the rate lock makes that CD inaccurate; the lock alone does not require correcting an unchanged, accurate CD. Unrelated charges do not become rate-dependent because they appear on the same document. [§1026.19(e)(3)(iv)(D), comments -1 and -2](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/19/#e-3-iv-D).

For path (E), do not restart an already timely intent to proceed merely because processing lasts longer than ten business days. The rule concerns when the consumer indicates intent relative to the applicable expiration period. A longer period communicated by the creditor matters. [Comments 19(e)(3)(iv)(E)-1 and -2](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/19/#Interp-19-e-3-iv-E).

### Worked example E — a small changed circumstance does not reset the bucket

The original aggregate is $2,000. A documented qualifying settlement-cost change increases one charge by $120, with no other change. That is a 6% aggregate increase, below the $200 allowance.

The creditor can give current information, but that particular path (A) event does not authorize changing the aggregate baseline from $2,000 to $2,120 for the good-faith test. The comparison remains anchored to $2,000. Otherwise, each small update could manufacture a new ten-percent cushion. [Comment 19(e)(3)(iv)(A)-1.ii](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/19/#Interp-19-e-3-iv-A).

Now use a separate file with an original $2,000 aggregate. A documented qualifying change adds $80. Later another documented qualifying change adds $150. Together they increase the applicable estimated aggregate by $230, or 11.5%.

When sufficient information establishes the second change and the aggregate threshold is crossed, the revision clock starts. The first smaller documented change is not ignored merely because it occurred earlier. A timely permitted revision can reflect the supported cumulative increase. Do not reset the baseline for unrelated increases, and do not wait for the eventual closing statement before starting the clock. [Comment 19(e)(4)(i)-1.ii](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/19/#Interp-19-e-4-i).

### Activity B — an examiner asks for the reason

An employee's sole explanation is “LE version 3 was sent.” Write the five missing facts that would establish whether the $275 appraisal increase in example D can be used in the tolerance comparison. A document number establishes sequence; it does not establish a permitted cause.

## 5. Two disclosure forms, several clocks

For a revision used in the good-faith calculation, §1026.19(e)(4)(i) requires the applicable revised LE or CD reflecting the estimate within three business days after receiving information sufficient to establish the revision reason, subject to paragraph (ii). This is not automatically the day a committee approves the change or the day the LOS operator updates the fee.

A credible uncertainty can matter: the commentary describes a reasoned doubt about an appraisal followed by corroboration. But the creditor must preserve the basis for that doubt. Routine internal delay is not equivalent to lacking sufficient information. [§1026.19(e)(4)(i), comment -1](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/19/#e-4-i).

### Keep the clocks separate

| Clock | Business-day basis and key limitation |
| --- | --- |
| Three days to provide a supported revised disclosure under (e)(4)(i) | General creditor-business-functions definition |
| Receipt of revised LE at least four business days before consummation | All calendar days except Sundays and specified federal legal public holidays |
| Deemed receipt of a non-in-person revised LE | Three business days using that more precise definition; earlier actual receipt requires appropriate evidence |
| Initial CD receipt at least three business days before consummation | More precise definition, not merely days the lending office opens |
| Corrected CD | Apply (f)(2): some changes need a new three-business-day waiting period; others do not |

Sources: [§1026.2(a)(6), including comments -1 and -2](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/2/#a-6); [§1026.19(e)(4)(i)–(ii)](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/19/#e-4); [§1026.19(f)(1)(ii)–(iii) and (f)(2)](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/19/#f-1-ii).

Electronic delivery is not an automatic same-day receipt assumption. Apply the applicable electronic-disclosure requirements and retain valid receipt evidence; otherwise use the rule's deemed-receipt treatment. A portal “sent” event is not necessarily a consumer “received” event.

### Worked example F — provision, receipt, and consummation

Assume the creditor operates substantially all business functions Monday–Friday. There are no federal legal public holidays in the period.

- Monday, September 14, 2026: sufficient information establishes a permitted revision.
- Thursday, September 17: the creditor mails a compliant revised LE, before providing any CD.
- Friday, September 25: consummation is scheduled.

The provision deadline is Thursday, September 17: Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday are the three general business days. For deemed receipt, count Friday the 18th, Saturday the 19th, and Monday the 21st. The consumer is considered to receive the revised LE on Monday the 21st.

There are four precise business days from that receipt to consummation: Tuesday the 22nd, Wednesday the 23rd, Thursday the 24th, and Friday the 25th. Both the provision and revised-LE receipt requirements are satisfied under these assumptions. The CD must independently satisfy its own delivery requirements.

If the revised LE is instead mailed Friday the 18th, deemed receipt is Tuesday the 22nd. Only three precise business days then remain to Friday's consummation, so the revised-LE receipt requirement is not met; the three-day provision deadline was also missed on these facts. A file reviewer must identify both defects rather than check only mailing or only closing.

### Once a CD is provided, do not issue another LE

The prohibition begins on the date the creditor **provides** the initial CD, not the later date of deemed receipt. No revised LE may be provided on that same date or afterward. A supported revision can instead be reflected on the CD or corrected CD under the current rule. [§1026.19(e)(4)(ii), comment -1](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/19/#e-4-ii).

Separate case: the creditor hand delivers the initial CD on Monday, September 21. The consumer requests a qualifying settlement change on Tuesday, September 22. Consummation is Friday, September 25. Assume the change does not make the APR inaccurate, does not change the disclosed loan product, and does not add a prepayment penalty.

The creditor must provide the applicable corrected CD so the consumer receives it at or before consummation. For this example, hand delivery on Friday also meets the three-general-business-day supported-revision deadline from Tuesday. A new LE is not permitted. [Comment 19(e)(4)(ii)-1.iii; §1026.19(f)(2)(i)](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/19/#Interp-19-e-4-ii).

Three changes require a new CD waiting period: the APR becomes inaccurate under §1026.22, the disclosed loan product changes, or a prepayment penalty is added. Not every APR movement is an inaccuracy under the rule; not every changed fee requires a new three-day wait. [§1026.19(f)(2)(ii), comments -1.i through -1.iii](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/19/#f-2-ii).

A corrected CD is not a way to resurrect a missed revision deadline. It must reflect accurate information, but use of its increased charges as new baselines still requires the applicable permitted reason and timing.

## 6. Close the loop: correction, not cosmetic redisclosure

If a file has a $75 zero-tolerance excess and a $30 aggregate excess, the combined identified excess is $105. Do not subtract an unrelated fee decrease. Do not count a consumer payment, seller contribution, or existing promised lender credit as a new refund merely by changing its label.

Under §1026.19(f)(2)(v), the specified good-faith compliance treatment requires refunding the excess no later than 60 days after consummation; the corresponding corrected disclosure reflecting the refund must also be delivered or placed in the mail by that deadline. This does not excuse every other possible violation, or establish that planning to overcharge and refund later is acceptable business practice. [§1026.19(f)(2)(v), comment -1](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/19/#f-2-v).

Identify problems before consummation whenever possible. Preserve the calculation, approval, actual consumer benefit, delivery evidence, and any post-closing refund. The current §1026.25(c)(1) has mortgage-specific retention rules; a generic two-year TILA retention label does not cover the LE/CD file. [§1026.25(c)(1)](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/25/#c-1).

Also keep two questions separate:

- Did the charge exceed the permissible estimate?
- Did the consumer pay more than the settlement provider actually received?

The second invokes §1026.19(f)(3), including its conditioned average-charge exception, and is developed in F03. A charge below a tolerance ceiling is not an authorization to retain an unsupported excess over the actual service charge. [§1026.19(f)(3)(i)–(ii)](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/19/#f-3).

### Consequences and a meaningful corrective control

The immediate file consequence can include recalculation, a corrected disclosure, consumer reimbursement, and delayed consummation where a waiting-period rule applies. Repeated errors also require review of affected files and the estimation/revision process. Penalties and broader liability depend on the governing provisions and facts; this lesson does not invent a fixed penalty amount.

A useful review samples original source information, actual recipients, provider lists, invoices, consumer requests, all disclosure versions, and delivery events. It then independently recalculates category membership and baselines. “The latest LE matches the CD” cannot detect an unsupported reset if both documents contain the same incorrect number.

Recommended review flags include an affiliate coded as an ordinary third party, an unused service in the baseline, a revised amount without a reason, an unrelated fee changed during a valid revision, a creditor-approval date substituted for sufficient-information receipt, and an LE issued after a CD. These are proposed controls, not mandatory software fields.

Activity A: choice 3. The baseline must exclude the unperformed service, and a real settlement-service charge cannot be relabeled as prepaid interest to avoid its rule. Correct the actual defect and preserve the calculation; do not modify facts to make the formula pass.
