## Learning objectives

By the end of this module the student will be able to:

- Apply a fixture test and explain which factor controls.
- Read a metes-and-bounds description well enough to spot an ambiguity, and say what a Maine
  mortgage inspection is and is not.
- Classify freehold and leasehold estates and diagram present and future interests.
- Distinguish joint tenancy, tenancy in common, condominium and cooperative ownership.
- Identify the questions a Maine landlord-tenant matter raises and where the answers live.
- Distinguish private land-use control from public land-use control and say who enforces each.

## Hour 15 — Real property, personal property, and fixtures

### 15.1 The three words

**Land** is the surface, the space above, and the ground beneath, to the extent the law recognises
rights in each. **Real estate** is land plus everything permanently attached by nature or by people.
**Real property** is real estate plus the legal interests a person may hold in it — and it is what
is actually bought and sold.

The traditional image is the **bundle of rights**: possession, use, exclusion, encumbrance,
transfer, and the right to do nothing. The sticks separate. A seller who conveys the surface and
reserves timber rights has sold real property and kept real property.

### 15.2 Fixtures

A **fixture** was personal property and became real property by attachment. Once a fixture, it
passes with the deed unless the contract says otherwise.

The test, in the common mnemonic **MARIA**:

**Method of annexation** — how it is attached, and what removal would do. Bolted, wired, plumbed, or
built in points to a fixture; so does damage on removal.

**Adaptation** — has it been fitted to this particular property? Custom-cut storm windows, a
built-in bookcase sized to an alcove, and the key to the front door are adapted, and adaptation
makes a fixture even where the physical attachment is slight.

**Relationship of the parties** — the same item is treated differently between landlord and tenant
than between seller and buyer, and doubt is generally resolved in favour of the weaker position.

**Intention**, judged objectively, which the courts treat as controlling. The first two are evidence
of it.

**Agreement**, which is decisive when it exists.

**Trade fixtures** are installed by a *tenant* for the tenant's trade or business, remain the
tenant's personal property, and must be removed before the term ends — after which they generally
pass to the landlord by **accession**. **Severance** runs the other way: standing timber is real
estate, cut timber is personal property.

### 15.3 The Maine practice point

Most fixture disputes are contracts that failed to say. At listing, walk the property and record
what conveys and what does not — light fittings, mounted televisions and brackets, appliances,
window treatments, generators, water-treatment equipment, sheds, above-ground pools, and anything
the seller intends to take.

Two Maine items recur: **heating equipment**, including free-standing stoves, propane tanks that are
frequently **leased from the dealer rather than owned**, and buried oil tanks; and **water-treatment
systems**, which on a private well are often the only reason the water is usable and are sometimes
rented.

A leased propane tank is not the seller's to convey. Discovering that at the walk-through is a
failure of the listing conversation, not of the contract.

## Hour 16 — Legal descriptions, survey systems, and mortgage inspections

### 16.1 Why an address is not a description

A street address identifies a mailbox. A **legal description** identifies a unique parcel in a way a
surveyor could reproduce on the ground. Deeds, mortgages, plans, and judgments use legal
descriptions; contracts should.

### 16.2 Metes and bounds

Maine is a **metes and bounds** state. A description begins at a **point of beginning**, proceeds by
**calls** of bearing and distance, and must **close** back at the point of beginning.

A call reads, for example, *N 42° 15′ 00″ E, 318.6 feet to an iron pin*. Bearings are read as a
deviation from north or south toward east or west; sixty minutes to a degree, sixty seconds to a
minute.

**Monuments** are the physical markers a call references — an iron pin, a drill hole in a ledge, a
stone wall, a large tree, the centreline of a road, the thread of a stream. The rule of construction
is that **monuments control over courses and distances**, because a monument is what a person on the
ground can find.

Maine descriptions carry the specific difficulties of a long-settled, heavily wooded, coastal state:
calls referencing stone walls and trees that are two centuries old; adjoining-owner references to
families long gone; **shoreline boundaries that move**; and descriptions carried forward unchanged
through generations of deeds while the physical world changed around them.

### 16.3 Recorded plans

A **lot and block** or recorded-plan description references a plan recorded at the Registry of
Deeds: *Lot 7 as shown on a plan entitled "…", recorded in the ___ County Registry of Deeds in Plan
Book __, Page __.* The plan itself carries the metes and bounds, the easements, and the surveyor's
certification.

**A lot does not exist until the plan is recorded.** Selling from an unrecorded sketch is selling
something with no legal description.

### 16.4 The rectangular survey system, and why Maine does not use it

The government survey system of principal meridians, base lines, townships six miles square and
sections of 640 acres governs most land west and north of the original states. **Maine is not a
public land survey state.** A student should know the arithmetic for a national licensing
examination — a quarter of a quarter section is 40 acres — but should not expect it in a Maine deed.

Maine's unorganised territory is described by **township and range** in its own historical system
(T5 R7 BKP WKR and the like), which is a Maine convention and not the federal rectangular system.

### 16.5 Survey, and the Maine "mortgage inspection"

A **boundary survey** is a licensed surveyor's determination of where the lines are on the ground,
and it is the only thing that answers a boundary question.

A **mortgage loan inspection**, sometimes called a mortgage inspection plan, is something else
entirely: a limited drawing prepared for a lender to show, approximately, that the improvements sit
within the apparent boundaries and outside easements. It is **not a boundary survey**, it is
**usually stamped to say so**, and it does not establish a line.

The distinction matters because buyers and licensees routinely treat the mortgage inspection as
though it were a survey. It is not, and a licensee who lets a buyer believe it is has caused the
problem that surfaces when the neighbour builds a garage.

When to recommend a survey: irregular parcels; older metes-and-bounds descriptions; visible fences,
walls or structures near a line; planned construction; unimproved land; waterfront; any mention of a
boundary dispute; and any right of way whose location is not obvious.

## Hour 17 — Freehold estates and future interests

### 17.1 Estates

An **estate** is the degree, quantity, nature and extent of a person's interest in land. The primary
division is **freehold** — of indefinite duration, involving ownership — and **leasehold** — of
fixed or determinable duration, involving possession.

### 17.2 Fee simple

**Fee simple absolute** is the largest estate: potentially infinite, freely transferable in life and
at death, subject only to the four government powers and to private restrictions the owner or a
predecessor accepted. A conveyance is presumed to convey a fee simple absolute unless the deed says
otherwise.

**Defeasible fees** may end on a stated event.

A **fee simple determinable** ends **automatically**, and the grantor keeps a **possibility of
reverter**. The language is durational — "so long as", "while", "until".

A **fee simple subject to a condition subsequent** gives the grantor a **right of re-entry** that
must be exercised. The language is conditional — "but if", "provided that", "on condition that".

Both are title matters and appear in the record. They belong to the title examiner and to counsel.

### 17.3 Life estates

A **life estate** is measured by a life. The **life tenant** has full use and possession, may lease
or convey what they have, and takes the income — but may not convey more than they own and may not
commit **waste**, meaning they must not damage the property or let it deteriorate and must
ordinarily pay taxes and interest on existing debt.

When the measuring life ends: a **reversion** if the property returns to the grantor or their heirs;
a **remainder** if it passes to a named third party.

A **life estate pur autre vie** is measured by someone else's life and is the one life estate that
can be inherited — if the holder dies while the measuring life continues, the balance passes to the
holder's heirs.

Life estates are common in Maine estate planning, and their practical effect on a sale is that
**everyone with an interest must sign**: the life tenant and the remaindermen. A licensee taking a
listing from a life tenant alone has taken a listing that cannot close.

### 17.4 The government powers

Every public limitation traces to one of four — **police power** (regulation for health, safety and
welfare, without compensation: zoning, codes, shoreland regulation, subdivision control),
**eminent domain** (taking for public use with just compensation, executed by condemnation),
**taxation**, and **escheat** (property passing to the State where an owner dies with no will and no
heirs).

## Hour 18 — Concurrent ownership, condominium, and cooperative

### 18.1 Severalty and co-ownership

**Severalty** is ownership by one person or one entity alone.

**Tenancy in common** is the default when two or more take title without specifying otherwise. Each
holds an **undivided fractional interest** — undivided meaning each may possess the whole, not a
marked-off part. Shares need not be equal. There is **no survivorship**: a deceased owner's share
passes to their heirs or devisees. Any co-tenant may transfer their share, and any may seek
**partition**.

**Joint tenancy** adds the **right of survivorship**: the deceased joint tenant's interest passes to
the survivors outside probate. The common-law unities are **time, title, interest and possession**.
Survivorship must be expressed; it is not presumed. A joint tenant who conveys their interest
destroys the joint tenancy as to that share, and the transferee holds as a tenant in common.

**Maine is not a community property state.** A student who has met that term in national material
should know it does not describe Maine title. Maine's protection for a surviving spouse operates
through Title 18-C, the Probate Code, and it is a legal question — the practical brokerage
consequence is that **a spouse may have an interest that must be released at closing**, and that is
determined by the closing attorney and the title examiner, not by the licensee.

### 18.2 Trusts and entities

Property may be held by a corporation, a partnership, a limited liability company, or a **trust**.
In a trust the trustee holds legal title and the beneficiary takes the benefit, and **the trustee
signs**. Whether the signatory has authority is evidenced by organisational documents, trust
instruments and certificates, and it is verified by the closing attorney.

### 18.3 Condominium

A condominium divides a project into individually owned **units** and **common elements** owned in
undivided shares by all unit owners, with **limited common elements** — a deck, an assigned parking
space, a storage bay — serving fewer than all units. The instruments are the **declaration**, the
**bylaws**, the recorded **plats and plans**, and the **rules**.

Maine's condominium regime sits in **Title 33**, and this course states no requirement from it,
because the chapter has not been read from primary text for this project. What a licensee must do is
obtain the governing documents, the budget, the reserve position, the minutes, the assessment
history, any pending or discussed special assessment, and the association's rules on leasing, pets
and alterations — before the offer, not after.

### 18.4 Cooperative

In a cooperative a **corporation owns the real property**; the resident owns **shares** and holds a
**proprietary lease**. The resident therefore owns **personal property**, not real property. There
is no deed, financing is a share loan rather than a mortgage, and the board typically has approval
rights over a transfer — subject always to fair housing law.

### 18.5 Assessments, reserves, and the special assessment risk

Regular assessments fund the budget; **special assessments** fund what the budget did not. The
second is the largest financial risk in common-interest ownership, and it is discovered by reading
the **reserve study and two years of minutes** — where a roof, a seawall, a private road or a septic
replacement is discussed long before it is levied. In coastal and lakefront Maine, shared shoreline
structures and private road maintenance are the recurring examples.

## Hour 19 — Leasehold estates and leases

### 19.1 The four leasehold estates

**Estate for years** — a fixed term with a definite beginning and end. It expires without notice.
The name misleads: a two-week lease is an estate for years.

**Periodic estate** — renews automatically for successive periods until one party gives notice.
Month-to-month tenancies are the common case.

**Estate at will** — possession with consent, no fixed period, terminable by either party, subject
to statutory notice.

**Estate at sufferance** — a tenant who entered lawfully and stayed past the term without consent.
It is distinguished from trespass only by the lawful entry, and removing such a tenant is a legal
process rather than a self-help matter.

### 19.2 A lease is two things at once

A lease is a **conveyance** — it transfers a leasehold estate and the right of exclusive possession
— and a **contract** — it creates mutual promises. The first explains why a tenant may exclude even
the landlord except as the lease and the law allow; the second explains how breach is analysed.

Essential terms: parties; premises; term; rent, its amount, due date and method; the security
deposit; utilities; permitted use and occupancy; maintenance on each side; the landlord's right of
entry and the notice required; rules on pets, smoking, parking and alterations; assignment and
subletting; default and remedies; renewal or termination; and required disclosures.

### 19.3 Assignment and sublease

**Assignment** transfers the tenant's **entire** remaining interest; **sublease** transfers less
than all of it, and the original tenant becomes a landlord to the subtenant while remaining a tenant
to the landlord.

In neither case is the original tenant automatically released. Release requires the landlord's
agreement, which is a **novation**.

### 19.4 What a sale or a foreclosure does to a lease

**A sale does not end a lease.** The buyer takes subject to the tenancy, and the tenant's rights run
against the new owner. Security deposits and prepaid rent must be transferred and accounted for, and
this is a term of the purchase contract that must be written rather than assumed.

**Foreclosure** is more complicated: whether a lease survives depends on whether it predates the
mortgage and on the statutory protections available to residential tenants. That is a legal question
with real consequences for occupied property, and it goes to counsel — not to a licensee's
assumption.

A licensee listing occupied property must, at intake: obtain every lease; confirm the deposits held
and where; confirm rent paid through what date; and confirm whether any tenancy is oral, because an
oral tenancy is still a tenancy.

## Hour 20 — Maine landlord-tenant fundamentals

### 20.1 Where the law is

Maine's residential landlord-tenant law is principally in **14 M.R.S.**, and it covers security
deposits, the landlord's obligations as to fitness and habitability, access, notice and termination,
the process for recovering possession, and prohibited conduct.

**This course states no Maine security deposit maximum, no return deadline, no notice period, and no
entry notice**, because **14 M.R.S. has not been read from primary text for this project**. Those
figures are exactly the ones a course gets wrong by carrying them from another state or from an
out-of-date summary, and exactly the ones on which a landlord loses a case.

### 20.2 The method, which is the examinable skill

**One — classify the tenancy.** Residential or commercial? A dwelling unit, a mobile home park lot,
or a seasonal rental? Within the statute's scope or excluded?

**Two — go to the statute**, at `legislature.maine.gov`, in its current text. Read the operative
section **and the sections around it**.

**Three — read the lease**, which may give the tenant more than the statute requires but not less
where the statute sets a floor.

**Four — refer.** Whether a notice was validly given, whether a breach is material, and what remedy
is available are legal conclusions.

### 20.3 What is certain, whatever the figures

**Self-help is unlawful.** Changing locks, removing possessions, or shutting off utilities to force
a tenant out is prohibited, and a licensee who advises a landlord client to do any of it has advised
a violation — against the duty in § 13273 1 E to comply with applicable law, and against § 13273 1 D
to comply with the Commission's rules.

**Deposits are the tenant's money held in trust.** Where an agency holds them, they are trust funds
under § 13178 and the designated broker is responsible for them.

**Habitability obligations run to the condition of the premises**, and the record of what was
reported and what was done is the whole of the defence in a dispute.

**Fair housing applies to leasing exactly as it applies to sale**, and screening is where discretion
— and therefore risk — lives. Written, objective, uniform criteria applied identically to every
applicant is the only defensible practice.

### 20.4 Maine-specific practicalities

Several conditions recur in Maine tenancies and belong in a licensee's intake list: **heating fuel
responsibility** and who fills the tank; **snow removal and winter road maintenance**;
**wood and pellet stoves** and their insurance implications; **seasonal and short-term rentals**,
which many municipalities now regulate separately; **lead-based paint** in a housing stock that is
substantially pre-1978, which triggers the federal disclosure and, for rentals, federal renovation
rules; and **radon**, which Maine addresses in the rental context by statute.

Each is a question to be raised and routed, and the statutory detail must be read rather than
recalled.

## Hour 21 — Private land-use controls

### 21.1 Encumbrances that affect use

An **encumbrance** is a claim or right held by someone other than the owner that affects title or
limits use. Those affecting **use** are easements, restrictive covenants and encroachments; those
affecting **title as security for money** are liens, and they belong to Hour 25.

### 21.2 Easements

An **easement** is a non-possessory right to use another's land for a stated purpose, and it is an
interest in land.

An **easement appurtenant** benefits a parcel: the benefited parcel is the **dominant tenement**, the
burdened one the **servient tenement**, and it **runs with the land** — it passes automatically to
the next owner. Shared driveways, access to a back lot, and drainage are the everyday cases.

An **easement in gross** benefits a person or entity rather than a parcel. Utility easements are the
common example.

**Creation:** by express grant or reservation in a deed; by **implication**, where a use was
apparent, continuous and necessary when a parcel was divided; by **necessity**, the landlocked-parcel
case, requiring common ownership at severance; by **prescription**, from open, notorious, continuous
and adverse use for the statutory period; and by **condemnation**.

**Termination:** by merger, release, abandonment shown by conduct rather than mere non-use, the end
of the necessity, or the easement's own terms.

**This course states no Maine prescriptive period and no adverse possession period**, because those
provisions of **14 M.R.S.** have not been read from primary text for this project. They are legal
determinations in any event.

Maine adds two access questions that arise constantly: **rights of way to water**, which may be
deeded, prescriptive, or merely tolerated; and **private roads**, where the existence, terms and
enforceability of a **road maintenance agreement** decide whether a buyer can reach their house in
February. Both are title and legal questions and both must be answered in writing before closing.

### 21.3 Licence, encroachment, party wall

A **licence** is revocable personal permission and is not an interest in land — the neighbour who
has "always" crossed the corner usually has one, unless the use has ripened into a prescriptive
easement, which is a legal determination on facts nobody in the conversation has established.

An **encroachment** is an unauthorised physical intrusion across a line. It is found by **survey**,
not by looking, and long-standing encroachments can mature into rights.

A **party wall** is a shared wall on a boundary, each owner typically owning to the centre with an
easement of support in the other half.

### 21.4 Restrictive covenants and associations

**Restrictive covenants** are private limits on use, recorded so they bind successors. They regulate
setbacks, minimum sizes, materials, outbuildings, signage, tree cutting, and short-term rental.

Three points:

**They can be stricter than zoning**, and where zoning permits and a covenant forbids, the owner is
bound by both and must satisfy the stricter.

**They are enforced privately** — by an association or a neighbour — not by the municipality.

**Discriminatory covenants are void and must not be honoured.** Historic racial covenants remain
physically present in Maine chains of title. They are unenforceable, and repeating them as though
they operated is itself a fair housing problem — Hour 51 returns to it.

Associations — condominium associations, road associations, lake associations, homeowners
associations — hold the enforcement power and the assessment power. A buyer needs the documents, the
budget, the reserve position, the minutes, and the assessment history.

## Hour 22 — Public land-use control: zoning, permits, and codes

### 22.1 The structure

Maine delegates land-use regulation to **municipalities** under **30-A M.R.S.**, and municipal
practice varies widely. In the **unorganised territory** the **Land Use Planning Commission** stands
in the municipality's place. Hour 23 takes the Maine statutes in detail; this hour builds the
national framework the Maine provisions attach to.

A **comprehensive plan** is the municipality's long-range policy statement. **Zoning** is the
ordinance that implements it and has legal force, dividing the municipality into districts and
prescribing for each the permitted **uses** and the **bulk** controls — minimum lot size, frontage,
setbacks, height, lot coverage, and often signage and parking.

### 22.2 The relief mechanisms, distinguished

A **variance** relieves a **dimensional** requirement where strict application causes an undue
hardship arising from the property itself — shape, topography, soils. It is granted by the board of
appeals. **A variance does not authorise a use the district forbids**, and self-created hardship and
mere financial disadvantage are not grounds. Maine's undue-hardship standard is set by statute and
is applied strictly; it is a legal question and this course states no element of it.

A **conditional use permit** or **special exception** authorises a use the ordinance lists as
permissible **subject to review and conditions**. It is the ordinance operating as written, not an
exception to it.

A **nonconforming use** or **nonconforming structure** was lawful when established and became
non-compliant on a later ordinance change. It may generally continue, typically may not be expanded,
and may be lost on abandonment or destruction.

**In Maine the nonconforming-structure question is unusually important**, because a very large part
of the shorefront housing stock is nonconforming as to setback from the water. What may be done to
such a structure — expanded, replaced, moved, rebuilt after fire — is governed by the municipality's
shoreland zoning ordinance and by state minimum standards, and the answer differs between
municipalities.

A **rezoning** is an amendment to the ordinance itself and is a legislative act of the municipal
legislative body.

### 22.3 Permits and codes

Construction is governed by building and life-safety codes and administered by the **code
enforcement officer**. Maine's statewide code, the **Maine Uniform Building and Energy Code**,
applies as adopted, and its applicability varies with municipal size and adoption — a fact that
surprises people who assume a single statewide rule. **Plumbing and subsurface wastewater** are
permitted through the local plumbing inspector, and **septic system design** is done by a licensed
site evaluator.

The **certificate of occupancy**, where the municipality issues one, is the document saying the
building may lawfully be occupied.

### 22.4 The licensee's boundary

Every question in this hour is answered by a public official and recorded in a public office. The
licensee's job is to know that the question exists, to know **who** answers it — the code
enforcement officer, the planning board, the assessor, the plumbing inspector, the Department of
Environmental Protection, the Land Use Planning Commission, the Department of Transportation — and
to send the client there **in writing**.

Under **§ 13273 1 B (4)** the seller agent must **advise the client to obtain expert advice on
material matters beyond the agent's expertise**, and under **§ 13283 3 A and B** a transaction broker
may not conduct an investigation or analysis for a party's benefit, nor verify the accuracy or
completeness of statements made by anyone. Both point the same way: raise the question, name the
office, and put it in writing.

### Module 03 engagement check

**Question.** A buyer is under contract on a lakefront camp built in 1962, twenty feet from the
water. They tell you they intend to demolish it and build a larger year-round house on the same
footprint, and ask whether that will be "fine since it's already there". They also mention that the
neighbour's driveway crosses the corner of the lot and that a mortgage inspection plan from the
seller's refinance "shows everything is okay". Respond.

**Expected answer.** Neither question is mine to answer, and both must be routed in writing.

**The structure is almost certainly a nonconforming structure** as to shoreland setback. What may be
done to it — expanded, replaced, demolished and rebuilt, moved — is governed by the municipality's
**shoreland zoning ordinance** and state minimum standards, differs between municipalities, and is
answered by the **code enforcement officer in writing** before the contingency period ends. "It's
already there" is not an answer, and a demolition frequently ends the nonconforming status the buyer
is relying on. A larger year-round house also raises **subsurface wastewater** capacity, which the
local plumbing inspector and a licensed site evaluator answer.

**The driveway is a boundary and title question.** It may be a recorded easement appurtenant running
with the land, a prescriptive easement, a revocable licence, or an encroachment — and which it is
decides whether the buyer can stop it. That is for the title examiner, a surveyor, and counsel.

**The mortgage inspection plan is not a survey.** It is a limited drawing prepared for a lender, it
is usually stamped to say it may not be used to establish boundaries, and it does not answer the
driveway question. I would recommend a **boundary survey** and confirm that recommendation in
writing.

Under **§ 13273 1 B (4)** — if the buyer is my client — I am required to advise them to obtain expert
advice on material matters beyond my expertise; as a **transaction broker** under § 13283 3 I am
barred from conducting the investigation or verifying anyone's statements. Either way the answer is
the same: name the offices, get it in writing, and do not opine.

**Municipal variation is stated as variation.** Maine land-use regulation is delegated to
