# Maine Sales Agent Course — Module 05

## Hours 29–34 · Property valuation and appraisal

**Commission topic covered:** Property Valuation and Appraisal (9%, 270 instructional minutes)
**Course version:** 1.0 submission draft
**Regulatory status:** Not approved. No Maine sales agent credit is available for this course.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module the student will be able to:

- Distinguish value, price and cost, and state the conditions in the definition of market value.
- Apply the principles of value to a property change and identify highest and best use.
- Adjust comparable sales in the correct direction and reconcile to a supported range.
- Compute value by direct capitalization and by gross rent and gross income multipliers.
- Compute value by the cost approach and classify depreciation by type.
- State what § 13251-A prohibits and how it constrains a Maine licensee's valuation work.

## Hour 29 (45–60) — Types of value, and where a sales agent stands

### 29.4 Value, price, and cost

**Value** is an opinion of what a property should bring under stated conditions. **Price** is what
was actually paid in a particular transaction, which may reflect unusual motivation or terms.
**Cost** is what it takes to create the improvements, which may be more or less than what they add
to value.

**Market value** is the classic definition, and its conditions do the work: the most probable price
in a competitive and open market, under conditions requisite to a fair sale, with buyer and seller
each acting prudently and knowledgeably, **neither under duress**, allowing a reasonable exposure
time, with payment in cash or its equivalent and terms typical of the market.

Strip out any one of those conditions — a distressed seller, a sale between family members, no market
exposure, seller financing at an off-market rate — and the observed price is not evidence of market
value without adjustment. That is the screen applied when comparables are selected.

Other values a licensee will meet: **assessed value**, set by the municipal assessor for taxation
and worked to a **certified ratio**; **insurable value**, which is a replacement cost concept and
excludes land; **investment value**, which is value to a particular investor with particular
objectives; and **liquidation value**, which assumes an inadequate exposure period.

### 29.5 The § 13251-A boundary, stated before the methods

Before learning the methods, a Maine sales agent must know the limit on using them:

> A real estate broker or associate broker **may not knowingly provide or offer an appraisal or
> opinion of market value**, as set forth in section 14004, on real estate in a transaction **where
> the broker or associate broker, or any other licensee licensed with the agency, is to receive a
> fee on that transaction.**

The prohibition reaches the **whole agency**, not the individual. Hour 34 returns to it with the
methods in hand.

## Hour 30 — Principles of value and highest and best use

### 30.1 The principles

**Supply and demand.** Value moves with the relationship between them, and real estate's supply is
slow to respond, so a demand change shows up first as price.

**Substitution.** A buyer will pay no more than the cost of acquiring an equally desirable
substitute. This is the foundation of all three approaches: sales comparison, cost and income each
ask what a substitute would cost.

**Anticipation.** Value reflects expected future benefits, not past cost.

**Contribution.** A component adds value equal to what it contributes to the whole — measured in the
market — which is frequently far less than it cost. This is the principle a seller most often needs
explained.

**Conformity, progression and regression.** Value is maximised where a property conforms reasonably
to its surroundings; a lesser property gains from better neighbours and a superior property is
dragged toward the level of lesser ones.

**Increasing and diminishing returns.** Additional investment adds value up to a point and then does
not.

**Competition.** Excess profit invites competition, which erodes it.

**Change.** No condition is permanent, which is why every value opinion has an effective date.

**Plottage** is the increment from combining adjacent parcels under one ownership; **assemblage** is
the act of combining them.

**Externalities.** Influences outside the property affect its value — the closed mill, the new
bypass, the neighbouring quarry.

### 30.2 Highest and best use

The reasonably probable use that is **legally permissible, physically possible, financially
feasible, and maximally productive** — all four tests, in that order.

Land is valued at its highest and best use **as though vacant**, which is why an obsolete building on
a well-located lot may sell for land value.

In Maine the **legally permissible** test carries unusual weight, because a parcel's use can be
constrained simultaneously by municipal zoning, shoreland zoning, subdivision status, subsurface
wastewater capacity, a conservation easement, a current-use classification, and private covenants.
A "highest and best use" conclusion that has not checked those is a guess.

### 30.3 The four forces

**Physical and environmental** — location, topography, soils, water, climate, improvements.
**Economic** — employment, income, credit, rents, construction costs.
**Governmental** — zoning, codes, taxation, public services.
**Social** — population, household formation, tastes.

Maine adds a seasonal dimension that a national text will not: in many markets the buyer pool, the
exposure period, and the achievable price all move with the season, and a sale in February is not
evidence of the same market as a sale in July.

## Hour 31 — The market data approach

### 31.1 The rule

**Adjust the comparable, never the subject.** The subject is the unknown; the comparables are the
evidence. Every adjustment answers: *what would this comparable have sold for if it had been like the
subject?*

**Comparable superior to the subject → adjust the comparable DOWN.**
**Comparable inferior to the subject → adjust the comparable UP.**

The memory hook: **comparable better, subtract**.

### 31.2 Selecting comparables

**Closed sales are evidence. Active listings are not** — an active listing is an unaccepted offer.
Pending sales are better evidence than actives, and **expired and withdrawn** listings are useful in
one specific way: they show prices the market **refused**, which brackets the top of the range.

Screen for **arm's-length conditions**: unrelated parties, adequate exposure, no unusual pressure,
market terms.

Then compare on: **real property rights conveyed**; **financing terms** including seller
concessions; **conditions of sale**; **expenditures made immediately after purchase**; **market
conditions**, which is the time adjustment; **location**; **physical characteristics**; and
**economic characteristics, use and non-realty components** — the boat, the furniture, the tractor
that were included in the price.

In Maine, **water frontage** is frequently the dominant variable and is measured in feet of frontage
as well as by quality of frontage — sand versus ledge, western versus eastern exposure, depth at the
shore, and whether the frontage is on a great pond, a river, or tidal water. **Seasonal versus
year-round** is a second dominant variable, and **acreage with or without development potential** a
third.

### 31.3 A worked grid

*Subject: 1,750 sq ft year-round house, 3 bed, 2 bath, detached garage, 150 feet of frontage on a
great pond.*

| | Comp A | Comp B | Comp C |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Sale price | $498,000 | $455,000 | $529,000 |
| Sold | 3 months ago | 7 months ago | 1 month ago |
| Market conditions +0.5%/month | +$7,470 | +$15,925 | +$2,645 |
| Frontage | 150 ft | 110 ft (−40) | 190 ft (+40) |
| Frontage adj. at $1,200/ft | $0 | +$48,000 | −$48,000 |
| Living area | 1,800 (+50) | 1,700 (−50) | 1,750 (same) |
| Area adj. at $95/sq ft | −$4,750 | +$4,750 | $0 |
| Garage | detached | none | detached |
| Garage adj. | $0 | +$18,000 | $0 |
| **Net adjustment** | **+$2,720** | **+$86,675** | **−$45,355** |
| **Adjusted price** | **$500,720** | **$541,675** | **$483,645** |

Note the directions. Comp A is **larger** — superior — so the area adjustment is negative. Comp B is
**inferior** on frontage, area and garage, so all three adjustments are positive. Comp C has **more
frontage** — superior — so that adjustment is negative.

### 31.4 Reconciling

The indicated range is **$483,645 to $541,675**, and reconciliation is judgment rather than
averaging. It weighs each comparable by reliability: how few adjustments it needed, how large the
**gross adjustment** was, how recent the sale, how close the location.

Comp B needed **$86,675** of gross adjustment on a $455,000 sale — nearly a fifth of the price — and
is therefore the weakest evidence, however tidy its arithmetic looks. Comps A and C are the stronger
indications, and a defensible conclusion sits between them, weighted toward A.

**Gross adjustment measures comparability; net adjustment does not.** A comparable whose large
adjustments happen to offset is not a good comparable; it is a lucky one.

## Hour 32 — The income approach

### 32.1 The statement

**Potential Gross Income** — all income at full occupancy at market rent, plus other income.
**less Vacancy and Collection Loss**, supported by market evidence.
**= Effective Gross Income.**
**less Operating Expenses** — fixed, variable, and **replacement reserves**.
**= Net Operating Income.**

Three items are **excluded** from operating expenses, and the exclusion is the most examinable point
in the hour:

**Debt service.** NOI measures the property's own performance independent of how any owner financed
it. Including the mortgage payment would give the same building a different NOI for every buyer.

**Depreciation.** An accounting and tax entry, not a cash expense.

**Capital expenditures.** A new roof is capital; the **replacement reserve** that spreads its
anticipated cost **is** an operating expense, but the outlay itself is not.

### 32.2 Direct capitalization

**Value = NOI ÷ capitalization rate**, written **V = I ÷ R**, with **I = V × R** and **R = I ÷ V**.

*Worked:* an 8-unit building at $1,350 per month plus $3,600 other annual income.
PGI = 8 × 1,350 × 12 + 3,600 = 129,600 + 3,600 = **$133,200**.
Vacancy and collection loss 6% = 7,992. **EGI = $125,208.**
Operating expenses $47,500, including a $4,000 replacement reserve.
**NOI = 125,208 − 47,500 = $77,708.**
At a market rate of 7.25%: **V = 77,708 ÷ 0.0725 = $1,071,834**, say $1,072,000.

Two consequences a licensee must be able to state. **The rate is derived from the market**, by
dividing comparable properties' NOI by their sale prices — it is not chosen. And **a higher rate
gives a lower value at the same NOI**: at 8.25% the same building indicates $941,915, about $130,000
less for one percentage point. So a market in which rates rise is a market in which values fall even
if rents do not change.

### 32.3 Multipliers

**Gross rent multiplier** relates sale price to **monthly** gross rent; **gross income multiplier**
relates it to **annual** gross income.

**GRM = sale price ÷ monthly gross rent**, then **value = GRM × the subject's monthly gross rent**.

*Worked:* comparable GRMs of 12.8, 13.4 and 13.1; the subject's monthly gross rent is $10,800. At
13.1: **value = 13.1 × 10,800 = $141,480**.

**What the multiplier omits is the point.** It uses **gross** rent, so it ignores vacancy and every
operating expense. Two buildings with identical gross rents and different expense structures look
identical to a GRM and are not. It is a screening tool for small residential income property, not a
valuation method for anything serious.

### 32.4 The Maine seasonal-rental complication

A great many Maine income properties are **seasonal**: a coastal cottage let by the week in summer, a
ski-area condominium let in winter. Applying an annual-rent model to them produces nonsense.

The honest treatment states the **season length**, the **achieved occupancy within the season**, the
**rate by week or night**, and the **costs the model must carry that a long-term rental does not** —
cleaning and turnover, management commission, booking platform fees, furnishings replacement,
utilities carried year-round, insurance rated for short-term use, and any municipal short-term rental
registration or restriction. Many Maine municipalities now regulate short-term rentals, and whether a
property may lawfully be let that way is a question for the municipality before it is a question for
the arithmetic.

## Hour 33 — The cost approach and depreciation

### 33.1 The formula

**Value = land value + cost new of the improvements − accrued depreciation.**

**Land value** is estimated separately, normally by sales comparison of vacant sites — which in Maine
means comparing frontage, acreage, soils and wastewater capacity, access, and development status
rather than simply acreage.

**Cost new** is either **reproduction cost** — an exact replica including its obsolete features — or
**replacement cost** — a substitute of equivalent utility built with current materials and methods.
Replacement cost is used more often, because nobody would rebuild the obsolete features.

### 33.2 The three depreciations

**Physical deterioration** — wear, tear and age. **Curable** where the cost to fix is recovered in
value; **incurable** where it is not.

**Functional obsolescence** — a defect in the design or utility of the improvement itself: one
bathroom for four bedrooms, a bedroom reached only through another, a kitchen that cannot fit a
dishwasher, a camp layout in a year-round market. Curable or incurable.

**External obsolescence** — loss caused by something **outside** the property: a closed employer, a
new road, a changed neighbourhood, a quarry. **Always incurable**, because the owner cannot fix it
from inside the boundary.

*Worked:* land value $185,000; replacement cost new $340,000; physical deterioration $61,000;
functional obsolescence $14,000; external obsolescence $9,000.
185,000 + 340,000 − 61,000 − 14,000 − 9,000 = **$441,000**.

### 33.3 When the cost approach is reliable

Most reliable for **new construction** and for **special-purpose property** with few comparable
sales. Least reliable for old improvements, where the depreciation estimate carries most of the
answer.

In Maine it has one common and useful application: a **seasonal camp** in a market where the buyer
is plainly paying for the land and the frontage. Where the cost approach indicates that the
improvements contribute little, that is itself the answer — and it is often the honest explanation a
seller needs.

## Hour 34 (0–15) — Integration, and the appraisal-law boundary

### 34.1 Reconciling three approaches

The three approaches answer the same question by different evidence, and reconciliation weights them
by the assignment and the property:

**Residential, owner-occupied** — the **market data approach** governs; the cost approach supports;
the income approach is largely irrelevant.
**Income property** — the **income approach** governs; market data supports.
**New construction and special purpose** — the **cost approach** carries more weight.

Reconciliation is **not averaging**, and a reconciled figure with no stated reasoning is a number
without evidence.

### 34.2 The boundary — § 13251-A and Title 32, chapter 124

Maine licenses appraisers separately, under **Title 32, chapter 124**, and § 13251-A refers to
**§ 14004** in that chapter for what constitutes an appraisal or opinion of market value.

Then it prohibits, in terms:

> A real estate broker or associate broker **may not knowingly provide or offer an appraisal or
> opinion of market value** … on real estate in a transaction **where the broker or associate broker,
> or any other licensee licensed with the agency, is to receive a fee on that transaction.**

Three features of that sentence a Maine licensee must hold:

**It reaches the agency, not just the individual.** If any licensee in the agency is to be paid a fee
on the transaction, no broker or associate broker in that agency may provide an appraisal or opinion
of market value on it.

**It applies to "an appraisal or opinion of market value" as § 14004 sets that out.** The scope of
that phrase is a legal question, and it is exactly why a licensee should not improvise near this
line.

**It does not prohibit brokerage.** Presenting market evidence to a client for the purpose of setting
a listing price or an offer price is ordinary brokerage work and is what §§ 13273 and 13274 expect a
Maine agent to do. What § 13251-A prohibits is providing or offering **an appraisal or opinion of
market value** into a transaction the agency is being paid on.

The practical rule for a sales agent: **present the evidence, describe the range it supports, never
style the work as an appraisal or an opinion of market value, never sign anything so titled, and
take any question near the line to the designated broker.** Where an appraisal is what is actually
needed, a licensed appraiser does it.

### Module 05 engagement check

**Question.** A seller under a listing agreement with your agency asks you to "give me a written
opinion of market value for my divorce lawyer" on the property you have listed. What do you do, and
why?

**Expected answer.** I decline, and I say why in writing.

**§ 13251-A** provides that a broker or associate broker may not knowingly provide or offer **an
appraisal or opinion of market value**, as set forth in § 14004, on real estate in a transaction
**where the broker or associate broker, or any other licensee licensed with the agency, is to receive
a fee on that transaction.** The agency holds the listing and will be paid on the sale, so the
prohibition applies — and it reaches **any** licensee in the agency, not just me.

What the client actually needs is an **appraisal by a licensed appraiser**, licensed under Title 32,
chapter 124, who can be engaged independently and whose work is intended for exactly this use. I
refer them there.

What I may still do is what I was engaged to do: present **market evidence** — closed comparable
sales, screened for arm's-length conditions, adjusted toward the subject, reconciled to a range with
my reasoning stated — for the purpose of setting a listing price. I must not style that work as an
opinion of market value, and I must not sign anything so titled. If the client presses, the question
goes to my **designated broker**, and under **§ 13273 1 B (4)** I am in any event required to advise
the client to obtain expert advice on a material matter beyond my expertise.

## Extended treatment — valuation topics the Commission's competencies require

### E5.1 The appraisal process, in sequence

A student should be able to reproduce the sequence, because it is examinable and because it explains
why an appraisal cannot simply be reused for a different purpose.

1. **Define the problem** — the client, the intended user, the intended use, the type of value, the
   effective date, the property rights being appraised, and any assumptions or limiting conditions.
2. **Determine the scope of work** — how much investigation the assignment requires.
3. **Collect data** — general data about the market and the area; specific data about the subject and
   the comparables.
4. **Analyse highest and best use** — as vacant and as improved.
5. **Estimate land value** separately, which the cost approach requires.
6. **Apply the approaches** — sales comparison, cost, income.
7. **Reconcile** into an opinion or a range.
8. **Report** in the form the assignment requires.

Change the intended use — a mortgage loan, a divorce, an estate, a tax appeal, a relocation — and the
scope of work and sometimes the type of value change with it. That is why an appraisal prepared for
one purpose is not evidence for another, and it is part of why § 13251-A treats an appraisal or
opinion of market value as a distinct product rather than as an incident of brokerage.

### E5.2 Deriving a capitalization rate

Hour 32 said the rate is derived rather than chosen. Three derivations a licensee should recognise:

**Market extraction.** Divide each comparable sale's NOI by its sale price and reconcile the results.
This is the most defensible method and it requires reliable NOI figures for the comparables, which is
the hard part.

*Worked:* three sales — NOI $84,000 at $1,150,000 gives 7.30%; NOI $61,500 at $860,000 gives 7.15%;
NOI $128,000 at $1,700,000 gives 7.53%. Reconciled, the market indicates roughly **7.3%**, and a
subject with a weaker tenant profile or deferred maintenance would carry a higher rate.

**Band of investment.** Weight the mortgage constant and the equity dividend rate by their shares of
the capital stack.

*Worked:* 70% debt at a mortgage constant of 0.0812 and 30% equity at a required dividend rate of
6.0%. (0.70 × 0.0812) + (0.30 × 0.060) = 0.05684 + 0.018 = **0.0748**, or **7.48%**.

**Built-up.** A safe rate plus increments for risk, illiquidity and management burden. It is the
least precise and the most argued about.

The point for a sales agent is not to produce a rate. It is to know that a rate quoted without a
derivation is an assertion, and that a seller's pro forma showing a "6% cap" is describing an asking
price rather than a market.

### E5.3 The reserve line, and how a pro forma is manipulated

The most common way an income statement is dressed is by **omitting the replacement reserve**. Leave
it out and NOI rises; capitalise the inflated NOI and value rises with it.

*Worked:* a building with a genuine NOI of $77,708 including a $4,000 reserve. Remove the reserve and
NOI appears to be $81,708. At 7.25% that is $1,127,007 rather than $1,071,834 — **$55,000 of apparent
value created by deleting one line**.

The second most common is presenting **market rent** as though it were **contract rent**. A building
with below-market leases is worth what its leases produce until they roll, and the difference is a
timing and risk question rather than a free upgrade.

The third, in Maine, is annualising a **seasonal** rental at its peak weekly rate. A cottage that
achieves $3,200 a week for ten weeks does not produce $166,400 a year.

### E5.4 A comparative market analysis, assembled

The method, which a Maine sales agent uses weekly and which must stay inside § 13251-A:

**Define the market area** by what actually changes buyer behaviour — the water body, the school
administrative unit, the town line that changes the mil rate, the road that is or is not plowed. A
ZIP code is a mail-delivery convenience, not a market area.

**Pull closed sales** within a defensible period — usually six to twelve months in a Maine market
where sales volume is thin, extended further only where the data requires it and the extension is
stated.

**Screen for arm's-length conditions.** Family sales, estate sales, foreclosure sales, and sales with
unusual seller financing are excluded or adjusted, and the exclusion is written down.

**Adjust toward the subject**, in the direction Hour 31 gave, and record every adjustment's basis.

**Reconcile to a range**, weighting by gross adjustment, recency and location — not by averaging.

**Disclose the limits** — how many sales, how old, what could not be verified, and what the analysis
did not consider.

**And describe the work accurately.** It is market evidence presented in the course of brokerage. It
is not an appraisal, it is not an opinion of market value as § 13251-A uses that phrase, and it must
not be titled as either.

### E5.5 The three approaches compared, in one table

| | Sales comparison | Cost | Income |
|---|---|---|---|
| Asks | What did substitutes sell for? | What would it cost to replace, less depreciation, plus land? | What income does it produce, and what is that worth? |
| Needs | Comparable closed sales | Reliable cost data and a land value | Reliable income and expense data and a market rate |
| Best for | Residential, owner-occupied | New construction; special purpose | Income property |
| Weakest when | Few or no comparables | Old improvements, where depreciation carries the answer | Owner-occupied property with no income |
| Maine complication | Thin sales volume; frontage and seasonality dominate | Seasonal building window; remote-site cost premiums | Seasonal rentals; municipal short-term rental rules |

### E5.6 Common valuation errors, and the correction

**Averaging the three approaches.** Reconciliation weights; it does not average.

**Using assessed value as evidence of market value.** It is an administrative figure produced on an
assessment cycle and worked to a certified ratio.

**Adjusting the subject.** Adjust the comparable, always.

**Treating a net adjustment as a measure of comparability.** Gross adjustment measures it.

**Using list prices as comparables.** They are unaccepted offers.

**Ignoring seasonality in a Maine market.** A February sale and a July sale are not evidence of the
same market, and the adjustment for market conditions has to carry it.

**Quoting a cap rate with no derivation.**

**Calling any of it an opinion of market value in a transaction the agency is paid on.** § 13251-A.

## Instructor source note for Module 05

Maine statements rest on text read directly from `legislature.maine.gov` on 2026-08-20 and recorded
in `maine/regulatory/primary-text-readings.md`: **32 M.R.S. § 13251-A**, quoted verbatim, and
**§ 13273 1 B (4)**. **§ 14004** is cited because § 13251-A cites it; **Title 32, chapter 124 has not
been read for this project**, and this course states no appraiser qualification, standard, or
definition from it.

**The scope of "appraisal or opinion of market value" is deliberately left as a legal question.**
Hour 34.2 states what § 13251-A prohibits, states that presenting market evidence in the course of
brokerage is what §§ 13273 and 13274 expect, and routes anything near the line to the designated
broker. Drawing that line precisely would require reading § 14004 and the Commission's guidance, and
neither has been read here.

**Appraisal method is taught because the Commission's outline requires it** — Property Valuation and
Appraisal is 9 percent of the course — and because a Maine licensee uses the method to present market
evidence. It is not taught as appraisal practice, and Maine licenses appraisers under a separate
chapter.

All arithmetic is worked in full so a reviewer can verify each figure. **The prices, rates,
adjustment amounts, capitalization rates and multipliers are illustrative teaching figures and are
not market data for any Maine market.** The $1,200 per foot of frontage in Hour 31.3 in particular is
a teaching number; real frontage adjustments vary enormously by water body, exposure, and shoreline
character, and are derived from paired sales.

**The seasonal-rental treatment in Hour 32.4 is stated as analytical practice**, and the observation
that many Maine municipalities now regulate short-term rentals is stated as a question for the
municipality. No municipal registration requirement or restriction is stated here.
