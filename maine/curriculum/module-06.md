# Maine Sales Agent Course — Module 06

## Hours 35–39 · Financing the transaction

**Commission topic covered:** Financing the Transaction (9%, 270 instructional minutes) — all 270 of them, ending at Hour 39 minute 30, where Module 07 begins.
**Course version:** 1.0 submission draft
**Regulatory status:** Not approved. No Maine sales agent credit is available for this course.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module the student will be able to:

- Describe the primary and secondary mortgage markets and name the participants.
- Distinguish the note from the mortgage, name the clauses that decide outcomes, and say why a Maine
  mortgage is not a deed of trust.
- Match a borrower or property scenario to a programme category without representing eligibility.
- Compute loan-to-value, points, interest, a payment from a factor, and qualifying ratios.
- Identify the consumer-protection statutes that bear on a residential loan and the signs of
  predatory lending and mortgage fraud.

## Hour 35 — Sources of funds and the market structure

### 35.1 The primary market

The **primary mortgage market** is where loans are originated: banks, savings institutions, **credit
unions**, mortgage bankers, and mortgage brokers. In Maine, credit unions and community banks
originate a substantial share of residential lending, and small local institutions are more likely to
hold loans in **portfolio** rather than sell them.

That matters practically. A **portfolio lender** sets its own underwriting standards and may lend on
property a secondary-market lender will not — a seasonal camp, an unconventional water supply, an
island property, a large acreage parcel, a property with no comparable sales. When a buyer's property
is unusual, the answer is often a local portfolio lender, and a licensee who knows that serves their
client without ever advising on credit.

### 35.2 The secondary market

The **secondary mortgage market** is where originated loans are sold. **Fannie Mae** and **Freddie
Mac** purchase conforming conventional loans; **Ginnie Mae** guarantees securities backed by
government loans. Selling loans returns capital to the originator so it can lend again, and it is why
underwriting guidelines exist at all: a loan that does not meet the purchaser's criteria cannot be
sold.

**MaineHousing** — the Maine State Housing Authority — operates first-time homebuyer and other
programmes through participating lenders. Its programmes have their own eligibility criteria and
change; a licensee names the programme and refers the buyer to a participating lender.

### 35.3 The participants

**Loan originator**, licensed under the federal SAFE Act framework and state law. **Processor**.
**Underwriter**. **Appraiser**. **Title and closing agent** — in Maine, frequently an attorney.
**Mortgage insurer** for conventional loans with a small down payment. **Servicer**, who collects
payments after closing and who is very often not the originator.

A licensee's role among them is coordination and deadline management, and nothing else. The licensee
does not complete the application, does not advise on credit, and does not receive anything of value
for a referral — which is Hour 39's RESPA point.

## Hour 36 — Notes, mortgages, and the Maine instrument

### 36.1 Two documents, two jobs

Every secured loan has two instruments, and confusing them is the most common error in this half of
the course.

**The promissory note** is the **debt** — the borrower's personal promise to repay, with the rate,
the payment, the term, late charges, prepayment terms, default and acceleration. It is a negotiable
instrument and it can be sold, which is what happens on the secondary market. **It is not recorded.**

**The mortgage** is the **collateral** — it pledges the real property as security for the note and
creates the lien. **It is recorded**, at the Registry of Deeds.

Pay off the note and the mortgage must be **discharged** of record. A recorded but undischarged
mortgage is one of the most common clouds a Maine title examination reveals.

### 36.2 Maine uses a mortgage, not a deed of trust

A **mortgage** has **two parties**: the **mortgagor**, who is the borrower and who gives the
mortgage, and the **mortgagee**, who is the lender.

A **deed of trust** has three — borrower, trustee, and lender — and the trustee conducts a sale on
default under a power of sale.

**Maine is a mortgage state**, and residential foreclosure in Maine is **judicial**: the lender
brings an action in court. A student who has learned "grantor, trustee, beneficiary" from a national
text, or from this project's Virginia course, must translate.

The practical consequences are real. Judicial foreclosure is slower, it produces a court record, and
Maine imposes procedural requirements on residential foreclosure — including notice requirements and
a **mediation programme** for owner-occupied residential property. **This course states no notice
period, no redemption period, and no mediation rule**, because those provisions of **14 M.R.S.** have
not been read from primary text for this project. A licensee meeting a distressed property routes the
borrower to counsel and to a **HUD-approved housing counselling agency**, and says nothing about
timing.

### 36.3 The clauses that decide outcomes

**Acceleration** — on default the lender may declare the whole balance due. Without it a lender could
sue only for missed payments, and every foreclosure begins here.

**Alienation, or due-on-sale** — the lender may accelerate if the property is transferred without
consent. This is what makes most modern loans **non-assumable**, and why "taking over payments" is
usually not available.

**Prepayment** — whether the borrower may pay early and on what terms.

**Defeasance** — on full payment the mortgage is defeated and must be **discharged**.

**Subordination** — an agreement to take a lower lien position than recording order would give.
Refinancing a first mortgage while a home equity line remains requires it, or the equity line would
advance to first position.

**Escrow or impound** — the lender collects taxes and insurance monthly and pays them when due,
protecting its own collateral against a tax lien or an uninsured loss.

### 36.4 Contract for deed and other structures

A **contract for deed** — also called an installment land contract or bond for a deed — leaves legal
title with the seller until payment is complete while the buyer takes possession and equitable title.
Maine regulates these arrangements, and this course states no requirement from that regime because it
has not been read from primary text for this project. A licensee who meets one routes it to counsel
before anything is signed.

**Assumption** — the buyer takes over the existing loan and becomes personally liable; the seller is
released **only** by a written release from the lender. **Subject to** — the buyer takes title with
the loan in place but assumes no personal liability, the seller stays liable, and the due-on-sale
clause is very likely triggered.

**Seller financing** — the seller takes back a note secured by a mortgage. Federal rules constrain
seller financing of owner-occupied residential property, and every version of it is a legal and tax
structure. The licensee's role is to say so.

## Hour 37 — Programme categories

### 37.1 The one rule for this hour

**Programme guidelines change, sometimes several times a year, and eligibility depends on facts the
licensee does not have.** A licensee describes **categories**; a lender determines **eligibility**.

Telling a buyer "you'll qualify" is a representation the licensee cannot support, and under
§ 13273 1 C the duty is to exercise reasonable skill and care.

### 37.2 Conventional

Not insured or guaranteed by a government agency. **Conforming** loans meet the purchase criteria of
Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, including a maximum loan amount that is set annually and varies by
county; **non-conforming** loans do not, and **jumbo** loans exceed the limit.

**Private mortgage insurance** is generally required where the down payment is under 20 percent, and
it protects **the lender**, not the borrower — the most persistent consumer misunderstanding in
residential lending. Federal law provides for cancellation and automatic termination of borrower-paid
PMI on the basis of the loan's amortisation; the thresholds are the lender's and the statute's to
state.

### 37.3 FHA

The **Federal Housing Administration** **insures** loans made by approved lenders; it does not lend.
Characteristic features: a low minimum down payment; more flexible credit and ratio criteria;
**mortgage insurance premiums**, upfront and annual; **maximum loan amounts that vary by county**;
and a requirement that the property meet **minimum property standards** and be appraised by an
FHA-approved appraiser. FHA loans are for **owner-occupants**.

The Maine consequence worth naming: an FHA appraisal serves the insurer's interest in the property's
condition as well as its value, and older Maine housing — with knob-and-tube wiring, peeling paint on
a pre-1978 house, an unpermitted septic system, or a failing sill — may need repair before the loan
can close. That is a real scheduling consequence for a seller.

### 37.4 VA

The **Department of Veterans Affairs** **guarantees** a portion of a loan made by an approved lender
to an eligible veteran, service member, or surviving spouse. No down payment in many cases; no monthly
mortgage insurance; a **funding fee** that varies and is waived in defined circumstances; entitlement
established by a **certificate of eligibility**; a VA appraisal producing a notice of value; and
**occupancy** requirements.

A licensee never tells a veteran whether they are eligible, how much entitlement they have, or
whether the funding fee is waived.

### 37.5 Rural Development

The **US Department of Agriculture** operates rural housing programmes, guaranteeing loans made by
approved lenders and, in some programmes, lending directly. No down payment; **geographic
eligibility** limited to designated rural areas defined by USDA maps that change; **household income
limits** by area and household size; owner-occupancy; and guarantee and annual fees.

**A very large part of Maine is within designated rural areas**, which makes this programme far more
relevant here than in most states — and makes the two eligibility gates, **the property's location**
and **the household's income**, worth checking early. Both are checked against USDA's own tools, and
both change.

### 37.6 MaineHousing and construction lending

**MaineHousing** operates first-time homebuyer programmes through participating lenders, with their
own eligibility criteria, purchase price limits and income limits, all of which change. Name the
programme; refer to a participating lender.

**Construction lending** is short-term, disbursed in **draws** against inspected progress, with
interest paid on the drawn balance, converting to or being replaced by permanent financing at
completion. In Maine the seasonal building window compresses the schedule, and a construction
timeline that assumes year-round work is a timeline that will slip.

## Hour 38 — The arithmetic

### 38.1 Loan-to-value and down payment

**LTV = loan amount ÷ the lesser of sale price or appraised value.**

*Price $340,000, loan $272,000.* LTV = 272,000 ÷ 340,000 = **80%**; down payment **$68,000**.

*Price $340,000, appraisal $325,000, lender lends 80% LTV.* The lender lends against the **lower**
figure: 0.80 × 325,000 = **$260,000**. The buyer must bring 340,000 − 260,000 = **$80,000** — the
$68,000 planned plus a **$15,000 appraisal gap**. This is the whole reason the appraisal contingency
exists.

### 38.2 Points and interest

**One point = 1% of the loan amount**, paid at closing. Discount points buy the rate down; an
origination fee compensates the lender for making the loan.

*Loan $272,000, 1.25 points:* 272,000 × 0.0125 = **$3,400**.

**Simple interest: I = P × R × T.** *$272,000 at 6.5% for one month:* 272,000 × 0.065 = $17,680 per
year; ÷ 12 = **$1,473.33**.

**Per diem interest at closing:** annual interest ÷ 365 × days. *Closing 18 September, first payment
1 November:* interest for 18–30 September, 13 days. 17,680 ÷ 365 = $48.44 per day; × 13 =
**$629.72**.

### 38.3 Payment and amortisation

A **payment factor** is the monthly payment per $1,000 of loan at a given rate and term.

*Factor 6.32 per $1,000, loan $272,000:* 272 × 6.32 = **$1,719.04** monthly principal and interest.
First month's interest is $1,473.33, so principal = 1,719.04 − 1,473.33 = **$245.71**, and the
balance after one payment is **$271,754.29**.

The payment is constant; its composition changes. Interest falls and principal rises across the term.

**PITI** adds taxes and insurance to principal and interest, plus mortgage insurance and any
association dues the lender includes in qualifying.

### 38.4 Qualifying ratios

**Front-end (housing) ratio = monthly housing expense ÷ gross monthly income.**
**Back-end (total debt) ratio = total monthly obligations ÷ gross monthly income.**

*Gross monthly income $7,400; PITI $2,090; other obligations $540.*
Front-end: 2,090 ÷ 7,400 = **28.2%**. Back-end: 2,630 ÷ 7,400 = **35.5%**.

**The thresholds are the lender's and the programme's, and they change. This course states none.** A
licensee computes a ratio to help a buyer think; the lender decides.

### 38.5 Cash to close

Cash to close = down payment + closing costs + prepaid items + escrow reserves − earnest money −
seller credits − lender credits.

*Worked:* price $340,000; down payment $68,000; closing costs $5,900; prepaid interest $629.72;
first-year homeowner premium $1,640; escrow reserves $1,310; earnest money $5,000; seller credit
$2,500.
68,000 + 5,900 + 629.72 + 1,640 + 1,310 = 77,479.72; less 5,000 and 2,500 = **$69,979.72**.

**This is an estimate.** The controlling figures are the lender's Closing Disclosure and the closing
agent's statement, and every estimate a licensee gives should say so in writing.

### 38.6 Credit

Credit reports and scores are produced by consumer reporting agencies and are governed by the **Fair
Credit Reporting Act**, which also requires an **adverse action notice** where credit is denied. A
licensee does not read, interpret, or advise on a buyer's credit, and should never tell a buyer to
pay off a collection, close an account, or move money without the loan officer's instruction —
because each of those can lower a score or create an undocumented deposit at exactly the wrong
moment.

## Hour 39 (0–30) — Lender requirements, predatory lending, and fraud

### 39.1 What the lender requires before it funds

**Title** examined and insured, with Schedule B-I requirements satisfied. **Hazard insurance** bound
effective the closing date, and **flood insurance** where the property is in a Special Flood Hazard
Area — a live question along Maine's coast and rivers. **Appraisal** supporting the value.
**Survey**, where required. **Conditions** cleared — prior-to-document conditions before the closing
package issues, prior-to-funding conditions before money moves.

The condition raised late is what breaks a closing date. A condition raised on Tuesday for a Thursday
closing is a condition that cannot be met in time, which is why they are chased weekly, in writing.

### 39.2 The consumer-protection layer

**TILA and Regulation Z** — disclosure of the cost of credit, principally the **annual percentage
rate**, and control of credit advertising.

**RESPA and Regulation X** — the settlement process. Two prohibitions reach a licensee personally:
**section 8(a)**, no fee, kickback or thing of value pursuant to an agreement or understanding for
the **referral** of settlement service business; and **section 8(b)**, no **unearned fees** for
services not actually performed. **Affiliated business arrangements** are permitted where disclosed
in the prescribed form, the referral is not required, and the only thing of value received is a
return on ownership interest.

A "marketing agreement" that is in substance payment for referrals is a violation whatever it is
called, and gifts, event sponsorship and desk rentals between settlement service providers and
licensees are where enforcement concentrates.

**TRID** — the **Loan Estimate**, delivered shortly after application, and the **Closing
Disclosure**, which must be received by the borrower a defined period before consummation. Certain
late changes **re-start** that period, which is why a change to the loan two days before closing can
move the closing date. No period is stated here; the lender states it.

**ECOA and Regulation B** — no discrimination in credit on the basis of race, colour, religion,
national origin, sex, marital status, age (where the applicant has capacity to contract), receipt of
public assistance income, or the good faith exercise of consumer credit rights.

**HMDA**, **FCRA**, the **SAFE Act**, and **Dodd-Frank's ability-to-repay and qualified mortgage
standards** complete the frame.

### 39.3 Predatory lending

Patterns to recognise: lending without regard to ability to repay; **equity stripping**; **loan
flipping** — repeated refinancing generating fees without benefit; packing loans with unnecessary
products; steering a qualified borrower to a higher-priced product; undisclosed balloon payments; and
pressure to sign without reading.

The licensee does not diagnose. The licensee **notices, says what has been noticed, and routes** — to
a HUD-approved housing counselling agency, to counsel, or to another lender for comparison — and
stays out of any arrangement in which their own benefit depends on the borrower choosing a particular
lender.

### 39.4 Mortgage fraud

**Fraud for property** is misrepresentation to obtain a loan the borrower intends to repay — inflated
income, an occupancy claim that is not true, an undisclosed gift dressed as savings. **Fraud for
profit** is organised: straw buyers, inflated appraisals, silent seconds, flipping schemes.

A licensee is exposed by participating in any of it, however peripherally. Two rules: **never sign,
initial, or forward a document you know to be inaccurate**, and **never characterise a buyer's
occupancy intention for them**. And Maine's own conflict rule sits beside it — **§ 13251-A** bars a
broker from providing an appraisal or opinion of market value where any licensee in the agency is to
receive a fee on the transaction, which is precisely the situation an inflated-value scheme needs.

### Module 06 engagement check

**Question.** A buyer wants a year-round camp on an island, reached by boat, with a dug well, a
composting toilet, and no comparable sales in the past three years. They ask which loan to apply for
and whether the property "will appraise". Answer.

**Expected answer.** I identify the **categories** and refer; I do not advise on either question.

The property is unusual in three ways that matter to underwriting: **access** — an island property
reached by boat raises marketability and insurance questions; **water and wastewater** — a dug well
and a composting toilet may not meet a secondary-market lender's property requirements, and FHA in
particular applies **minimum property standards**; and **no comparable sales**, which is an appraisal
problem before it is a lending one.

The category most likely to work is a **local portfolio lender** — a Maine bank or credit union that
holds loans rather than selling them and therefore sets its own property standards. That is a
category, not a recommendation of any institution, and the buyer must speak to lenders directly.

**Whether it will appraise is not mine to say**, and saying it would be a representation I cannot
support. Under **§ 13273 1 B (4)**, if the buyer is my client, I am required to **advise them to
obtain expert advice on material matters beyond my expertise** — and under **§ 13251-A** I could not
provide an opinion of market value on this transaction in any event, because my agency is to be paid
a fee on it. I put the referrals in writing and I diary the financing and appraisal deadlines,
because on a property like this they will be tight.

## Extended treatment — finance developed

### E6.1 Repayment structures compared

| Structure | Payment behaviour | Principal | Risk carried by the borrower |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fully amortising fixed | Constant | Retired over the term | None from rate movement |
| Adjustable rate | Changes at each adjustment | Retired over the term | **Rate movement**, bounded by caps |
| Interest only | Constant during the period | **None** during the period | No equity from amortisation |
| Balloon | Constant, then the balance falls due | Partly retired | **Refinance or sale risk at maturity** |
| Graduated payment | Rises on a schedule | May negatively amortise early | Payment shock |
| Buydown, temporary | Rises to the note rate | Normal | Payment increase at the step |
| Reverse | None required | Grows | Occupancy, tax, insurance and maintenance obligations continue |

The question a buyer needs answered about any of them is not the start rate but **what the payment
becomes**, and that is the lender's to answer.

### E6.2 An amortisation schedule, first three months

*Loan $272,000 at 6.5%, monthly payment $1,719.04.*

| Month | Opening balance | Interest | Principal | Closing balance |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | $272,000.00 | $1,473.33 | $245.71 | $271,754.29 |
| 2 | $271,754.29 | $1,472.00 | $247.04 | $271,507.25 |
| 3 | $271,507.25 | $1,470.66 | $248.38 | $271,258.87 |

Three months of payments totalling $5,157.12 have retired **$741.13** of principal. That arithmetic
is what a buyer means when they say the early years "all go to interest", and it is worth showing
rather than asserting — particularly to a buyer weighing points against a short expected holding
period.

### E6.3 The buyer behaviours that break an approved loan

Give this list in writing at ratification, because every item on it happens:

Do not change or leave a job. Do not open credit accounts, and do not finance furniture, a vehicle,
or appliances. Do not close accounts or pay off collections without asking the loan officer first —
either can move a score. Do not make large deposits that cannot be documented; a gift needs a gift
letter and a paper trail. Do not move money between accounts unnecessarily. Do not co-sign for
anyone. Do not miss a payment on anything.

Underwriting is frequently re-verified immediately before closing, so a purchase made a week before
closing can undo an approval given a month earlier.

### E6.4 Managing the financing contingency

Read the clause and extract four things: **the loan type and amount** it protects; **the deadline**
for the commitment; **what notice** must be given and to whom; and **what happens** if the deadline
passes without action — which under many forms is that the protection is lost.

Then work backwards. Application within the contract's period. Documentation to the lender
immediately. Appraisal ordered early enough that a reconsideration of value is possible. Conditions
chased **weekly, in writing**, with a record of each request and response. And escalate to the
designated broker the moment a deadline is at risk.

On a Maine property with a private well, a septic system, or an unusual structure, add one more:
**find out early whether the lender's product has property standards the property will not meet**,
because discovering an FHA minimum property standard problem two weeks before closing is discovering
it too late.

## Instructor source note for Module 06

Maine statements rest on text read directly from `legislature.maine.gov` on 2026-08-20 and recorded
in `maine/regulatory/primary-text-readings.md`: **32 M.R.S. §§ 13251-A and 13273 1 B (4) and 1 C**.

**That Maine is a mortgage state with judicial residential foreclosure** is stated as the structural
fact it is, and the hour translates the deed-of-trust vocabulary a student may have met elsewhere.
**No Maine foreclosure notice period, redemption period, or mediation requirement is stated**,
because those provisions of **14 M.R.S.** have not been read from primary text for this project.
Likewise **no requirement is stated from Maine's regulation of contract-for-deed arrangements**.

**Federal programme features are stated as programme characteristics, not as eligibility.** No
conforming loan limit, FHA county limit, mortgage insurance duration, VA funding fee, PMI
cancellation threshold, USDA income or geographic limit, MaineHousing purchase price or income limit,
TRID waiting period, or qualifying-ratio threshold is given. Each changes on its own schedule, and
Hour 37.1 states the rule the module is built on: the licensee describes categories, the lender
determines eligibility.

**MaineHousing is named as a programme operator**, and no eligibility criterion of any of its
programmes is stated.

All arithmetic in Hour 38 is worked in full. Prices, rates and factors are illustrative.

The observation in Hour 35.1 that Maine portfolio lenders will consider property a secondary-market
lender will not is stated as **market practice**, not as a rule, and Hour 39's engagement check turns
on identifying it as a category to explore rather than as advice.
