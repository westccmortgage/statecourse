# Maine Sales Agent Course — Module 04

## Hours 23–29 · Product knowledge II: Maine land use, environment, transfer, and construction

**Commission topic covered:** Product Knowledge (28%, 840 instructional minutes) — this module
carries the remaining 390, and includes Case Study 1.
**Course version:** 1.0 submission draft
**Regulatory status:** Not approved. No Maine sales agent credit is available for this course.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module the student will be able to:

- Name the Maine land-use regimes a property may sit under and say which office administers each.
- Identify the environmental conditions a Maine transaction commonly raises and route each.
- Explain Maine's current-use tax classifications and the withdrawal risk they carry.
- Distinguish voluntary from involuntary transfer and read a chain of title for gaps and clouds.
- Name the information source that answers each Maine due-diligence question.
- Describe residential construction well enough to report a condition accurately without diagnosing
  it.

## Hour 23 — Maine land-use statutes

### 23.1 Who regulates what

**Organised municipalities** regulate land use under authority delegated by **30-A M.R.S.**, through
a comprehensive plan, a zoning ordinance, a subdivision ordinance, a shoreland zoning ordinance, and
building and plumbing administration. Practice varies substantially between municipalities, and
several Maine towns have no zoning at all — which is itself a fact a buyer needs to know.

The **Land Use Planning Commission** is the planning and zoning authority for Maine's **unorganised
and deorganised areas**, plantations, and townships — roughly half the state's land area. Where the
LUPC has jurisdiction, it performs the functions a municipality would.

The **Department of Environmental Protection** administers the state environmental permitting
regimes that sit above municipal control.

### 23.2 Subdivision

Maine regulates subdivision at two levels.

**Municipal subdivision review** under 30-A M.R.S. applies where land is divided into the statutory
number of lots within a defined period. The review runs through the planning board against criteria
in the statute and the local ordinance, and it ends in a **recorded plan**.

**State-level review** applies to larger developments through the DEP's site location of development
programme and the stormwater and natural resources protection permitting regimes.

**This course states no lot count, no acreage threshold, and no time period**, because the
subdivision provisions of **30-A M.R.S.** and the DEP's thresholds have not been read from primary
text for this project. The reason to be careful here is specific: **an unlawful subdivision is a
title and marketability problem**, and a licensee who tells a seller that dividing a lot "should be
fine" has stated a conclusion with real consequences.

The one rule that carries: **a lot does not exist until a plan creating it is recorded**, and where
subdivision review applies, it must have been completed first.

### 23.3 Shoreland zoning

Maine requires municipalities to adopt **shoreland zoning** meeting state minimum guidelines, and
the LUPC administers equivalent standards in its jurisdiction. Shoreland zoning applies within a
defined distance of the normal high-water line of great ponds, rivers, and coastal wetlands, and
within a distance of the upland edge of freshwater wetlands.

What it controls, in outline: **setbacks** from the water; **lot size and frontage**; **vegetative
buffers and tree cutting**, which is the provision owners breach most often and least knowingly;
**expansion, replacement and relocation of nonconforming structures**; **new construction and land
disturbance**; and **subsurface wastewater**.

The practical consequences for brokerage are large. A very substantial part of Maine's waterfront
housing stock is **nonconforming** as to setback. Whether such a structure may be expanded, replaced
after a fire, or demolished and rebuilt is answered by the municipality's ordinance and the state
minimum standards — and the answer differs between municipalities. **Clearing a view** frequently
requires a permit and can produce enforcement and civil penalties. And a buyer's plan for a
property, if it depends on any of this, must be confirmed **in writing with the code enforcement
officer** before the contingency period ends.

**This course states no setback distance, no shoreland zone width, and no buffer standard.** Those
are in the state minimum guidelines and in each municipality's ordinance, neither of which has been
read from primary text for this project.

### 23.4 Minimum lot size and wastewater

Minimum lot size in Maine is driven as much by **subsurface wastewater disposal** as by zoning. A
lot must have soils capable of supporting a septic system sized to the proposed use, established by
a **site evaluation** performed by a licensed site evaluator and permitted through the local
plumbing inspector. A physically attractive lot with unsuitable soils is not a buildable lot.

Where public sewer is available, the question becomes capacity and connection permission — and
"available" is not the same as "permitted".

### 23.5 Other regimes a property may sit under

**Natural resources protection** for work in, on or adjacent to protected natural resources.
**Stormwater** permitting for disturbed area above thresholds. **Erosion control**. **Flood hazard**
regulation through municipal ordinances tied to the federal mapping. **Historic district** review
where adopted. **Right-to-farm** and forestry provisions that limit nuisance claims against existing
agricultural and forestry operations. And **road access**, where a driveway or entrance onto a state
or state-aid highway needs a **Department of Transportation entrance permit**.

Each is named here so a licensee recognises the question. None is answered here.

## Hour 24 — Environmental issues in Maine transactions

### 24.1 Lead-based paint

Federal law applies in Maine and is not optional. For **target housing** — most residential housing
built **before 1978** — a seller or landlord must disclose known lead-based paint and hazards,
provide available records and reports, give the EPA-approved pamphlet, include the prescribed
warning and acknowledgments in the contract or lease, and, **for sales, give the purchaser a
ten-day opportunity to conduct a risk assessment or inspection** unless the parties agree in writing
to a different period.

**Maine's housing stock is substantially pre-1978**, so this is a routine requirement rather than an
occasional one. **33 M.R.S. § 173(4)(B)** also brings lead-based paint for pre-1978 homes into the
Maine property disclosure statement expressly.

Maine additionally regulates lead in rental housing and in renovation work, and the federal
Renovation, Repair and Painting rule requires certified firms for disturbing painted surfaces in
target housing. A licensee's role is to ensure the sale-side requirements are met and the
acknowledgment retained, and to route the rest.

### 24.2 Radon

Radon is common in Maine soils and Maine well water, and it appears in the **§ 173(4)(C)** disclosure
list. It is measured by testing, and mitigation is a defined trade. Maine has legislated on radon in
rental housing; that provision has not been read from primary text for this project and no
requirement from it is stated here.

For a buyer the professional advice is straightforward: **test, in both air and — on a private well —
water**, within the inspection period.

### 24.3 Arsenic and other private-well contaminants

Naturally occurring **arsenic** in bedrock is a well-documented Maine groundwater issue, alongside
**uranium**, **radon in water**, **manganese**, **fluoride**, **nitrates**, and **bacteria**. Water
from a private well is not tested by any public authority; it is tested because someone paid for it.

**§ 173(1)** requires the seller to disclose, for a private supply, the type, location, any
malfunctions, **the date of the most recent water test**, and **whether the seller has experienced a
problem such as an unsatisfactory water test or a test with notations.** A blank in that field is
information, and it is a question for the buyer's investigation rather than an answer.

### 24.4 Subsurface wastewater and shoreland septic

**§ 173(3)** requires detailed septic disclosure — type, tank size and type and location,
malfunctions, installation dates for tank and leach field, most recent servicing and the contractor —
and, **for systems within shoreland zones, the disclosures required by 30-A M.R.S. § 4216.**

The recurring problems: systems older than their design life; systems whose permits cannot be found;
systems sized for a two-bedroom camp being bought by a family who intend four bedrooms; and
**cesspools and other non-conforming systems**, which frequently cannot be replaced in the same
location under current setbacks.

### 24.5 Heating oil and underground tanks

**§ 173(4)(D)** brings **underground oil storage tanks as required under 38 M.R.S. § 563(6)** into
the disclosure statement.

Buried tanks are a Maine legacy issue. An abandoned tank that was never properly removed or closed
is an environmental liability that attaches to the property, and above-ground tanks have their own
age, condition, and insurance consequences. **The heating disclosure under § 173(2-A)** — type, age,
servicing company, most recent service call, **annual fuel consumption**, malfunctions within two
years, and **most recent chimney and vent inspection** — is one of the most informative sections of
the whole statement, and annual fuel consumption is the line a buyer should read first.

### 24.6 Asbestos, mould, PFAS, and wetlands

**Asbestos** — § 173(4)(A) — in insulation, flooring, siding and pipe wrap in older housing.

**Mould** — a condition question, assessed by a qualified assessor. A licensee never characterises a
stain.

**PFAS** — per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances have become a significant Maine issue, particularly
where **licensed sludge or septage was historically spread on farmland**. The State has an
investigation and testing programme, and affected properties may have well contamination and
restrictions on agricultural use. This is a live and developing area, it is a question for the
Maine Department of Environmental Protection and the Department of Agriculture, Conservation and
Forestry, and **a licensee must not characterise a property's PFAS status**. Where a property has
been farmed, or is near land that has been, the question belongs in the buyer's investigation list.

**Wetlands and flood plains** — presence, mapping, and the permitting consequences of any proposed
work. Both are answered by mapping, by the DEP or LUPC, and by the municipality.

### 24.7 The rule that governs all of it

The licensee is not the person who determines an environmental condition. The licensee is the person
who **knows the question exists, discloses what is actually known, and routes the question to the
person who answers it** — under § 13273 1 B (4) for a client, and within the § 13283 3 prohibitions
for a transaction broker.

## Hour 25 — Taxation, exemptions, current use, and transfer

### 25.1 Property tax

Maine property tax is levied by the **municipality** on assessed value, at a rate set annually. It
is administered by the municipal **assessor**, and Maine Revenue Services administers the
unorganised territory and the state-level programmes.

**Assessed value is not market value**, and a licensee must never present it as evidence of value.
Maine assessors work to a **certified ratio**, and the relationship between assessment and market is
itself a stated figure in the municipality's commitment.

### 25.2 Exemptions

Maine's exemption programmes — the **homestead exemption**, the **veteran exemption**, the
**blind exemption**, and the property tax relief programmes the Legislature adjusts periodically —
are applied for by the owner and are administered by the assessor.

**This course states no exemption amount and no eligibility rule.** Both change, they are set in
**36 M.R.S.**, and the applicant's own circumstances decide. A licensee tells a client the programmes
exist and directs them to the assessor and to Maine Revenue Services.

### 25.3 Current-use classification — the Maine trap

Maine's current-use programmes — **Tree Growth**, **Farmland**, **Open Space**, and **Working
Waterfront** — value enrolled land at its current use rather than its market value, which
substantially reduces the tax.

The consequence a buyer must understand before closing: **withdrawal from the programme triggers a
penalty**, and for Tree Growth in particular that penalty can be very large. Whether the buyer
intends to continue the classification, whether the land qualifies under their intended use, and who
bears any withdrawal penalty are **contract terms**, not afterthoughts.

**This course states no penalty formula and no eligibility acreage.** They are in **36 M.R.S.** and
have not been read from primary text for this project. What a licensee must do is: identify from the
assessor's records whether the land is enrolled; disclose it; and get the buyer to the assessor,
Maine Revenue Services, a forester where Tree Growth is involved, and counsel — in writing, before
the contingency period ends.

### 25.4 Transfer tax and liens

Maine imposes a **real estate transfer tax** on conveyances, collected at the Registry of Deeds on a
declaration of value, and conventionally shared between the parties. **This course states no rate**;
it is in 36 M.R.S. and the closing agent computes it.

**Property tax liens** attach for unpaid taxes and are enforced by the municipality through a
statutory lien and foreclosure process on its own timetable. Unpaid taxes are discovered from the
assessor and the tax collector, and they are paid at closing.

**Other liens** — mortgages, judgment liens, mechanics' liens for labour and materials, state tax
liens, and municipal sewer and water liens — are found by the title examination and cleared at
closing.

### 25.5 Voluntary and involuntary transfer

**Voluntary:** by **deed**, in life; by **will**, at death, to a devisee; by **gift**.

**Involuntary:** by **intestate succession** under Title 18-C where there is no will; by
**foreclosure**; by **tax lien foreclosure**; by **eminent domain**; by **adverse possession**; by
**escheat**; and by **partition**.

Each appears in a chain of title as something other than an ordinary deed, and each carries its own
questions about authority — did the personal representative have power to sell, was the foreclosure
completed properly, was the tax lien matured. Those are the title examiner's and counsel's
questions, and they are why a Maine closing runs through an attorney.

## Hour 26 — Recording, deeds, covenants, and title defects

### 26.1 The Registry of Deeds

Maine records land instruments in the **county Registry of Deeds**. Recording gives **constructive
notice to the world**, which is what allows a stranger to a transaction to discover what interests
exist in a parcel without asking anyone.

**Recording does not transfer title.** Title passes on **delivery and acceptance** of the deed.
Recording protects the grantee against later claims, which is why it is done immediately.

### 26.2 The deed

A valid deed requires a **competent grantor**; an **identifiable grantee**; a recital of
**consideration**; **words of conveyance**; an adequate **legal description**; the **grantor's
signature**; and **delivery and acceptance**. **Acknowledgment** before a notary or other authorised
officer is what makes it recordable.

**The grantee does not sign a deed.**

Maine deed types by what the grantor promises:

**Warranty deed** — warrants title against all defects, whenever arising. The norm in Maine
residential resale.

**Quitclaim deed with covenant** — a Maine form that warrants against defects arising **during the
grantor's ownership**, and which functions as a limited or special warranty deed. **This is a Maine
usage that misleads people from other states**, where "quitclaim" means no warranty at all.

**Release deed**, sometimes called a quitclaim without covenant — conveys whatever the grantor has
and warrants nothing. A curative instrument.

**Fiduciary deeds** — personal representative's, trustee's, guardian's, and deeds from a
municipality after tax foreclosure — each carrying the authority under which the signer acts.

**In Maine the deed is prepared by an attorney.** Selecting between deed forms is legal advice, and a
licensee asked "which deed?" has one correct answer: your attorney or the closing agent, and here is
how to reach them.

### 26.3 Chain of title, gaps, clouds

A **chain of title** is the succession of recorded owners. A searcher works backward from the current
owner and then forward again, checking each ownership period for liens and adverse instruments.

A **gap** is a break — a conveyance out from someone who never appears as a grantee, often an
unrecorded deed, an unrecorded probate, or a name change. A **cloud** is a recorded instrument or
claim casting doubt without necessarily defeating title.

**Marketable title** is title a reasonable, well-informed purchaser would accept — not perfect title,
but title free from reasonable doubt and the threat of litigation.

Maine has a **Marketable Title Act** in **33 M.R.S.** which extinguishes certain ancient interests
after a statutory period. **This course states no period from it**, because it has not been read from
primary text for this project.

### 26.4 Title insurance

Title insurance is **backward-looking indemnity** against defects existing at the policy date and not
excepted. One premium, paid at closing.

An **owner's policy** insures the buyer for the purchase price; a **lender's policy** insures the
lender for the loan amount, declining as it is repaid. **A lender's policy does not protect the
buyer**, and a buyer who pays for one and declines the other has bought protection for someone else.

A **commitment** carries **Schedule A** facts, **Schedule B-I requirements** — actions that must be
completed before the policy issues — and **Schedule B-II exceptions** — risks the insurer declines,
commonly including rights of parties in possession and **matters an accurate survey would show**.
**Exclusions** are printed categories, including zoning and eminent domain.

**Title insurance covers no physical condition of the property.** Buyers conflate the two constantly.

### 26.5 Covenants in the record

Restrictive covenants, easements, and reservations discovered in the chain bind successors whether
or not anyone mentioned them at the showing. Maine specifics that recur: **rights of way to water**;
**private road maintenance obligations**; **timber and mineral reservations**; **conservation
easements**, which are common in Maine and permanently limit development; and **historic
discriminatory covenants**, which are void, must not be honoured, and must not be repeated as though
they operated.

## Hour 27 — Where the answers live

### 27.1 The Registry of Deeds

Deeds, mortgages and discharges, easements, restrictive covenants, recorded plans, foreclosure
documents, and lis pendens. Indexed by grantor and grantee. This is where **what is recorded** is
found — and only that.

### 27.2 The municipal office

**Code enforcement officer** — zoning district and requirements, permits issued, shoreland status,
nonconforming status, open violations, and the certificate of occupancy where one issues. The single
most useful office in a Maine transaction.

**Assessor** — assessed value, the certified ratio, tax status, acreage and mapping, exemptions, and
**current-use classification** including Tree Growth, Farmland and Open Space.

**Tax collector** — what is owed and whether any lien has matured.

**Plumbing inspector** — subsurface wastewater permits, the septic design on file, and internal
plumbing permits.

**Town clerk and planning board** — ordinances, comprehensive plan, subdivision approvals, board of
appeals decisions, and minutes.

**Road commissioner or public works** — whether a road is public, private, or a discontinued way, and
whether it is plowed in winter. This question decides more Maine purchases than people expect.

### 27.3 State agencies

**Department of Environmental Protection** — natural resources protection, stormwater, site location
of development, PFAS programmes, underground tanks, and shoreland guidance.

**Land Use Planning Commission** — the planning and zoning authority in the unorganised territory.

**Department of Transportation** — entrance permits and road status on state and state-aid highways.

**Maine Revenue Services** — transfer tax, current-use programmes, and the unorganised territory.

**Department of Health and Human Services** — drinking water programmes and testing guidance.

### 27.4 Service providers and private records

Well drillers for well logs and yield; septic contractors for pumping and service history; fuel
dealers for delivery history, which corroborates the § 173(2-A) annual consumption figure; chimney
sweeps for inspection records; and utilities for availability and capacity.

### 27.5 What the licensee does with all of it

Not answer. **Route, and record the routing.** A licensee's investigation prompt list for a Maine
property should name, for each question, the office that answers it and the document that records
the answer. A buyer who is handed that list, in writing, has been served properly whether the
licensee is their agent or a transaction broker.

## Hour 28 — Construction, condition, and the vocabulary to describe it

### 28.1 Why the vocabulary matters

Not to inspect. To **describe accurately, recognise a question, and route it**. A licensee who calls
a truss a rafter, a service panel a meter, or a leach field a septic tank will be misunderstood by
the professionals they are trying to involve.

### 28.2 Maine house types

Older Maine housing has recognisable forms: the **Cape**; the **Colonial** and its saltbox and
gambrel variants; the **New Englander** or upright-and-wing; the **connected farmstead** — the
"big house, little house, back house, barn" arrangement, which raises specific fire-separation and
insurance questions; the **camp**, often seasonal, uninsulated, and on posts or piers rather than a
foundation; the **ranch** and **raised ranch**; and the **contemporary** and **post-and-beam** forms.

The seasonal camp is the type most often misdescribed. **A camp is not automatically a year-round
dwelling**, and converting one raises foundation, insulation, water supply, wastewater, access and
code questions all at once.

### 28.3 Components

**Site** — grading and drainage away from the structure, the driveway, and utility connections. Poor
site drainage is behind a large share of residential water problems.

**Foundation** — full basement, crawl space, slab, or posts and piers; footings below the local
**frost depth**, which in Maine is deep; foundation material, which in older housing is frequently
fieldstone or brick rather than poured concrete; damp-proofing and perimeter drainage.

**Framing** — sills, which in older Maine housing are the component most often rotted at the
foundation line; floor joists; stud walls; headers; and roof **rafters** or engineered **trusses**,
which must never be cut or modified.

**Envelope** — sheathing, weather barrier, **flashing** at every penetration and intersection,
cladding, windows and doors, roof covering and underlayment, and **ice-and-water membrane at the
eaves**, which matters in a climate that produces ice dams. Flashing is where roofs and walls
actually leak, and it is invisible from the ground.

**Insulation and ventilation** — R-values, air sealing, attic and crawl-space ventilation, and vapour
control appropriate to a cold climate.

**Systems** — electrical service and panel, branch circuits, grounding, and ground-fault and
arc-fault protection; plumbing supply material, drain-waste-vent, water heater with its relief valve
and discharge, and shutoffs; heating, including oil, propane, natural gas where available, wood,
pellet and heat pumps, with the flue or venting and the age of each component; and the ventilation
terminations, which must exit the building rather than the attic.

### 28.4 Condition vocabulary, and the boundary

**Physical deterioration** is wear and age. **Functional obsolescence** is a defect in the design or
utility of the improvement — one bathroom for four bedrooms, a bedroom reachable only through
another. **External obsolescence** is loss caused by something outside the property and is always
incurable from inside the boundary.

The licensee's sentence, in every case: *I can see it, I have written it down, and here is who can
tell you what it is.* "It's just settling", "that stain is old", and "the system has plenty of life
left" are opinions outside the licence, they are frequently wrong, and a wrong one is a
misrepresentation.

## Hour 29 (0–45) — Case Study 1: rural and waterfront property investigation

### 29.1 The facts

*A 1958 camp on 3.2 acres with 210 feet of frontage on a great pond, reached by a private gravel
road shared with four other camps. The seller has owned it since 1994. The listing says "year-round
potential". The property has a dug well and a septic system the seller believes was installed "in
the eighties". The land is enrolled in Tree Growth. The seller's disclosure statement leaves the
water test date blank and states the heating source as a propane wall furnace, age unknown, with an
annual consumption figure of 210 gallons. There is a small bunkhouse near the water. The neighbour's
boat is stored on the corner of the lot each winter.*

### 29.2 The deliverable

The student produces, for a buyer, an investigation prompt list. Each line must name **the question,
the office or professional who answers it, and the document that would record the answer** — and the
student must separately identify **what the licensee must disclose**, and under which section,
depending on whether the licensee is a seller agent or a transaction broker.

### 29.3 The lines the case is designed to produce

**"Year-round potential"** is a marketing claim about a 1958 camp, and it is the most dangerous
sentence in the listing. Year-round use raises **insulation, foundation and frost depth, water supply
in winter, wastewater capacity, road maintenance and plowing, and code** — every one of which is
answered by someone other than the licensee. A licensee who wrote that line must be able to say what
it was based on.

**The private road.** Public, private, or a discontinued way? Is there a recorded **road maintenance
agreement**, and does it bind successors? Who plows, and who pays? Registry of Deeds and the
municipal road commissioner.

**The dug well.** Dug wells are shallow and are the most vulnerable Maine supply type. The blank
water test date is information: **§ 173(1)** required the seller to state the date of the most recent
test and whether they had experienced an unsatisfactory test or one with notations. The buyer tests
for bacteria, nitrates, **arsenic**, uranium, and **radon in water**.

**The septic system.** "Believes, in the eighties" is not a record. The **plumbing inspector** holds
the permit and design if one exists, and the design tells the buyer how many bedrooms the system is
sized for. **§ 173(3)** required the seller to state the tank type, size and location, the leach
field location, installation dates, and the most recent servicing and contractor, and — because this
is a shoreland property — the disclosures required by **30-A M.R.S. § 4216**.

**The bunkhouse near the water.** Shoreland setback, whether it was permitted, and whether it is a
**nonconforming structure** — and whether it may be replaced or expanded. **Code enforcement officer,
in writing.**

**Tree Growth.** Enrolled land, so the buyer must understand the **withdrawal penalty** before
closing, whether their intended use qualifies, and who bears any penalty. Assessor, Maine Revenue
Services, a licensed forester, and counsel — and it is a **contract term**.

**The propane furnace with age unknown and 210 gallons a year.** 210 gallons is a low consumption
figure and is consistent with seasonal use rather than year-round occupancy, which bears directly on
the "year-round potential" claim. **§ 173(2-A)** also required the servicing company, the most recent
service call, and the most recent chimney and vent inspection. And the **propane tank is very often
leased from the dealer**, in which case it is not the seller's to convey.

**The neighbour's boat.** A licence, a prescriptive claim, or an encroachment — and the difference
decides whether the buyer can stop it. Survey, title examination, counsel.

**1958 construction.** Pre-1978, so **target housing**: the federal lead-based paint disclosure, the
pamphlet, the records, the contract acknowledgments, and the **ten-day** assessment opportunity —
plus **§ 173(4)(B)** in the Maine statement.

### Module 04 engagement check

**Question.** From the case study, the buyer asks you two things: "Can I take down the trees between
the camp and the water so we get the view?" and "The listing says year-round potential — so I can
live there in winter, right?" Answer both as a Maine licensee.

**Expected answer.** Neither is mine to answer, and answering either would be the mistake this module
exists to prevent.

**The trees** sit in the **shoreland zone**, where vegetative buffer and tree-cutting standards apply
under the municipality's shoreland zoning ordinance and the state minimum guidelines. Clearing
frequently requires a permit and unpermitted clearing carries enforcement and civil penalties. The
question goes to the **code enforcement officer, in writing**, before the contingency period ends.

**"Year-round potential" is a marketing claim, not a determination.** Whether the camp can lawfully
and practically be occupied year-round depends on insulation and heating, the foundation and frost
depth, winter water supply, **wastewater capacity — the septic design on file at the plumbing
inspector tells the buyer how many bedrooms it is sized for** — road maintenance and winter plowing,
and code. The stated **210 gallons of annual propane** is itself evidence pointing toward seasonal
rather than year-round use, and I would say so as an observation and route it.

If the buyer is my **client**, **§ 13273 1 B (4)** requires me to **advise them to obtain expert
advice on material matters beyond my expertise**, which both of these plainly are. If I am a
**transaction broker**, **§ 13283 3 A** bars me from conducting an investigation or analysis for
their benefit and **§ 13283 3 B** from verifying the accuracy or completeness of the seller's
statements — including "year-round potential". Either way: name the office, put it in writing, and do
not opine.

## Instructor source note for Module 04

Maine statements rest on text read directly from `legislature.maine.gov` on 2026-08-20 and recorded
in `maine/regulatory/primary-text-readings.md`: **33 M.R.S. § 173(1), (2-A), (3) and (4)** with
**§ 172** named as the exemption section, including the express cross-references in § 173 to
**30-A M.R.S. § 4216** for shoreland septic disclosures and **38 M.R.S. § 563(6)** for underground
oil storage tanks; and **32 M.R.S. §§ 13273 1 B (4) and 13283 3 A and B**.

**Deliberately not stated, for want of a primary reading:** every threshold in the **30-A M.R.S.**
subdivision provisions and the DEP's site location thresholds; every **shoreland zoning** setback,
zone width, buffer standard and nonconforming-structure rule, which sit in the state minimum
guidelines and in each municipality's ordinance; every figure in **36 M.R.S.** — exemption amounts,
current-use eligibility and **withdrawal penalties**, and the **transfer tax rate**; the period in
Maine's **Marketable Title Act** in 33 M.R.S.; Maine's **radon in rental housing** provisions; and
the **condominium** provisions of Title 33. Each is named where a curriculum hour reaches it, and the
hour routes the question rather than answering it.

**PFAS is stated as a live and developing programme**, not as a settled rule, and the hour instructs
the licensee not to characterise a property's PFAS status. That is a deliberate limit: the State's
investigation and testing programme is active, the science and the response are both moving, and a

**The Maine "quitclaim deed with covenant" is flagged in Hour 26.2 deliberately.** It functions as a
limited warranty deed, which is not what "quitclaim" means in most other states, and it is a
recurring source of confusion for licensees and buyers arriving from elsewhere.

Construction content in Hour 28 is general residential construction practice and is not a statement
of the **Maine Uniform Building and Energy Code** or of any municipal code. Frost depth, code
applicability, and permit requirements are set locally and are routed to the code enforcement
officer.
