# Principles of Real Estate - Module 02

## Property, ownership, and interests in real estate

**Course version:** 1.0 submission draft
**Module time:** 4 clock hours (240 instructional minutes)
**Regulatory status:** Not approved. No Michigan prelicensure credit is available for this course.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module the student will be able to:

- Distinguish real property from personal property and apply the fixture tests to a disputed item in a Michigan transaction.
- Identify the estates in land and state what each one gives its holder and what it does not.
- Explain how Michigan's forms of concurrent ownership differ in what happens on death, on divorce, and when one owner's creditor comes calling.
- Describe tenancy by the entirety and its effect on the creditors of one spouse, which is where Michigan differs sharply from most states.
- Identify the ownership structures a licensee meets in practice — condominium, cooperative, land contract, life estate — and what each one means for a sale.
- Recognise when an ownership question has become a legal question and route it.

## 1. What is real property

### 1.1 Land, and everything attached to it

Real property is the land, everything permanently attached to it, and the rights that go with it. That definition does three jobs at once, and each part produces disputes.

**The land itself** means the surface, what lies beneath it, and the air above it — historically to the centre of the earth and to the heavens, in practice limited by aviation law, by mineral severance, and by regulation.

**What is permanently attached** means buildings, and also the things attached to buildings. This is where fixtures live, and section 2 is entirely about the question of when a thing stops being a chair and becomes part of the house.

**The rights that go with it** are the bundle: possession, use, exclusion, enjoyment, and disposition. Ownership of real property is ownership of that bundle, and almost everything in this course is about carving pieces off it — a lease takes possession for a term, an easement takes a use, a mortgage takes a security interest, a restriction takes some of the right to use.

### 1.2 Personal property

Everything that is not real property is personal property. The distinction matters commercially for three reasons a licensee meets constantly.

**Transfer works differently.** Real property passes by deed and is recorded. Personal property passes by bill of sale or by delivery, and is not.

**Financing works differently.** A mortgage secures real property. Personal property is secured under Article 9 of the Uniform Commercial Code, which is a different system with different filings.

**Taxation works differently.** Michigan's General Property Tax Act taxes real property through the assessment system in module 04. Personal property is taxed under different provisions, and residential personal property generally is not.

### 1.3 Things that move between the categories

The interesting category is items that change classification.

**A growing tree** is real property. **Cut into logs**, it is personal property. The act of severance changes the category.

**Annual crops** planted and cultivated — emblements — are treated as personal property of the tenant who planted them, even though they are growing in the soil, because the labour is what produced them.

**A mobile home** may be either. Placed on a permanent foundation on land the owner owns, with the title surrendered under Michigan's procedure, it becomes part of the real property. Sitting in a park on rented ground with a certificate of title, it is personal property, sold like a vehicle, and a purchase agreement drafted for real property is the wrong instrument.

That last one is a live Michigan issue. A licensee who lists a manufactured home must establish which it is before deciding what to sign, and the answer turns on facts — foundation, land ownership, whether the title was surrendered — that the licensee can gather but should not adjudicate.

#### Checkpoint 1.1

A seller's listing includes "all appliances." At the walkthrough the buyer finds the refrigerator gone; the seller says it was hers because it was not built in. Where does the analysis start?

*Answer:* With the contract, not with fixture law. The parties addressed the question expressly by listing appliances, so the agreement governs and the seller has removed something she agreed to leave. Fixture law decides what is included when the contract is silent. The lesson for a licensee is that the cheapest fixture dispute is the one prevented by writing the item into the agreement.

## 2. Fixtures

### 2.1 The tests

A fixture is personal property that has become part of the real property. Michigan, like most states, decides it by looking at three things together.

**Annexation.** How is the item attached? Bolted, wired, plumbed, mortared, buried — the more the removal damages the structure, the more it looks like a fixture. A hook holding a mirror is weak annexation; a mirror set into tile is strong.

**Adaptation.** Is the item made for or adapted to this particular property? Storm windows cut for these openings, a key fitted to this lock, custom shelving built for this alcove — adaptation makes an item a fixture even where annexation is slight.

**Intention.** What did the person who installed it intend? This is the most important of the three, and courts infer it from the other two plus the relationship of the parties. A tenant installing a display counter intends to take it; a homeowner installing a furnace intends it to stay.

### 2.2 Trade fixtures

A tenant's trade fixtures — items installed for the tenant's business — remain the tenant's personal property and may be removed at the end of the tenancy, provided the tenant repairs the damage removal causes and removes them before the term ends. A restaurant's walk-in cooler, a salon's stations, a shop's shelving are the standard examples.

The exception matters in Michigan commercial practice because leaving them past the term can convert them into the landlord's property. A licensee representing a departing commercial tenant should be raising the removal deadline early.

### 2.3 The items that generate disputes

Experience produces a short list, and a licensee who addresses these in writing before they become arguments earns their fee.

| Item | Usual analysis | Why it is disputed |
|---|---|---|
| Window treatments | Rods are fixtures, curtains are not | Nobody agrees where the rod ends |
| Mounted televisions | Mount is a fixture, television is not | The mount is useless without the set |
| Appliances | Built-in yes, freestanding no | "Built-in" is a spectrum |
| Above-ground pool | Usually personal property | Until it is decked in |
| Garage refrigerator | Usually personal property | The seller assumed it conveyed |
| Playscape or swing set | Depends on anchoring | Children's attachment is not a legal test |
| Chandelier | Fixture | Sellers routinely want to keep a family piece |

### 2.4 The licensee's job

None of this requires the licensee to decide the law. It requires the licensee to see the item, ask the question, and get the answer into the contract.

The practical routine is: walk the property with the seller and ask what is not staying; list those items expressly as excluded; list anything ambiguous that is staying as expressly included; and repeat the exercise with the buyer so the two lists agree. A licensee who does that has removed the most common source of walkthrough conflict from the transaction.

#### Checkpoint 2.1

A seller replaced a builder-grade dining light with an antique chandelier inherited from her grandmother and wants to take it. What should the licensee do?

*Answer:* Have her replace it before the property is photographed or shown, or expressly exclude it in the listing and in the purchase agreement. A chandelier wired into the ceiling is a fixture, so silence means it conveys. Sentimental value is not a legal category. The failure mode is the seller removing it the night before closing and the buyer discovering a bare wire.

## 3. Estates in land

### 3.1 Freehold estates

A **fee simple absolute** is the largest interest a person can hold: unlimited in duration, inheritable, and freely transferable. Almost every residential transaction a Michigan licensee handles conveys a fee simple.

A **defeasible fee** is a fee that can end if a stated event occurs — property conveyed "so long as it is used as a library." These appear in older deeds and in charitable conveyances, and their effect is a legal question the licensee must route.

A **life estate** lasts for the life of a named person. The life tenant may possess and use the property, must not commit waste, and must maintain it and pay the taxes. When the measuring life ends, the property passes to the **remainderman** — or reverts to the grantor's estate, in which case the interest is a **reversion**.

Life estates matter in practice because they appear in family arrangements and in Michigan estate planning, particularly the "ladybird" deed that reserves a life estate with retained powers. A property held that way cannot be sold by the life tenant alone unless the instrument gives that power, and whether it does is a legal question.

### 3.2 Leasehold estates

A leasehold gives possession for a period without ownership.

An **estate for years** has a fixed beginning and end and terminates automatically without notice. A twelve-month written lease is the common example, and it does not require notice to end.

A **periodic tenancy** runs from period to period — month to month — and continues until one party gives notice. Michigan practice makes this the default when a tenant holds over with the landlord's consent.

A **tenancy at will** exists with the owner's permission and no fixed term, terminable by either party with statutory notice.

A **tenancy at sufferance** is what remains when a tenant stays after the right to possession ends. The holdover is not a trespasser — they entered lawfully — but has no estate, and the landlord's remedy is the summary proceeding in module 10.

### 3.3 What a licensee needs from this

Two practical points carry most of the value.

**Leases survive sales in Michigan unless the lease says otherwise.** A buyer purchasing a tenanted property generally takes subject to the existing lease, which means the buyer who plans to move in on closing day needs to know the term before they write the offer. Whether a particular lease binds a particular buyer is a legal question — but the licensee who does not ask for the leases has failed the client before the question arises.

**Security deposits transfer with the obligation.** Michigan's Truth in Renting Act and the security deposit statute govern how deposits are held and accounted for, and a buyer of rental property inherits duties about money they may never have received. Deposits are a closing item, not an afterthought.

#### Checkpoint 3.1

A buyer wants to occupy a duplex on closing day. The upper unit has a tenant on a written lease with seven months to run. What does the licensee do?

*Answer:* Obtain the lease before the offer is written, and tell the buyer plainly that the term exists and that whether they can obtain possession is a legal question. Then structure the transaction around the answer — a later closing, a negotiated buyout handled by the seller before closing, or acceptance of the tenancy. What the licensee must not do is assure the buyer that the tenant "will have to leave."

## 4. Concurrent ownership

### 4.1 The three Michigan forms

**Tenancy in common.** Two or more owners hold undivided fractional interests. Shares may be unequal. Each owner may sell or mortgage their share, and on death a share passes by will or intestacy — not to the co-owners. This is the default in Michigan when a deed to two unmarried people does not say otherwise.

**Joint tenancy with right of survivorship.** Owners hold equal undivided interests, and on the death of one, the survivors take the whole. The survivorship right avoids probate for the property. The interest can be severed by one owner conveying their share, which converts that share to a tenancy in common.

**Tenancy by the entirety.** Available only to a married couple, and in Michigan it is much more than a form of joint ownership.

### 4.2 Tenancy by the entirety, and why Michigan practice turns on it

A Michigan married couple who take title together are presumed to hold as tenants by the entirety. The consequences are substantial:

**Neither spouse can convey or mortgage alone.** Both signatures are required. A deed signed by one spouse conveys nothing.

**A creditor of one spouse generally cannot reach the property.** This is the protective feature, and it is the reason Michigan families hold homes this way. A judgment against one spouse alone does not attach to entireties property in the way it would to a tenancy in common share.

**Survivorship is automatic** on the death of either spouse.

**Divorce ends it.** The entireties form depends on the marriage; when it ends, the tenancy generally converts, and what it converts to is a question the judgment of divorce and the law decide.

For a licensee the operational rules are simple even though the doctrine is not. If the sellers are married and took title together, get both signatures on everything. If one spouse is unavailable, that is a problem to solve before the listing, not at the closing table. If the sellers are divorcing, the judgment of divorce controls what each may do and the licensee must not read it for them.

### 4.3 Partition

Co-owners who cannot agree may seek partition — a court dividing the property or ordering it sold and the proceeds split. Partition actions arise most often among inheriting siblings, and a licensee approached by one heir who wants to sell over another's objection is looking at a legal proceeding, not a listing.

### 4.4 Entity ownership

Property is frequently held by a limited liability company, a corporation, a partnership, or a trust. Two questions follow every time:

**Who may sign for the entity?** An operating agreement, corporate resolution, partnership agreement, or trust instrument answers it. The licensee obtains the document; the lawyer or title company reads it.

**Does the entity exist and is it in good standing?** An LLC whose registration has lapsed can create a real problem at closing, and it is cheap to check early.

#### Checkpoint 4.1

Two brothers inherit a house as tenants in common. One wants to sell, the other refuses. The willing brother asks a salesperson to list his half. What is the answer?

*Answer:* A tenant in common may convey their own undivided interest, so in principle something is saleable — but a half interest in a house with an unwilling co-owner is a product with almost no market, and listing it invites a buyer into a partition action. The honest response is to explain that his remedy is likely a legal one, refer him to a lawyer, and decline to market a fractional interest as though it were a house.

## 5. Ownership structures a licensee meets

### 5.1 Condominium

Michigan's Condominium Act governs. A condominium unit is owned in fee, together with an undivided interest in the common elements. The master deed and bylaws define what is unit and what is common element, and the association administers.

For a sale, three things matter: what the association's documents require to be delivered to a buyer, what the association's finances look like, and whether there are pending special assessments. A licensee who does not obtain the documents early has left a contingency to chance.

### 5.2 Cooperative

A cooperative owner owns shares in a corporation and a proprietary lease of a unit. This is personal property, not real property — a difference that changes the financing, the transfer, and often the licensee's own authority. Cooperatives are far less common in Michigan than in some states, and a licensee who meets one should slow down.

### 5.3 Land contract

Michigan uses land contracts more than most states, and a new licensee will meet them. The seller retains legal title while the buyer takes possession and pays in instalments; title passes on completion. The buyer has equitable title and a real stake; the seller has a security position and remedies including forfeiture and foreclosure.

A licensee's exposure here is high, because the instrument looks like a sale and behaves like financing. What a licensee must not do is advise on the forfeiture consequences, draft the instalment terms, or opine on what happens if the buyer misses payments. What a licensee must do is recognise the instrument, get it in front of a lawyer, and make sure the client knows before signing that a land contract is not a mortgage and the remedies are different.

### 5.4 Timeshare and fractional interests

Michigan regulates timeshare offerings, and the products are difficult to resell. A licensee approached about listing a timeshare should establish what the interest actually is before agreeing to market it, and should be candid about the resale market rather than repeating an owner's hopes.

#### Checkpoint 5.1

A buyer wants to purchase on a land contract because he cannot qualify for a mortgage. His agent is asked to "just write up the terms." What is the correct handling?

*Answer:* Decline to draft the terms, and say why. A land contract's payment schedule, interest, balloon, tax and insurance provisions, and default remedies are the substance of a financing agreement, and drafting them is legal work. The licensee's job is to identify the structure, insist both parties have counsel before signing, and document that advice. This is the module's clearest example of section 6 in module 01 applied to a real instrument.

## 6. Rights that travel with the land

### 6.1 Water

Michigan is defined by water, and two doctrines matter.

**Riparian rights** attach to land bordering a river or stream. **Littoral rights** attach to land bordering a lake or the Great Lakes. Both give the owner rights of reasonable use and access, and both are bounded by the rights of others on the same body.

The practical issues in Michigan are the ones that show up in listings: whether the property has actual frontage or an easement to the water, whether a shared access is deeded or permissive, whether a dock is permitted, and how the ordinary high-water mark affects the boundary. Every one of them is a factual question the licensee gathers and a legal question the licensee routes. "Lake access" in an advertisement is a claim, and a licensee who repeats an owner's description without seeing the instrument that creates it is repeating an unverified representation.

### 6.2 Mineral and subsurface rights

Michigan has significant oil and gas activity, and mineral rights are frequently severed from surface ownership. A buyer of a rural parcel may be acquiring surface only, with a third party holding the right to extract beneath.

Severance is recorded and discoverable. The licensee's obligation is to notice the possibility, ask the title company, and disclose what is found — not to explain what a lease permits the mineral owner to do.

### 6.3 Air rights and encroachments

Air rights are owned but limited. Encroachments — a neighbour's structure over the line, an eave, a fence, a driveway — are among the most common surprises in a Michigan transaction and are usually discovered by survey.

The licensee's routine is to recommend a survey where anything suggests a boundary problem, to disclose what is visible, and to route the legal question of what the encroachment means to counsel.

### 6.4 Government's four powers

Four powers sit above all private ownership, and a student should be able to name them and say what each does.

**Police power** — regulation for health, safety, and welfare, which produces zoning, building codes, and the land-use controls in module 04.

**Eminent domain** — the taking of private property for public use with just compensation.

**Taxation** — the property tax lien that outranks nearly everything, covered in module 04.

**Escheat** — property passing to the state when an owner dies with no heirs and no will.

The mnemonic PETE is worth learning, because the four together explain why ownership is never absolute and why a buyer's expectations sometimes have to be adjusted before an offer is written.

#### Checkpoint 6.1

A listing describes a parcel as "150 feet of private lake frontage." The seller says a neighbour has always used a path across the corner to reach the water. What must the licensee do?

*Answer:* Establish whether the neighbour's use is a recorded easement, an unrecorded claim, or permission — and until that is established, stop advertising the frontage as private. A licensee who repeats "private" after learning of a competing use is making a representation they have been told is doubtful. The path may also raise a prescriptive-rights question, which is legal work and gets referred.

## Key terms

**Adaptation** — the degree to which an item has been fitted to a particular property; one of the three fixture tests.

**Emblements** — annual crops produced by cultivation, treated as the personal property of the person who planted them.

**Land contract** — an instalment sale in which the seller retains legal title until the price is paid and the buyer takes possession with equitable title.

**Littoral rights** — the rights of an owner whose land borders a lake or sea.

**Partition** — a court proceeding dividing co-owned property or ordering its sale.

**Remainderman** — the person who takes the property when a life estate ends.

**Riparian rights** — the rights of an owner whose land borders a river or stream.

**Tenancy by the entirety** — Michigan's marital form of co-ownership, requiring both signatures and generally protecting the property from the creditors of one spouse alone.

**Trade fixture** — an item installed by a tenant for their business, removable by the tenant before the term ends.

## Practice problems

**Problem 1.** A seller installs a satellite dish bolted to the roof and wired into the house. He wants to take it. Analyse under the three tests.

*Working:* Annexation is strong — bolted and wired, with removal leaving holes. Adaptation is moderate; the dish is generic but aimed and fitted. Intention is what remains, and a rooftop installation reads as permanent. The dish is probably a fixture, which means the answer is not to argue about it but to exclude it expressly in the listing and the purchase agreement.

**Problem 2.** A married couple in Michigan own their home together. The husband has a judgment against him from a failed business. May a buyer take clear title if only the wife signs the deed?

*Working:* No, on two grounds. Entireties property requires both signatures to convey, so a deed from one spouse conveys nothing. And the judgment question — whether the creditor can reach entireties property — is a legal question that belongs with the title company and counsel, not with the licensee. The transaction cannot proceed on one signature regardless of how the creditor question resolves.

**Problem 3.** Two unmarried people buy a house together and the deed says only "to A and B." What form of ownership is it, and what happens if A dies?

*Working:* Absent language creating survivorship, Michigan treats it as a tenancy in common. If A dies, A's share passes under A's will or by intestacy — not to B. This is the single most consequential thing a licensee can raise with unmarried co-buyers before closing, and the way to raise it is to tell them the choice exists and send them to a lawyer to make it.

**Problem 4.** A commercial tenant's lease ends on 31 March. On 5 April the tenant returns for the walk-in cooler he installed. What is the position?

*Working:* Trade fixtures are the tenant's, but the right to remove them generally has to be exercised before the term ends, and a tenant who leaves them may lose them to the landlord. Whether this tenant has lost the cooler is a legal question. What the licensee should have done is raised the removal deadline weeks earlier — this is a foreseeable, preventable loss.

**Problem 5.** A rural buyer is told by the seller that "the mineral rights come with it." The title commitment shows a 1974 severance. What now?

*Working:* The seller's statement is contradicted by the record. The licensee's duty is to disclose what the commitment shows, in writing, and to stop repeating the seller's claim. What the severance permits the mineral owner to do on the surface is a legal question and goes to counsel. A buyer relying on the seller's sentence to plan a house site needs the correct information before the inspection contingency expires.

**Problem 6.** A manufactured home sits on land the seller owns, on a poured foundation, and the seller cannot find the certificate of title. Is this real property?

*Working:* Probably, but "probably" is not good enough to decide which purchase agreement to use. Affixation to owned land on a permanent foundation with the title surrendered converts a manufactured home to real property; a missing title raises the question of whether the surrender ever occurred. The licensee gathers the facts, involves the title company early, and does not choose the instrument alone.

## Module summary

**Real property is land, what is permanently attached, and the bundle of rights.** Everything else is personal property, and the categories carry different transfer, financing, and tax systems.

**Fixtures are decided by annexation, adaptation, and intention** — and prevented by writing the item into the contract. The licensee's job is the list, not the doctrine.

**Estates describe what a holder has and for how long.** Fee simple, defeasible fee, life estate with its remainder or reversion; and the four leaseholds, of which the estate for years ends without notice and the periodic tenancy does not.

**Michigan's concurrent forms differ on death, on divorce, and on creditors.** Tenancy by the entirety requires both spouses to sign anything and generally shields the property from one spouse's creditor — which is why the marital status of sellers is an operational fact, not a social one.

**Land contracts are common in Michigan and are financing instruments.** Recognise, route to counsel, and never draft the terms.

**Water, minerals, and boundaries are where Michigan listings go wrong.** Verify before advertising, disclose what you find, and refer what the instrument means.

**Four government powers sit above every ownership** — police power, eminent domain, taxation, escheat.
