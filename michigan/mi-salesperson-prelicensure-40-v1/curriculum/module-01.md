## Michigan license law, the Occupational Code, and the regulated activity

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module the student will be able to:

- Determine whether a described activity requires a Michigan real estate licence, applying the definition of a real estate broker in the Occupational Code rather than a job title.
- Distinguish the salesperson's licence from the associate broker's and the broker's, and explain why the salesperson's authority runs through the employing broker.
- Identify who is exempt from licensure in Michigan and explain why each exemption exists.
- State what the Department of Licensing and Regulatory Affairs regulates, what it does not, and where a consumer complaint goes.
- Rank the sources of Michigan real estate law and decide which one controls a given question.
- Identify the point at which a licensee must stop and refer a question to a lawyer, and say what a competent referral contains.

## 1. What Michigan actually regulates

### 1.1 The activity, not the job title

Michigan does not license people who call themselves real estate agents. It licenses people who do particular things for other people for compensation. Article 25 of the Occupational Code, 1980 PA 299, defines a real estate broker functionally: an individual or business entity who, **with intent to collect or receive a fee, compensation, or valuable consideration**, sells or offers for sale, buys or offers to buy, provides or offers to provide market analyses, lists or offers to list, rents or leases or offers to rent or lease, or negotiates the purchase, sale, or exchange of real estate — or who negotiates the mortgage of real estate, or who is engaged in the business of leasing or managing real estate for others.

Read that definition slowly, because three separate ideas are packed into it and each of them decides real cases.

**"With intent to collect or receive."** The licence requirement attaches to the expectation of compensation, not to its receipt. A person who negotiates a sale hoping to be paid, and is then not paid, has still engaged in licensed activity. This is why "I never got a commission" is not a defence, and why an unlicensed person who helps a friend sell a house in exchange for a promise of something valuable is exposed even if the promise is broken.

**"Or offers to."** Offering to list, offering to sell, offering to lease — each is licensed activity on its own. The regulated conduct starts before the transaction does. A person who advertises that they will find buyers for owners' homes has offered to provide a licensed service whether or not anyone accepts.

**"For others."** Selling your own property is not licensed activity. The moment the work is done for another person's account, the analysis changes. This is the hinge on which most of the exemptions in section 3 turn.

### 1.2 Market analyses, and why Michigan names them

Michigan's definition expressly includes providing or offering to provide **market analyses**. This matters more than it looks. A broker price opinion or comparative market analysis is a licensed service in Michigan, and offering to prepare one — for a lender considering a short sale, for an owner deciding whether to list, for an estate valuing an asset — is broker activity.

It is not an appraisal. Appraisal is separately regulated, and a licensee who prepares a market analysis must not present it as an appraisal, must not use appraisal terminology to describe it, and must not accept an engagement that requires an appraisal. The distinction is covered in module 09; what matters here is that the market analysis sits inside the real estate licence and outside the appraisal licence, and that both boundaries are enforced.

### 1.3 Leasing and property management

Michigan includes in the definition anyone "engaged in the business of leasing or managing real estate for others." Property management in Michigan is real estate brokerage. The person who advertises vacancies, shows units, negotiates leases, collects rent for an owner, and takes a management fee is doing licensed work, and the rules that govern trust money in module 10 apply to their operating accounts.

Owners managing their own property are outside this, as are salaried employees of an owner acting only for that owner — a resident manager on a payroll, not a commission. The distinction is between acting for others as a business and acting for your employer as staff.

### 1.4 What "compensation" reaches

Compensation is not limited to a commission cheque. Fee, compensation, or **valuable consideration** covers a reduced rent, a referral fee, a share of a management fee, a finder's payment, a car, forgiveness of a debt. The statute is drafted broadly on purpose: a narrow definition would let an unlicensed intermediary structure around it in an afternoon.

The practical rule for a new licensee is simpler than the statute. If money or anything worth money is expected to move because you helped someone else buy, sell, lease, or value real estate, a licence is involved. Find out whose.

### 1.5 How the definition is applied in practice

Regulators and courts do not analyse the definition abstractly. They ask four questions in order, and a student who learns the order will handle almost every fact pattern in this course.

**Was there an act on the list?** Selling, offering to sell, buying, offering to buy, listing, offering to list, renting, leasing, offering to rent or lease, negotiating a purchase, sale or exchange, negotiating a mortgage, providing or offering to provide a market analysis, or being engaged in the business of leasing or managing for others. If the conduct is not on the list, the analysis stops.

**Was it for another person?** A principal acting on their own account is outside the definition however sophisticated the transaction.

**Was compensation expected?** Not received — expected. And compensation means anything of value.

**Does an exemption apply?** Only at this point, and the exemptions are narrow and specific.

The order matters because it prevents the two most common errors. The first is deciding that something is unlicensed because no money changed hands, which skips question three's actual wording. The second is reaching for an exemption before establishing that the conduct was licensed activity at all — an exemption from something that was never regulated is not a finding, it is a distraction.

### 1.6 A worked example

Consider a man who owns four rental duplexes. He advertises the vacancies, shows the units, signs the leases, and collects the rent. No licence is required: he is acting for himself as owner.

Now his neighbour, seeing that he is good at it, asks him to do the same for her two duplexes for ten percent of the rent. Everything changes at that sentence. He is now leasing and managing real estate for another, in the business of it, for compensation. A licence is required, and because a salesperson may only act through a broker, he cannot simply obtain a salesperson's licence and continue as before — he needs a broker, or he needs to become one.

Now change one fact: instead of ten percent, the neighbour offers to let him park a boat on her lot each winter. The analysis does not change. Valuable consideration is not limited to money.

Now change it again: the neighbour is his wife, and the duplexes are jointly owned. The analysis changes back, because he is again dealing with property he owns.

The point of running the example four ways is that the same conduct — advertise, show, lease, collect — moves in and out of regulation depending on facts that have nothing to do with skill or honesty. The licence requirement is about whose account the work is done on and whether value is expected to move, and nothing else.

#### Checkpoint 1.1

An unlicensed person maintains a website listing homes for sale by their owners. She charges owners a flat fee to appear, answers buyer enquiries about the properties, arranges viewings, and passes offers between the parties. She takes no percentage of any sale. Is she engaged in licensed activity in Michigan?

*Answer:* Yes. Arranging viewings and conveying offers is negotiating the purchase or sale of real estate for others, and the flat fee is compensation. That the fee is not a percentage and is paid whether or not a sale closes does not remove it from the definition — the statute says fee, compensation, or valuable consideration, not commission.

## 2. Broker, associate broker, salesperson

### 2.1 Three licences, one chain of authority

Michigan issues three individual real estate licences and one to business entities.

A **salesperson** is licensed to perform brokerage activity **only** while employed by or associated with a licensed broker. The salesperson's licence has no independent existence in practice: it authorises work done for the broker's account.

An **associate broker** has met the broker's qualification requirements — including the additional education and the experience — but works under another broker rather than operating a brokerage. Michigan distinguishes a principal associate broker, who holds supervisory responsibility, from a non-principal associate broker.

An **individual broker** may operate a brokerage in their own name. A **broker company** licence covers a business entity, and each branch office is separately licensed.

### 2.2 Why the chain matters more than the titles

Everything a salesperson signs, promises, or agrees to on behalf of a client is the **broker's** obligation. The listing agreement is between the seller and the brokerage. The buyer's representation agreement is between the buyer and the brokerage. The trust account belongs to the broker. The transaction file belongs to the broker. The advertising is the broker's advertising and, under R 339.22158, must carry the name of the approved school when it advertises education and the broker's name when it advertises property.

Three consequences follow that new licensees routinely get wrong:

**A salesperson cannot hold a client's deposit.** Money received belongs in the broker's trust account, promptly, under the rules covered in module 10. A salesperson who keeps a cheque in a desk drawer over a weekend has created a problem for the broker as well as for themselves.

**A salesperson cannot take a fee directly from a client.** Compensation flows to the broker and from the broker to the salesperson under whatever agreement they have.

**A salesperson who moves to another brokerage does not take the listings.** The listing is the former broker's contract. What transfers is the licensee, not the file.

### 2.3 Supervision as a duty, not a courtesy

The broker's supervisory obligation is affirmative. It is not discharged by having a policy manual and it is not reduced by classifying salespeople as independent contractors for tax purposes. A broker who does not know what their licensees are advertising, what representations they are making, or how their transaction files are documented is not supervising, and the Department's discipline reaches the broker for it.

For a student, the useful frame is this: your broker is accountable for your work, which means your broker gets to set standards above the legal minimum, and those standards bind you. Where office policy is stricter than the law, office policy governs your conduct.

### 2.4 The business entity licence

A broker company licence is issued to the entity, and the entity must designate an individual broker who is responsible for it. The individual's licence and the entity's licence are separate records with separate obligations. When a designated broker leaves, the entity has a problem that must be solved on a clock, and the licences of every salesperson associated with the entity depend on the solution.

### 2.5 What each licence lets a person actually do

| Licence | May contract with clients | May supervise | May hold trust funds | Works for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Salesperson | No — the broker contracts | No | No | An employing broker |
| Non-principal associate broker | No — the broker contracts | No | No | An employing broker |
| Principal associate broker | No — the broker contracts | Yes, as designated | No | An employing broker |
| Individual broker | Yes | Yes | Yes | Own account |
| Broker company | Yes, through its designated broker | Through its designated broker | Yes | Own account |

The table repays study because the first column is the one students misread. A salesperson negotiates, advises, and advocates — but does not contract. The person who signs a listing signs for the brokerage.

### 2.6 The employment relationship, and why the tax label does not matter

Most Michigan salespeople are treated as independent contractors for tax purposes. That classification governs how income is reported and whether taxes are withheld. It governs nothing about the regulatory relationship.

The broker still supervises. The broker is still accountable for the salesperson's conduct in the practice. The brokerage is still the party to the client's contract. A salesperson who says "I'm an independent contractor, so my broker isn't responsible for how I advertise" has confused two systems that do not speak to each other.

The practical test a new licensee should apply is simple: if the conduct affects a client, a member of the public, or a transaction, assume the broker is accountable for it and behave accordingly.

### 2.7 Transferring, lapsing, and what happens to the work

A salesperson who leaves one brokerage for another does not carry the listings, the buyer agreements, or the files. Those belong to the former broker. What the salesperson carries is their licence, transferred through the Department's system, and their own relationships — which are not property and cannot be contracted away, but are also not a right to service the former broker's clients.

A licence that lapses does not simply pause the licensee's ability to earn. It ends their authority to perform licensed activity, which means a transaction in progress becomes a problem for the broker on the day the lapse takes effect. The obligation to keep the licence current sits with the licensee, and the consequence of failing lands on more people than the licensee.

#### Checkpoint 2.1

A salesperson negotiates a listing, and the seller wants the agreement to name the salesperson personally because they trust her and not her firm. Can it?

*Answer:* No. The listing is a contract for brokerage services and the brokerage is the party; the salesperson signs on the broker's behalf. Naming the salesperson personally would either be meaningless or would purport to make an unlicensed-in-that-capacity individual the service provider. The right answer to the seller is that the salesperson's involvement can be recorded in the agreement, but the contracting party is the firm.

## 3. Exemptions, and the reason each exists

### 3.1 Who does not need a licence

Michigan exempts several categories from the licence requirement. The list is worth learning as a set of reasons rather than as a list, because the reasons predict the edges.

**Owners dealing in their own property.** The definition reaches activity "for others." An owner selling their own building is a principal, not an intermediary. The exemption ends where the owner starts acting for someone else's account.

**Attorneys at law.** An attorney acting in the ordinary practice of law is exempt. This is narrower than it sounds: it covers the practice of law, not the operation of a brokerage business by someone who happens to hold a law licence.

**Persons acting under court order or as a fiduciary appointed by a court.** A receiver, a personal representative, a trustee in bankruptcy selling property under the authority of a court is acting under that authority, not as an intermediary in the market.

**Persons holding a duly executed power of attorney from an owner**, used for a particular transaction rather than as a business. The instrument, not a licence, supplies the authority — and the exemption does not turn a power of attorney into a licence to run a practice.

**Certain resident managers and employees** acting for a single employer, discussed in section 1.3.

### 3.2 What the exemptions do not do

An exemption from licensure is not an exemption from the rest of the law. An exempt owner still owes the seller-disclosure duties in module 06. An exempt fiduciary still cannot discriminate under the statutes in module 07. Fair housing law reaches the transaction, not the licence.

Nor does an exemption transfer. An unlicensed assistant working for a licensed salesperson is not covered by the salesperson's licence. The assistant may perform administrative tasks — scheduling, data entry, preparing marketing material for the licensee's approval — but the moment the assistant answers a substantive question about a property, negotiates a term, or conducts a showing, they are performing licensed activity without a licence, and both the assistant and the supervising licensee are exposed.

### 3.3 The out-of-state licensee

A licence issued by another state does not authorise brokerage activity on Michigan property. A Michigan licence is required for Michigan work. Cooperative arrangements between brokerages in different states are structured so that the licensed activity in Michigan is performed by a Michigan licensee, with compensation shared broker to broker. A new licensee should assume that an out-of-state colleague cannot show, negotiate, or advertise Michigan property, and should route the work accordingly.

### 3.4 The unlicensed assistant, in detail

This is the exemption question that arises most often in a working brokerage, because every busy licensee eventually wants help, and the line is drawn in a place that is easy to describe and easy to cross.

**An unlicensed assistant may:** schedule appointments; prepare and place advertising the licensee has approved; enter listing data supplied by the licensee; order and collect reports; assemble file documents; arrange access with vendors; write and send administrative correspondence that contains no substantive representation; sit at an open house to greet visitors and hand out prepared material.

**An unlicensed assistant may not:** answer substantive questions about a property, its condition, its terms, or its price; discuss or convey what a seller might accept; conduct a showing; negotiate any term; solicit listings or buyers; prepare a market analysis; or be compensated in a way that varies with whether a transaction closes.

The last item is the one people miss. Compensation that turns on the outcome of a transaction is the hallmark of brokerage compensation, and paying an unlicensed assistant a bonus per closing converts the arrangement into unlicensed activity even if every task the assistant performed was administrative.

### 3.5 Where the exemptions run out

Each exemption is drawn for a reason, and each stops where the reason stops.

An **owner** who begins acting for other owners has stepped outside. A developer selling its own units is a principal; the same developer marketing a neighbouring owner's units is not.

An **attorney** in the ordinary practice of law is exempt. An attorney who opens a brokerage and advertises for listings is operating a real estate business and is not, in that capacity, practising law.

A **court-appointed fiduciary** acts under the court's authority for the estate or the receivership in question. That authority does not extend to the fiduciary's other business.

A **power of attorney** authorises acts for a particular principal, usually a particular transaction. It is not a business licence, and a person collecting powers of attorney from multiple owners in order to trade in their properties is doing precisely what the licence requirement exists to reach.

#### Checkpoint 3.1

An unlicensed personal assistant employed by a busy salesperson sits at an open house while the salesperson is at another showing. A visitor asks whether the seller would consider $10,000 below the asking price. What may the assistant say?

*Answer:* Nothing that answers the question. The assistant may greet visitors, hand out prepared material, record names, and say that the salesperson will respond. Conveying a price the seller might accept, or inviting an offer, is negotiating for others and requires a licence. The supervising salesperson is exposed as well, because the arrangement was theirs.

## 4. The Department, and where a complaint goes

### 4.1 What LARA regulates

Real estate licensing in Michigan sits with the **Department of Licensing and Regulatory Affairs**, through its Bureau of Professional Licensing. The Department issues licences, approves prelicensure courses and instructors, sets and enforces the rules in R 339.22101 and following, investigates complaints, and imposes discipline under section 602 of the Occupational Code.

The licensing examination is administered by **PSI** on the Department's behalf. Application, renewal, transfer, and modification run through the Department's **MiPLUS** system.

### 4.2 What it does not regulate

The Department does not resolve commission disputes between brokerages, does not enforce private contracts, and does not award damages. A client whose complaint is really a contract claim is in the wrong forum, and part of a licensee's job is knowing that well enough to say so without giving legal advice.

Trade-association membership is separate again. The Michigan Realtors association and its local boards are private organisations with their own codes and their own arbitration. A licensee may be disciplined by an association without any state action, and disciplined by the state without any association involvement. Neither substitutes for the other, and neither is a defence to the other.

### 4.3 Discipline, and the education-specific grounds

Section 602 of the code supplies the penalties. R 339.22155 adds grounds specific to schools and instructors, and one of them belongs in a student's mind from the first hour of the course: a school or instructor may be disciplined for **revealing or attempting to discover, or soliciting, encouraging, or inducing an individual to reveal, the questions on a real estate licence examination**.

That rule explains something students often find strange. A school that tells you what is on the state examination is not being generous; it is committing a disciplinary offence, and the material it gives you is unreliable precisely because it was obtained improperly. A course that teaches the subject prepares you for the examination as a by-product. A course that claims to have the examination is telling you something about itself.

### 4.4 Records and the paper trail

The Department's authority to inspect is broad and its expectations about records are concrete. For a school, R 339.22154 requires a permanent record for every student. For a brokerage, the transaction file is the evidence of what was done and when, and the file is the broker's.

The habit to build now is that an action without a record is, for regulatory purposes, an action that may as well not have happened. Not because regulators are cynical, but because a complaint arrives months later and the file is all anybody has.

### 4.5 What a complaint actually looks like

A regulatory complaint does not usually announce itself. It begins as a phone call from a former client, or a letter from a lawyer, or a Department enquiry that arrives months after a transaction closed and asks a narrow question about one document.

What happens next is decided almost entirely by the file. The Department asks what the licensee represented, when, to whom, and on what basis. A brokerage that can produce the listing agreement, the disclosure the seller signed and dated, the confirming email sent the same afternoon, and the note recording what was referred to counsel is in a completely different position from one that can produce a recollection.

This is the practical reason the recordkeeping rules in this course are not administrative trivia. The record is what a licensee has when their memory of an ordinary Tuesday eighteen months ago is the only other evidence.

### 4.6 The examination, and how it relates to this course

Michigan's salesperson examination is administered by PSI and covers national real estate principles together with Michigan law and practice. Passing it is a separate requirement from completing this course, and the two are related but not identical: this course exists to satisfy MCL 339.2504's education requirement and to make a competent licensee, and a student who understands the material will be prepared for the examination as a consequence.

What this course will never do is teach to specific examination items, because obtaining them would be a disciplinary offence under R 339.22155(b) and because material obtained that way is unreliable. If any provider offers you the questions, the correct response is to doubt everything else they have told you.

### 4.7 After the licence: what comes next

A new salesperson's first act after passing is to associate with a broker, because the licence has no independent operational effect. Choosing that broker well matters more to a first year than almost anything else: supervision quality, training, the office's file standards, and how the broker handles trust money are the conditions under which a new licensee either builds good habits or inherits bad ones.

Continuing education follows on a renewal cycle, and prelicensure credit and continuing-education credit do not substitute for one another in either direction — a rule stated expressly in MCL 339.2504.

#### Checkpoint 4.1

A client tells a salesperson that the buyer's brokerage promised a $2,000 credit at closing and has not paid it, and asks the salesperson to report the other brokerage to LARA. Is that the right forum?

*Answer:* Probably not, and the honest answer is that this is a contract question. The Department disciplines licensees for violations of the code and rules; it does not adjudicate who owes whom money. If the promise induced the client to act and was never intended to be kept, there may be a misrepresentation issue the Department would take. The licensee's move is to write down exactly what the client says, tell the client the Department does not award money, escalate to their broker, and suggest the client speak to a lawyer about the contract.

## 5. Which source of law controls

### 5.1 The order

Michigan real estate questions are answered from a stack, and the answer is the highest applicable source.

1. **The United States Constitution and federal statutes** — fair housing, settlement procedures, lending disclosure, environmental rules.
2. **The Michigan Constitution and the Michigan Compiled Laws** — the Occupational Code for licensing; the Seller Disclosure Act, the Land Division Act, the Condominium Act, the transfer-tax acts, and the civil-rights acts for practice.
3. **Administrative rules** — R 339.22101 and following, promulgated by the Department under authority the code grants it. A rule cannot exceed the statute it implements, and where it appears to, the statute wins.
4. **Case law** — Michigan appellate decisions interpreting all of the above.
5. **Local ordinances** — zoning, rental registration, point-of-sale inspection requirements. These vary enormously between municipalities and are the most common source of a surprise late in a transaction.
6. **Private agreements** — the listing, the purchase agreement, the deed restrictions, the condominium documents.
7. **Brokerage policy** — binding on the licensee, and frequently stricter than the law.

### 5.2 Forms are not law

A form is a private document that someone drafted to reflect their reading of the law. Association forms, brokerage forms, and title-company forms are conveniences, not authority. Two consequences:

A form can be wrong, or out of date, or unsuited to the transaction in front of you. Using it does not make its content correct.

A form's blanks are where the law is usually violated — not in the printed paragraphs but in what a licensee writes into them. Module 06 returns to this.

### 5.3 Reading a citation

Students meet three citation shapes in this course and should be able to tell them apart on sight.

| Shape | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **MCL 339.2504** | Michigan Compiled Laws, a statute | The 40-hour prelicensure requirement |
| **R 339.22157** | An administrative rule promulgated by the Department | The six distance-learning requirements |
| **1976 PA 453** | A public act, cited by year and number | The Elliott-Larsen Civil Rights Act |

A public act and an MCL section are two ways of naming the same law: the act is what the legislature passed, the MCL section is where it lives in the compiled laws.

### 5.4 When sources conflict

Conflicts are usually apparent rather than real, and the first move is to check whether the two sources are answering the same question. A federal rule and a Michigan rule that appear to conflict are often addressing different transactions or different actors.

Where the conflict is real, the higher source controls, with one important asymmetry that runs through module 07: a state or local law that provides **more** protection than a federal one is not in conflict with it. Michigan's civil-rights statutes protect classes the federal Fair Housing Act does not, and both apply.

### 5.5 Reading a statute without being a lawyer

A licensee reads law constantly and is not permitted to interpret it for clients. Those two facts sit together more comfortably once a student learns to read a provision structurally.

**Find the actor.** Who does the provision bind — a licensee, a broker, a seller, a landlord, a school?

**Find the trigger.** What has to happen before the obligation attaches? "Before receiving the certificate," "within 5 business days," "with intent to collect."

**Find the obligation.** What must be done, or not done?

**Find the exception.** Almost every provision has one, and the exception is where the drafting effort went.

Run that on R 339.22154(7): the actor is a real estate school; the trigger is the conclusion of the last course; the obligation is to submit the names of successful students in a department-approved format; the timing is within 5 business days. Four questions, and the rule is understood well enough to comply with.

What that reading does not permit is telling a client what a provision means for their situation. Structure is not interpretation, and the line in section 6 is not softened by having read the statute carefully.

### 5.6 Local law, and the surprise late in a transaction

Michigan municipalities regulate a great deal that touches a sale, and they do not do it uniformly. Point-of-sale inspection requirements, certificates of occupancy, rental registration, sidewalk and water-line ordinances, and local transfer fees exist in some communities and not in others a mile away.

The failure mode is predictable: a licensee who works mostly in one municipality assumes its rules apply in the next. The habit that prevents it is to check the municipality every time, early, and to write down what was checked and when. A buyer who learns eight days before closing that the city requires an inspection nobody ordered has a real problem, and it will be attributed to the licensee who did not ask.

#### Checkpoint 5.1

A brokerage's office policy requires two business days between receiving an offer and presenting a counter-offer, "to allow considered advice." A seller wants to counter the same afternoon. What governs?

*Answer:* Not the policy, not in this direction. Office policy binds the licensee's conduct but cannot override the licensee's duty to the client or the client's right to direct their own transaction. A policy that delays the presentation of a client's counter-offer is a problem with the policy. The licensee should escalate to the broker immediately rather than sit on the seller's instruction — and this is exactly the kind of conflict that belongs in writing.

## 6. Where the licence stops and legal practice begins

### 6.1 The line

A licensee may fill in the blanks of a form prepared for the transaction and explain what the form says. A licensee may not draft provisions, advise on the legal effect of a document, opine on the state of title, or answer a question whose answer is a legal conclusion.

The line is not about difficulty and it does not move with experience. A twenty-year licensee who tells a buyer that a deed restriction is unenforceable has practised law; a first-week salesperson who says "that is a question for a lawyer, and here is why it matters to your deadline" has done the job correctly.

### 6.2 The questions that require referral

Some questions are recognisable on sight. These are the common ones in Michigan practice:

- Whether a restriction in a deed or a condominium document can be enforced against this buyer.
- What a divorce judgment, a probate order, or a trust instrument permits a seller to do.
- Whether a boundary occupied for decades has become the legal line.
- What tax consequences a structure produces.
- Whether an inherited property can be sold before an estate is closed.
- Whether a lease survives a sale.

### 6.3 Referring well

A referral that consists of "you'll need a lawyer" is technically correct and practically useless. A competent referral has four parts:

**Name the limit.** "I'm not permitted to advise on that, and I'd be guessing."

**Give what you can.** The facts you have, the document, the section, where it sits in the transaction.

**Connect it to the timeline.** "Your inspection contingency ends on the 14th, so this needs an answer before then."

**Record it.** A line in the file and a confirming message to the client. The referral is a professional act and it belongs in the record like any other.

### 6.4 The three answers that get licensees in trouble

**"It's standard."** Nothing is standard about a particular buyer's transaction, and the phrase substitutes reassurance for advice.

**"I've seen this a hundred times."** Frequency is not authority.

**"Don't worry about that paragraph."** Directing a client away from a term of their own contract is close to the worst thing a licensee can do, and it is the sentence that appears most often in complaints.

### 6.5 Two boundaries that look like the legal-practice line but are not

**Appraisal.** A licensee may prepare a market analysis. A licensee may not prepare an appraisal or describe a market analysis as one. This is a licensing boundary, not a legal-practice boundary, and it is enforced by a different set of rules — but the discipline it requires of a licensee is the same: know what you are producing, label it accurately, and refuse the engagement that requires the other thing.

**Inspection and engineering.** A licensee may observe and must disclose material facts they know. A licensee may not opine on the condition of a structural system, the remaining life of a roof, or the adequacy of a foundation. "It looks fine to me" is an opinion a licensee is not qualified to give and will be held to.

The common structure of all three boundaries — law, appraisal, inspection — is that a licensee's job is to see the issue, name it accurately, route it to the right professional, and record what they did. That is not a limitation on the role. It is the role.

### 6.6 Building the habit now

Students often assume this problem belongs to a later stage of their career. It does not: the first months are when it bites hardest, because a new licensee is most eager to be helpful and least equipped to recognise which questions are traps.

Three habits, built now, do most of the work.

**Notice the question type before you answer.** "Can they" and "do I have to" and "is that enforceable" are legal questions almost every time. "What does this say" and "when is this due" and "who do I ask" are not.

**Have the sentence ready.** "That's a legal question and I'd be guessing — let me get you to someone who can answer it properly, and here's why we need it before the fourteenth." A sentence you have rehearsed is a sentence you will actually say under pressure.

**Write it down the same day.** Not because anyone doubts you, but because in fourteen months nobody will remember, including you.

#### Checkpoint 6.1

A buyer asks whether the shed that sits three feet over the neighbour's line will have to be moved. What is the licensee's answer?

*Answer:* That the encroachment is real and material, that the licensee cannot advise on whether it must be moved or whether long occupation has changed the boundary, that it should go to a lawyer and probably to a surveyor, and that the inspection or title contingency date is when the answer is needed. Then write it in the file and confirm it to the buyer in writing. Anything that sounds like "they'll never make you move it" is a legal opinion.

## Extended worked example: four weeks of one transaction

The abstractions in this module are easier to hold if they are watched happening to one person. What follows is a single Michigan transaction from first contact to the point where the licensing questions are resolved. Read it once for the story, then again for the six places where the analysis in sections 1 through 6 decides what happens next.

### Week one: the first phone call

A salesperson, licensed six weeks, takes a call from a woman whose mother has died. The mother owned a house in Grand Rapids. The daughter says she and her brother have "agreed" that she will handle the sale, and she wants to list it on Friday.

**The licensing question is not hers, it is the salesperson's.** Listing property is licensed activity, so a licence is required — the salesperson has one, and the listing will be with the brokerage, not with her personally. That part is settled by section 2.

**The question that stops the transaction is different.** Who owns the house, and who is authorised to sell it? The mother's estate does, in some form the salesperson cannot determine. Whether the daughter may sign a listing depends on whether an estate has been opened, whether a personal representative has been appointed, whether that is her, and what authority the appointment carries. Every one of those is a legal question under section 6.

**What a competent licensee does on that call:** takes the facts, says plainly that the authority to sell has to be established before a listing can be signed and that this is a question for a probate lawyer, asks whether an estate has been opened, and offers to prepare everything else in the meantime. What an incompetent licensee does is book the listing appointment for Friday and let it become someone else's problem in six weeks.

### Week two: the brother

The brother telephones. He says he has a power of attorney from his mother and can sign for the sale himself.

**A power of attorney from a person who has died has no effect.** The authority ended at death. The salesperson does not need to know the doctrine to handle this correctly — she needs to recognise that whether an instrument still authorises anything is a legal question, and route it. What she must not do is say "that should be fine," which is the sentence in section 6.4 that appears most often in complaints.

She also now has a second fact worth recording: the two heirs have different beliefs about who may act. That is not gossip. It is a material feature of the transaction, and it belongs in the file with the date.

### Week three: the neighbour

While the estate question is unresolved, a neighbour approaches the daughter directly and offers to buy the house. The daughter asks the salesperson to "just write it up" and says she will pay her a flat $3,000 at closing rather than a commission.

Three separate problems are stacked in that request.

**Writing it up.** Preparing the purchase agreement means completing a form prepared for the transaction — permissible — but the transaction has an unresolved authority problem, and a licensee who papers over it has not solved it.

**The compensation.** A flat $3,000 is compensation, so the activity is licensed regardless of the label. And compensation flows to the broker, not to the salesperson personally. A salesperson who accepts a client's cheque directly has created a problem with her own broker and with the Department, whatever the amount.

**The listing that does not exist yet.** There is no agreement in place with the brokerage. Working without one is not unlawful in itself, but it means the salesperson is performing licensed work with no contract, no defined scope, and no agreed compensation — which is how a licensee ends up in the commission dispute described in section 4.2, in the forum that cannot help her.

### Week four: resolution

The estate is opened, the daughter is appointed personal representative, and her lawyer confirms in writing what she may sign. The brokerage takes a listing signed by her in that capacity. The neighbour's offer is presented, negotiated, and accepted. The salesperson's file, when the transaction is reviewed a year later because the brother complains, contains: the dated note from week one recording that authority was unresolved and referred; the confirming email; the dated note from week two; the lawyer's confirmation; the listing signed in a representative capacity; and every communication with both heirs.

The complaint goes nowhere, and it goes nowhere because of section 4.4 rather than because everyone behaved well.

### What the example teaches

The licensing analysis in this module is rarely the hard part of a real transaction. What is hard is noticing, early and under time pressure from a client who wants to move, that a question has crossed out of the licensee's authority. The four moments in this narrative where that happened were all in the first two weeks, and all four were answered with a referral, a note, and a confirming message.

## Additional practice problems

**Problem 5.** A licensed salesperson wants to buy an investment property for herself. She finds one listed by another brokerage and makes an offer. Is she acting as a licensee?

*Working:* She is acting as a principal — buying for her own account — so the licence requirement is not triggered by the purchase itself. But two obligations follow her anyway. Her licensed status is a material fact and must be disclosed to the seller; a licensee buying without disclosing that they are one is the classic misrepresentation-by-silence case. And her broker's policies about licensee purchases apply to her, because office policy binds her conduct under section 5.1.

**Problem 6.** A brokerage advertises "no commission - flat fee listings" on a billboard that shows only the team's name and a phone number. Identify the problem.

*Working:* Michigan requires advertising to identify the brokerage; a team name and telephone number that does not identify the licensed firm is the recurring advertising violation in this area. R 339.22158 makes the parallel requirement for education advertising, which must name the approved school. The pricing claim is a separate question - it must be accurate - but the identification failure is the one visible from the road.

**Problem 7.** A salesperson at an open house is asked by a visitor, "What's the lowest they'd take?" The seller told her privately that he would accept $15,000 less. What may she say?

*Working:* Nothing that reveals it, unless the seller has authorised her to. Her duty of confidentiality to her client, covered in module 05, forbids disclosing the seller's negotiating position. This is not a licensing-boundary question at all - it is an agency question - and it is included here because students routinely mix the two. Section 6's referral line is about legal conclusions; this one is about loyalty.

**Problem 8.** An unlicensed person forms a company, hires two licensed salespeople, and calls himself the owner of the brokerage. Is that lawful?

*Working:* A broker company licence requires a designated individual broker responsible for the entity. Ownership of the business by an unlicensed person is a separate question from who holds and is accountable under the licence, but the entity cannot operate as a brokerage without a designated broker. The arrangement described has none, so the entity is not licensed and its salespeople have no broker to act through - which means their activity is unauthorised as well.

**Problem 9.** A client asks a salesperson to recommend a lawyer, and the salesperson's brother-in-law is a real estate attorney who pays her $250 for each referral. Analyse.

*Working:* Two issues. The undisclosed financial interest in the recommendation is a conflict the client is entitled to know about before relying on it. And R 339.22137 restricts a licensee entitled to a commission from also receiving a referral fee or other valuable consideration in connection with the same transaction without the prior written consent of the parties, and prohibits it where other law - including RESPA - forbids it. The correct handling is written disclosure and consent, or declining the fee.

**Problem 10.** A licensee is asked by a buyer whether the seller has to accept a full-price offer. What is the answer?

*Working:* This one is answerable, carefully. A listing at a price is an invitation to make an offer rather than an offer itself, and a seller is generally not obliged to accept any particular one - but whether this seller is bound in these circumstances is a contract question the licensee should not resolve. The safe form of the answer describes the general structure, declines to apply it to the specific facts, and offers to get a lawyer's answer before the buyer relies on it.

## Key terms

**Associate broker** — an individual who has met broker qualifications but works under another broker rather than operating a brokerage.

**Broker company licence** — a licence issued to a business entity, which must designate a responsible individual broker.

**Market analysis** — an opinion of value prepared for another for compensation; licensed real estate activity in Michigan and not an appraisal.

**MiPLUS** — the Department's online licensing system for application, renewal, transfer, and modification.

**Principal associate broker** — an associate broker holding supervisory responsibility.

**Valuable consideration** — anything of value, not only money, the expectation of which brings an activity within the licence requirement.

## Practice problems

**Problem 1.** A property manager collects rent for eleven owners, keeps ten percent, and holds tenant security deposits in her personal savings account. Identify two separate regulatory problems.

*Working:* First, managing real estate for others as a business is licensed activity, so she needs a licence and must work under a broker unless she is one. Second, money held for others belongs in a broker trust account, not a personal account — the commingling problem in module 10. The second problem exists whether or not the first is solved.

**Problem 2.** A salesperson's brother is selling his house. The salesperson lists it, and the brother says he will "take care of" the salesperson afterwards rather than paying a commission. What is the analysis?

*Working:* The activity is licensed regardless — listing for another for expected valuable consideration. The informal payment arrangement does not remove it from the licence; it creates a second problem, because the compensation must flow through the broker rather than privately between the salesperson and the client. And the family relationship is a material fact that has to be disclosed to a buyer under module 05.

**Problem 3.** A Michigan salesperson is contacted by an Ohio broker whose client wants to buy a warehouse in Toledo, Michigan — there is no Toledo in Michigan, and the property turns out to be in Ohio. Who may do what?

*Working:* Michigan licensure authorises Michigan work. If the property is in Ohio, the Michigan salesperson may not act as the broker for the transaction; the arrangement is a referral to an Ohio-licensed brokerage, made through the Michigan salesperson's broker, with any fee flowing broker to broker. The lesson is that the location of the property, not the location of the parties, decides which licence is needed.

**Problem 4.** An unlicensed website operator argues that she is exempt because she never handles money and never attends closings. Evaluate.

*Working:* Neither fact is in the definition. The definition reaches negotiating, offering to list, and providing market analyses for others with the intent to receive compensation. Handling money and attending closings are not elements of it. The exemption she is reaching for does not exist.

## Module summary

**Michigan licenses activity, not titles.** Selling, offering to sell, listing, leasing, managing for others, negotiating, and providing market analyses — done for another with the intent to receive anything of value — is broker activity.

**The salesperson's authority runs through the broker.** Contracts are the brokerage's, money is the broker's to hold, files are the broker's, and supervision is an affirmative duty that independent-contractor status does not reduce.

**Exemptions follow reasons.** Owners act for themselves; attorneys practise law; court-appointed fiduciaries act under court authority; a power of attorney supplies authority for one transaction. None of them exempts anyone from fair housing or disclosure law, and none of them extends to an unlicensed assistant.

**LARA licenses, approves courses and instructors, investigates, and disciplines.** It does not adjudicate contract disputes, and trade associations are separate from it in both directions.

**A school that offers you the state examination questions is describing its own misconduct** — R 339.22155(b) makes it a disciplinary ground.

**Answer from the highest applicable source**, remember that forms are not law, that stricter office policy binds your conduct, and that a state law giving more protection than a federal one does not conflict with it.

**Refer whenever the answer would be a legal conclusion**, and refer properly: name the limit, give what you can, connect it to the deadline, and record it.
