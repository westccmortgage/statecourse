# Principles of Real Estate - Module 06

## Contracts, purchase agreements, and the Michigan seller disclosure

**Course version:** 1.0 submission draft
**Module time:** 4 clock hours (240 instructional minutes)
**Regulatory status:** Not approved. No Michigan prelicensure credit is available for this course.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module the student will be able to:

- State the elements of an enforceable real estate contract and explain why Michigan requires a writing.
- Distinguish offer, counter-offer, acceptance, and rejection, and identify the moment a contract forms.
- Explain what each principal clause of a Michigan purchase agreement does and what goes wrong when it is completed carelessly.
- Apply contingency mechanics — inspection, financing, appraisal, sale of an existing home — including deadlines and the consequences of missing one.
- Apply the Michigan Seller Disclosure Act: who must deliver, what the statement covers, when it must be delivered, and what a buyer may do if it is not.
- Apply the federal lead-based paint disclosure requirement to Michigan target housing.

## 1. Contract fundamentals

### 1.1 Elements

An enforceable contract requires:

**Competent parties.** Legal age and capacity. A contract with a minor is generally voidable by the minor; a contract with a person lacking capacity raises questions a licensee routes rather than resolves.

**Mutual assent.** An offer, and an acceptance of that offer on its terms.

**Consideration.** Something of value exchanged. In a purchase agreement, the promises themselves are the consideration; earnest money is not what makes the contract binding, which surprises many new licensees.

**Lawful object.** A contract to do something unlawful is unenforceable.

**Writing, for real estate.** Michigan's statute of frauds requires contracts for the sale of land, and leases longer than one year, to be in writing and signed by the party to be charged.

### 1.2 What the writing requirement means in practice

Three consequences follow, and each answers a question clients ask.

**Oral agreements about real estate are generally unenforceable.** A seller who says "we have a deal" on the telephone has not made one.

**Amendments must be written too.** A texted agreement to extend an inspection deadline may or may not satisfy the requirement, and the safe practice is a signed written amendment every time.

**Everything material belongs in the document.** Side agreements that are not in the writing are, at best, an argument.

### 1.3 Void, voidable, unenforceable

**Void** — no contract ever existed, as with an unlawful object.

**Voidable** — a contract one party may avoid, as with a minor's contract or one induced by fraud or duress.

**Unenforceable** — valid between the parties but which a court will not enforce, as with an oral land contract.

The distinction matters because a licensee should not describe a document as "not a contract" without knowing which of the three they mean, and should not be describing it at all where the answer decides a client's rights.

### 1.4 Performance and breach

Contracts end by **performance** — both sides do what they promised — or by agreement, or by breach.

Remedies for breach of a real estate contract include damages, **specific performance** (because land is unique, a court may order the sale to proceed), and forfeiture of earnest money where the contract provides for it. Which remedy is available in a given case is a legal question, and it is the question clients most want a licensee to answer when a deal falls apart.

#### Checkpoint 1.1

A buyer and seller agree terms by text message, including price and closing date, and the seller then accepts another offer. Does the buyer have a contract?

*Answer:* Possibly not, and the licensee must not answer it. The statute of frauds requires a signed writing, and whether an exchange of texts satisfies it is a legal question that depends on their content and on Michigan law about electronic signatures. The licensee's job was to get a signed agreement rather than let a negotiation live in a text thread.

## 2. Offer and acceptance

### 2.1 The offer

An offer is a definite proposal made with intent to be bound. A listing is not an offer; it is an invitation. A buyer's written purchase agreement, signed and delivered, is the offer.

An offer may be revoked any time before acceptance is communicated, unless it is held open by an option supported by consideration. It terminates by rejection, by counter-offer, by lapse of a stated time, or by the death or incapacity of the offeror.

### 2.2 Counter-offers

A response that changes any term is a counter-offer, and a counter-offer **rejects** the original. The original offer is dead and cannot be accepted afterwards.

This is the mechanic that produces the most avoidable disasters in residential practice. A seller who counters at a higher price and then, when the buyer walks, tries to "accept the original offer" has nothing to accept. A licensee who understands this will advise a seller to think carefully before countering an offer they would in fact have accepted.

### 2.3 Acceptance

Acceptance must be unqualified, must be of the offer as made, and must be **communicated** to the offeror. An accepted agreement sitting on a desk is not an acceptance until the other side learns of it.

The practical rule for licensees: deliver promptly, record the time of delivery, and do not tell a client they have a deal until acceptance has actually been communicated.

### 2.4 Multiple offers

Where several offers arrive, the seller decides. The licensee's obligations are to present them all, to advise on their terms rather than only their prices, to disclose the agency relationships involved, and to treat all buyers fairly regardless of protected characteristics.

What a licensee must not do is misrepresent the existence of other offers to drive a price. Saying there is another offer when there is not is a misrepresentation, and it is discoverable.

#### Checkpoint 2.1

A seller counters a $290,000 offer at $299,000. The buyer says nothing for two days, then says "fine, I accept the original $290,000." Where do the parties stand?

*Answer:* There is no contract at $290,000, because the counter-offer rejected that offer. What the buyer has now made is a new offer at $290,000, which the seller may accept or not. The practical lesson is for the licensee representing the seller, who should have made sure the seller understood, before countering, that they were giving up the ability to accept.

## 3. The Michigan purchase agreement

### 3.1 What the document has to do

A purchase agreement identifies the parties and the property, states the price and how it will be paid, states what is included and excluded, sets the timeline, allocates costs, states the deed to be delivered, states what happens if either party fails, and lists the conditions on which the buyer's obligation depends.

A licensee completes such a form; a licensee does not draft its provisions. Everything below is about completing it competently.

### 3.2 The clauses that decide the transaction

**Property description.** The legal description, from the deed, plus the address. Module 03.

**Price and terms.** Cash, conventional, FHA, VA, land contract. The financing type carries consequences beyond the buyer's own qualification: appraisal requirements and property condition standards differ.

**Earnest money.** Amount, who holds it, and what happens to it on default. Earnest money does not make the contract binding; it evidences commitment and funds a remedy.

**Inclusions and exclusions.** Module 02's fixture list, written out.

**Closing and possession.** These are separate dates and are treated as one by clients constantly. Possession after closing needs a written occupancy agreement, a daily rate, an escrow, and a clear statement of who insures what.

**Prorations.** Taxes, association dues, fuel. Michigan tax prorations are their own subject and are covered in module 10.

**Title and survey.** What the seller must deliver, and the buyer's window to object.

**Deed.** Which instrument the seller will give. Module 03.

**Default.** What each party may do if the other fails.

**Contingencies.** Section 4.

### 3.3 The blanks are where the failures happen

The printed paragraphs of a form have been drafted and reviewed. The blanks have not. Six recurring failures:

**Dates that do not work.** An inspection contingency expiring after the financing deadline, or a closing date on a Sunday.

**Ambiguous inclusions.** "All appliances" without saying which.

**Silent occupancy.** Closing and possession on different days with nothing about the interval.

**Blank spaces left blank.** A form with an unfilled deadline is a form with no deadline, or a form with a dispute about what was intended.

**Handwritten additions that contradict printed terms.** Which controls is a legal question; the licensee's job is to avoid creating it.

**Names that do not match title.** The sellers on the agreement must be the owners on the deed, in the same capacity.

### 3.4 Amendments

Any change after signature is an amendment, in writing, signed by both. Extending a deadline by email and closing on the strength of it is how a transaction that everyone thought was fine becomes a dispute.

#### Checkpoint 3.1

The purchase agreement gives closing on 15 October and possession on 1 November, and says nothing else. Identify what is missing.

*Answer:* Everything about the seventeen days. Who is responsible for the property, who insures it, what the seller pays for occupancy, what is held back to secure it, what happens if the seller does not leave, and what condition it must be in at possession. This is a written occupancy agreement, and it should have been prepared alongside the purchase agreement rather than discovered at closing.

## 4. Contingencies

### 4.1 How a contingency works

A contingency makes the buyer's obligation conditional on something happening by a date. Three elements: what must occur, by when, and what happens if it does not — usually the buyer may terminate and recover the earnest money.

A contingency that lapses without being exercised is generally waived, and the buyer becomes obligated. **The date is the whole mechanism**, and a licensee's diary is a professional instrument.

### 4.2 The inspection contingency

The buyer obtains inspections within a period and may then terminate, accept, or request repairs. A request for repairs is a negotiation, not a right, unless the contract says otherwise.

A licensee's role: recommend inspection in writing every time; never recommend a single inspector as the only choice; never attend as an interpreter of findings; never characterise a finding as minor; and get the buyer's decision documented before the deadline.

### 4.3 Financing and appraisal

A financing contingency protects a buyer who cannot obtain the loan described. It has to describe the loan — type, amount, rate ceiling — because a contingency for "a mortgage" is satisfied by any mortgage on any terms.

An appraisal contingency addresses value coming in below the price. In Michigan practice the parties then renegotiate, the buyer covers the gap, or the transaction ends according to what the contract says.

### 4.4 Sale of an existing home

A contingency on the sale of the buyer's current property is common and is the most likely to fail. It should state precisely what "sold" means — under contract, or closed — and it is usually paired with a clause allowing the seller to continue marketing and to require the buyer to remove the contingency within a short period if another offer arrives.

### 4.5 Other conditions worth writing

Well and septic evaluation where the county requires it; municipal point-of-sale inspection; association document review; zoning or use verification where the buyer's plan depends on it; survey.

Each of these is a question raised in earlier modules. The contingency is where the answer gets a deadline.

#### Checkpoint 4.1

A buyer's inspection contingency expires on the 12th. On the 13th the inspector's report reveals an active roof leak. What is the buyer's position?

*Answer:* Weak, and the licensee should have prevented it. Once the contingency lapses, the buyer has generally waived the right to terminate on condition. Whether anything survives — a seller's failure to disclose, a misrepresentation — is a legal question. The licensee's failure was scheduling the inspection so late that the report arrived after the deadline, which is a diary failure and nothing else.

## 5. The Michigan Seller Disclosure Act

### 5.1 What it requires

Michigan's Seller Disclosure Act requires a seller of residential real property of one to four dwelling units to deliver to the buyer a written **Seller's Disclosure Statement** on the statutory form, disclosing the condition of the property as the seller knows it.

The statement covers, among other things: appliances and systems and whether they are in working order; the roof, basement, and structural components; water and sewer; heating and cooling; known settling, flooding, or drainage problems; known environmental issues; known encroachments and easements; and known legal claims affecting the property.

### 5.2 Timing, and the buyer's remedy

The statement must be delivered before the parties are bound. Where it is delivered after an offer has been made, the buyer generally has a statutory right to terminate within a stated period after delivery.

**The licensee's rule is to get the statement to a buyer before the offer**, because a transaction that begins with a termination right hanging over it is unstable and because the buyer is entitled to the information when it can still affect their decision.

### 5.3 Who is exempt, and what exemption does not do

The Act exempts a number of transfers — including certain court-ordered transfers, transfers by a fiduciary administering an estate or trust, transfers between co-owners, and transfers to a spouse or lineal relative, among others. Bank-owned and estate sales are commonly exempt.

An exemption from the statutory statement does **not** exempt the seller from liability for fraud, and it does not exempt the licensee from disclosing known material facts. A buyer purchasing an exempt property is buying with less information, not with fewer rights against misrepresentation.

### 5.4 What the licensee does with it

Obtain it at listing. Read it. Compare it against what the licensee has seen and been told. Any inconsistency is a finding to raise with the seller, in writing.

What the licensee must never do is complete the form for the seller, suggest that an item be answered "unknown" when the seller knows, or transmit a form the licensee knows to be inaccurate. The statement is the seller's. The licensee's own disclosure duty runs alongside it and is not satisfied by it.

### 5.5 "Unknown" as an answer

The form permits "unknown", and it is a legitimate answer for something the seller genuinely does not know. It is not a place to put something the seller knows and would rather not say, and a seller who marks "unknown" beside a system they have repaired twice has created evidence rather than avoided it.

#### Checkpoint 5.1

A seller marks "unknown" for basement water, and the licensee has seen a sump pump, a water line on the wall, and a dehumidifier. What now?

*Answer:* Raise it with the seller directly and in writing, and explain that what the licensee has observed is inconsistent with the answer. If the seller will not correct the statement, the licensee still has an independent duty to disclose the known material fact to the buyer, and should escalate to the broker. The licensee cannot transmit a statement they know to be false.

## 6. Lead-based paint, and other required disclosures

### 6.1 The federal lead requirement

For **target housing** — most residential property built before 1978 — federal law requires the seller to:

- Disclose known lead-based paint and lead hazards, and provide any records or reports.
- Give the buyer the EPA pamphlet.
- Give the buyer a **10-day opportunity** to conduct a risk assessment or inspection, unless the buyer waives it in writing.
- Include the prescribed warning statement and signatures in the contract.

The licensee's obligations are separate and personal: ensure compliance, and retain the signed disclosure for three years. Failure carries federal penalties that fall on the licensee as well as the seller.

Exempt housing includes property built in 1978 or later, zero-bedroom units, some rentals, and housing designated for the elderly or persons with disabilities where no child under six resides.

### 6.2 Other disclosures a Michigan transaction meets

**Well and septic evaluation** where the county requires it — county-specific, and not uniform.

**Radon** — a Michigan issue in certain areas; a result once obtained is a known fact.

**Flood zone** — from the map, not from the seller's memory.

**Association documents** for a condominium or a property in an association.

**Homeowners' insurance claims history**, which affects a buyer's ability to insure.

**Utility and assessment status**, including any special assessment levied or pending.

### 6.3 The disclosure habit

The pattern that runs through this entire course: what you know, you disclose; what you are told, you attribute; what you have not verified, you do not assert; and what you did, you record on the day you did it.

A licensee who follows that pattern will occasionally lose a transaction they might have kept. They will not lose a licence.

### 6.4 Records

Michigan schools keep student records permanently (R 339.22154). Brokerages keep transaction files under their broker's policy and under the practical necessity that a complaint arrives long after memory fails. Lead disclosures have their own three-year federal retention.

The working rule for a new licensee is simple: keep everything, date everything, and assume that in two years the file will be the only thing that speaks for you.

#### Checkpoint 6.1

A 1962 house is under contract. The lead disclosure is signed at closing along with everything else. What is wrong?

*Answer:* The timing. The disclosure, the pamphlet, and the 10-day opportunity have to come before the buyer is obligated, not at closing. Signing at closing documents that the buyer never had the opportunity the rule exists to give them. The licensee has a personal federal exposure here and cannot fix it retroactively.

## Key terms

**Contingency** — a condition that must be satisfied by a date, failing which the buyer may generally terminate.

**Counter-offer** — a response changing a term, which rejects the original offer.

**Earnest money** — a deposit evidencing commitment; not what makes the contract binding.

**Specific performance** — a court order requiring the sale to proceed, available because land is unique.

**Statute of frauds** — the requirement that contracts for land, and leases over a year, be in writing and signed.

**Target housing** — most residential property built before 1978, subject to federal lead-based paint disclosure.

**Voidable** — a contract that one party may avoid.

## Practice problems

**Problem 1.** A seller accepts an offer but writes "subject to my attorney's approval" beside the signature. Is there a contract?

*Working:* That is a counter-offer, not an acceptance, because it adds a term. The buyer may accept or not. The licensee's job is to recognise it as a counter and treat it as one, rather than telling the buyer they have a deal.

**Problem 2.** An inspection contingency says the buyer may terminate if "the inspection is unsatisfactory." Evaluate the drafting.

*Working:* It is broad and subjective, which favours the buyer, and it is exactly the kind of provision a licensee should not be improvising. The point for a student is to notice that the words in the blank have consequences and that unclear words create arguments. What it actually means is a legal question.

**Problem 3.** A buyer waives the appraisal contingency in a competitive market and the appraisal comes in $18,000 low. What are the consequences?

*Working:* The buyer is generally obligated at the contract price and must cover the gap or default. The licensee's obligation was to ensure the buyer understood the risk before waiving, in writing. Advising on whether to waive is a business judgment the licensee may discuss; the legal consequence of the waiver is not.

**Problem 4.** A seller of an estate property refuses to complete a seller's disclosure statement, saying she never lived there. Is she required to?

*Working:* Transfers by a fiduciary administering an estate are among the Act's exemptions, so possibly not — but whether this transfer qualifies is a legal question for the estate's lawyer. And the exemption does not remove the licensee's own duty to disclose known material facts, or the seller's exposure for actual fraud.

**Problem 5.** The purchase agreement lists the seller as "John Smith" and title shows "John Smith and Mary Smith, husband and wife." What is the problem?

*Working:* Entireties property requires both signatures, from module 02. The agreement is signed by someone who cannot convey alone. This must be corrected before it becomes a closing-day discovery, and it is the kind of error prevented by comparing the agreement against the deed at the listing appointment.

**Problem 6.** A buyer asks whether she can back out because she has changed her mind, two days after signing and with all contingencies intact.

*Working:* Contingencies exist for their stated purposes, and "changed my mind" is not one of them. Whether any of the buyer's live contingencies could be exercised, and what the consequences of a default would be, are legal questions. The licensee's answer names the contingencies and their deadlines and refers the rest.

## Module summary

**Real estate contracts need a signed writing in Michigan**, and amendments do too.

**A counter-offer rejects the original**, which is the mechanic that ends more transactions than any other single misunderstanding.

**Acceptance must be communicated.** Nobody has a deal until the other side knows.

**The blanks are where forms fail** — dates that do not work, silent occupancy, ambiguous inclusions, names that do not match title.

**Contingencies are conditions with deadlines**, and a lapsed contingency is generally a waived one. The diary is a professional instrument.

**Michigan's Seller Disclosure Act requires the statutory statement for one to four dwelling units**, delivered before the parties are bound, with a termination right where it comes late. Exemptions exist and do not excuse fraud or the licensee's own duty.

**"Unknown" is for what the seller does not know**, not for what they would rather not say.

**Lead-based paint disclosure is federal, applies to pre-1978 housing, carries a 10-day opportunity, and exposes the licensee personally.** Three-year retention.
