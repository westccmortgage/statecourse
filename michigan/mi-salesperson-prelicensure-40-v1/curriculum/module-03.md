# Principles of Real Estate - Module 03

## Legal descriptions, transfer of title, and recording in Michigan

**Course version:** 1.0 submission draft
**Module time:** 4 clock hours (240 instructional minutes)
**Regulatory status:** Not approved. No Michigan prelicensure credit is available for this course.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module the student will be able to:

- Read the three legal-description systems used in Michigan and say which one a given description uses.
- Locate a parcel from a government survey description and explain how Michigan's meridian and base line organise the state.
- Distinguish the deed types used in Michigan and state what each one warrants and what it does not.
- Explain the elements a deed needs to be valid and the separate question of what recording accomplishes.
- Describe how Michigan's race-notice recording act decides priority between competing claims.
- Identify what title insurance covers, what it excludes, and why the licensee reads the commitment early.

## 1. Describing land

### 1.1 Why a street address is not a description

A street address identifies a mailbox. It can change, it can be duplicated, it does not describe boundaries, and it does not exist at all for vacant land. A legal description identifies the land itself, permanently and unambiguously, so that a court could locate it from the words alone.

Every deed, mortgage, and land contract carries one. A licensee does not draft legal descriptions — that is surveying and conveyancing work — but a licensee who cannot read one will not notice when the description on a purchase agreement does not match the property they showed.

### 1.2 Metes and bounds

The oldest method: a description that walks the boundary. It starts at a **point of beginning**, follows a series of courses and distances, and closes back at the point of beginning.

"Beginning at an iron pipe at the northeast corner of Lot 4; thence South 89 degrees 15 minutes East, 210.00 feet; thence South 00 degrees 45 minutes West, 145.50 feet; thence North 89 degrees 15 minutes West, 210.00 feet; thence North 00 degrees 45 minutes East, 145.50 feet to the point of beginning."

Three things a licensee should notice:

**It must close.** A description that does not return to its point of beginning is defective.

**Monuments outrank measurements.** Where a description references a physical monument — an iron pipe, a river, a road — and the stated distance conflicts with it, the monument generally controls. This is why old descriptions referencing a "large oak" create problems when the oak is gone.

**It is used in Michigan for irregular parcels**, including waterfront and split rural land, which is precisely where boundary disputes are most common.

### 1.3 Government survey

Most of Michigan is described by the rectangular survey system. Two reference lines organise the whole state: the **Michigan Meridian**, running north and south, and the **Michigan Base Line**, running east and west. Township lines and range lines are laid out from them.

- A **township** is six miles square, containing 36 **sections**.
- A **section** is one mile square and contains **640 acres**.
- Sections are numbered 1 to 36, beginning in the northeast corner and running boustrophedonically — west along the top row, then east along the next, and so on.

A description reads from the smallest unit outward: "The Northwest Quarter of the Southeast Quarter of Section 14, Town 2 North, Range 11 West."

**Computing acreage** is division. 640 acres divided by 4 is 160 for a quarter; a quarter of that quarter is 40 acres. The NW¼ of the SE¼ is 640 ÷ 4 ÷ 4 = 40 acres. Each "of" in the description is another division by the fraction that precedes it.

### 1.4 Lot and block

Recorded plats divide land into numbered lots within blocks in a named subdivision, and the plat itself is recorded with the register of deeds. The description then reads "Lot 17, Block 3, Sunnybrook Estates, according to the recorded plat thereof as recorded in Liber 22, Page 8, Kent County Records."

This is the most common description in Michigan residential practice. The plat is a public document, it shows easements and setbacks, and a licensee who pulls the plat before listing a subdivision property will often find the answer to a question the seller could not answer.

Michigan's **Land Division Act** governs how land is divided and platted, and division of a parcel without complying with it creates a real problem for a buyer. A licensee asked to sell "the back forty" off a larger parcel is looking at a land-division question that belongs with a lawyer and the municipality before anything is marketed.

#### Checkpoint 1.1

A purchase agreement describes the property only as "412 Maple Street, Ypsilanti." Is that adequate?

*Answer:* No. A street address does not identify boundaries and will not support a conveyance. The agreement needs the legal description, which comes from the deed, the tax record, or the title commitment. A licensee who submits an offer with only a street address has created work for the title company at best and a dispute about what was sold at worst.

## 2. Reading a description in practice

### 2.1 Where descriptions come from

The description on a new deed comes from the prior deed, the plat, or a survey. It is not composed fresh for each sale. That is why an error propagates: a description mis-typed in 1988 is still being copied.

The licensee's role is comparison, not composition. Compare the description on the tax record, the description on the seller's deed, and the description on the title commitment. If those three do not agree, the transaction has a problem that must be solved before closing, and it is solved by the title company and counsel.

### 2.2 Surveys

A survey locates the boundaries on the ground and shows improvements relative to them. A **mortgage survey** or location sketch is a limited product. A **boundary survey** with staked corners is the thorough one.

Recommend a survey when: the boundary is not obvious; there is a fence, drive, or structure near a line; the description is metes and bounds; the parcel is being divided; there is waterfront; or the buyer intends to build.

A licensee who recommends a survey and records the recommendation has done their job even if the buyer declines. A licensee who assures a buyer that the fence is the line has practised surveying and law in one sentence.

### 2.3 Acreage and square footage

Two numbers that get licensees in trouble.

**Acreage** stated in a description or a tax record is often approximate, and "more or less" appears in descriptions for a reason. Advertising acreage that came from an assessor's record without saying where it came from is a representation.

**Square footage** of a house is measured differently by assessors, appraisers, and builders — above-grade finished area is not the same as total finished area, and a finished basement is counted by some and not others. State the source of any figure you publish.

### 2.4 Legal description on the listing

The routine that prevents most problems: obtain the seller's deed at the listing appointment; compare it to the tax record; note any discrepancy in the file; and use the deed's description on the listing and on the purchase agreement.

#### Checkpoint 2.1

The tax record shows 2.5 acres; the deed's metes and bounds description computes to about 2.1. Which goes in the advertisement?

*Answer:* Neither, unqualified. The discrepancy is itself the finding, and it goes to the title company and probably to a surveyor. If the property is advertised at all before it is resolved, the acreage should be described by its source — "tax record shows 2.5 acres; survey recommended" — rather than asserted. Publishing 2.5 as fact after seeing the deed is a representation the licensee knows is doubtful.

## 3. Deeds

### 3.1 What a deed does

A deed is the written instrument that transfers an interest in real property from a **grantor** to a **grantee**. It is not the ownership; it is the evidence and the act of conveyance.

### 3.2 Elements of a valid deed

Michigan requires, in substance:

- A **competent grantor** — of legal age and sound mind.
- A **grantee named** with reasonable certainty.
- **Words of conveyance** — the granting clause.
- An adequate **legal description**.
- The grantor's **signature**, properly witnessed and acknowledged for recording.
- **Delivery** to and **acceptance** by the grantee.

Two of these deserve emphasis because they are where deeds fail.

**Delivery** is the act that makes a deed effective, and it requires the grantor's present intent to convey. A deed signed and left in a drawer conveys nothing. A deed handed over with instructions not to record until the grantor dies raises a delivery question that has produced a great deal of litigation.

**Consideration** is recited but need not be substantial; Michigan deeds commonly recite a nominal sum. A gift deed is valid.

### 3.3 The deed types used in Michigan

**Warranty deed.** The grantor warrants title against all defects, including those arising before the grantor owned the property. This is the strongest conveyance and the ordinary instrument in a Michigan residential sale.

**Covenant deed** (limited or special warranty). The grantor warrants only against defects arising during the grantor's own ownership. Common from institutional sellers, relocation companies, and estates.

**Quit claim deed.** Conveys whatever interest the grantor has, with no warranty at all — possibly nothing. Used between family members, to clear a cloud, to transfer between entities, and in divorce. A quit claim is not a defective deed; it is a deed that makes no promises, and a buyer accepting one in an arm's-length purchase should be asking why.

**Deed in lieu of foreclosure.** A borrower conveys to the lender to avoid foreclosure. Whether it extinguishes the debt is a legal question with real consequences for the borrower.

**Personal representative's deed, trustee's deed, sheriff's deed.** Deeds given in a representative or official capacity, each carrying its own authority questions.

### 3.4 What the deed does not do

A deed does not prove the grantor owned anything. A grantor can execute a warranty deed for property they do not own; the deed is valid on its face and the warranty is a promise that will have to be enforced later, against a person who may have nothing.

This is the entire reason title examination and title insurance exist, and it is why a licensee's answer to "do I really need title insurance?" is never "no."

#### Checkpoint 3.1

A seller offers a quit claim deed at closing because "it's simpler." The buyer's agent is asked whether that is acceptable. What is the answer?

*Answer:* That it is a question for the buyer's lawyer and the title company, and that the licensee cannot advise on it — but also that the purchase agreement specifies the deed to be delivered, and a change to it is a change to the contract. The licensee's job is to notice that the seller is proposing a materially different instrument and to stop the substitution from happening quietly at the closing table.

## 4. Other ways title moves

### 4.1 Involuntary transfer

**Foreclosure** — Michigan uses both foreclosure by advertisement and judicial foreclosure, and the redemption period after a sheriff's sale is a Michigan-specific fact with major consequences for a buyer's ability to take possession. The length of the redemption period varies with the circumstances, and a licensee must not state it from memory.

**Tax foreclosure** — Michigan counties foreclose for unpaid property taxes on a statutory schedule, and the process extinguishes most interests. A property acquired through it may carry title questions.

**Condemnation** — eminent domain, with compensation.

**Adverse possession** — occupation that is actual, open, notorious, exclusive, hostile, and continuous for the statutory period can ripen into title. Whether it has is a question only a court answers, and a licensee who tells a client that a long-used strip "is theirs now" has given a legal opinion.

**Escheat** — to the state when there are no heirs.

### 4.2 Transfer on death

Property passes by **will** or by **intestate succession** when there is none. It may also pass outside probate — by survivorship in a joint tenancy or tenancy by the entirety, by a trust, or under Michigan's ladybird deed arrangement.

The licensee's operational question is always the same: who is authorised to sign, and what document proves it? Letters of authority for a personal representative, a certificate of trust for a trustee, a death certificate for a survivorship claim. Gather; do not interpret.

### 4.3 Michigan transfer taxes

A conveyance of Michigan real property is generally subject to a **state real estate transfer tax** and a **county real estate transfer tax**, customarily paid by the seller. Exemptions exist and are listed in the statutes.

Two practical points: the tax is calculated on the value conveyed, and it appears on the closing statement in module 10; and whether a particular transfer is exempt is a question for the title company or counsel, not a calculation the licensee performs for the client.

#### Checkpoint 4.1

A buyer is interested in a property that was sold at a sheriff's sale four months ago. What is the first thing the licensee should establish?

*Answer:* Whether the redemption period has expired, because until it does the former owner may have a right to redeem, and what the buyer would be purchasing is uncertain. The licensee should not state the length of the period from memory; they should route the question to the title company and to counsel and get the answer in writing before the buyer commits.

## 5. Recording and priority

### 5.1 What recording does

Recording a deed with the county **register of deeds** does not make the deed valid — delivery does. Recording gives **constructive notice** to the world of the interest, and constructive notice is what protects the holder against later claims.

The practical effect: an unrecorded deed is good between the parties and dangerous against everyone else.

### 5.2 Michigan's race-notice rule

Michigan is a **race-notice** state. A subsequent purchaser who takes **in good faith, for value, and without notice** of a prior unrecorded interest, and who **records first**, prevails over the earlier unrecorded interest.

Both halves are required, which is what "race-notice" means. A later buyer who knew about the earlier deed is not protected however fast they record. A later buyer who did not know but recorded second is not protected either.

**Notice** comes in three forms and a student should hold all three:

- **Actual notice** — you were told, or you read it.
- **Constructive notice** — it was recorded, so the law charges you with knowledge whether or not you looked.
- **Inquiry notice** — something visible would have prompted a reasonable person to investigate. A tenant in possession is the classic example: a buyer who never asks who is living there is charged with what asking would have revealed.

Inquiry notice is why a licensee walks the property and asks about occupants. It is not curiosity; it is the buyer's protection.

### 5.3 The chain of title

A title examination traces conveyances from the present owner backwards through recorded instruments. Gaps, missing signatures, deeds from people who never appear as grantees, and unreleased mortgages are the defects that surface.

A **cloud on title** is any claim or apparent defect that impairs marketability. Clouds are cleared by corrective deeds, releases, affidavits, or by a **quiet title** action in court.

### 5.4 What gets recorded

Deeds, mortgages, discharges, land contracts and their memoranda, easements, liens, notices of commencement, judgments, plats, and restrictive covenants. Anything affecting title, recorded, becomes constructive notice.

#### Checkpoint 5.1

A buyer's walkthrough reveals a tenant living in what was represented as a vacant house. Beyond the practical problem, what legal significance does the tenant's presence carry?

*Answer:* Possession is inquiry notice. A buyer who proceeds without asking the occupant what their rights are may be charged with whatever an enquiry would have revealed — including an unrecorded lease or an unrecorded purchase interest. The licensee's job is to stop, document what was found, and get the question to the title company and counsel before closing.

## 6. Title insurance

### 6.1 What it is

Title insurance indemnifies against loss from defects in title that existed **before** the policy date. It is backward-looking, unlike other insurance, and it is paid for once.

Two policies exist. The **owner's policy** protects the buyer, generally for the purchase price. The **lender's policy** protects the mortgage lender for the loan amount and protects the buyer not at all.

### 6.2 The commitment, and why it is read early

Before closing, the title company issues a **commitment**: what it will insure, and on what conditions. It has two critical schedules.

**Schedule B-I: requirements.** What must happen before the policy issues — payoffs, releases, signatures, documents.

**Schedule B-II: exceptions.** What the policy will *not* cover. Easements, restrictions, mineral severances, encroachments a survey would show, rights of parties in possession.

**The exceptions are the point.** A buyer who reads only the front page learns that they are insured; a buyer who reads Schedule B-II learns what they are buying. Reading the commitment when it arrives, rather than the night before closing, is one of the clearest markers of a competent licensee.

### 6.3 What it does not cover

Standard exclusions include defects the buyer created or knew about and did not disclose, governmental regulation including zoning, eminent domain, and — unless a survey exception is removed — matters an accurate survey would show. Title insurance is not an inspection, not a survey, and not a guarantee of value.

### 6.4 The licensee's routine

Obtain the commitment as soon as it is issued. Read Schedules B-I and B-II. Compare the legal description against the deed and the listing. Identify anything that would surprise the client. Send it to the client with a plain-language note saying what it is, that the licensee cannot interpret it, and that questions go to the title company or a lawyer — before the contingency date.

That routine takes twenty minutes and prevents most of the title surprises that end up in complaints.

#### Checkpoint 6.1

Schedule B-II lists "an easement for ingress and egress in favour of the adjoining parcel, recorded in Liber 1108, Page 421." The buyer asks whether the neighbour can drive across the yard. What does the licensee do?

*Answer:* Obtain the recorded document and give it to the buyer, and tell them plainly that what it permits is a legal question for their lawyer and that it needs answering before the contingency expires. The licensee may say what the exception is and where to find it. The licensee may not say what it means or how far it extends.

## Key terms

**Chain of title** — the recorded sequence of conveyances leading to the present owner.

**Cloud on title** — an apparent defect or claim impairing marketability.

**Constructive notice** — knowledge the law charges a person with because an instrument is recorded.

**Delivery** — the grantor's act, with present intent to convey, that makes a deed effective.

**Inquiry notice** — knowledge charged to a person because visible circumstances would have prompted investigation.

**Point of beginning** — the starting and closing point of a metes and bounds description.

**Race-notice** — Michigan's recording rule: a good-faith purchaser for value without notice who records first prevails.

**Schedule B-II** — the exceptions schedule of a title commitment; what the policy will not cover.

## Practice problems

**Problem 1.** Compute the acreage of "the South Half of the Northwest Quarter of the Southeast Quarter" of a section.

*Working:* Start at 640. Southeast Quarter: 640 ÷ 4 = 160. Northwest Quarter of that: 160 ÷ 4 = 40. South Half of that: 40 ÷ 2 = 20 acres.

**Problem 2.** A seller signed a deed to his daughter in 2019, told her about it, and kept it in his safe. He died in 2026 and the deed was found. Did the daughter get the property in 2019?

*Working:* Probably not, because delivery requires present intent to convey and a deed retained by the grantor generally has not been delivered. But this is precisely a legal question with a large consequence, and the licensee's role is to recognise it, decline to answer it, and make sure it reaches a probate lawyer before anyone lists the house.

**Problem 3.** A buyer takes a deed and does not record it. Three months later the seller deeds the same property to someone else who knows nothing about the first sale and records immediately. Who prevails in Michigan?

*Working:* The second buyer, under the race-notice rule — good faith, for value, without notice, recorded first. The first buyer's remedy is against the seller, who may have nothing. This is the whole argument for recording promptly, and it is why title companies record at closing rather than afterwards.

**Problem 4.** A title commitment's Schedule B-I requires a discharge of a mortgage paid off in 2011. The seller says it was paid. What has to happen?

*Working:* The discharge has to be recorded. A paid mortgage that was never discharged of record still appears in the chain, and the title company will not insure over it. The seller's recollection is not a document. The licensee's job is to raise it immediately, because obtaining a discharge from a lender that may have merged twice since 2011 takes time the transaction may not have.

**Problem 5.** A listing describes a parcel using a metes and bounds description that does not close by four feet. What is the significance?

*Working:* The description is defective and the parcel it purports to describe cannot be located with certainty. This is not a rounding issue; it is a title problem that requires a surveyor and probably a corrective instrument. Marketing the property with the defective description unresolved risks a contract for something nobody can identify.

**Problem 6.** A buyer asks why he needs an owner's policy when the lender is already requiring title insurance.

*Working:* Because the lender's policy insures the lender, for the loan balance, and pays the lender. If a defect surfaces, the lender is made whole and the buyer is not. The licensee can say that much — it is a description of the two products, not legal advice — and should then leave the decision to the buyer with the title company available to explain further.

## Module summary

**A legal description identifies land; an address identifies a mailbox.** Michigan uses metes and bounds, government survey from the Michigan Meridian and Base Line, and lot and block from recorded plats.

**Sections are 640 acres and descriptions divide from the smallest unit outward.** Each "of" is another division.

**A deed needs a competent grantor, a named grantee, words of conveyance, a description, a signature, and delivery.** Delivery is the element that fails most often.

**Warranty, covenant, and quit claim differ in what is promised, not in what is conveyed.** A quit claim makes no promises, and a buyer accepting one in an arm's-length sale should ask why.

**Recording does not validate a deed; it gives notice.** Michigan is race-notice: good faith, for value, without notice, recorded first.

**Notice comes in three forms**, and inquiry notice is why a licensee asks who is living in the house.

**Title insurance is backward-looking, and the exceptions schedule is the document that matters.** Read it when it arrives, send it to the client, and route the interpretation.
