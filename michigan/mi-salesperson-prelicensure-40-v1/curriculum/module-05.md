# Principles of Real Estate - Module 05

## Agency, the service provision agreement, and disclosure

**Course version:** 1.0 submission draft
**Module time:** 4 clock hours (240 instructional minutes)
**Regulatory status:** Not approved. No Michigan prelicensure credit is available for this course.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module the student will be able to:

- Explain what a service provision agreement is under Michigan law and what it must contain.
- State the duties a licensee owes a client and how they differ from what is owed a customer.
- Apply the agency disclosure requirement at the correct moment in a Michigan transaction.
- Distinguish seller agency, buyer agency, dual agency, and designated agency, and explain what informed consent requires.
- Identify a material fact and explain the licensee's obligation to disclose it, including the limits set by Michigan's disclosure rules.
- Recognise the conflicts that arise from referral fees, personal interests, and team arrangements, and handle each correctly.

## 1. Agency, and Michigan's service provision agreement

### 1.1 What agency is

Agency is a relationship in which one person, the agent, acts on behalf of and subject to the control of another, the principal, in dealings with third parties. In real estate the principal is the client, the agent is the brokerage, and the salesperson acts for the brokerage.

That last point, from module 01, has consequences here. The agency relationship is between the **client and the broker**. The salesperson performs it. When a salesperson leaves, the agency does not leave with them.

### 1.2 The service provision agreement

Michigan's rules use the term **service provision agreement** for the written agreement under which a licensee provides services. R 339.22131 governs it, and R 339.22135 prohibits a licensee from becoming a party to a **net** service provision agreement — an arrangement where the licensee keeps everything above a figure the owner names — as a means of securing a commission.

The prohibition on net arrangements is worth understanding rather than memorising. A net listing puts the licensee's interest in direct opposition to the client's: every dollar of price above the owner's number is the licensee's, so the licensee is rewarded for the owner underestimating their own property. Michigan bars it because it corrupts the advice.

### 1.3 What the agreement should establish

Whatever a particular brokerage's form looks like, a service provision agreement addresses:

- Who the parties are — the client and the **brokerage**.
- What services are provided.
- The term, and how it ends.
- Compensation: how much, from whom, and when it is earned.
- Whether the representation is exclusive.
- What happens if the client transacts with someone introduced during the term, after it ends.
- The agency relationship being created.

### 1.4 Types of listing

**Exclusive right to sell.** The brokerage is paid if the property sells during the term, whoever finds the buyer, including the owner.

**Exclusive agency.** The brokerage is paid unless the owner sells it themselves.

**Open listing.** Non-exclusive; the brokerage is paid only if it produces the buyer.

Buyer representation agreements mirror the same structure. Which one is used is a business decision; what matters for the licensee is that the client understands which they have signed, because the difference decides whether they owe a commission on a sale they arranged themselves.

#### Checkpoint 1.1

A seller says he will take $200,000 and the licensee can keep anything above it. What is the licensee's answer?

*Answer:* That Michigan prohibits it. R 339.22135 forbids a licensee becoming a party to a net service provision agreement as a means of securing a commission. The correct response is to explain that the compensation will be stated as an amount or a percentage, and to prepare a market analysis so the seller sets the price on information rather than on a floor.

## 2. Duties

### 2.1 To the client

The duties a Michigan licensee owes a client are the ordinary agency duties, and the useful habit is to be able to name them and say what each forbids.

**Loyalty.** Put the client's interests first. This forbids acting for your own account against the client, and it is why a licensee's personal interest in a transaction has to be disclosed.

**Obedience.** Follow lawful instructions. Not unlawful ones — a client's instruction to conceal a material defect or to screen buyers by protected class is not to be followed, and the licensee's obligation is to refuse and, where appropriate, to withdraw.

**Disclosure.** Tell the client what you know that is relevant to their decision. Including things they will not want to hear.

**Confidentiality.** Do not reveal the client's negotiating position, motivation, or financial circumstances. This duty survives the end of the relationship.

**Accounting.** Handle and account for the client's money and documents properly — the trust account rules in module 10.

**Reasonable care and skill.** Perform competently, which includes knowing the limits of your competence and referring beyond them.

### 2.2 To everyone else

A person on the other side of the transaction is not owed loyalty or confidentiality. They are owed:

**Honesty.** No misrepresentation, no half-truths.

**Disclosure of known material facts** about the property, subject to the rules in section 5.

**Fair dealing**, and the equal treatment required by the civil-rights statutes in module 07.

The most common error a new licensee makes is treating the second list as though it were nothing. It is not nothing. A licensee who tells a buyer something untrue about a property has a problem whether or not the buyer is their client.

### 2.3 Confidentiality in practice

The information most often disclosed by accident: that the seller is divorcing, has already bought elsewhere, has had two deals fall through, will take less, is behind on payments, or is moving for a job on a date certain. Any of it, said casually to a cooperating licensee, damages the client's position.

The rule to build early: nothing about the client's circumstances or motivation leaves the licensee's mouth without the client's authority. Not to be helpful, not to build rapport, not to move a stalled negotiation.

### 2.4 When the relationship ends

An agency ends by completion, expiration, mutual agreement, revocation, renunciation, or by operation of law. Two duties outlive it: **confidentiality**, indefinitely, and **accounting** for anything held.

#### Checkpoint 2.1

A seller instructs a licensee not to present any offer below the asking price. Must the licensee obey?

*Answer:* Instructions must be lawful, and this one is problematic rather than unlawful on its face — but a licensee generally must present all offers, and an instruction to suppress them conflicts with the duty of disclosure to the client. The correct handling is to explain that all offers will be presented, put the instruction and the response in writing, and escalate to the broker. Silently discarding offers is how a licensee ends up in front of the Department.

## 3. Agency disclosure

### 3.1 The requirement

Michigan requires that the agency relationship be disclosed in writing to a prospective buyer or seller, and that it be done **before the licensee discloses confidential information** — in practice, at the first substantive contact about a specific property or transaction, not at the closing table and not when the offer is written.

The disclosure identifies whom the licensee represents, and the person receiving it acknowledges receipt. The acknowledgment is the licensee's evidence that it happened.

### 3.2 What "before confidential information" means

A licensee showing a property to an unrepresented buyer is in the position where this matters most. The buyer, being human, starts explaining what they can afford, why they are moving, and what they will pay. Every one of those is information the buyer would not volunteer if they understood the licensee represents the seller.

The disclosure exists to prevent that misunderstanding, and its timing is what makes it work. Given at the front door, it does its job. Given after the buyer has explained their maximum, it documents a failure.

### 3.3 The customer who thinks they are a client

The single most common agency problem in residential practice is the unrepresented buyer who believes their seller's-agent contact is "their agent" because that person has been helpful, responsive, and pleasant.

The licensee's obligations are to disclose clearly at the outset, to say so again when the buyer's language suggests they have forgotten — "just to be clear, I represent the seller here" — and to record having done both. Helpfulness is not representation, but a buyer cannot be expected to know that unless they are told plainly.

### 3.4 Recording the disclosure

Signed acknowledgment in the file, dated. Where a person declines to sign, the licensee records that the disclosure was made, when, and that signature was declined. An unrecorded disclosure is, months later, indistinguishable from one that never happened.

#### Checkpoint 3.1

At an open house a visitor says, "We're pre-approved to $400,000 but we'd go to $415,000 for the right house." The licensee represents the seller. What should have happened, and what happens now?

*Answer:* The disclosure should have been made when the visitor began discussing a specific transaction, which is before that sentence. Now: the licensee tells them immediately that they represent the seller, records the disclosure and the time, and must recognise that the duty of disclosure to the client means the seller is entitled to know material information the licensee has learned. The prevention is a disclosure at the door.

## 4. Forms of representation

### 4.1 Seller agency

The brokerage represents the seller, owes them the full duties, and owes the buyer honesty and disclosure of known material facts.

### 4.2 Buyer agency

The brokerage represents the buyer. Compensation may come from the seller, from the listing brokerage, or from the buyer directly, and the source of compensation does not determine who is represented — a point that has to be explained to clients rather than assumed.

Buyer representation agreements have become the norm and should be in place before substantive work begins, both because the client is entitled to know what they have and because a licensee working without one has no agreement about compensation.

### 4.3 Dual agency

Dual agency arises where one brokerage represents both the seller and the buyer in the same transaction. It is permitted in Michigan **only with the informed written consent of both parties**.

The problem with dual agency is not the paperwork; it is that the duties become impossible to perform fully. A dual agent cannot advocate for the seller's highest price and the buyer's lowest, cannot advise either on negotiating strategy against the other, and cannot use what each has told them in confidence. What remains is honest, competent facilitation — which is a real service, but it is not what most clients think they are getting when they hear "my agent."

**Informed consent means the client understood what they gave up.** A signature obtained by saying "this is just a form we all use" is not informed consent, and it will not look like one in a complaint.

### 4.4 Designated agency

A brokerage may designate one licensee to represent the seller and another to represent the buyer, so that each client has an advocate while the brokerage as a whole has both. Michigan practice permits this with disclosure and consent, and the supervising broker's position — knowing both sides — has to be handled carefully.

### 4.5 Teams

Team arrangements inside a brokerage raise the same questions in a form clients find harder to see. Two members of the same team on opposite sides of a transaction are, at brokerage level, the same agency. Advertising that presents a team as though it were an independent firm compounds the confusion, which is why the identification requirements in section 6 matter.

#### Checkpoint 4.1

A listing agent's own buyer client wants to buy the listing. Both parties say they are "fine with it." What must happen before the offer is written?

*Answer:* Written informed consent from both, obtained after a real explanation of what changes — no advocacy on price against the other, no use of confidential information from either, no negotiating advice. The broker should be involved in the decision, and designated agency may be the better structure if the brokerage supports it. What must not happen is a form signed at the kitchen table with the sentence "this is standard."

## 5. Material facts

### 5.1 What must be disclosed

A material fact is one that would affect a reasonable person's decision to buy, or the price they would pay. Known material facts about the property's condition must be disclosed to a buyer, whether or not the buyer is a client, and whether or not asked.

Michigan residential sellers have their own statutory disclosure obligation under the Seller Disclosure Act, covered in module 06. The licensee's obligation is separate and is not discharged by the seller's form: a licensee who knows something the seller omitted is not relieved by the omission.

### 5.2 What is not required

Michigan law addresses several categories that buyers ask about and that licensees must handle carefully. In particular, the fact that a property was the site of a death, a suicide, or a crime, or was occupied by a person with a condition not transmissible through occupancy, is not a material fact requiring disclosure under Michigan law, and disclosing certain such information can itself raise civil-rights problems.

The licensee's correct handling of a direct question in this area is not to lie, and not to volunteer. It is to say that the licensee does not provide information of that kind, that the buyer may make their own enquiries, and — where the question touches a protected characteristic — that the licensee cannot participate in it. Section 6 of module 07 returns to this.

### 5.3 Puffing, misrepresentation, and silence

**Puffing** is opinion — "a wonderful family home." It is not actionable, and it is also not useful.

**Misrepresentation** is a false statement of fact. It does not require intent to deceive; a licensee who repeats a seller's inaccurate square footage has misrepresented it.

**Silence** becomes misrepresentation when there is a duty to speak, which for known material defects there is.

The safe practice: state facts with their source, and stop. "The seller states the roof was replaced in 2019; the buyer should verify" is defensible. "The roof is five years old" is a representation the licensee cannot support.

### 5.4 Stigma, condition, and the honest answer

Buyers ask questions licensees cannot answer: whether the neighbourhood is "good", whether the schools are "the best", whether the neighbours are "nice". Each of those, answered, is either an opinion presented as fact or — far worse — an invitation to the steering problem in module 07.

The professional answer directs the buyer to sources: the district publishes data, the municipality publishes crime statistics, the buyer may visit at different hours. Giving the buyer the means to answer their own question is a service. Answering it for them is a liability.

#### Checkpoint 5.1

A seller tells the licensee privately that the basement floods every spring but has been dry since a drain was installed. The seller does not want it disclosed. What must the licensee do?

*Answer:* Disclose it. Known material facts about condition are disclosed to the buyer, and a history of flooding is material regardless of the remedy. The seller's instruction is not a lawful instruction the licensee must obey. The correct sequence is to explain that it will be disclosed, escalate to the broker, document the conversation, and withdraw from the representation if the seller insists on concealment.

## 6. Conflicts and advertising

### 6.1 The licensee's own interest

A licensee buying, selling, or holding an interest in a property involved in a transaction must disclose it. This includes buying for their own account, buying through an entity, a family member's interest, and an interest in a business the client is being referred to.

R 339.22137 restricts a licensee entitled to a commission from also receiving a referral fee or other valuable consideration in connection with the same transaction — for placing a loan, or from a title, home warranty, abstract, or other settlement service provider — without the prior written consent of the parties, and forbids it where other law, including the federal Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act, prohibits it.

The practical rule: any money flowing to the licensee from anyone other than their broker, in connection with a client's transaction, is disclosed in writing before it flows.

### 6.2 Referrals and the appearance of independence

A licensee who recommends an inspector, a lender, a title company, or a lawyer is trusted to be recommending on quality. Where the licensee has a financial relationship with the person recommended, the recommendation is not independent, and the client is entitled to know before relying on it.

Recommending several rather than one, and saying plainly that the client may use anyone, is both good practice and good protection.

### 6.3 Advertising

Michigan requires advertising to identify the brokerage. A team name, a personal brand, or a slogan that does not identify the licensed firm is the recurring violation.

Beyond identification, three rules:

**Accuracy.** Every factual claim in an advertisement — square footage, acreage, taxes, school district, zoning, water frontage — is a representation. State the source or verify it.

**Authority.** A licensee advertises property with the owner's authority, through their broker. Advertising a property the brokerage does not have a listing on is misleading whatever the intention.

**Fair housing.** Advertising is where fair-housing violations most often appear, and module 07 covers the language and imagery rules in detail.

### 6.4 Online and social advertising

The rules do not change with the medium. A social post about a listing is advertising, identification requirements apply, and screenshots outlive deleted posts. Automated syndication does not relieve the licensee of responsibility for what a third-party site publishes from their data — the discipline is to get the data right at the source.

#### Checkpoint 6.1

A licensee posts a listing on social media using only her personal brand name and mobile number, and describes the home as "perfect for a young family in the best school district in the county." Identify three problems.

*Answer:* The brokerage is not identified. "Best school district" is an unverifiable claim presented as fact. And "perfect for a young family" expresses a preference based on familial status, which is a fair-housing violation under both federal and Michigan law — module 07. The post is a compact example of how three separate rules are broken in a single sentence.

## Key terms

**Customer** — a party to a transaction who is not represented, owed honesty and disclosure of known material facts but not loyalty or confidentiality.

**Designated agency** — one brokerage representing both sides through separate designated licensees.

**Dual agency** — one brokerage representing both parties, permitted in Michigan only with informed written consent of both.

**Informed consent** — agreement given after a real explanation of what is being given up.

**Material fact** — a fact that would affect a reasonable person's decision to buy or the price they would pay.

**Net service provision agreement** — an arrangement in which the licensee retains all proceeds above a figure set by the owner; prohibited by R 339.22135.

**Puffing** — opinion or sales talk, not a statement of fact.

**Service provision agreement** — the written agreement under which a Michigan licensee provides services.

## Practice problems

**Problem 1.** A buyer's agent learns from the listing agent's assistant that the seller must close by 30 June because of a job transfer. May the buyer's agent tell their client?

*Working:* Yes, and probably must. The buyer's agent owes the buyer disclosure of information relevant to their decision. The failure belongs to the listing side, which disclosed its client's motivation. This problem is included to show confidentiality from the other direction: the duty binds the client's own agent, and information that escapes is not put back.

**Problem 2.** A licensee lists a property and, three weeks later, decides to buy it herself through an LLC she owns. What is required?

*Working:* Full written disclosure of her interest to the seller, her broker's involvement, and — realistically — a hard look at whether she can continue to advise the seller on price while bidding on it. The loyalty problem is not solved by disclosure alone; disclosure is the minimum, and withdrawal from the representation is often the honest answer.

**Problem 3.** A seller instructs a licensee not to show the property to buyers from a particular country of origin. What does the licensee do?

*Working:* Refuse, explain that the instruction is unlawful under federal and Michigan civil-rights law, document the instruction and the refusal, notify the broker, and terminate the representation if the seller persists. Obedience runs only to lawful instructions, and following this one exposes the licensee personally. Module 07 covers the statutes.

**Problem 4.** A licensee receives $400 from a title company for each closing she directs to them, and discloses it verbally at the closing table.

*Working:* Too late and in the wrong form. R 339.22137 requires prior written consent where a licensee entitled to a commission also receives consideration from a settlement service provider, and RESPA independently prohibits certain payments outright. Verbal disclosure at closing, after the client has relied on the recommendation, is not consent.

**Problem 5.** An unrepresented buyer asks a listing agent, "Should I offer full price?" What is the correct response?

*Working:* To decline the advice and explain why: the licensee represents the seller and cannot advise the buyer on what to offer. The licensee may state facts — days on market, the listing price, comparable sales that are public. Advising the buyer on negotiation would breach loyalty to the seller. Offering to refer the buyer to a buyer's agent is the right next sentence.

**Problem 6.** A dual agency consent form is signed by both parties at the same time as the purchase agreement. What is the problem?

*Working:* Consent has to be informed, and informed consent to dual agency has to happen before the licensee is in the conflicted position — that is, before negotiating begins, not simultaneously with the offer. Signing at the moment of the offer means the parties consented after the licensee had already been acting in both interests.

## Module summary

**Agency is between the client and the broker.** The salesperson performs it and does not own it.

**Michigan prohibits net service provision agreements** because they set the licensee's interest against the client's.

**Six duties to the client — loyalty, obedience, disclosure, confidentiality, accounting, care.** Obedience runs only to lawful instructions. Confidentiality survives the relationship.

**Honesty, disclosure of known material facts, and fair dealing are owed to everyone**, and treating that as nothing is the standard new-licensee error.

**Disclose agency in writing before confidential information is shared** — at the door, not at the closing table — and record it.

**Dual agency is permitted only with informed written consent**, and informed means the client understood what they gave up.

**State facts with their source and stop.** Puffing is opinion; repeating an unverified number is misrepresentation; silence about a known material defect is misrepresentation with extra steps.

**Any money reaching the licensee from anyone but their broker is disclosed in writing first**, and some of it is prohibited outright.
