# Principles of Real Estate - Module 07

## Civil rights law and equal opportunity in housing

**Course version:** 1.0 submission draft
**Module time:** 4 clock hours (240 instructional minutes)
**Statutory status:** These four clock hours are the instruction on civil rights law and equal opportunity in housing required by MCL 339.2504. No other module's time is counted toward this requirement.
**Regulatory status:** Not approved. No Michigan prelicensure credit is available for this course.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module the student will be able to:

- Identify the classes protected by the federal Fair Housing Act and the additional classes protected by Michigan law, and say which statute supplies each.
- Recognise the prohibited practices — refusal, discriminatory terms, steering, blockbusting, discriminatory advertising, and false representation of availability — in the form they actually appear in a working brokerage.
- Apply the reasonable accommodation and reasonable modification requirements to a real request.
- Apply the advertising rules to language, imagery, and targeting, including online advertising.
- Explain how a complaint proceeds under federal and Michigan law and what exposure a licensee personally carries.
- Answer the questions buyers ask about neighbourhoods, schools, and safety without steering.

## 1. The federal Fair Housing Act

### 1.1 What it covers

Title VIII of the Civil Rights Act of 1968, as amended, prohibits discrimination in the sale, rental, and financing of dwellings, and in the provision of brokerage services, on the basis of:

- **Race**
- **Colour**
- **Religion**
- **National origin**
- **Sex**, which HUD interprets to include sexual orientation and gender identity
- **Familial status** — households with children under 18, and pregnant persons
- **Disability**

Race and colour were in the 1968 Act. Sex was added in 1974. Familial status and disability were added by the Fair Housing Amendments Act of 1988, which also strengthened enforcement.

### 1.2 What it prohibits

The Act's operative prohibitions, in the language a licensee will recognise:

**Refusing to sell or rent**, or refusing to negotiate, because of a protected characteristic.

**Discriminating in terms, conditions, or privileges** — a different deposit, a different set of rules, a different level of service.

**Making, printing, or publishing** any advertisement that indicates a preference, limitation, or discrimination.

**Representing that a dwelling is unavailable** when it is, because of a protected characteristic.

**Blockbusting** — inducing sales or rentals by representations about the entry of persons of a protected class into a neighbourhood.

**Steering** — directing people toward or away from areas on the basis of a protected characteristic. Section 3 treats this at length because it is the violation licensees commit without deciding to.

**Discrimination in residential real-estate transactions**, including lending and appraisal.

**Denying access to a multiple listing service** or other facility.

**Coercion, intimidation, threats, or interference** with anyone exercising fair-housing rights or helping another to.

### 1.3 The exemptions, and how narrow they are

Limited exemptions exist. The sale or rental of a single-family house by a private owner who owns no more than three, without a broker and without discriminatory advertising. Rooms or units in an owner-occupied dwelling of no more than four units — the "Mrs. Murphy" exemption. Certain religious organisations and private clubs. Housing for older persons, in respect of familial status only, where statutory conditions are met.

**Two things a licensee must hold about these exemptions.** First, the moment a licensee is involved, the transaction is no longer exempt under the private-owner provision. Second, **no exemption applies to race**, because 42 U.S.C. § 1982 — the Civil Rights Act of 1866 — prohibits racial discrimination in property transactions without exception, as the Supreme Court confirmed in *Jones v. Alfred H. Mayer Co.* (1968).

A licensee should never be relying on an exemption. If the analysis has reached the point of looking for one, something has gone wrong.

### 1.4 The 1866 Act

Section 1982 is short and absolute: all citizens have the same right as white citizens to inherit, purchase, lease, sell, hold, and convey real and personal property. It has no exemptions, and it reaches private conduct.

#### Checkpoint 1.1

A landlord with two rental houses tells a licensee he does not want to rent to families with children, and adds that he is exempt because he is a small owner. Is the licensee free to follow the instruction?

*Answer:* No, twice over. The private-owner exemption is unavailable where a broker is used, so involving a licensee removes it. And a licensee following such an instruction is directly liable for the discrimination regardless of the owner's status. The licensee refuses, explains, documents, notifies the broker, and declines the engagement if the owner persists.

## 2. Michigan's civil rights statutes

### 2.1 The Elliott-Larsen Civil Rights Act

Michigan's principal civil-rights statute, 1976 PA 453, prohibits discrimination in housing and other areas. Its protected classes include **religion, race, colour, national origin, age, sex, sexual orientation, gender identity or expression, familial status, and marital status**.

Two of those are the reason a Michigan licensee cannot work from the federal list alone.

**Age.** The federal Fair Housing Act does not protect age as such. Elliott-Larsen does. An advertisement or a screening practice that disadvantages a person because of age is a Michigan violation even where it would not be a federal one.

**Marital status.** Also protected in Michigan and not federally. Refusing to rent to an unmarried couple, or treating a single applicant differently, raises a Michigan claim.

Michigan's protection of **sexual orientation and gender identity or expression** is express in the statute following its 2023 amendment, and does not depend on interpretation of the word "sex".

### 2.2 The Persons with Disabilities Civil Rights Act

1976 PA 220 addresses discrimination on the basis of disability in housing among other areas, and operates alongside the federal disability provisions.

### 2.3 Where Michigan goes further

The principle from module 01 applies: a state law giving **more** protection than a federal one is not in conflict with it. Both apply, and the licensee complies with the more protective.

The practical consequence is a working list a Michigan licensee should be able to recite:

| Protected in Michigan | Federal | Michigan |
|---|---|---|
| Race, colour, religion, national origin | Yes | Yes |
| Sex | Yes | Yes |
| Familial status | Yes | Yes |
| Disability | Yes | Yes |
| **Age** | No | **Yes** |
| **Marital status** | No | **Yes** |
| Sexual orientation, gender identity or expression | By interpretation | **Expressly** |

### 2.4 Local ordinances

A number of Michigan municipalities have human-rights ordinances protecting additional characteristics, including source of income in some places. A licensee working across municipal lines has to know that the list can grow locally, and the safe operating assumption is to treat every characteristic that has nothing to do with a person's ability to perform the contract as off limits.

#### Checkpoint 2.1

An owner instructs a licensee to prefer "a mature, settled tenant, not students." Which statutes are engaged?

*Answer:* Michigan's, at least. Age is protected under Elliott-Larsen and "mature" is an age preference. "Not students" may also operate as a proxy for age and, depending on how it is applied, for familial or marital status. The federal Act's list would not catch age directly, which is exactly why a Michigan licensee cannot work from the federal list alone.

## 3. The practices, as they actually appear

### 3.1 Steering

Steering is directing a person toward or away from an area, building, or neighbourhood on the basis of a protected characteristic. It is the violation most often committed by licensees who would never refuse anyone anything, because it usually arrives disguised as helpfulness.

It happens when a licensee:

- Shows a buyer homes only in areas the licensee associates with the buyer's background.
- Describes a neighbourhood by the characteristics of the people who live there.
- Volunteers that a buyer "might be more comfortable" somewhere else.
- Answers "is this a good area for us?" with anything other than a redirection to objective sources.
- Selects which listings to send based on an assumption about what the buyer wants, where the assumption is rooted in a protected characteristic.

**The test is not intent.** A licensee acting entirely from a wish to be helpful, who narrows a buyer's options along a protected line, has steered.

**The defence is method.** Ask what the buyer wants in objective terms — price, size, commute, schools by name, features. Show everything that matches. Let the buyer narrow. Record the criteria they gave you.

### 3.2 Blockbusting

Inducing a sale or rental by representations about the entry into a neighbourhood of persons of a protected class. Historically, panic selling; today it also appears in subtler forms — remarks about how a neighbourhood is "changing", or solicitation letters timed to demographic shifts.

### 3.3 Redlining and lending discrimination

Denying or pricing loans or insurance by geography as a proxy for a protected characteristic. Lending discrimination is also reached by the Equal Credit Opportunity Act and Regulation B, covered in module 08. A licensee who becomes aware of it in a client's transaction has a client to protect and a broker to inform.

### 3.4 False representation of availability

Telling a person a property is sold, rented, or unavailable when it is not. This is the practice fair-housing testers most often document, because it is easy to test and unambiguous when it happens.

### 3.5 Discriminatory terms and service

Different earnest money, different showing arrangements, different responsiveness, different willingness to write an offer. Service differences are discrimination even where no one says anything.

A useful private audit: if a licensee's practice with one client differs from their practice with another, they should be able to state a non-protected reason for the difference, in writing, before it matters.

#### Checkpoint 3.1

A buyer asks, "What kind of people live in this subdivision?" What is the correct answer?

*Answer:* That the licensee does not describe neighbourhoods by their residents, and that the buyer can find objective information for themselves — the municipality publishes crime statistics, the school district publishes performance data, the census publishes demographics, and visiting at different times of day tells them more than anyone's summary. Then move to what the licensee can help with: price, size, condition, taxes, commute. Answering the question as asked is steering however it is answered.

## 4. Disability: accommodation and modification

### 4.1 The definition

A person with a disability, for fair-housing purposes, is one with a physical or mental impairment substantially limiting one or more major life activities, one with a record of such an impairment, or one regarded as having one.

### 4.2 Reasonable accommodation

A **reasonable accommodation** is a change in rules, policies, practices, or services necessary to give a person with a disability equal opportunity to use and enjoy a dwelling. The housing provider bears the cost of accommodating the policy change itself.

The archetypal example is the assistance animal in a building with a no-pets policy. An assistance animal is **not a pet**. Pet deposits, pet rent, and breed or weight restrictions do not apply to it. A provider may ask, where the disability or the need is not obvious, for information confirming the disability-related need — and may not ask for the person's diagnosis, medical records, or a demonstration of the animal's training.

Other common accommodations: a reserved parking space nearer an entrance; permission for a live-in aide; a different payment date matching benefit receipt; permission for a co-signer.

### 4.3 Reasonable modification

A **reasonable modification** is a physical change to the premises necessary for full enjoyment. In private housing the **tenant** ordinarily pays for it, and the provider may in appropriate cases require restoration of the interior at the end of the tenancy.

Ramps, grab bars, widened doorways, lowered counters, visual alarms. A provider may not refuse a reasonable modification, and may not refuse to permit it merely because the building would look different.

### 4.4 Design and construction

Multifamily housing built for first occupancy after March 1991, with four or more units, must meet accessible design and construction requirements — accessible entrances on covered units, usable doors and routes, accessible controls, reinforced bathroom walls for later grab bars, usable kitchens and bathrooms.

### 4.5 The questions that may not be asked

A licensee may not ask whether a person has a disability, the nature or severity of one, or whether they can live independently. Where an accommodation is requested and the need is not obvious, the enquiry is limited to whether there is a disability-related need for the specific accommodation — not what the condition is.

#### Checkpoint 4.1

An applicant with a no-pets building asks to keep a support animal and offers a letter from a therapist. The landlord asks the licensee to obtain the applicant's diagnosis. What does the licensee do?

*Answer:* Decline, and tell the landlord why. Where the need is not obvious, the permitted enquiry is confirmation of a disability-related need for the animal, which the letter addresses. The diagnosis, the medical records, and proof of training are outside what may be requested. The licensee who obtains them anyway has participated in the violation.

## 5. Advertising

### 5.1 The rule

An advertisement may not indicate a preference, limitation, or discrimination based on a protected characteristic. This applies to **words, images, and where the advertisement is placed or targeted.**

### 5.2 Language

The safe discipline is to describe the **property**, not the **person**.

| Avoid | Why | Instead |
|---|---|---|
| "Perfect for a young family" | Familial status, age | "Three bedrooms, fenced yard" |
| "Ideal bachelor pad" | Sex, marital status | "One bedroom, open plan" |
| "Christian community" | Religion | Describe the property |
| "No children" | Familial status | Nothing; unlawful |
| "Mature tenants preferred" | Age | Describe the unit |
| "Walking distance to synagogue" | Religion | "Half a mile from Main Street" |
| "Exclusive neighbourhood" | Reads as a coded exclusion | Describe the property |
| "English speaking" | National origin | Nothing; unlawful |
| "Must be employed" | Can proxy for age, disability, source of income | State objective income criteria applied to everyone |

Some descriptors are fine and are treated as unsafe out of over-caution: "master bedroom" has been widely replaced but describes a room; "walk-in closet" describes a closet; "family room" describes a room. The test is whether the words describe the property or signal who should live there.

### 5.3 Imagery

Photographs of people in advertising should reflect the community broadly. A campaign whose imagery consistently depicts one group signals a preference as effectively as a sentence would.

### 5.4 Targeting, and the online problem

Where an advertisement is placed is part of the advertisement. Choosing to advertise a property only in a publication read by one group, or only in one area, can itself indicate a preference.

Online targeting makes this sharper. Ad platforms allow audiences to be defined by age, location, interests, and behaviour, and a housing advertisement targeted by any of those may exclude protected classes by proxy even where nobody selected a protected characteristic. Platforms now restrict targeting for housing advertisements for exactly this reason, and a licensee's obligation is to use those restricted settings and to keep records of how a campaign was configured.

**Exclusion by algorithm is still exclusion.** A licensee who cannot explain who saw their advertisement and who did not has a problem they cannot answer.

### 5.5 The equal housing statement

Displaying the equal housing opportunity logo and statement is standard practice and is expected in brokerage advertising. It is not a defence to a violation, but its absence is noticed.

#### Checkpoint 5.1

A brokerage runs a social campaign for a condominium targeted to users aged 25 to 40 within three miles, with interests in nightlife and fitness. Identify the problems.

*Answer:* The age band is a direct age restriction — protected in Michigan under Elliott-Larsen, and reaching familial status federally, since the excluded older and younger audiences include households at different life stages. The narrow radius may operate as a geographic proxy. The interest targeting compounds it. The campaign should be run without demographic targeting under the platform's housing-ad settings, and the configuration should be retained.

## 6. Enforcement, exposure, and daily practice

### 6.1 How a complaint proceeds

**Federally**, a person may file with HUD within one year of the alleged violation. HUD investigates and may conciliate; if it finds reasonable cause, the case proceeds before an administrative law judge or, at either party's election, in federal court. A private civil action may be brought within two years, independent of an administrative complaint. The Department of Justice may bring pattern-or-practice cases.

**In Michigan**, a complaint may be filed with the **Michigan Department of Civil Rights**, which investigates and may proceed before the Civil Rights Commission, or a civil action may be brought.

**And separately**, LARA may discipline a licensee under the Occupational Code for the same conduct. A licensee facing a fair-housing complaint may be facing three proceedings at once, and none of them substitutes for the others.

### 6.2 Personal exposure

A licensee is **personally liable** for their own discrimination. The broker's supervision does not absorb it, an employer's instruction does not excuse it, and a client's instruction is not a defence — a licensee who follows a discriminatory instruction has discriminated.

Remedies include actual damages, injunctive relief, civil penalties, attorney fees, and in Michigan licence discipline. There is no good outcome available here, which is the argument for the habits in section 6.4.

### 6.3 Testers

Fair-housing organisations conduct testing — matched pairs of testers with equivalent profiles differing in one protected characteristic — and testers have standing to sue. A licensee cannot identify a tester and should not try. The only protection is treating everyone the same, which is the protection anyway.

### 6.4 The habits that prevent all of this

**Ask objective criteria and record them.** Price, size, bedrooms, commute time, named schools, features. Then show everything that matches. A written criteria list is the evidence that the search was driven by the buyer's stated requirements.

**Give sources instead of characterisations.** Every question about neighbourhood quality, safety, or schools gets a source, not an opinion.

**Say the same thing to everyone.** Scripts for availability, showing procedures, offer procedures, and qualification criteria, applied uniformly.

**Refuse discriminatory instructions in writing, and tell your broker.** The written refusal is what separates the licensee from the client's conduct.

**Keep the advertising configuration.** Who was targeted, on what platform, with what settings.

**Never explain a neighbourhood by its residents.** Not once, not casually, not in answer to a direct question.

### 6.5 The question that will be asked

Sooner or later a buyer will ask a licensee some version of "is this a good area?" or "what are the schools like?" or "is it safe?" The answer, every time, is the same shape: the licensee does not characterise neighbourhoods or their residents; here are the sources that publish the data; and here is what I can help with — the property, the price, the taxes, the condition, the process.

That answer costs nothing, is more useful than an opinion, and is the difference between a career and a complaint.

#### Checkpoint 6.1

A licensee has shown a couple eleven properties, all in the same three subdivisions, after the couple mentioned their religious observance and the need to walk to services. Is this steering?

*Answer:* This one is genuinely difficult, and the distinction is who set the criterion. A buyer who states a specific objective requirement — walking distance to a named place — has given a criterion the licensee may search on, exactly as they would search on a commute time. What the licensee must not do is infer the requirement, expand it into an assumption about where the couple would be "comfortable", or omit properties that meet the stated criterion. Record the criterion the buyer gave, in their words, and search on that and nothing more.

## Key terms

**Blockbusting** — inducing sales or rentals by representations about the entry of protected-class persons into a neighbourhood.

**Elliott-Larsen Civil Rights Act** — Michigan's principal civil-rights statute, 1976 PA 453, protecting age and marital status in addition to the federal classes.

**Familial status** — households with children under 18, and pregnant persons; a federally protected class.

**Reasonable accommodation** — a change in rules, policies, practices, or services necessary for equal opportunity to use a dwelling.

**Reasonable modification** — a physical change to premises necessary for full enjoyment, ordinarily at the tenant's expense in private housing.

**Redlining** — denying or pricing loans or insurance by geography used as a proxy for a protected characteristic.

**Steering** — directing persons toward or away from areas on the basis of a protected characteristic.

**Tester** — a person who poses as a buyer or renter to detect discriminatory practices, with standing to sue.

## Practice problems

**Problem 1.** A seller instructs that the property is not to be shown on Saturdays for religious reasons. Is that discriminatory?

*Working:* No. The restriction applies to everyone equally and is about the seller's own observance, not about who may buy. Compare it with an instruction not to show to buyers of a particular religion, which is unlawful. The distinction is whether the restriction sorts people or sets a uniform condition.

**Problem 2.** A landlord requires income of three times the rent. An applicant's income comes from disability benefits and meets the threshold, but the landlord says he "prefers employment income."

*Working:* Applying the income criterion uniformly is lawful; refusing income that meets it because of its source is not, and here it operates directly against a person with a disability. Some Michigan municipalities also protect source of income by ordinance. The licensee refuses to implement the preference and documents it.

**Problem 3.** An advertisement reads: "Quiet building, ideal for professionals, no loud gatherings."

*Working:* "Ideal for professionals" describes the occupant and can operate against familial status, age, and source of income. "Quiet building" and a uniform noise policy describe the property and the rules. The fix is to delete the occupant description and keep the property description.

**Problem 4.** A licensee is asked by a buyer to find "a neighbourhood like the one we came from." What is the safe handling?

*Working:* Convert it into objective criteria and confirm them in writing — lot size, house age, price band, commute, named schools, walkability. Do not infer demographic content from the request, and do not narrow the search on anything the buyer has not stated objectively. If the buyer supplies a protected characteristic as the criterion, the licensee cannot search on it and should say so plainly.

**Problem 5.** An applicant requests a ramp at a rented duplex built in 1970. Who pays, and may the landlord refuse?

*Working:* This is a reasonable modification, so in private housing the tenant ordinarily bears the cost, and the landlord may not unreasonably refuse permission. The 1991 design and construction requirements do not apply to a 1970 building. The landlord may in appropriate cases require restoration of the interior at the end of the tenancy; an exterior ramp is generally treated differently.

**Problem 6.** A licensee's brokerage sends a mailing to one subdivision saying "several of your neighbours have already sold — call before the market changes."

*Working:* On its face this is a solicitation. It becomes blockbusting if the "change" being invoked is the entry of protected-class persons, or if the mailing is timed and targeted to demographic movement. The safe practice is to say nothing about who is moving in or out, and to be able to explain the mailing's targeting on objective grounds.

## Module summary

**Seven federal classes, and Michigan adds age and marital status** — which is why a Michigan licensee cannot work from the federal list.

**No exemption applies to race.** Section 1982 of the 1866 Act is absolute, and involving a licensee removes the private-owner exemption anyway.

**Steering is the violation licensees commit while being helpful.** Intent is not the test; method is the defence. Objective criteria, recorded, and show everything that matches.

**Accommodation changes a rule and the provider bears that cost; modification changes the premises and the tenant ordinarily pays.** Assistance animals are not pets, and the diagnosis may not be requested.

**Advertising covers words, images, and targeting** — and an online campaign that excludes by algorithm has excluded.

**A licensee is personally liable**, a client's instruction is not a defence, and a single course of conduct can produce a HUD complaint, a Michigan Department of Civil Rights complaint, and LARA discipline simultaneously.

**Every neighbourhood question gets a source, never a characterisation.**
