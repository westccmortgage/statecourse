# Principles of Real Estate - Module 09

## Valuation, appraisal, and real estate mathematics

**Course version:** 1.0 submission draft

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module the student will be able to:

- Distinguish market value, price, and cost, and explain why the three differ.
- Apply the four elements of value and the principles that explain market behaviour.
- Perform a sales comparison analysis, selecting comparables and adjusting in the correct direction.
- Explain the cost and income approaches and when each is the appropriate one.
- State the boundary between a licensee's market analysis and an appraisal, and act on it.
- Compute area, commission, proration, and investment measures accurately.

## 1. Value

### 1.1 Three different numbers

**Market value** is the most probable price a property should bring in a competitive and open market, with buyer and seller each acting prudently and knowledgeably, neither under undue duress, and with a reasonable exposure time.

**Price** is what was actually paid. It can differ from market value for many reasons — a distressed seller, a related-party transaction, an unusually motivated buyer.

**Cost** is what it took to build or improve. Cost does not equal value, and the gap between them is where owners' expectations most often sit. A $45,000 pool rarely adds $45,000, and in some markets adds very little.

Every one of those sentences is a conversation a licensee will have with a seller.

### 1.2 The four elements of value

Value requires all four; the absence of any one destroys it.

**Demand** — someone must want it, with the means to act.
**Utility** — it must satisfy a use.
**Scarcity** — supply must be limited relative to demand.
**Transferability** — the interest must be capable of being conveyed.

The mnemonic DUST is worth carrying, mainly because it explains failures. Land with a defective title has no transferability, so it has no market value however desirable it is.

### 1.3 The principles

**Substitution.** A buyer will pay no more than the cost of an equally desirable substitute. This is the foundation of the sales comparison approach.

**Highest and best use.** Value is measured by the use that is legally permissible, physically possible, financially feasible, and maximally productive. A house on a corner zoned commercial may be worth more as a site than as a house.

**Conformity.** Value is maximised where a property conforms reasonably to its surroundings.

**Progression and regression.** A modest house among expensive ones benefits; an expensive house among modest ones is dragged down. This is why over-improvement is a real financial risk and why a licensee should be candid with a seller who has spent heavily.

**Contribution.** An improvement adds what it contributes to value, not what it cost.

**Supply and demand**, **change**, **anticipation**, and **externalities** complete the ordinary set.

#### Checkpoint 1.1

A seller has spent $80,000 finishing a basement and adding a sunroom, and wants the price raised by $80,000. What principles apply?

*Answer:* Contribution — improvements add what they contribute, which is measured in the market, not what they cost. Regression, if the improvements have pushed the house above its neighbourhood. The licensee's job is to show the seller comparable sales with and without similar features and let the data carry the conversation, rather than arguing about the number.

## 2. The sales comparison approach

### 2.1 The method

Find recent sales of similar properties, adjust them for their differences from the subject, and reconcile the adjusted figures into an indication of value.

It is the primary approach for residential property because substitution is how residential buyers actually behave.

### 2.2 Choosing comparables

Recent, nearby, and similar. In practice: closed sales within six months where the market allows, in the same neighbourhood or a genuinely comparable one, of similar age, size, style, and condition, sold in ordinary arm's-length conditions.

Three sales is the working minimum. Sales that are not arm's length — between relatives, foreclosure sales, estate sales — are used only with care and only where the market consists of them.

### 2.3 Adjusting, and the direction that confuses everyone

**Adjust the comparable, never the subject.** The subject is what you are trying to value; you do not know its value yet.

**If the comparable is better than the subject, subtract.** If the comparable is worse, add.

Say the subject has two bathrooms and the comparable has three, and the market shows a bathroom contributing $9,000. The comparable is superior, so its sale price is adjusted **downward** by $9,000 to indicate what it would have sold for with two.

Students reverse this constantly. The sentence to memorise: **CBS — comparable better, subtract.**

### 2.4 A worked analysis

Subject: 1,780 square feet, three bedrooms, two baths, attached two-car garage, no basement finish, built 1996.

| | Comp A | Comp B | Comp C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sale price | $312,000 | $298,500 | $324,000 |
| Square feet | 1,820 | 1,700 | 1,780 |
| Baths | 2 | 2 | 3 |
| Garage | 2-car | 1-car | 2-car |
| Finished basement | No | No | Yes |
| Adjustments | | | |
| Size at $55/sq ft | −2,200 | +4,400 | 0 |
| Bath at $9,000 | 0 | 0 | −9,000 |
| Garage at $8,000 | 0 | +8,000 | 0 |
| Basement at $14,000 | 0 | 0 | −14,000 |
| **Adjusted** | **$309,800** | **$310,900** | **$301,000** |

Reconciliation is judgment, not arithmetic. The three adjusted figures cluster between $301,000 and $310,900. Comp C required the largest gross adjustment, which makes it the least reliable indicator. A defensible indication is roughly $308,000 to $310,000, weighted toward A and B.

**Never average.** An average treats a poorly matched comparable as equal to a good one, and reconciliation exists precisely to avoid that.

### 2.5 Where adjustment values come from

From the market — paired sales showing what buyers actually paid for the difference — not from cost and not from an owner's estimate. A licensee who cannot support an adjustment should be conservative with it and should say in the analysis where it came from.

#### Checkpoint 2.1

A comparable sold for $284,000 and has one fewer bedroom than the subject, with the market indicating $12,000 per bedroom in this range. What is the adjusted figure?

*Answer:* $296,000. The comparable is inferior — fewer bedrooms — so the adjustment is upward. If it had one more bedroom the adjustment would be downward to $272,000.

## 3. The cost approach

### 3.1 The method

Estimate the value of the land as though vacant; add the cost to construct the improvements new; subtract accrued depreciation.

Land value plus depreciated improvement cost equals the value indication.

### 3.2 Reproduction and replacement

**Reproduction cost** builds an exact replica, including obsolete features. **Replacement cost** builds a structure of equivalent utility using current materials and methods. Replacement is the ordinary basis; reproduction matters for historic property.

### 3.3 Depreciation

**Physical deterioration** — wear, age, deferred maintenance. Curable where the cost to fix is recovered in value; incurable where it is not.

**Functional obsolescence** — a defect in the structure itself. A three-bedroom house with one bathroom; a bedroom reachable only through another; a floor plan the market has rejected. Curable or incurable.

**External obsolescence** — caused by something outside the property. A new arterial road, a closed employer, an adjacent industrial use. **Always incurable**, because the owner cannot fix what is off their land.

### 3.4 When the cost approach is the right one

New construction, where depreciation is minimal. Special-purpose property — a church, a school, a fire station — with no meaningful sales market. Insurance valuation. And as a check on the other approaches.

It is weakest for older residential property, where depreciation estimates dominate the answer and are themselves estimates.

#### Checkpoint 3.1

A house backs onto a parcel where a distribution centre has just been approved. Which kind of depreciation, and can it be cured?

*Answer:* External obsolescence, and no. The cause is off the property and outside the owner's control, which is what makes external obsolescence incurable by definition. It is a genuine value effect and a material fact for disclosure once known.

## 4. The income approach

### 4.1 Direct capitalisation

For income property, value is derived from the income it produces.

**Value = Net Operating Income ÷ Capitalisation Rate.**

Net operating income is effective gross income less operating expenses. It does **not** subtract debt service, depreciation, or income taxes — a point students get wrong.

Worked: a small building with $86,000 potential gross income, 6 percent vacancy and collection loss, and $31,500 of operating expenses.

- Effective gross income: 86,000 − (86,000 × 0.06) = 86,000 − 5,160 = $80,840.
- Net operating income: 80,840 − 31,500 = $49,340.
- At an 8.25 percent capitalisation rate: 49,340 ÷ 0.0825 = **$598,061**.

Rearranged: cap rate = NOI ÷ value; NOI = value × cap rate. A student should be able to solve for any of the three.

### 4.2 The gross rent multiplier

A rough residential tool. GRM = sale price ÷ gross monthly rent. Derived from comparable sales and applied to the subject's rent.

If comparables show a GRM of 138 and the subject rents for $1,850 monthly, the indication is 138 × 1,850 = $255,300. Its weakness is that it ignores expenses entirely, which is why it is a screen and not a valuation.

### 4.3 Reconciliation

An appraiser weights the approaches according to the property and the data, and reconciles to a single conclusion. Residential work weights sales comparison heavily; income property weights the income approach; special-purpose property leans on cost.

#### Checkpoint 4.1

An owner reports NOI of $52,000 but has subtracted his mortgage payments to get there. What must be corrected?

*Answer:* Debt service is not an operating expense and must be added back. Two owners of identical buildings with different loans would otherwise show different NOIs for the same property, which is precisely what the definition excludes. Correct the figure before capitalising it.

## 5. Market analysis and the appraisal boundary

### 5.1 What a licensee may do

From module 01: providing or offering to provide a market analysis is licensed real estate activity in Michigan. A licensee may prepare a comparative market analysis for a client and may be paid for it.

### 5.2 What a licensee may not do

Prepare an appraisal, describe a market analysis as an appraisal, use appraisal terminology to dress one up, or accept an engagement that calls for an appraisal — a federally related transaction, a lending decision requiring one, a court proceeding requiring an appraiser's opinion.

Appraisal is separately licensed and separately regulated in Michigan, and the appraiser works to the Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice.

### 5.3 Preparing a defensible market analysis

**Label it accurately** — a comparative market analysis, prepared by a real estate licensee, not an appraisal.

**Show the comparables and the adjustments**, so the client can see the reasoning rather than being handed a number.

**State the source of every figure** — closed sales data, the source of adjustment values, the assessor's record where used.

**Give a range and explain the reconciliation.** A single number implies a precision the method does not have.

**Date it**, because a market analysis is a statement about a moment.

**Disclose your interest.** A market analysis prepared in the hope of a listing is honest work with an obvious incentive, and saying so costs nothing.

### 5.4 Pricing conversations

A seller's number and the market's number differ often. Three things help.

**Lead with the data, not the conclusion.** The comparables carry more weight than the licensee's opinion.

**Separate cost from value** — module 09, section 1.1 — because the seller is usually thinking about what they spent.

**Be explicit about the consequences of overpricing:** longer time on market, a stale listing, and often a lower final price than an accurate one would have produced. That is a professional judgment supported by market behaviour, and it is the advice the seller is paying for.

What a licensee must not do is inflate an analysis to win a listing. It is a misrepresentation, it damages the client, and it produces the price reduction conversation six weeks later from a worse position.

#### Checkpoint 5.1

A lender asks a licensee for "a quick valuation" for a home-equity decision, offering a fee. May the licensee accept?

*Answer:* No. A valuation for a lender's credit decision is appraisal work, separately licensed, and describing a market analysis as adequate for it would misrepresent both the product and the licensee's authority. The correct response is to decline and refer the lender to a licensed appraiser.

## 6. Applied mathematics

### 6.1 Area

**Rectangle:** length × width. **Triangle:** ½ × base × height. **Composite shapes:** divide into rectangles and triangles and sum.

An acre is **43,560 square feet**. A parcel 250 by 300 feet is 75,000 square feet, or 75,000 ÷ 43,560 = **1.72 acres**.

A section is 640 acres — module 03 — and description fractions divide from there.

### 6.2 Commission

A commission of 6 percent on $327,500 is $19,650. Split evenly between listing and selling brokerages, each receives $9,825. If the salesperson's agreement with their broker is 65 percent, the salesperson receives 9,825 × 0.65 = **$6,386.25**.

Working backwards: a salesperson who received $5,400 on a 60 percent split from a brokerage side of a 3 percent share had a brokerage side of 5,400 ÷ 0.60 = $9,000, on a sale price of 9,000 ÷ 0.03 = **$300,000**.

### 6.3 Proration

Prorations divide a shared expense at closing between seller and buyer. The two conventions are a 360-day banker's year with 30-day months, and a 365-day calendar year. **The contract states which.**

Michigan tax prorations carry a local complication: whether taxes are treated as paid in advance or in arrears is a matter of local custom that differs across the state, and the purchase agreement should say. A licensee must not assume the convention from another county.

Worked, on the 365-day method: annual tax of $3,650 on a closing of 12 September, with the day of closing charged to the buyer.

- Daily rate: 3,650 ÷ 365 = $10.00.
- Seller's days, 1 January to 11 September: 254.
- Seller's share: 254 × 10.00 = **$2,540**. Buyer's share: $1,110.

Whether that appears as a credit or a charge depends on whether the bill has been paid, which is why the closing statement in module 10 is read line by line.

### 6.4 Investment measures

**Return on investment** = annual net income ÷ investment. $18,600 on $240,000 is 7.75 percent.

**Cash-on-cash return** = annual pre-tax cash flow ÷ cash invested. On $62,000 invested with $5,890 cash flow, 9.5 percent.

**Equity** = market value less liens. A $340,000 property with a $214,000 mortgage carries $126,000 of equity.

**Appreciation.** A property bought at $265,000 and worth $312,000 has appreciated 47,000 ÷ 265,000 = **17.7 percent**.

### 6.5 Percentages, and the direction of the base

The most common arithmetic error in this field is dividing by the wrong number. A property that sold for 4 percent below its $325,000 list price sold for 325,000 × 0.96 = $312,000. But a property that sold for $312,000 after a reduction *from* $325,000 fell by 13,000 ÷ 325,000 = 4 percent — and if someone then says the buyer "paid 4 percent more" than $312,000, that is 312,000 × 1.04 = $324,480, which is not $325,000.

Always identify the base before computing the percentage.

#### Checkpoint 6.1

A parcel measures 400 feet along the road and 550 feet deep, with a triangular addition 400 feet wide at the base and 120 feet deep. How many acres?

*Answer:* Rectangle 400 × 550 = 220,000 square feet. Triangle ½ × 400 × 120 = 24,000. Total 244,000 ÷ 43,560 = **5.60 acres**.

## Key terms

**Capitalisation rate** — the rate converting net operating income into value.

**Comparative market analysis** — a licensee's opinion of probable selling price, drawn from market data; not an appraisal.

**External obsolescence** — loss in value from a cause outside the property; always incurable.

**Gross rent multiplier** — sale price divided by gross rent; a screening tool.

**Highest and best use** — the legally permissible, physically possible, financially feasible, and maximally productive use.

**Net operating income** — effective gross income less operating expenses, before debt service and taxes.

**Reconciliation** — the weighted judgment producing a single value conclusion from several indications.

**Regression** — the drag exerted on a superior property by inferior surroundings.

## Practice problems

**Problem 1.** A comparable sold at $349,000. It has a three-car garage where the subject has two, worth $10,000 in this market, and 120 fewer square feet at $60 per foot. Adjust it.

*Working:* Garage superior: −$10,000. Size inferior: +$7,200. Adjusted: 349,000 − 10,000 + 7,200 = **$346,200**.

**Problem 2.** A building produces $124,000 potential gross income with 8 percent vacancy and $46,800 of operating expenses. At a 7.5 percent cap rate, what is the indicated value?

*Working:* EGI = 124,000 − 9,920 = $114,080. NOI = 114,080 − 46,800 = $67,280. Value = 67,280 ÷ 0.075 = **$897,067**.

**Problem 3.** A house sells for $289,000 at 5.5 percent commission, split 50/50 between brokerages, with the listing salesperson on a 70 percent split. What does the salesperson receive?

*Working:* Total 289,000 × 0.055 = $15,895. Listing brokerage 15,895 ÷ 2 = $7,947.50. Salesperson 7,947.50 × 0.70 = **$5,563.25**.

**Problem 4.** Annual taxes of $4,745 on a 30 June closing, 365-day method, seller pays through the day before closing, taxes paid in advance by the seller. Who owes whom?

*Working:* Daily 4,745 ÷ 365 = $13.00. Seller's days 1 January to 29 June = 180. Seller's share 180 × 13.00 = $2,340. The seller has paid the whole year, so the buyer reimburses 4,745 − 2,340 = **$2,405** as a credit to the seller.

**Problem 5.** An investor pays $410,000, puts $95,000 down, and clears $8,075 after debt service in year one. Compute cash-on-cash return.

*Working:* 8,075 ÷ 95,000 = **8.5 percent**.

**Problem 6.** A seller insists on listing at $389,000 when the analysis supports $352,000 to $358,000. What does the licensee do?

*Working:* Present the data, state the professional opinion in writing including the expected consequences, and let the seller decide — it is their property. If the licensee takes the listing, the file should contain the analysis and the recommendation, so that the later price-reduction conversation begins from a record rather than an argument. What the licensee must not do is adopt the seller's number as though the analysis supported it.

## Module summary

**Market value, price, and cost are three different numbers**, and the gap between cost and value is where seller expectations live.

**Value needs demand, utility, scarcity, and transferability** — all four.

**Substitution drives residential valuation**, which is why sales comparison is the primary approach.

**Adjust the comparable, never the subject; comparable better, subtract.**

**Reconcile, do not average.** The comparable requiring the largest adjustment is the least reliable.

**Depreciation is physical, functional, or external**, and external is always incurable.

**NOI excludes debt service.** Value equals NOI divided by the cap rate, and the relation solves in any direction.

**A market analysis is licensed work; an appraisal is not the licensee's to give.** Label it accurately, show the reasoning, state sources, give a range, and date it.

**Identify the base before computing any percentage.**
