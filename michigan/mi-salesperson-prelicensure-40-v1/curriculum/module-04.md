# Principles of Real Estate - Michigan Salesperson Prelicensure — Module 04

## Encumbrances, liens, taxes, and land-use control

**Course version:** 1.0-submission-draft
**Module time:** 4 clock hours (240 instructional minutes)
**Regulatory status:** Not approved. No Michigan prelicensure credit is available for this course.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module the student will be able to:

- Distinguish a lien from a non-financial encumbrance and explain how each affects a sale.
- Rank competing liens on a Michigan property and explain why the property tax lien sits where it does.
- Explain Michigan's construction lien process, including the notice of commencement, and identify the transaction risk it creates.
- Describe the kinds of easement, how each is created, and how each ends.
- Explain how Michigan property is assessed and taxed, what the principal residence exemption does, and what happens to taxable value on a transfer.
- Identify the land-use controls that affect a transaction and state which questions belong to the municipality, which to counsel, and which to the licensee.

## 1. Encumbrances

### 1.1 The two families

An encumbrance is any claim, right, or interest held by someone other than the owner that affects the property. They divide cleanly.

**Financial encumbrances — liens.** A claim against the property as security for a debt. Mortgages, property taxes, judgments, construction liens, condominium assessments, income tax liens. A lien is satisfied with money.

**Non-financial encumbrances.** Easements, licences, encroachments, deed restrictions, leases. These are not paid off; they are lived with, released, or litigated.

The distinction matters at the closing table. A lien can generally be resolved with a payoff from the proceeds. A recorded easement cannot, and a buyer who discovers one the day before closing is either accepting it or not closing.

### 1.2 Marketable title

Marketable title is title a reasonable buyer would accept — free of defects and of encumbrances that would expose the buyer to litigation. It is not perfect title. An ordinary utility easement does not make title unmarketable; an unresolved boundary dispute does.

Purchase agreements ordinarily require the seller to convey marketable title, which makes this an obligation the seller has to satisfy, and the title commitment is where the licensee first sees whether they can.

### 1.3 What a licensee does about encumbrances

Read the commitment. Compare its exceptions with what the seller disclosed and with what is visible on the ground. Every difference between those three is a finding: an easement in the commitment that the seller never mentioned, a shed on the neighbour's side that no document explains, a lease nobody disclosed.

Findings go to the client in writing, with the document attached, and with the plain statement that the licensee cannot say what it means.

#### Checkpoint 1.1

The title commitment lists a recorded utility easement across the rear ten feet. The buyer intends to build a garage there. Is title unmarketable?

*Answer:* No — an ordinary recorded utility easement is a common exception and does not make title unmarketable. But it is directly inconsistent with the buyer's plan, which makes it material to this buyer. The licensee's obligation is to put it in front of them at once, with the recorded document, and to make sure the question reaches counsel and the utility before the contingency expires.

## 2. Liens and priority

### 2.1 The categories

**Voluntary or involuntary.** A mortgage is voluntary — the owner grants it. A judgment lien is involuntary.

**General or specific.** A judgment lien is general: it attaches to all the debtor's property in the county. A mortgage is specific: it attaches to the described property.

**Statutory or equitable.** Property tax liens and construction liens are created by statute. Others arise from agreement or from a court.

### 2.2 Priority

Priority decides who is paid first from a limited fund, and the ordinary rule is first in time, first in right, measured by recording. Two exceptions dominate Michigan practice.

**Property tax liens take priority over almost everything**, including a first mortgage recorded years earlier. This is why lenders escrow taxes and why unpaid taxes are the fastest way to lose a property in Michigan.

**Construction liens can relate back** to a date earlier than their recording — the date work first began or materials were first supplied on the project. A lien recorded in September may therefore outrank a mortgage recorded in July. Section 2.4 explains why this matters so much.

### 2.3 The common Michigan liens

| Lien | Created by | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Property tax | Statute | Superior priority; forecloses on a statutory schedule |
| Special assessment | Municipality | For local improvements; may be payable in instalments |
| Mortgage | Agreement | Priority by recording date |
| Construction lien | Statute | May relate back to commencement of work |
| Judgment lien | Court, then recording | General; attaches to property in the county |
| Condominium assessment | Master deed and statute | Association's remedy for unpaid dues |
| Federal tax lien | Statute and filing | Its own priority rules |
| State tax lien | Statute and filing | Michigan taxes |

### 2.4 Construction liens, and the notice of commencement

Michigan's construction lien act protects contractors, subcontractors, and suppliers who improve real property. The mechanism a licensee must understand is the **notice of commencement**: recorded at the start of a project, it establishes the reference point for the lien rights that follow, and subcontractors serve notices in relation to it.

The risk in a residential sale is concrete. A seller who had a kitchen remodelled six weeks before listing may have unpaid subcontractors the seller has never heard of, because the seller paid the general contractor and the general contractor did not pay them. Those subcontractors may still be able to record liens after the closing, relating back to a date before it.

The controls are the seller's sworn statement identifying everyone who supplied labour or materials, full unconditional waivers of lien from each, and the title company's own requirements. A licensee who learns that recent work was done should raise it immediately and should never treat "I paid the contractor" as the end of the enquiry.

### 2.5 Release and discharge

A lien is removed from the record by a discharge, a release, or a waiver, and it is not gone until that instrument is recorded. A paid mortgage with no recorded discharge is still an exception on a title commitment, and obtaining a discharge years later from a lender that has since merged is one of the more common causes of delayed Michigan closings.

#### Checkpoint 2.1

A seller mentions casually that she had her basement finished in the spring and paid the contractor in cash. What does the licensee do?

*Answer:* Raise it with the title company immediately and make sure the seller understands that a sworn statement and unconditional lien waivers from every subcontractor and supplier will be needed. Payment to the general contractor does not extinguish a subcontractor's lien rights. A cash payment with no paper is worse, not better, because there is nothing to evidence it.

## 3. Easements

### 3.1 What an easement is

An easement is a right to use another's land for a specific purpose. It is an interest in land, and when recorded it binds subsequent owners.

An **easement appurtenant** benefits a parcel — the **dominant estate** — and burdens another — the **servient estate**. It runs with the land, so both burden and benefit pass to new owners automatically. A driveway easement across a neighbour's land to reach a road is the standard example.

An **easement in gross** benefits a person or entity rather than a parcel. Utility easements are the everyday case.

### 3.2 How easements are created

**By express grant or reservation** — written, and normally recorded.

**By implication** — from prior use, where a parcel is divided and one part has obviously been serving the other.

**By necessity** — where a division leaves a parcel landlocked, the law may imply access.

**By prescription** — from open, notorious, continuous, adverse use for the statutory period. This is the adverse-possession analysis applied to a use rather than to possession, and whether it has happened is a question only a court decides.

### 3.3 How easements end

By expiration of a stated term; by merger, when one owner acquires both estates; by written release from the holder; by abandonment, which requires more than non-use; or by the purpose becoming impossible.

The point for a licensee is that easements are difficult to remove and easy to overlook, and that a buyer's plans — a fence, a garage, a pool, a driveway — routinely collide with them.

### 3.4 Licences and encroachments

A **licence** is permission to use land, revocable, and not an interest in land. The neighbour who has always cut across the corner may hold a licence rather than an easement, and the difference is the whole case.

An **encroachment** is a physical intrusion — a garage over the line, an eave, a fence. Encroachments are found by survey, and their legal consequences depend on how long they have been there and what the parties did about it.

#### Checkpoint 3.1

A buyer is told by the seller that "the neighbour has permission to use the drive, but I could stop it any time." The title commitment shows nothing. What is the licensee's position?

*Answer:* The seller has described a licence, and licences are revocable — but whether long use has ripened into a prescriptive easement is a legal question and the seller is not qualified to answer it either. The licensee documents what the seller said, discloses it to the buyer in writing, and refers the question. What the licensee must not do is repeat the seller's confidence as though it were the legal position.

## 4. Private restrictions

### 4.1 Deed restrictions and covenants

Restrictive covenants recorded against a subdivision or a parcel limit what owners may do: minimum square footage, setbacks, materials, outbuildings, fences, vehicles, short-term rentals, business use. They are private agreements running with the land, enforced by other owners or by an association — not by the municipality.

Michigan subdivisions frequently have them, and they are frequently ignored until someone objects. A buyer intending to park a commercial vehicle, run a business, or build a pole barn needs to read them before writing an offer.

### 4.2 Associations

A homeowners' or condominium association enforces the documents, collects assessments, and may hold a lien for unpaid dues. Its documents, budget, reserves, minutes, and any pending special assessment are all material to a buyer.

### 4.3 Restrictions that are void

A restrictive covenant purporting to limit ownership or occupancy by race, colour, religion, national origin, sex, familial status, disability, or any other protected class is **unenforceable and unlawful**. Michigan properties, like properties everywhere in the United States, still carry such language in old recorded documents.

A licensee's duties here are absolute and are covered again in module 07: never repeat such a restriction as though it operated; never use it in marketing; never explain a neighbourhood by reference to it. Michigan law provides a process for a property owner to have such language removed from the record, and a licensee who encounters it should tell the owner the process exists and route them to counsel.

### 4.4 Reading restrictions for a client

The licensee obtains the documents, gives them to the client, identifies the provisions likely to matter given what the client has said they intend to do, and states plainly that whether a provision is enforceable is a legal question.

#### Checkpoint 4.1

A recorded 1955 subdivision restriction states that no lot may be occupied by any person not of the Caucasian race. A buyer asks what it means. What does the licensee say?

*Answer:* That the provision is unlawful and unenforceable, that it has no effect, and that Michigan provides a procedure for having such language struck from the record which their lawyer can explain. What the licensee must never do is treat it as operative, describe it neutrally as "one of the restrictions," or let it influence any advice about the neighbourhood.

## 5. Michigan property tax

### 5.1 How the tax is computed

Michigan taxes real property under the General Property Tax Act. Three numbers matter and students confuse them constantly.

**Assessed value** is, in principle, fifty percent of true cash value — the assessor's estimate of market value.

**State equalized value (SEV)** is assessed value after county and state equalisation.

**Taxable value** is the number the tax is actually computed on. Since Proposal A, annual growth in taxable value is capped at the lesser of five percent or the rate of inflation, for as long as ownership does not change.

The tax is taxable value multiplied by the **millage** rate, where one mill is one dollar per thousand dollars of taxable value.

### 5.2 The uncapping on transfer

This is the Michigan fact a licensee must never get wrong. When a property is **transferred**, the cap resets: in the year following the transfer, taxable value is uncapped and becomes equal to state equalized value.

For a long-held property, that can be an enormous increase. A house held for twenty-five years may have a taxable value far below its SEV, and the buyer's first full tax bill can be very different from the seller's last one.

**The licensee's rule:** never quote the seller's tax bill as the buyer's future tax. Explain that uncapping exists, direct the buyer to the assessor for an estimate of the uncapped figure, and put it in writing. Quoting the current bill without the uncapping warning is one of the most damaging routine errors available to a Michigan licensee.

Certain transfers are statutorily exempt from uncapping — some transfers between relatives, for example. Whether a particular transfer qualifies is a question for the assessor or counsel.

### 5.3 The principal residence exemption

A property that is the owner's principal residence may be exempt from a portion of school operating tax. The owner claims it by filing with the local unit, and it must be rescinded when the property stops being the principal residence.

This appears in almost every Michigan residential transaction: whether the exemption is in place, whether the seller must rescind, whether the buyer must file, and by which deadline. The licensee's job is to make sure the parties know the forms exist and go to the assessor, not to complete them or to advise on eligibility.

### 5.4 Special assessments and delinquency

Special assessments fund local improvements — paving, sewer, water — and are levied against benefited parcels. Whether an assessment is levied, pending, or merely proposed is material to a buyer, and the answer comes from the municipality.

Delinquent taxes in Michigan run on a statutory schedule toward forfeiture and foreclosure. A licensee who sees delinquency on a tax record should treat it as urgent and get it in front of the title company at once.

#### Checkpoint 5.1

A buyer asks what the taxes will be. The listing shows $2,180. The sellers have owned the house since 1994. What is the correct answer?

*Answer:* That $2,180 is what the sellers pay, that Michigan uncaps taxable value in the year after a transfer, that a house held since 1994 will very likely see a substantial increase, and that the assessor can estimate the uncapped figure. Then send that in writing. Saying "about $2,180" is an inaccurate representation about a material financial term.

## 6. Public land-use control

### 6.1 Zoning

Zoning is the police power applied to land: districts, permitted uses, lot sizes, setbacks, height, coverage, parking. It is entirely local, and Michigan municipalities differ enormously.

A **nonconforming use** is a use that was lawful before the ordinance changed and is allowed to continue, usually with limits on expansion and rules ending it if abandoned. This is the "grandfathered" situation, and whether a particular use retains protection is a legal question.

A **variance** relieves an owner from a dimensional requirement where strict application would cause practical difficulty. A **special land use** or conditional use is a use the ordinance permits in a district subject to approval.

**Rezoning** is a legislative change to the ordinance and is neither quick nor certain.

### 6.2 The licensee and the buyer's plan

The most damaging thing a licensee can do in this area is answer confidently. "You can definitely put an accessory dwelling there," "a pole barn is fine in this township," "the setback is 25 feet" — each is a statement about a local ordinance that changes across a municipal boundary and over time.

The correct routine: ask the buyer what they intend to do; tell them plainly that the municipality answers zoning questions; give them the department's contact; and make the answer a contingency if it is important enough to their decision. Then record that this was done.

### 6.3 Building codes, permits, and unpermitted work

Michigan municipalities issue building permits and inspect. Unpermitted work is common and is a real disclosure issue: a finished basement, a deck, or an addition built without a permit may need to be brought into compliance, and the obligation can land on the buyer.

If a licensee learns that work was done without a permit, that is a material fact and it is disclosed. Whether the municipality will require correction is a question for the municipality.

### 6.4 Environmental and site issues

Several Michigan-specific and federal issues affect residential and rural transactions.

**Wetlands** are regulated by the state, and a wetland determination can prevent a buyer building where they intended.

**Septic and well.** Many Michigan counties require point-of-sale evaluation of a septic system and well. Requirements are county-specific and non-uniform, which makes assuming from one county to the next a reliable way to miss a requirement.

**Underground storage tanks** appear on older rural and commercial properties.

**Lead-based paint.** Federal law requires disclosure for target housing built before 1978 — covered with the seller disclosure obligations in module 06.

**Flood zones.** Location in a special flood hazard area drives insurance costs and lender requirements, and the map is the source, not the seller's recollection.

**Radon.** Present in parts of Michigan; testing is a buyer decision and results are a disclosure issue once known.

#### Checkpoint 6.1

A buyer wants to run a dog-grooming business from the attached garage of a home in a residential district. What does the licensee do?

*Answer:* Send them to the municipality before the offer, because whether a home occupation of that kind is permitted, permitted with approval, or prohibited is decided by the local ordinance and by nothing else. If the answer matters to the purchase — and here it clearly does — it belongs in the agreement as a contingency. What the licensee must not do is answer from experience in another township.

## Key terms

**Easement appurtenant** — an easement benefiting one parcel and burdening another, running with the land.

**Millage** — the tax rate expressed in dollars per thousand dollars of taxable value.

**Nonconforming use** — a use lawful before an ordinance changed, permitted to continue subject to limits.

**Notice of commencement** — the recorded instrument beginning a Michigan construction project and organising the lien rights arising from it.

**Principal residence exemption** — Michigan's exemption from a portion of school operating tax for an owner's principal residence.

**Taxable value** — the figure Michigan property tax is computed on, capped annually until ownership transfers.

**Uncapping** — the reset of taxable value to state equalized value in the year following a transfer of ownership.

**Variance** — relief from a dimensional zoning requirement.

## Practice problems

**Problem 1.** A property has a taxable value of $84,000 and a total millage of 38.5 mills. Compute the annual tax.

*Working:* 84,000 ÷ 1,000 = 84. 84 × 38.5 = $3,234.

**Problem 2.** The same property has an SEV of $137,000 and is sold. What happens to the tax the following year, approximately?

*Working:* Taxable value uncaps to SEV, so the computation becomes 137,000 ÷ 1,000 × 38.5 = $5,274.50 — an increase of over $2,000. The licensee's duty is to have told the buyer this before the offer, not to compute it afterwards.

**Problem 3.** A subcontractor records a construction lien three weeks after a closing, for work done two months before it. Whose problem is it?

*Working:* Potentially the buyer's, because the lien may relate back to a date before the closing. This is exactly the risk the sworn statement and unconditional waivers exist to prevent, and it is why recent work must be disclosed to the title company. The buyer's recourse depends on what the title policy covers and on the seller's warranties — legal questions.

**Problem 4.** A rural buyer intends to build on a parcel that a state wetland inventory shows as partly wetland. What is the licensee's role?

*Working:* Disclose the inventory finding, tell the buyer that a determination comes from the state and that the inventory is not itself a determination, and make the buyer's ability to build a contingency if it drives the purchase. The licensee neither interprets the map nor predicts the outcome.

**Problem 5.** A condominium buyer asks whether the association can assess for a new roof. Where does the answer come from?

*Working:* From the master deed and bylaws, which the licensee obtains and delivers, and from the association's minutes and reserve position, which reveal whether an assessment is being discussed. Whether the documents permit it is a legal reading; whether one is coming is a factual enquiry the licensee can and should make.

**Problem 6.** A seller's tax record shows two years of delinquency. What is the urgency?

*Working:* High. Michigan's delinquency process runs toward forfeiture and foreclosure on a statutory schedule, and a property far enough along may not be saleable in the ordinary way. The licensee raises it with the title company at listing, not at closing, and does not attempt to compute where in the schedule the property sits.

## Module summary

**Encumbrances divide into liens, which are paid, and non-financial rights, which are not.** Marketable title tolerates ordinary encumbrances and not litigation risk.

**Priority is first in time, with two Michigan exceptions that matter**: the property tax lien outranks nearly everything, and construction liens can relate back to the commencement of work.

**Recent construction work is a transaction risk.** Sworn statement and unconditional waivers, every time; "I paid the contractor" is not an answer.

**Easements run with the land, are hard to remove, and collide with buyers' plans.** A licence is revocable; whether long use has become an easement is a court's question.

**Discriminatory restrictions are void and must never be treated as operative.**

**Michigan taxes taxable value, and it uncaps in the year after a transfer.** Never quote the seller's bill as the buyer's tax. Send them to the assessor and put it in writing.

**Zoning, permits, septic and well, wetlands and flood zones are local and non-uniform.** The municipality and the county answer; the licensee asks, records, and makes it a contingency when it matters.

## Required applied exercise

Prepare a one-page encumbrance review memorandum for the property supplied with this module. Identify every encumbrance disclosed by the commitment, the seller, or the site; classify each as financial or non-financial; rank the liens by priority and note any that could relate back; identify the tax consequence of the transfer including uncapping; list the questions belonging to the municipality, to the title company, and to counsel; and state what the buyer must know before the inspection contingency expires. The approved instructor scores it under the common 10-point applied-memo rubric; 8/10 with no zero on legal accuracy or professional boundaries is satisfactory, and a needs-revision result receives feedback and must be resubmitted.
