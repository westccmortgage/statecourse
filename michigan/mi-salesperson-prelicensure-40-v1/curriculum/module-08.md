# Principles of Real Estate - Michigan Salesperson Prelicensure — Module 08

## Real estate finance and settlement law

**Course version:** 1.0-submission-draft
**Module time:** 4 clock hours (240 instructional minutes)
**Regulatory status:** Not approved. No Michigan prelicensure credit is available for this course.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module the student will be able to:

- Distinguish the promissory note from the mortgage and explain what each does.
- Identify the loan types a Michigan buyer uses and the property consequences of each.
- Explain amortisation, points, loan-to-value, and the ratios a lender applies, and compute each.
- Describe Michigan foreclosure, including foreclosure by advertisement and the redemption period, well enough to know what to ask and what not to state.
- Apply the federal settlement and lending rules — RESPA, TILA and the integrated disclosures, ECOA — to a licensee's conduct.
- Recognise predatory and fraudulent lending patterns and state what a licensee does on encountering one.

## 1. The instruments

### 1.1 Note and mortgage are two documents

The **promissory note** is the promise to repay: amount, rate, term, payment, default terms. It is the debt.

The **mortgage** is the security instrument: it pledges the property as collateral and gives the lender the right to foreclose. It is recorded; the note is not.

Students conflate them constantly, and the distinction decides real questions. A note can exist without a mortgage — an unsecured loan. A mortgage without a note secures nothing. When a loan is sold, the note is what is transferred, and the mortgage follows it.

Michigan is a **lien theory** state: the borrower holds title and the lender holds a lien. The lender does not own the property during the loan.

### 1.2 The clauses that matter

**Acceleration** — on default, the whole balance becomes due. Without it a lender could only sue for missed payments.

**Due-on-sale** — the balance becomes due if the property is transferred. This is why a buyer generally cannot simply take over a seller's mortgage, and why "assumable" is a specific feature rather than a default.

**Prepayment** — whether early payoff carries a penalty.

**Escrow** — the lender collects taxes and insurance monthly and pays them. Ubiquitous in Michigan residential lending, and the reason property tax delinquency on a mortgaged property is unusual.

**Subordination** — an agreement that one lien will rank behind another. This is how a first mortgage is refinanced without a second lien moving into first position.

### 1.3 Discharge and satisfaction

When the note is paid, the lender must record a **discharge**. An undischarged mortgage remains an exception on the title commitment even though the debt is gone — module 03 — and obtaining a discharge from a lender that has since been acquired is a recurring source of delay.

#### Checkpoint 1.1

A buyer asks whether he can "take over" the seller's 3.1 percent mortgage rather than borrowing at current rates. What does the licensee say?

*Answer:* That most mortgages contain a due-on-sale clause making the balance payable on transfer, that assumability is a specific feature of some government-backed loans and not a general right, and that whether this particular loan may be assumed is answered by the lender and the loan documents. The licensee gets the note and the mortgage to the lender or to counsel and does not read them for the buyer.

## 2. Loan types

### 2.1 Conventional

Not insured or guaranteed by a government agency. Conforming loans meet the limits and standards of the secondary-market purchasers; non-conforming and jumbo loans do not.

**Private mortgage insurance** is generally required where the loan-to-value exceeds 80 percent, and under federal law it is cancellable in defined circumstances as the balance falls.

### 2.2 FHA

Insured by the Federal Housing Administration, with a lower down payment and more flexible credit. Two consequences for property rather than borrower:

**Property standards.** FHA appraisals identify condition issues that must be corrected before closing — peeling paint on pre-1978 housing, missing handrails, roof condition, non-functioning systems.

**Mortgage insurance premiums**, upfront and annual, which change the payment materially.

A listing agent whose seller accepts an FHA offer should expect condition items and should raise them with the seller before the appraisal rather than after.

### 2.3 VA

Guaranteed by the Department of Veterans Affairs for eligible veterans and service members. Frequently no down payment, no monthly mortgage insurance, and a funding fee. VA appraisals carry their own minimum property requirements.

A licensee must never treat a VA buyer's offer as weaker for being VA. Beyond the commercial error, refusing or disadvantaging offers by loan type can operate against protected classes.

### 2.4 Rural development, MSHDA, and land contract

**USDA rural development** loans serve eligible rural areas with income limits — much of Michigan outside the metropolitan areas qualifies geographically.

**MSHDA** — the Michigan State Housing Development Authority — administers down-payment assistance and first-time buyer programmes with their own eligibility rules and property requirements.

**Land contract**, from module 02, remains a genuine Michigan financing route and carries the same warning: recognise, refer, never draft.

### 2.5 Structures

**Fixed rate** — rate constant for the term.

**Adjustable rate** — an index plus a margin, adjusting on a schedule, with caps per adjustment and over the life. A licensee should be able to explain that structure in a sentence and should send every specific question to the lender.

**Balloon** — payments computed on a long amortisation with the balance due early. Common in land contracts and seller financing, and the source of the failure when the balloon arrives and refinancing is unavailable.

#### Checkpoint 2.1

A seller receives two offers: $305,000 cash with no contingencies, and $312,000 FHA. What should the licensee explain?

*Answer:* The difference in net and in risk — the FHA offer is higher but carries an appraisal and property-condition requirements the cash offer does not, and a longer timeline. The licensee lays out both and lets the seller decide. What the licensee must not do is characterise the FHA buyer as less desirable in a way that becomes a pattern of disadvantaging a loan type.

## 3. The arithmetic

### 3.1 Loan-to-value

LTV = loan amount ÷ lesser of price or appraised value.

A $240,000 loan on a $300,000 property is 80 percent. If the appraisal comes in at $290,000, the same loan is 82.8 percent of value and the lender will reduce the loan, not raise the value.

### 3.2 Down payment

A 20 percent down payment on $285,000 is $57,000, leaving a $228,000 loan. Students should be able to move between the three numbers in either direction.

### 3.3 Points and interest

One **point** is one percent of the loan amount, paid at closing. Two points on a $228,000 loan is $4,560. Discount points buy a lower rate; origination points are a fee.

Simple interest for one period: principal × rate. A $228,000 loan at 6.5 percent accrues $14,820 in the first year, about $1,235 in the first month. In an amortising loan the early payments are mostly interest, which is why a borrower's balance falls slowly at first.

### 3.4 Amortisation

An amortised loan's constant payment covers accruing interest first and applies the remainder to principal. As principal falls, the interest portion falls and the principal portion grows.

Worked, on $228,000 at 6.5 percent with a monthly payment of $1,441.16:

- Month 1 interest: 228,000 × 0.065 ÷ 12 = $1,235.00. Principal: $206.16. Balance: $227,793.84.
- Month 2 interest: 227,793.84 × 0.065 ÷ 12 = $1,233.88. Principal: $207.28. Balance: $227,586.56.

Two payments in, the borrower has repaid $413.44 of $228,000. A licensee who can show that to a client explains more about mortgage debt in thirty seconds than a brochure does.

### 3.5 Qualifying ratios

**Housing ratio** — housing expense ÷ gross monthly income. Housing expense is principal, interest, taxes, insurance, and association dues where applicable.

**Total debt ratio** — housing expense plus other monthly obligations ÷ gross monthly income.

A borrower with $7,500 gross monthly income and a proposed housing expense of $2,100 has a 28 percent housing ratio. With $600 of other obligations, the total debt ratio is 36 percent.

**The licensee does not qualify buyers.** Lenders do. Understanding the ratios lets a licensee ask useful questions and recognise when a buyer's plan is unrealistic; it does not license a licensee to tell a buyer what they can afford.

#### Checkpoint 3.1

A buyer earning $6,000 gross monthly wants a house with a $1,900 housing expense and has a $520 car payment. Compute both ratios and comment.

*Answer:* Housing ratio 1,900 ÷ 6,000 = 31.7 percent. Total debt ratio 2,420 ÷ 6,000 = 40.3 percent. Both are above conventional guideline figures, which does not mean the buyer will be declined — programmes and compensating factors vary. The comment a licensee should make is that these are questions for the lender, and that the buyer should have a written pre-approval before shopping at this price.

## 4. Michigan foreclosure

### 4.1 Two routes

Michigan permits **foreclosure by advertisement** — a non-judicial process where the mortgage contains a power of sale — and **judicial foreclosure**, brought as a court action. Foreclosure by advertisement is the ordinary residential route and involves published notice, posting, and a sheriff's sale.

### 4.2 Redemption

After the sheriff's sale, Michigan provides a **redemption period** during which the borrower may redeem the property by paying the sale amount with interest and costs. The length depends on the circumstances, including the property type and how much of the original loan has been repaid, and it can be shortened where the property is abandoned.

**A licensee must not state the length of a redemption period from memory.** It is a fact with large consequences for a buyer's ability to take possession and for what the buyer is actually acquiring, and it comes from the title company and counsel in writing.

### 4.3 What a buyer of foreclosed property is buying

During redemption, the purchaser at the sale holds a sheriff's deed subject to the right of redemption. Occupancy, condition, access, and even the ability to inspect may be restricted. Title questions are more common than in an ordinary sale.

The licensee's role is to make sure the buyer knows they are in a different kind of transaction, to get the title company involved early, and to refuse to predict outcomes.

### 4.4 Short sales and deeds in lieu

A **short sale** is a sale for less than the balance, requiring the lender's approval. Timelines are long and uncertain, approval is not guaranteed by any lender statement short of a written approval, and whether the borrower remains liable for the shortfall is a legal and tax question the licensee must not answer.

A **deed in lieu** conveys the property to the lender to avoid foreclosure, and whether it extinguishes the debt is likewise a legal question.

### 4.5 Tax foreclosure

Separate from mortgage foreclosure, Michigan counties foreclose for delinquent property taxes on a statutory schedule that ends in the loss of the property. It moves faster than most owners expect, and a licensee who sees delinquency should treat it as urgent — module 04.

#### Checkpoint 4.1

A buyer wants to make an offer on a property that went to sheriff's sale five weeks ago. What does the licensee establish first, and from whom?

*Answer:* Whether a redemption period is running and when it ends, from the title company and counsel, in writing. Until that is answered the buyer does not know what they would be purchasing or when they could occupy. The licensee should not state the period from memory and should not let the buyer proceed on an assumption.

## 5. Federal settlement and lending law

### 5.1 RESPA

The Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act, implemented by **Regulation X**, governs federally related mortgage loans on residential property. Three parts touch a licensee directly.

**Section 8 prohibits kickbacks and unearned fees.** Giving or accepting anything of value for the referral of settlement service business is prohibited. This reaches payments from title companies, lenders, inspectors, warranty companies, and anyone else in the settlement chain. It also reaches arrangements dressed as marketing agreements where the payment is really for referrals.

**Affiliated business arrangements** are permitted where the relationship is disclosed in writing, the consumer is told they are not required to use the affiliate, and no fee is paid for the referral itself.

**Section 9 prohibits a seller from requiring the buyer to use a particular title insurer** as a condition of sale.

Combined with R 339.22137 from module 05, the working rule is: any value flowing to a licensee from a settlement service provider is presumptively a problem, and is disclosed and examined before it flows.

### 5.2 TILA and the integrated disclosures

The Truth in Lending Act, implemented by **Regulation Z**, requires disclosure of the cost of credit, expressed as the **annual percentage rate**, which includes finance charges and not merely the note rate.

The integrated disclosures apply to most closed-end consumer mortgages:

**The Loan Estimate** must be delivered within three business days of application.

**The Closing Disclosure** must be received by the borrower at least **three business days before consummation**. Certain changes — an APR increase beyond tolerance, a change in loan product, the addition of a prepayment penalty — restart the three-day period.

**Why a licensee cares:** the three-day rule is a hard constraint on closing dates. A change late in a transaction can move the closing, and a licensee who promises a date without accounting for it has promised something the timeline may not allow.

**Advertising triggers.** Stating certain credit terms in an advertisement — a down payment amount, a payment amount, a term, a rate — triggers required additional disclosures. The safe practice for a real estate licensee is not to advertise financing terms at all.

### 5.3 ECOA and Regulation B

The Equal Credit Opportunity Act prohibits discrimination in credit on the basis of race, colour, religion, national origin, sex, marital status, age, receipt of public assistance income, or the good-faith exercise of consumer-protection rights.

Note that **marital status, age, and public assistance income** appear here and not in the federal Fair Housing Act. The licensee's fair-housing obligations in module 07 and the lending rules overlap without being identical.

### 5.4 Other federal rules a licensee meets

**The Home Mortgage Disclosure Act**, which produces the lending data used to identify redlining.

**The Fair Credit Reporting Act**, governing credit information — relevant where a licensee handles a rental application.

**Anti-money-laundering reporting** in certain non-financed transactions in defined areas, which title companies administer.

#### Checkpoint 5.1

A title company offers a licensee $150 per closing, described as a "marketing co-op fee", with no marketing services identified. Evaluate.

*Answer:* This is a referral payment with a label on it, and RESPA section 8 prohibits it. A genuine marketing agreement requires actual services at fair market value, documented, and unrelated to the volume of referrals. The licensee declines, tells their broker, and does not accept an arrangement whose only variable is how many closings they send.

## 6. Predatory lending and fraud

### 6.1 Patterns to recognise

**Equity stripping** — a loan the borrower cannot repay, made on the strength of the equity rather than the income.

**Loan flipping** — repeated refinancing that generates fees and erodes equity.

**Steering to higher-cost products** where the borrower qualifies for better.

**Balloon structures** presented as ordinary payments, where the balloon arrives without a refinancing plan.

**Fee packing** — inflated or duplicated charges buried in the closing costs.

**Undisclosed adjustable terms** — a payment that rises sharply after a teaser period the borrower did not understand.

### 6.2 Mortgage fraud, and how licensees get drawn into it

Two families of fraud, and the second one catches honest people.

**Fraud for profit** — the organised kind: straw buyers, inflated appraisals, fictitious sellers, silent second mortgages concealed from the primary lender.

**Fraud for housing** — a buyer who wants a house misstating income, occupancy intention, or the source of the down payment.

The licensee's exposure arises through documents. A purchase agreement with a "seller concession" that does not appear on the closing statement, a side agreement for cash back after closing, a second document with a different price, an occupancy declaration everyone knows is false — a licensee who participates in any of these has participated in mortgage fraud, and the intent to help does not change that.

### 6.3 What a licensee does

**Refuse to sign or transmit a document you know to be inaccurate.**

**Refuse to prepare a second version of an agreement for the lender.**

**Escalate to the broker immediately** — this is not a judgment to make alone.

**Document what you were asked and what you did.**

**Withdraw** where the client persists.

There is no version of this where going along is the smaller risk.

### 6.4 The honest sentence

Most of what this module asks of a licensee reduces to one habit: describe accurately, refer specifics to the lender, and never let a document say something you know is untrue. A licensee who does that will occasionally be the reason a transaction slows down, and will never be the reason one becomes a criminal matter.

#### Checkpoint 6.1

A buyer asks the licensee to write the purchase agreement at $315,000 with a $15,000 "repair credit" instead of $300,000, so he can finance the down payment. What is the answer?

*Answer:* No, and the reason must be said plainly: the structure misstates the price to the lender, which is mortgage fraud, and the licensee would be participating by preparing it. The licensee refuses, tells the buyer why, informs the broker, and documents it. A repair credit supported by an actual repair, disclosed to the lender and appearing on the closing statement, is a different thing entirely — and the difference is disclosure.

## Key terms

**Acceleration clause** — makes the entire balance due on default.

**Annual percentage rate** — the cost of credit expressed as a yearly rate, including finance charges beyond the note rate.

**Due-on-sale clause** — makes the balance due on transfer of the property.

**Lien theory** — the doctrine, followed in Michigan, under which the borrower holds title and the lender holds a lien.

**Loan-to-value** — loan amount as a percentage of the lesser of price or appraised value.

**Point** — one percent of the loan amount, paid at closing.

**Redemption period** — the period after a sheriff's sale during which the borrower may redeem; length varies and must be verified.

**Short sale** — a sale for less than the mortgage balance, requiring lender approval.

## Practice problems

**Problem 1.** A property sells for $268,000 with 15 percent down. Compute the down payment, loan amount, and LTV.

*Working:* Down payment 268,000 × 0.15 = $40,200. Loan 268,000 − 40,200 = $227,800. LTV = 227,800 ÷ 268,000 = 85 percent, which is above 80 and so will ordinarily carry mortgage insurance.

**Problem 2.** The buyer pays 1.5 discount points on that loan. What is the cost?

*Working:* 227,800 × 0.015 = $3,417.

**Problem 3.** First-month interest on the same loan at 6.75 percent.

*Working:* 227,800 × 0.0675 ÷ 12 = $1,281.38.

**Problem 4.** A lender issues a revised Closing Disclosure two days before a Friday closing, increasing the APR beyond tolerance. What happens to the date?

*Working:* The three-business-day period restarts, so the closing moves. The licensee's job is to have told all parties that this could happen and to manage the possession and moving arrangements that depend on the date — not to argue with the lender about a federal timing rule.

**Problem 5.** A seller offers to pay the buyer's agent a bonus directly if the transaction closes by month end. Analyse.

*Working:* Compensation to a salesperson flows through the broker, from module 01. A direct payment is improper on that ground alone. Whether the bonus must also be disclosed to the lender and appear on the closing statement is a settlement question, and undisclosed payments in a financed transaction raise RESPA and fraud issues. Route it through the broker and disclose it.

**Problem 6.** A buyer says she will occupy the property as her residence, and mentions to the licensee that she actually intends to rent it out immediately.

*Working:* The occupancy declaration in the loan file will be false, and the licensee now knows it. The licensee cannot transmit or facilitate the false declaration, must tell the buyer that investment financing is a different product, and should escalate. Saying nothing because "it's the lender's problem" is participation.

## Module summary

**The note is the debt and the mortgage is the security.** Michigan is a lien theory state.

**Due-on-sale is why assumption is a feature, not a right.** Acceleration is why default reaches the whole balance.

**Loan type changes the property**, not only the borrower — FHA and VA property standards, MSHDA and USDA eligibility, and the land contract's separate legal world.

**LTV, points, and the qualifying ratios are arithmetic a licensee should do in their head** — and qualification is still the lender's job.

**Michigan foreclosure has two routes and a redemption period whose length must be verified, never recalled.**

**RESPA prohibits paying or accepting anything of value for a referral**, and a marketing agreement with no services is a referral fee with a label.

**The Closing Disclosure's three-business-day rule constrains closing dates**, and certain changes restart it.

**ECOA protects marital status, age, and public assistance income** — classes the federal Fair Housing Act does not reach.

**Never transmit a document you know to be inaccurate.** The intent to help a buyer does not convert a false statement to a lender into anything other than fraud.

## Required applied exercise

Prepare a one-page financing review memorandum for the transaction supplied with this module. Compute the loan amount, LTV, points, first-month interest, and both qualifying ratios; identify the loan type and every property consequence it carries; identify each federal timing rule that constrains the closing date; identify any payment, credit, or side arrangement that raises a RESPA or fraud question and state what you would do about it; and state what you would refer to the lender and what to counsel. The approved instructor scores it under the common 10-point applied-memo rubric; 8/10 with no zero on legal accuracy or professional boundaries is satisfactory, and a needs-revision result receives feedback and must be resubmitted.
