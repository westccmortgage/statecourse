# FREC Course I — video instruction plan

Planning only. No video has been scripted, recorded, or rendered, and none may be until curriculum
authoring is authorised.

---

## What Florida actually says about video, which is less than you would expect

Two rules used to govern it and **both are repealed**:

- Rule 61J2-3.016, *Video Tape Quality Standards* — repealed 4 May 2008.
- Rule 61J2-3.017, *Video Tape Classroom Viewing Conditions* — repealed 27 April 2008.

So Florida has **no surviving media-quality standard** for pre-licence courses. That is a real
finding rather than an absence of research: the chapter index at `flrules.org` still lists both
rules, carrying "(Repealed)" in their titles, which is precisely the kind of listing that gets cited
by someone who read the title and not the status.

What governs video instead is the general distance-education criteria in Rule 61J2-3.008(4)(b)3,
which say nothing about media as such and a great deal about time, interaction and evidence. Three
of them bear directly on video and none of them is about production quality.

**Hours are hours.** Sub-item 1 requires distance study "on a classroom hour per classroom hour
basis," and sub-item 2 makes a 50-minute distance hour the equivalent of a 50-minute classroom hour.
A minute of video is a minute of the 3,000-minute instructional budget, exactly like a minute of
anything else. Video does not buy time and does not cost less than it takes.

**Stated hours must be provable.** Sub-item 3.e requires "evidence by means of an objective study
that the stated course hours are consistent with actual hours required to complete the course." If a
video counts toward the hour budget, its running time is part of what that study has to account for
— which means run times must be known and stable before the time study is run, not adjusted
afterwards to make the arithmetic work.

**The course must be interactive.** Sub-item 2 requires the course to be "interactive, providing for
the interchange of information between the student, and instructor." Video on its own is not
interchange. It can sit inside a course that provides interchange; it cannot be what provides it.

## The product rules, which are stricter than Florida's

StateCourse's own decisions govern the rest, and on video they are more demanding than the
regulator's silence. Restated here because the silence is exactly where a decorative feature gets
in.

- **Selective.** Video appears where moving pictures teach something that text cannot — a document
  walked through field by field, a computation performed, a sequence shown in order. It does not
  appear because a section looked plain.
- **Instructional.** No welcome messages, no motivational segments, no talking-head restatement of
  what the page already says. If a video's script would read as an adequate paragraph, it should be
  a paragraph.
- **Optional and non-blocking.** No student is ever prevented from progressing by a video. Nothing
  waits on playback and nothing is gated behind a completed view.
- **Captioned.** Every video, without exception, and captions accurate enough to be read instead of
  the audio rather than alongside it.
- **No examination content, ever.** Nothing in a video, its script, its captions, its thumbnail, its
  transcript, or any poster frame may reproduce or paraphrase an end-of-course examination item, its
  distractors, or its answer.

---

## The tension this creates, and how it resolves

Optional-and-non-blocking sits awkwardly beside hour-per-hour. If a video is optional, a student who
skips it has spent less time than the timeline says; if it counts toward the 3,000 instructional
minutes, skipping it makes the stated hours untrue for that student, and Rule 61J2-3.008(4)(b)3.e
requires the stated hours to be defensible.

The resolution is to keep the two apart rather than to compromise either:

**Video sits outside the instructional-hour budget.** The 3,000 minutes are carried entirely by
material every student receives. Video is enrichment layered on top of a section that already meets
its own time allocation without it.

That has three consequences worth stating plainly, because each is a constraint on later work:

1. A video may never be the only place a required point is taught. Whatever it shows must also be
   present in the section's own material.
2. The time study measures the course without video. Video run time is reported separately and
   claimed as nothing.
3. Section hour allocations are computed from written and interactive material alone, so adding or
   removing a video later never disturbs the hour architecture or invalidates the time study.

The alternative — counting video toward the hours — would force it to be mandatory, blocking, and
tracked to the second, which contradicts three product rules to buy nothing Florida asked for.

## Confidentiality, restated because video leaks differently

The examination-content rule needs its own paragraph because video has more surfaces than text does,
and most of them are easy to forget.

A page can be checked by reading it. A video has a script, a rendered audio track, a caption file, a
transcript, a poster frame, a thumbnail, chapter markers, and a filename — and each of those is a
separate artifact that can carry a leak past a reviewer who watched the video and thought that was
the check. Any confidentiality control built for Florida video has to cover all of them, and the
check has to run against the artifacts rather than against the viewing experience.

The same applies to anything a reviewer receives. Rule 61J2-3.008(4)(b) requires the school to give
the Commission access to the course in the format in which it is offered to students; that access
must not become a route by which examination material reaches a surface it should not be on.

## What happens next, and what does not

Nothing here is authorised to proceed. When curriculum authoring is authorised, the order is:

1. Curriculum written and paginated, section by section, in syllabus order.
2. Section hour allocations computed from that material alone.
3. Time study run against the course without video.
4. Video candidates re-tested against the finished sections — several of the five above will
   probably not survive contact with the written material, which is the point of testing them
   afterwards rather than committing now.
5. Scripts written, captioned, and checked against the confidentiality rules across every artifact
   listed above.

No rendering, no production, and no scripting happens before step 4.
