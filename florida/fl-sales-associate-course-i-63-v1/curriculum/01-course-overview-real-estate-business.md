## How to work this section

Real estate is not one job. It is an industry in which different professionals bring different authority, training, data, and duties to the same property. A sales associate should be able to classify the requested service before offering it. For every problem in this section, use four questions:

1. **What act is requested?** Selling, leasing, managing, valuing, arranging credit, developing land, and constructing improvements are different acts.
2. **For whom is the act performed?** Florida's broker definition repeatedly uses the phrase “for another.” An owner handling the owner's own property presents a different licensing analysis from a person acting for a customer.
3. **What compensation or expected benefit is involved?** Section 475.01(1)(a), Florida Statutes, reaches direct and indirect compensation, promises of compensation, and intent to collect it.
4. **What credential and supervision are required?** A real estate license does not automatically confer appraisal, mortgage-origination, construction, legal, engineering, or community-association-management authority.

The answer is not controlled by a business card. A person called a “consultant” may be performing brokerage; a real estate sales associate called a “valuation expert” may still be prohibited from issuing an appraisal report; and a property manager may perform some acts that fall within the broker definition. Classify conduct first and titles second.

Ethics is part of that classification discipline. A professional should state the limits of a service, identify material assumptions, avoid using a protected credential, and refer work outside the professional's competence or authority. A referral is not an admission of weakness. It is a way to protect the consumer and the transaction.

## Objective-level application pages

### Objective 1.1 — curriculum page 2

**Teachable proposition.** Brokerage is defined by acts performed for another for compensation or an expected valuable consideration, not merely by the label “sale.” Under section 475.01(1)(a), the covered chain includes appraising within the limits of Part I, auctioning, selling, exchanging, buying, renting, and offering, attempting, or agreeing to do those things. It also includes advertising that one is in that business, procuring parties or prospects, and directing or assisting in negotiation or closing. Section 475.01(3) makes one covered act sufficient, subject to the statutory exemptions taught in Section 2.

A broker may coordinate a transaction, market property, obtain and communicate market information, solicit prospects, negotiate, supervise associates, manage entrusted transaction steps, and close through the appropriate professionals. A sales associate may perform broker-defined acts only under the direction, control, or management of another person as stated in section 475.01(1)(j). Section 475.01(2) also warns that calling the relationship an independent-contractor relationship does not erase statutory duties.

Brokerage frequently includes sales and leasing, but the FREC outline also places property management, valuation, financing awareness, counseling, development, construction, government, and professional organizations in the industry overview. Some are adjacent professions, not powers granted by a real estate license. “REALTOR®,” for example, identifies membership in a trade organization; it is not the name of the Florida license. A licensee may be a broker or sales associate without being a member of that organization, and membership cannot replace licensure.

**Worked Florida example.** Dana, who has no real estate license, offers to locate owners of small apartment buildings, introduce them to an investor, and help negotiate a purchase price in exchange for one percent of any completed purchase. Dana never signs a listing agreement and calls the work “consulting.” The requested acts include procuring sellers and assisting negotiations for another, with promised transaction-based compensation. Those facts fit the broker-definition analysis even though Dana avoids the words “broker” and “commission.” Before reaching a final conclusion, the analyst must also check section 475.011 for an exemption; the label alone does no work.

**Guided primary-source task.** Read section 475.01(1)(a), (j), and subsection (3). Create a two-column list. In the first column, quote no more than eight words identifying each covered act; in the second, give an original example. Circle the phrases addressing expected compensation and a single act. Then cite the paragraph and subsection that explain why “independent contractor” does not eliminate duties.

**Independent new-fact application.** Lee receives a flat $500 to identify potential tenants for a building owned by an unrelated investor, but Lee does not negotiate a lease. Separately analyze the act, the “for another” element, the compensation element, and any exemption that must be researched. State what additional fact you would request before advising whether Lee may proceed.

### Objective 1.2 — curriculum page 3

**Teachable proposition.** The five major sales specialties in the prescribed syllabus are residential, commercial, industrial, agricultural, and business brokerage. The categories help a professional select relevant facts and specialists; they do not create five different real estate licenses.

| Specialty | Typical property or interest | Information that commonly matters | A risk question to raise |
|---|---|---|---|
| Residential | Housing and residential lots | comparable sales, condition, financing, occupancy | Is a fact material to residential value and not readily observable? |
| Commercial | Retail, office, hospitality, or income property | leases, tenant quality, expenses, net operating income | Are income assumptions supported by documents? |
| Industrial | Warehouses, manufacturing, logistics, or specialized plants | access, utilities, loading, power, environmental and zoning constraints | Can the proposed use lawfully and physically operate there? |
| Agricultural | Farms, ranches, groves, timber, or other productive acreage | soil, water, access, production history, conservation and use restrictions | Are production claims and land-use rights independently verified? |
| Business opportunity | A business enterprise or opportunity, sometimes with real property | assets, liabilities, financial statements, contracts, goodwill, inventory | Which interests are being transferred and which specialist must verify them? |

A broker or associate may “farm” a geographic area or target market by learning its inventory, customers, ownership patterns, and market data. Farming is a marketing method, not permission to mislead, solicit unlawfully, or act outside the employing broker's supervision. Specialization likewise does not turn a licensee into an engineer, environmental consultant, accountant, lawyer, appraiser, or mortgage loan originator.

Mixed transactions require more than one classification. A sale of a restaurant may involve a business opportunity, a lease assignment, equipment and inventory, licenses, and perhaps land. The practitioner should inventory the components instead of forcing the transaction into a single label.

**Worked Florida example.** A buyer wants a citrus grove with a packing building and intends to convert part of it to an event venue. Calling the property “agricultural” is only the beginning. Production history and water access concern the existing agricultural use; the building raises industrial or commercial facts; the proposed event use raises zoning, capacity, and professional-inspection questions. The sales specialty organizes the investigation but cannot resolve those separate issues.

**Guided primary-source task.** Read the list under “Five major sales specialties” on FREC syllabus p. 4 and the definitions of “broker” and “real property” in section 475.01(1)(a) and (i). Identify why a business opportunity can fall within Part I even when land is not the only asset. Submit the syllabus page, statutory pinpoint, and a one-sentence finding in your own words.

**Independent new-fact application.** A transaction includes a marina business, a long-term lease of waterfront land, boats, fuel inventory, and customer contracts. Classify every component that can be identified from the facts, select the primary sales specialty, and list three professionals or information sources that may be needed. Explain why the primary specialty does not erase the other components.

### Objective 1.3 — curriculum page 4

**Teachable proposition.** A property manager acts to protect the owner's investment and pursue the objectives stated in the management agreement. Typical work includes developing operating budgets, marketing vacancies, screening and communicating with applicants within applicable law, administering leases, collecting and accounting for funds, coordinating maintenance, documenting property condition, handling vendor relationships, reporting income and expenses, and recommending longer-term repairs or capital work. The written agreement should identify the property, authority delegated, approval thresholds, compensation, reporting, handling of funds, term, and termination.

An absentee owner may depend heavily on the manager, but distance does not enlarge the manager's authority. The manager must distinguish an owner's goals from the powers actually granted. A direction to “maximize return” does not authorize deferred safety work, discrimination, concealment, misuse of deposits, or a contract outside delegated authority.

Property management can include broker-defined acts such as renting another's real property, advertising it, procuring tenants, or negotiating leases. Licensing analysis therefore applies. Section 475.011 contains narrow exemptions, including a salaried employee of an owner or the owner's registered broker working in an onsite rental office of an apartment community, and a salaried manager of a condominium or cooperative complex for rental-related activity involving individual units when the arranged rentals do not exceed one year. Each fact and limitation must fit; “property manager” is not itself a blanket exemption.

The syllabus also instructs learners to distinguish a property manager from a community association manager (CAM). The roles can overlap in ordinary speech, but the credentials are not interchangeable. This section does not decide whether a particular community-association task requires CAM licensure. Identify the proposed task and verify the current governing law before accepting it.

**Worked Florida example.** An onsite salaried apartment employee shows units and prepares leasing paperwork for the apartment owner. The facts direct the analyst to section 475.011(4). If the same employee starts locating tenants for unrelated single-family owners on evenings and receives a fee per lease, the owner, property, compensation method, and place of work have changed. The apartment-community exemption should not be carried over by job title.

**Guided primary-source task.** Read section 475.011(4) and (5). Build a fact checklist for each exemption: employer, compensation method, location or property type, activity, and rental duration where stated. Then read FREC syllabus p. 4, “Property Management,” and add two duties that belong in a management agreement. Do not generalize either exemption beyond its words.

**Independent new-fact application.** A salaried manager of a condominium complex arranges a fourteen-month lease of one unit and separately coordinates roof repairs for the association. Analyze the rental exemption element by element. Flag the separate community-association function for current Chapter 468 review rather than assuming that a real estate license or the rental exemption resolves it.

### Objective 1.4 — curriculum page 5

**Teachable proposition.** Valuation work must be classified by the assignment, the expected audience and use, the credential held, and the way the result is communicated.

- An **appraisal** or appraisal service is defined in section 475.611(1)(a) through assignments involving analysis, opinions, reviews, or conclusions about identified real property. An appraisal report is regulated even if the author gives it a different title.
- A **comparative market analysis (CMA)** is an ordinary-course brokerage analysis that uses relevant market evidence to assist a brokerage decision, commonly a proposed listing or offer strategy. It is not an appraisal and must not be presented as one.
- A **broker price opinion (BPO)** is also a broker's price or value opinion in the ordinary course of brokerage. Statutes group ordinary-course CMAs, price opinions, and opinions of value together for the relevant carve-outs; a course should not invent a legal distinction the statute does not supply.

Section 475.612(1) restricts protected appraiser titles and who may issue an appraisal report. Subsection (2) preserves specified valuation work by Florida real estate licensees who do not misrepresent appraiser credentials. Subsection (3) says an ordinary-course CMA, price opinion, or opinion of value may not be referred to or construed as an appraisal. Section 475.25(1)(t) similarly distinguishes ordinary-course CMA/BPO work from appraisal standards. A lender, court, agency, client instruction, or federal rule may require a licensed or certified appraiser; relabeling a CMA cannot satisfy that requirement.

USPAP means the Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice, defined in section 475.611(1)(bb) as the most recent standards approved and adopted by the Appraisal Standards Board of The Appraisal Foundation. Florida's appraisal board adopts professional standards by rule. Because editions and incorporation can change, always verify Rule 61J1-9.001 and the applicable assignment requirements rather than memorizing an edition from a course.

**Worked Florida example.** A listing broker prepares a market comparison to help a seller select an asking price and labels it “Certified Residential Appraisal.” The market-comparison purpose may be consistent with a CMA, but the protected title and “appraisal” label are not. If a lender requires an appraisal for underwriting, the same document does not become acceptable merely because it contains comparable sales. The broker should accurately label the service, state its brokerage purpose and limitations, and refer the appraisal assignment to an appropriately credentialed appraiser.

**Guided primary-source task.** Read sections 475.611(1)(a), (e), (bb), and (cc); 475.612(1)–(3); and 475.25(1)(t). Prepare a three-row chart for appraisal, CMA, and BPO. For each, state who may perform or issue it, what name may be used, which paragraph supports the conclusion, and one fact that would require further authority. Resolve any apparent tension by giving effect to all cited provisions rather than relying on one sentence.

**Independent new-fact application.** A probate attorney asks a sales associate for a written “appraisal” of a waterfront house for use in a contested estate hearing and offers a separate fee. Identify at least four facts that make this different from an ordinary listing CMA. State what the associate may not promise and which source you would consult before the brokerage accepts any valuation assignment.

### Objective 1.5 — curriculum page 6

**Teachable proposition.** A mortgage transaction moves through related but distinct decisions: the consumer explores financing; submits financial and property information; the creditor or its agents verify information; underwriting evaluates credit, collateral, and program requirements; title, insurance, appraisal, and other conditions are addressed; a commitment or other decision is communicated; closing documents are prepared and signed; funds are disbursed; and the loan may later be serviced or transferred. Not every transaction follows the same sequence, and a prequalification or preapproval is not the same as a final commitment to lend.

The real estate licensee helps the parties coordinate contract and financing deadlines, communicates facts accurately, and directs financing questions to the proper professional. The licensee should not promise approval, dictate an appraisal result, alter financial documents, or negotiate mortgage terms unless separately authorized to perform that work.

Section 494.001(17) defines a loan originator by conduct such as soliciting or accepting a mortgage-loan application or negotiating mortgage-loan terms for compensation or gain. Section 494.00312(1) requires an individual who acts as a loan originator to be licensed under that section, subject to Chapter 494's exemptions. Section 494.00331 addresses the originator's relationship with a mortgage broker or lender. A real estate sales associate license is not a mortgage loan originator license.

**Worked Florida example.** A buyer asks a sales associate to compare two written lender worksheets. The associate may help the buyer identify contract deadlines and suggest questions for the lenders. If the associate selects a rate, offers to negotiate loan terms with a lender for a fee, or accepts the buyer's financial application as the person originating the loan, the facts raise Chapter 494 licensure issues. The safe response is not simply “I work in real estate”; it is to identify the requested act and stay within the credential actually held.

**Guided primary-source task.** On the official Florida Legislature site, read sections 494.001(17), 494.00312(1), and 494.00331(1). Record the statute year and access date. List the verbs that define loan-origination conduct, identify the licensure sentence, and explain why merely handing a consumer a lender's public contact information presents different facts from negotiating the consumer's loan terms.

**Independent new-fact application.** A brokerage offers an associate a bonus for collecting buyers' income documents, recommending a specific loan program, and transmitting completed applications to an affiliated mortgage company. Separate ministerial facts from solicitation, application, and negotiation facts. State which facts require review by the employing broker and a Chapter 494 specialist before the program begins.

### Objective 1.6 — curriculum page 7

**Teachable proposition.** The three phases prescribed by the FREC outline are **land acquisition**, **subdividing and development**, and **construction**. Marketing and occupancy may occur in the project life cycle, but they are not a substitute for the syllabus's three-phase sequence.

1. **Land acquisition.** The developer identifies a site, investigates title and access, studies physical and legal feasibility, estimates demand and cost, controls or acquires the land, and evaluates financing and exit risk. A purchase before sufficient investigation can lock the project into a site that cannot support the planned use.
2. **Subdividing and development.** Subdividing creates legally described parcels under applicable approvals. Development makes land suitable for its intended use through planning, approvals, grading, drainage, roads, utilities, and other improvements. A subdivision plat map depicts the approved parcel layout and related information. Dedication is the transfer or setting apart of land or an interest for public use; proposed dedication should be read from the approved instruments rather than assumed from a drawing.
3. **Construction.** Plans are converted into improvements under applicable contracts, permits, codes, inspections, schedules, and budgets. Construction risk includes labor and material cost, defects, delay, safety, weather, financing, and mismatch between the completed product and market demand.

The developer coordinates these phases but does not replace the surveyor, engineer, architect, contractor, attorney, environmental specialist, lender, title professional, or public authority. A broker marketing the project should verify approvals and present status precisely—for example, distinguishing “application submitted” from “approved” and “proposed plat” from “recorded plat.”

**Worked Florida example.** A developer signs a contract for fifty acres contingent on feasibility, obtains local approval for a residential plat and utility plan, records the approved plat, completes roads and drainage, and then contracts for homes. The facts map in order to acquisition, subdividing/development, and construction. If the developer begins advertising “approved waterfront lots” while the plat application is still pending, marketing has occurred, but phase-two approval has not. Advertising cannot move the regulatory fact forward.

**Guided primary-source task.** Read FREC syllabus pp. 4–5 under “Development and Construction.” Write the three phase names in the syllabus's order. Define subdivision plat map and dedication using the context supplied there, then list one county or municipal primary record you would inspect to verify each concept for an actual project. Do not count sales or occupancy as one of the prescribed three phases.

**Independent new-fact application.** A developer holds an option on raw land, has a conceptual site plan, has requested rezoning, and has accepted nonbinding expressions of interest from builders. Place each fact in the three-phase framework, identify what has not yet occurred, and rewrite the statement “construction-ready lots available” so it does not imply an approval or completed improvement absent from the facts.

### Objective 1.7 — curriculum page 8

**Teachable proposition.** The three syllabus categories of residential construction are **speculative (spec), tract, and custom**. They differ in the identity of the initial customer, repetition of design, site selection, choice, timing, and inventory risk.

| Category | Typical start of construction | Buyer choice | Builder's inventory exposure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spec | Builder begins without a particular buyer committed | Usually limited to selections still open when the buyer contracts | Builder bears the risk of holding the completed or partly completed home |
| Tract | Builder develops multiple homes from a limited plan set, sometimes in phases | Choice among offered lots, plans, elevations, and options, subject to stage | Risk is spread across a planned inventory but remains sensitive to absorption and phase timing |
| Custom | Particular owner engages a builder for a selected site and individualized design | High, within budget, law, site, plans, and contract | Allocation depends on the contract; the project is tied to an identified client rather than general inventory |

The categories describe a production and marketing pattern, not construction quality. A spec home can be high-end; a custom home can use standard components. A tract builder may build some inventory before sale and other homes after buyer selection. Classify the facts rather than assuming every project fits perfectly.

Contract and title facts also matter. Ask who owns the lot during construction, who approves changes, whether allowances or options are fixed, how draws and inspections work, when risk transfers, and what warranties or remedies the governing documents provide. A sales associate can coordinate and explain transaction facts within competence but should not give engineering, code, or legal opinions.

**Worked Florida example.** A builder buys twenty lots, offers four floor plans, releases five lots at a time, and allows buyers to choose finishes before a cutoff date. The project is primarily tract construction even though two homes begin before buyers sign contracts and become inventory. Those two units have a spec feature; the entire development does not become a custom project because buyers choose finishes.

**Guided primary-source task.** Read the construction categories on FREC syllabus p. 5. For each category, identify the likely initial customer, degree of plan repetition, and principal inventory-risk holder. Then explain in one sentence why neither price nor quality alone determines the category.

**Independent new-fact application.** A homeowner owns an infill lot and hires a builder to modify an existing plan extensively. A second builder starts the same base plan on a nearby lot with no buyer. Classify both projects, identify the fact that changes the classification, and list two contract or permit records a careful licensee should verify before describing either home to the public.

## Integrated application lab

Prepare a service-classification memorandum for this new fact pattern:

> Coastal Grove Holdings owns an apartment complex and an undeveloped adjacent parcel. It asks a sales associate to (1) market apartment leases, (2) manage rent and repairs, (3) state a value for refinancing under a document titled “appraisal,” (4) collect borrowers' financial data for the lender, (5) market future homes before the plat is approved, and (6) describe the future homes as custom although the builder will offer three standard plans.

For each numbered request, identify the specialty, governing role, license or credential issue, primary source to consult, material missing facts, and a compliant next step. Treat each request separately. A correct response does not assume one credential covers the entire project and does not imply that an approval, plat, value conclusion, financing decision, or construction category exists before the supporting facts exist.
