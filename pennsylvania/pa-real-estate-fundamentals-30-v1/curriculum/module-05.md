# Pennsylvania Real Estate Fundamentals — Module PA-F-05

## Contracts — general principles

**Course:** Real Estate Fundamentals · **Instructional hours: 5.0** of 30.0
**Course version:** 1.0 submission draft

> **Where this module sits.** This is the general contract law a licensee needs before the

> § 35.332, § 35.333 and § 35.334** are covered in *Real Estate Practice*; what follows is the framework

## Learning objectives

1. State the essential elements of a valid contract.
2. Distinguish void, voidable, unenforceable and valid.
3. Apply offer, acceptance, counteroffer and the mirror image rule.
4. Identify the defects in reality of consent.
5. State what the Statute of Frauds requires of a real estate contract.
6. Distinguish the remedies for breach, and explain why specific performance is available in real estate.
7. State the boundary § 35.292(a)(11) and (12) draw around a licensee and a contract.

## A. Essential elements

- **Competent parties** — legal capacity to contract.
- **Mutual assent** — offer and acceptance, a meeting of the minds.
- **Consideration** — something of legal value exchanged.
- **Lawful object** — a purpose the law permits.
- **A writing**, where the Statute of Frauds requires one — which it does for contracts conveying an
  interest in real property.

**Recording and notarisation are not elements of a contract.** They belong to the conveyance and to the
recording statute. A contract can be entirely valid, fully enforceable and never recorded.

### A.1 Four states a contract can be in

| State | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Valid** | Meets all elements; enforceable by either party. |
| **Void** | Never had legal effect at all. |
| **Voidable** | Valid **until the party with the power to avoid it elects to do so**. |
| **Unenforceable** | Valid in substance, but a court will not enforce it — most commonly for want of a writing. |

The practical difference between void and voidable is **who holds the power.** A contract with a minor is
voidable **at the minor's election** — the adult party cannot escape it. A contract induced by fraud is
voidable **at the defrauded party's election** — the party who lied cannot use their own fraud to get out.

### A.2 Classifications worth holding

**Bilateral** (a promise for a promise — most agreements of sale) versus **unilateral** (a promise for an
act — most option contracts on their face). **Express** versus **implied**. **Executory** (still being
performed) versus **executed** (fully performed).

An agreement of sale is **executory** from signing until settlement. That period — with duties running,
deadlines falling and money in escrow — is where a licensee does most of their work.

## B. Offer and acceptance

An **offer** is a manifestation of willingness to be bound on stated terms. It may be **revoked** by the
offeror **at any time before acceptance**, unless consideration was given to hold it open — which is what
an **option** is.

An offer **terminates** by: **revocation**; **rejection**; **lapse of a stated or reasonable time**;
**death or incapacity of either party**; **destruction of the subject matter**; or **counteroffer**.

### B.1 The mirror image rule, and why the last item on that list matters most

Acceptance must be **unqualified** and must **mirror** the offer. A purported acceptance that **changes
any term is a counteroffer** — and a counteroffer **rejects and terminates the original offer**, putting
the roles the other way round: the former offeree is now the offeror.

Work it through, because a licensee sees this weekly. A buyer offers Monday. The seller signs Tuesday but
strikes the settlement date and writes a new one. That is **not an acceptance.** The buyer's offer is
**gone** — it cannot be revived by the buyer changing their mind on Wednesday and signing the original.
What exists is the seller's counteroffer, which the buyer is free to accept, reject or counter.

**Acceptance must also be communicated.** A signed agreement sitting in a drawer has accepted nothing.

### B.2 Which brings in a duty the licensee owes regardless of side

**§ 35.292(a)(3)** — owed to **all** consumers and **not waivable**:

> *"**Present, in a reasonably practicable period of time, all offers, counteroffers, notices and
> communications to and from the parties in writing**, unless the property is subject to an existing
> contract and the seller/landlord has agreed in a written waiver."*

**All** offers. Not the ones the licensee thinks are worth presenting. The single exception is narrow and
has two conditions together: the property must already be **under contract**, **and** the seller must have
agreed **in a written waiver.**

## C. Reality of consent

Assent must be real. It is defective where there is:

- **Fraud** — an intentional misrepresentation of a material fact, relied upon, causing harm.
- **Misrepresentation** — a false statement of material fact, which may be innocent or negligent.
- **Duress** — assent compelled by unlawful pressure.
- **Undue influence** — improper pressure from a position of trust or dominance.
- **Mistake** — mutual mistake of material fact may permit rescission; unilateral mistake usually does
  not.

In each case the contract is **voidable at the election of the injured party.**

**Puffing** is opinion a reasonable person would not take as fact — *"a charming home."* **Misrepresentation
is a false statement of fact** — *"2,400 square feet"*, *"the roof is five years old"*, *"the basement has
never taken water."* The line moves toward misrepresentation as the statement gets **more specific and more
verifiable**, and a licensee who wants to stay on the safe side keeps to what they can document.

**§ 35.282** is the Pennsylvania rule on this and its title is the whole point: **Misleading advice,
assurances and representations.**

## D. The Statute of Frauds

Certain contracts must be **in writing** and **signed by the party to be charged** to be enforceable —
including **contracts for the sale of an interest in real property.** The consequence of failing is
**unenforceability**, not invalidity.

**Pennsylvania's Statute of Frauds was not read for this project.** This course therefore **states no
Pennsylvania lease-term threshold and no Pennsylvania exception** to it.

**What was read is the licensing rule that reaches further**, and it is broader than the Statute of
Frauds in an important way. **§ 35.281(a):**

> *"**All contracts, commitments and agreements** between a broker, or a licensee employed by the broker,
> and a principal or a consumer **who is required to pay a fee, commission or other valuable consideration
> shall be in writing** and contain the information specified in § 35.331."*

A listing agreement and a buyer agency agreement are **employment agreements**, not contracts to convey —
so the Statute of Frauds may or may not reach them. **§ 35.281 reaches them regardless**, and it does so
as a condition of the licence. A licensee who asks "does the Statute of Frauds require this in writing?"
has asked the wrong question. The right question is what the Commission's rule requires, and the answer is
**in writing, with the § 35.331 contents.**

### D.1 The parol evidence rule

Where a written contract is a complete expression of the parties' agreement, evidence of **prior or
contemporaneous oral agreements offered to vary its terms** is barred. Combined with the writing
requirements above, the working rule for a licensee is simple: **every change goes on paper, signed.**

## E. Performance, breach and remedies

A contract discharges by **performance**, by **mutual agreement**, by **operation of law**, or by
**breach**.

**Assignment** transfers rights and **generally leaves the assignor liable**. **Novation** substitutes a
new party and **releases** the original — which is why it requires **all** parties' agreement. Confusing
the two is how a seller discovers, after an assignment they approved, that they are still on the hook.

### E.1 Remedies for breach

- **Damages** — compensation for loss.
- **Liquidated damages** — a sum the parties fixed in advance. In real estate this is commonly the
  earnest money deposit, where the agreement so provides.
- **Rescission** — unwinding the contract and restoring the parties.
- **Specific performance** — a court order compelling performance of the contract itself.

**Specific performance is available in real estate because each parcel is unique**, so damages are often
an inadequate remedy. That is the legal consequence of the physical characteristic taught in Module
PA-F-02: no two parcels are the same, so money cannot buy a substitute.

### E.2 Where the deposit sits while any of this happens

**§ 35.327**, and it is worth reading before any dispute arises:

> *"If a dispute arises between the parties to a real estate transaction over entitlement to money that is
> being held in escrow by a broker, **the broker shall retain the money in escrow until the dispute is
> resolved**. If resolution of the dispute appears remote without legal action, the broker may, **following
> 30 days' notice to the parties, petition the county court having jurisdiction in the matter to interplead
> the rival claimants**."*

**The broker does not decide who wins.** The broker holds. The escrow module in the companion course
develops this fully; it appears here because the moment a contract breaks is the moment a licensee is
asked to release money, and the answer is always the same.

## F. Where a licensee stands in relation to a contract

Two sentences from § 35.292(a), read together, define the whole of it:

> **(11)** ***"Provide assistance with document preparation."***
> **(12)** ***"Advise the consumer about compliance with laws pertaining to real estate transactions
> without rendering legal advice."***

**Assistance with document preparation is inside the job.** Completing the blanks on a form, assembling
the addenda a transaction needs, making sure the required contents are present — all of that is what a
licensee is for, and (11) says so affirmatively.

**Composing contract language is not.** Drafting a clause to protect a buyer against a survey problem,
writing a custom contingency, or advising what a provision will mean if litigated — those are the practice
of law, and (12)'s closing words say so.

The reliable test is not the length of what you write. It is whether you are **filling in a form** or
**inventing a term.** When you are inventing a term, the answer is an attorney, and § 35.292(a)(8) makes
sending them there a duty rather than a courtesy.
