# Pennsylvania Real Estate Fundamentals — Module PA-F-04

## Transfer of title, deeds and recording

**Course:** Real Estate Fundamentals · **Instructional hours: 5.0** of 30.0
**Course version:** 1.0 submission draft
**Regulatory status:** Development. Not approved. No Pennsylvania prelicensing credit is available.

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish voluntary from involuntary transfer.
2. State the essential elements of a valid deed.
3. Distinguish the deed types by the covenants each carries.
4. Explain what recording does and does not accomplish.
5. Distinguish an abstract, a title opinion and title insurance, and distinguish owner's from lender's
   coverage.
6. Identify what happens at settlement in Pennsylvania and who does it.

---

## A. Voluntary transfer

**By deed**, during life. **By will**, at death — the maker is the **testator**, the gift of real property
a **devise**, its recipient a **devisee**. **By dedication**, where an owner gives land to public use.

## B. Involuntary transfer

- **Intestate succession** — no will; the Commonwealth's statute directs distribution. **Pennsylvania's
  intestacy statute was not read for this project; no share, priority or spousal allowance is stated.**
- **Escheat** — no heirs and no will.
- **Eminent domain** — condemnation with just compensation.
- **Foreclosure** — sale to satisfy a lien. **Pennsylvania's foreclosure procedure was not read for this
  project; no notice period, redemption right or deficiency rule is stated.**
- **Tax sale** — for unpaid property taxes, under a statute this project did not read.
- **Adverse possession** — title by long possession that is, in the usual formulation, **open, notorious,
  continuous, exclusive, hostile and under a claim of right for the statutory period.**
  **Pennsylvania's statutory period was not read for this project and is not stated here.** That the
  doctrine exists is the licensee's cue: a long-standing fence, a neighbour's driveway, a shed across a
  line — all are questions for a surveyor and counsel.
- **Accretion, erosion, avulsion and reliction** — the slow or sudden work of water on a boundary.

## C. The deed

### C.1 Essential elements

- **Grantor** with legal capacity, and **grantee** identified with certainty.
- **Words of conveyance** — the granting clause.
- **Consideration** recited.
- **Legal description** of the property.
- **Signature of the grantor.** *(The grantee does not sign a deed.)*
- **Delivery and acceptance** — a deed takes effect on delivery, not on signing.

**Acknowledgement before a notary is required for recording**, and recording is what makes the
conveyance effective against third parties. A deed that is valid between grantor and grantee but never
recorded is a problem waiting for a subsequent purchaser.

### C.2 The clauses

**Premises** (parties, consideration, description and granting clause); **habendum** (*to have and to
hold* — defining the estate conveyed); **reddendum** (reservations); **covenants**; **exceptions and
reservations**; **execution and acknowledgement.**

### C.3 Deed types, ranked by what the grantee gets

| Deed | What it warrants |
|---|---|
| **General warranty deed** | The fullest covenants, reaching back through the **entire chain of title** — not merely the grantor's own period of ownership. |
| **Special (limited) warranty deed** | Covenants only against defects **arising during the grantor's ownership**. |
| **Bargain and sale deed** | Implies the grantor holds title, with covenants varying by form. |
| **Quitclaim deed** | Conveys whatever interest the grantor has — **possibly none** — with **no warranty at all**. |

The usual covenants of a general warranty deed: **seisin**, **right to convey**, **against
encumbrances**, **quiet enjoyment**, **further assurance**, and **warranty forever.**

**A quitclaim deed is not a lesser-quality conveyance of good title. It is a conveyance of whatever the
grantor happens to hold, warranting nothing** — which is exactly why it is the right instrument for
clearing a cloud and the wrong one for a purchase. Recording does not improve it: **a recorded quitclaim
deed is still a quitclaim deed.**

**Special-purpose deeds** — executor's, administrator's, sheriff's, trustee's, and a **deed in lieu of
foreclosure** — are named here so a licensee recognises them in a chain of title. Each raises questions
of authority that belong to a title examiner and counsel.

## D. Recording

### D.1 What recording does

Recording in the office of the **recorder of deeds for the county where the property lies** gives
**constructive notice** to the world. Everyone is treated as knowing what the record contains, whether
they looked or not.

**Actual notice** is what a person in fact knows. **Constructive notice** is what the record imputes.
**Inquiry notice** is what a person would have learned had they investigated a fact that should have
prompted investigation — someone else in possession, for example.

### D.2 What recording does not do

**Recording does not validate an invalid instrument.** A forged deed is void whether recorded or not.
**Recording does not improve the estate conveyed** — see the quitclaim note above. **Recording does not
establish that the grantor had good title**; it establishes only that the instrument is of record.

### D.3 Priority

**Pennsylvania's recording statute was not read for this project.** This course therefore **states no
Pennsylvania recording priority rule and no recording deadline.** What the licensee needs to know is the
practical consequence, which is universal: **an unrecorded interest is an exposure**, and prompt recording
is how a purchaser protects the thing they just bought.

**Related, and read:** § 35.321(c) provides that where a broker receives money under an **installment
land purchase agreement**, the transaction is considered consummated for escrow purposes *"when the buyer
has been afforded the opportunity, by means of the **seller's written acknowledgement on or affixed to the
agreement, to record the agreement**, unless the agreement specifies otherwise."* Even the escrow rule
turns on the buyer's ability to record.

## E. Evidence of title

- **Chain of title** — the successive conveyances from some root to the present owner.
- **Abstract of title** — a condensed history of everything of record affecting the title.
- **Title search and examination** — the work of running the chain and reading what it discloses.
- **Cloud on title** — an outstanding claim or apparent defect: an uncancelled mortgage, a gap in the
  chain, an unreleased judgment, an heir who never joined in a deed.
- **Marketable title** — title a reasonable buyer would accept, free of reasonable doubt.
- **Quiet title action** — a lawsuit to resolve a cloud.

### E.1 Title insurance

An indemnity policy against loss from covered title defects, **paid for once**, covering **defects
existing at the policy date** — not future events.

**Two policies, and they protect different people:**

- **Lender's (mortgagee's) policy** — protects the **lender**, in the amount of the loan, decreasing as
  the loan is paid. It is almost always required by the lender, and the **buyer usually pays for it.**
- **Owner's policy** — protects the **owner**, in the amount of the purchase price.

**A buyer who pays for a lender's policy has bought the lender protection, not themselves.** That is the
single most useful thing a licensee can explain to a first-time buyer at the settlement table, and it
takes one sentence.

Every policy has **exceptions** — matters it does not cover, listed in Schedule B, often including
matters a **survey** would disclose. **This course states no policy term, exception, endorsement or
premium rate**, because no title policy form and no Pennsylvania rate manual was read for this project.
Coverage questions go to the title company and to counsel.

## F. Settlement in Pennsylvania

**Settlement** is where the documents are signed, the funds are exchanged, and the deed and mortgage are
delivered for recording.

### F.1 What the licensee owes before it — § 35.334

> **(a)** *"**Before an agreement of sale is executed**, the brokers involved in the transaction shall
> provide **each party** with a **written estimate of reasonably foreseeable expenses** associated with
> the sale that the party may be expected to pay"* — including **the broker's commission; the mortgage
> payments and financing costs; taxes and assessments; and settlement expenses.**
>
> **(b)** *"The estimates ... shall be **as accurate as may be reasonably expected of a person having
> knowledge of, and experience in, real estate sales**."*

Note the timing: **before the agreement is executed**, not before settlement. And note the standard in
(b) — it is not "best efforts," it is the accuracy expected of someone who knows the business. The rule
then prints an **exemplary Statement of Estimated Costs to Buyer at Settlement**, whose line items name
the costs a Pennsylvania buyer actually meets: **title search and insurance; conveyancing or preparation
of papers; recording fees for the deed and the mortgage; the mortgagee's charges including appraisal fee,
credit report, and origination and placement fees; mortgage service charge; fire insurance; local realty
transfer tax; and Pennsylvania realty transfer tax.**

**This course states no realty transfer tax rate, state or local**, because Pennsylvania's realty
transfer tax statutes and every local transfer tax ordinance were **not read for this project.** Every
practice problem supplies the rate as a given inside the problem.

### F.2 Prorations

Items paid in advance or in arrears are **prorated** between seller and buyer as of settlement — real
estate taxes, municipal charges, homeowner association dues, rents and security deposits on tenanted
property, and fuel remaining in a tank.

**A proration is always a double entry**: whatever is credited to one party is debited to the other. A
prorated item that appears once has been done wrong.

**Which party owns the day of settlement, and whether a 360-day or 365-day convention is used, are matters
of contract and local practice. This course states neither as a Pennsylvania rule**, because 49 Pa. Code
Chapter 35 states neither. Practice problems specify the convention inside the problem.

### F.3 Who conducts settlement

In Pennsylvania settlement is commonly conducted by a **title company** or an **attorney**. **The licensee
does not conduct settlement, does not prepare the deed, and does not give an opinion on the state of the
title.** § 35.292(a)(11) does say a licensee shall *"**provide assistance with document preparation**"* —
and (a)(12) says advise about compliance *"**without rendering legal advice**."* Assistance with the
forms of the transaction is inside the job. Drafting a conveyance is not.
