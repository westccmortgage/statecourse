# Pennsylvania Real Estate Fundamentals — Module PA-F-03

## Legal descriptions, land use control and environmental considerations

**Course:** Real Estate Fundamentals · **Instructional hours: 5.0** of 30.0
**Course version:** 1.0 submission draft
**Regulatory status:** Development. Not approved. No Pennsylvania prelicensing credit is available.

## Learning objectives

1. Read the three legal description methods and know which Pennsylvania uses.
2. Distinguish a legal description from an address, a tax parcel number and a plat reference.
3. Identify the four government powers that limit private ownership.
4. Distinguish zoning, variances, special exceptions and nonconforming uses.
5. State the § 35.333(a)(6) zoning-classification requirement and the consequence of omitting it.
6. Identify the environmental conditions a licensee must route rather than opine on.

## A. Legal descriptions

An **address** identifies a mailbox. A **tax parcel number** identifies an assessment record. **Neither
describes land**, and neither belongs in a deed as the description.

### A.1 Metes and bounds

**Pennsylvania is a metes and bounds Commonwealth.** A metes and bounds description begins at a **point
of beginning**, runs a sequence of **courses** (directions) and **distances**, calls **monuments** —
natural or artificial markers — and **returns to the point of beginning.**

Two working facts follow:

- **A description that does not close describes no parcel.** If the courses and distances do not return
  to the point of beginning, something is wrong, and it is a surveyor's problem, not a licensee's.
- **Monuments generally control over distances** where they conflict, because a called monument is
  physical evidence of what the parties actually meant. That is a rule of construction applied by courts,
  and it is named here so a licensee knows a conflict is resolvable — by a surveyor and counsel, not by
  the licensee.

**A fence is not a boundary.** It is evidence of where somebody once thought the boundary was. Where a
fence, a hedge, a driveway or a shed sits near a line, the answer is a **current survey**.

### A.2 Rectangular (government) survey

Townships, ranges, sections and quarter-sections, referenced to principal meridians and base lines. **A
section is one square mile and contains 640 acres.** Pennsylvania is not a public-land-survey state, but
this system is examinable and appears in national content.

### A.3 Reference to a recorded plat (lot and block)

Common in subdivisions: *Lot 14, Block C, of the [named] Plan of Lots, as recorded in Plan Book __, Page
__.* The recorded plat supplies the metes and bounds by reference. **The plat is only as good as its
recording**, which is why a subdivision lot's description points to a book and page.

### A.4 What a licensee actually does with a description

Read it against a **survey** and against the **deed**. Confirm the description in a listing matches the
description in the seller's deed. If the seller says the property "includes the strip behind the garage"
and the deed does not describe it, you have found a question — and § 35.292(a)(8) tells you what to do
with it.

## B. Government powers over private property

Four, and they are superior to any private right:

- **Police power** — regulation for public health, safety, morals and general welfare. Zoning, building
  codes, subdivision regulation and environmental rules all rest here. **No compensation is paid**,
  because nothing is taken.
- **Eminent domain** — the power to take private property for public use, **with just compensation.**
  The proceeding is **condemnation**.
- **Taxation** — the power to levy against real property to fund government.
- **Escheat** — property reverts to the state when an owner dies with no heirs and no will.

**PETE** is the usual mnemonic. What matters underneath it is the compensation line: **police power
regulates without paying; eminent domain takes and pays.** A regulation so severe that it destroys all
economic use may be argued to be a taking — and that argument is a lawyer's, not a licensee's.

## C. Zoning and land use

### C.1 The instruments

- **Comprehensive plan** — the municipality's long-range policy document.
- **Zoning ordinance** — the enforceable rule: **use districts** (residential, commercial, industrial,
  agricultural, mixed), plus **bulk and dimensional controls** — setbacks, height, lot coverage, minimum
  lot area, frontage.
- **Subdivision and land development ordinance** — governs dividing land and developing it.
- **Building codes** — govern construction, with permits and a **certificate of occupancy**.

**Pennsylvania's Municipalities Planning Code and every local ordinance were not read for this project.
This course states no setback, height limit, lot size, use category, permit fee or approval deadline.**

### C.2 The four relief mechanisms — and how they differ

| Mechanism | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Variance** | Permission to depart from a **dimensional or use** requirement, granted on application, generally on a showing of hardship not created by the owner. |
| **Special exception / conditional use** | A use the ordinance **already contemplates** in that district, allowed once stated conditions are met. |
| **Nonconforming use** | A use that **lawfully predates** the ordinance. Its permitted scope, expansion limits and conditions of continuation are set by the ordinance itself. |
| **Rezoning** | Amendment of the ordinance itself — a legislative act, not an adjudication. |

Students merge the first three constantly. Hold the difference: a **variance** asks for an exception to
the rule; a **special exception** asks for something the rule already allows on conditions; a
**nonconforming use** asks for nothing because it was there first.

**"Grandfathered" is not a legal term and it is not a broker's conclusion.** When an owner uses it, what
they mean is a legal nonconforming use, whose scope is defined by an ordinance the licensee has not read.
Say what is known — *the owner states the use predates the current zoning* — and route the rest.

### C.3 The rule that puts zoning into every Pennsylvania agreement of sale

**§ 35.333(a)(6)** requires an agreement of sale to contain:

> *"**The zoning classification of the property**, except if the property (or each parcel thereof, if
> subdividable) is **zoned solely or primarily to permit single-family dwellings**, together with a
> statement that **the failure of the agreement of sale to contain the zoning classification of the
> property shall render the agreement voidable at the option of the buyer** and, if voided, **deposits
> tendered by the buyer shall be returned to the buyer without a requirement of court action**."*

Three things to take from it.

**First, the exception is narrow.** It covers property zoned **solely or primarily** for **single-family
dwellings** — and *"each parcel thereof, if subdividable."* A duplex, a mixed-use building, a commercial
parcel, farmland, and a subdividable tract all need the classification stated.

**Second, the consequence runs against the seller.** The agreement becomes **voidable at the buyer's
option** — the buyer chooses. A seller cannot use the omission to escape.

**Third, the deposit comes back without litigation.** *"Without a requirement of court action"* means the
broker holding the deposit does not need an order. That is unusual, and it is why this requirement gets
its own paragraph in the companion course's module on agreements of sale.

### C.4 § 35.333(a)(10) — the highway occupancy permit statement

> *"A statement that **access to a public road may require issuance of a highway occupancy permit from
> the Department of Transportation**."*

Required in every agreement of sale. Its practical importance is largest on unimproved land, where a
buyer may discover after closing that the driveway they planned needs a permit they cannot get.
**This course states no permit standard, fee or process**, because PennDOT's regulations were not read.

## D. Environmental considerations

A licensee is neither an inspector nor an environmental professional. § 35.292(b) says so directly:

> *"**A licensee is not required to conduct an independent inspection of the property.**"*

What a licensee **is** required to do is **§ 35.292(a)(8)** — advise the consumer to seek expert advice
beyond the licensee's expertise — and, under **§ 35.292(a)(4)**, to **comply with 68 Pa.C.S. Chapter 73,
the Real Estate Seller Disclosure Law**, which the companion course covers.

### D.1 Conditions to recognise and route

- **Lead-based paint** — the federal disclosure obligation attaches to **target housing**, generally
  pre-1978 dwellings, and reaches **known** lead-based paint and known hazards. Painting over it does not
  remove it. **The federal regulation was not read for this project; no notice period or record retention
  requirement is stated here.**
- **Radon** — a soil gas, measured by testing. An old test result is not a current reading. Pennsylvania
  has geology that makes this a live question in much of the Commonwealth; the answer is a **current
  test**, not a licensee's reassurance.
- **Asbestos**, **mould**, **underground storage tanks**, **wetlands**, **on-lot septic suitability**,
  **well water quality**, and **abandoned mine subsidence** — each investigated by a different specialist
  or agency.
- **Mine subsidence** deserves a specific note in Pennsylvania. Where a property sits above former deep
  mining, subsidence risk and any available protection are questions for the Commonwealth's own programme
  and for an insurer. **This project read no Pennsylvania mining or subsidence statute or programme
  document, and states nothing about coverage, eligibility or cost.**

### D.2 The habit that carries a licensee through all of it

Separate three things and say which is which, every time:

1. **What you know** — you saw it, measured it, or hold the document.
2. **What you were told** — attribute it to whoever told you.
3. **What you are guessing** — say nothing, and route it.

Every disclosure failure in either of these courses traces back to one of the three being presented as
another.
