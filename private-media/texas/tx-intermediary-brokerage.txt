If you have studied real estate anywhere else, you have met the phrase dual agent. Put it down.

Search Chapter 1101 of the Occupations Code and the words dual agent and dual agency are not in it. Texas built a different role, with a different name and different rules.

Here is the problem it solves. One brokerage lists a house and also represents the buyer who wants it. The seller’s floor is protected. The buyer’s ceiling is protected. Full agency to both is not a hard case; it is an impossible one.

Section 1101.561(b) settles it: a broker must agree to act as an intermediary under this subchapter if the broker agrees to represent in a transaction a buyer or tenant and a seller or landlord. Must.

The consumer notice states the mechanics. To act as an intermediary between the parties, the broker must first obtain the written agreement of each party to the transaction. The written agreement must state who will pay the broker and, in conspicuous bold or underlined print, set forth the broker’s obligations as an intermediary.

Written, from each party, stating who pays, with the prohibitions emphasized—conspicuous, and no point size, because the statute names none.

Then what the broker does. Must treat all parties to the transaction impartially and fairly. And may, with the parties’ written consent, appoint a different license holder associated with the broker to each party, owner and buyer, to communicate with, provide opinions and advice to, and carry out the instructions of each party to the transaction.

Two consents doing two different jobs.

So a Texas brokerage on both sides runs in one of two configurations. Without appointments: the broker is intermediary, impartial, carrying information—and nobody in the brokerage advises either party. With appointments: the broker stays impartial, and each party has a licensee who may give opinions and advice during negotiations, to that party only.

The difference is advice. It is not confidentiality. Because confidentiality is identical in both.

The intermediary must not, unless specifically authorized in writing to do so by the party, disclose: that the owner will accept a price less than the written asking price; that the buyer or tenant will pay a price greater than the price submitted in a written offer; and any confidential information or any other information that a party specifically instructs the broker in writing not to disclose, unless required to do so by law.

The seller’s floor and the buyer’s ceiling stay on their own sides of the wall.

Two things to carry out of here. An appointment is not a release. Section 1101.651(d) binds the broker and every license holder the broker appoints, so an appointed agent advises inside a confidentiality cage.

And the notice is a summary: the statute carries qualifiers the form does not print, including that confidentiality never covers information materially relating to the condition of the property. It protects negotiating position. It has never protected a defect.
