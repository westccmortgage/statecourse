> ### Why the Sales Comparison Approach is Level 3
>
> The Commission states the reason in its own note, transcribed:
>
> *"Because the theory and methodology involved in this appraisal approach **mirrors that used when a
> broker performs a BPO/CMA on a residential property**, students **should practice its use**, with a

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish an appraisal, a CMA and a BPO, and state who regulates appraisers.
2. Distinguish value, cost and price, and list the four elements of value.
3. State the forces and factors that influence value.
4. **Apply the basic economic principles of value to a described property.** *(Level 2)*
5. State the eight steps of the valuation process in order.
6. **Estimate probable sales price by the Sales Comparison Approach, adjusting comparables correctly.** *(Level 3)*
7. **Apply the Cost Approach, including land value, cost estimation and depreciation.** *(Level 2)*
8. **Apply the Direct Capitalization and GRM income methods.** *(Level 2)*
9. **Reconcile the three approaches and explain why weighting beats averaging.** *(Level 2)*

## A. Introduction

### A.1 Key definitions

Three terms the Commission separates, and they are not interchangeable:

- **Real estate appraisal**
- **Comparative Market Analysis (CMA)**
- **Broker Price Opinion (BPO)** — with the Commission's note: **"On the National exam section, BPOs are
  performed specifically for lenders."**

That note is an examination instruction, and it is narrow on purpose. Whatever a BPO may be used for in
the wider market, the national section of the licence examination treats it as **a lender product.** A
student who answers from general practice rather than from that definition will lose the item.

The distinction that matters underneath the vocabulary is authorship. An **appraisal** is an opinion of
value produced by a **licensed or certified appraiser** under professional standards. A **CMA** and a
**BPO** are produced by a **broker**. They can rest on similar reasoning — the Commission says so in the
Level 3 note above — but they are not appraisals and must never be presented as appraisals.

### A.2 Purpose

The Commission asks for the **purpose** of appraisal as its own subtopic.

### A.3 Regulation of appraisers

**Federal.** The **Financial Institutions Reform, Recovery, and Enforcement Act (FIRREA)**:

- *"Provided guidelines for regulating financial institutions"*
- *"Included amendments to improve competency of appraisers"*
- *"Created Appraisal Foundation"*

**The Appraisal Foundation** (the Syllabus prints www.appraisalboard.org):

- *"Developed appraisal standards"*
- *"Established minimum qualifications for appraisers"*

**State.** **"State licensing and certification by NC Appraisal Board"** (http://www.ncappraisalboard.org/).

**Standards.** **"Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice (USPAP) appraisal form."**

Two regulators, and they are different bodies with different jobs. The **North Carolina Real Estate
Commission** licenses brokers. The **North Carolina Appraisal Board** licenses and certifies appraisers.
A broker complaint goes to the Commission; an appraiser complaint goes to the Board. **This project has
not read USPAP, FIRREA, or any North Carolina Appraisal Board rule**, and states no appraiser
qualification, standard, or requirement beyond the Syllabus's listing above.

## B. Basic valuation concepts

### B.1 Definitions

**Appraisal. Value. Cost. Price / market price. Market value.**

These five are listed separately because they are five different things, and confusing them is the most
common valuation error a new broker makes.

- **Cost** is what was spent to create it.
- **Price** is what someone actually paid.
- **Market value** is what it should bring under defined market conditions.

A house can cost $400,000 to build, be priced at $360,000, and have a market value of $375,000, all at
the same moment, with no contradiction. The Commission returns to this in D.1 when it warns that a
feature adjustment is made at **market value**, *"which will probably be less than actual cost."*

### B.2 Assessment for tax purposes

Listed as its own subtopic. Assessed value is a fourth figure, set for taxation, and it need not equal
market value.

### B.3 The four elements of value

- **Demand**
- **Utility**
- **Scarcity**
- **Transferability**

All four must be present. A thing everyone wants, that is useful and rare, has no market value if it
cannot be transferred.

### B.4 Forces and factors influencing property value

- **Social trends**
- **Economic forces**
- **Government controls and regulations**
- **Environmental conditions**

**Required Activity, transcribed: "Provide examples of how each affects value."** Delivered in this
module's worked material, and the examples are drawn from concepts already taught in this course —
Module NC-04's zoning and land-use controls for government controls, Module NC-04's environmental
material for environmental conditions — so students see the connection rather than a fresh list.

### B.5 Basic economic principles of value *(Level 2 — this subtopic)*

**Required Activity, transcribed: "Provide an example of each principle."** Nine principles, each with an
example, and four of them carry the Commission's own notes:

| Principle | The Commission's note |
|---|---|
| **Anticipation** | — |
| **Change** | — |
| **Competition** | — |
| **Conformity** | — |
| **Contribution** | *"This principle is based on the improvement's **increasing or diminishing return, not on its cost**."* |
| **Highest and Best Use** | *"There is **only one** highest and best use at any given time **but it can change over time**. This principle is **a major factor for the appraisal process**."* |
| **Plottage** | *"**Combination or merger of adjacent lots into a single large parcel will generally result in a higher value than the combined values of the individual lots. Assemblage is the merger process.**"* |
| **Substitution** | *"This principle is **the basis for the Sales Comparison Approach** to value."* |
| **Supply and Demand** | *"In real estate, it is frequently seen as a **buyer's market or a seller's market**."* |

Four of those notes deserve a sentence each.

**Contribution** is the principle a broker uses every time a seller asks whether to renovate before
listing. The question is never what the work costs. It is what the finished feature adds to what the
property will bring — and the Commission's note says so directly. A $60,000 kitchen that adds $30,000 of
value contributed $30,000.

**Highest and best use** is stated with a precision worth keeping: **one** at a time, and it **changes**.
A single-family house on a corner that has been rezoned commercial may have a highest and best use that
is no longer a house.

**Plottage and assemblage** are a pair, and the Commission defines both in one sentence. **Assemblage is
the process**; **plottage is the resulting increment of value.** Merging is the verb; the extra value is
the noun.

**Substitution** is the load-bearing principle of Part D. A buyer will pay no more for this property than
for an equally desirable substitute. Everything the Sales Comparison Approach does is an attempt to find
that substitute and measure the difference.

## C. The valuation process — eight steps, in order

1. **Define the problem (task)**
2. **Determine appropriate scope of work**
3. **Collect, record, and verify data**
4. **Analyze data to determine highest and best use**
5. **Estimate value of land**
6. **Apply approaches to value**
7. **Reconcile approaches and estimate final value**
8. **Complete appraisal report**

Note where **highest and best use** sits — step 4, before any approach is applied. The Commission's note
in B.5 called it *"a major factor for the appraisal process,"* and this is why: it is determined before
the valuation methods run, and it conditions them.

Note also that **land is valued separately at step 5**, before the approaches. That reappears in the Cost
Approach, where land is estimated separately and **never depreciated.**

## D. Approaches to value *(Level 2 except as noted)*

### D.1 Sales Comparison (Market Data) Approach *(Level 3 — this subtopic)*

#### D.1.a General information

- **Subject property** — the one being valued.
- **Comparable property (Comp)** — one recently sold.
- *"Estimates value by comparing subject property with recently sold similar or comparable properties;
  **based on substitution principle**."*
- *"**Most reliable and most commonly used approach for single-family properties** or any property that
  has an active market with recent comparable sales available."*

#### D.1.b Procedures

**Data collection and analysis**, then **selection of comparables**:

> *"Need **3-4 comps of the same property type** (e.g., residential, single-family detached, townhome,
> condo, etc.)"*
>
> *"As similar as possible to subject —* **Location; Physical characteristics and condition; Terms and
> condition of sale; Date of sale.**"
>
> ***"Cannot use currently listed or under contract property as a comp."***

That last rule is absolute and it is examinable. A **listing** is an asking price — evidence of what a
seller hopes for, not of what a buyer paid. A property **under contract** has a price that has not yet
survived appraisal, financing or due diligence. Only a **closed sale** is a comp.

**Adjustments to sale prices of comparables.** The single most important rule in this section, in the
Commission's own words:

> ***"Make adjustments to sale prices of the comps; never adjust the subject property."***

Say it as a sentence students can repeat: **you adjust the comps, not the subject.** The subject is the
unknown. Adjusting it would be adjusting the answer.

Which direction? The Commission names the two cases — **superior comparable** and **inferior comparable**
— and the Required Activity requires them to be worked:

> **Required Activity, transcribed: "Provide examples of how adjustments are made to the sales prices of
> superior and inferior comps."**

The logic follows from *"never adjust the subject."* You are making each comp into the subject on paper.
If the comp is **superior** — it has something the subject lacks — you **subtract** from the comp's sale
price, because the subject would not have brought that extra. If the comp is **inferior**, you **add**.
Superior down, inferior up.

Then two limits on the adjustment amounts:

> *"**No standard for adjustment amounts** — Value assigned for a feature adjustment is **market value,
> which will probably be less than actual cost.** — **Paired sales analysis.**"*
>
> *"**No adjustment needed for minor differences.**"*

**Paired sales analysis** is the method for deriving an adjustment honestly: find two sales alike in
everything but the one feature, and the difference between them is what the market paid for that feature.
It is the answer to *"no standard for adjustment amounts"* — the market supplies the number, the
appraiser does not invent it. And it is a direct application of **contribution** from B.5, which the
Commission said is measured by return and not by cost.

**Reconciliation and determination of the value estimate:**

> *"Adjusted sales prices of comps should provide a **tight range of value**."*
> *"**Single indication of value should not be obtained by simple averaging** of the adjusted sales
> prices of comps."*
> *"**Weighted averaging.**"*

Averaging treats a comp that needed $2,000 of adjustment and a comp that needed $40,000 of adjustment as
equally good evidence. They are not. The comp that needed least adjustment was most like the subject and
deserves most weight. And if the adjusted figures do **not** form a tight range, the message is that the
comps were poorly chosen — go back to selection, do not average the spread away.

### D.2 Cost Approach

#### D.2.a General information

> *"Involves **separate estimates of value for the building and the land** that are combined for a value
> estimate for the entire improved property; **also based on the principle of substitution.**"*
>
> *"**Best approach for unique, non-income producing properties or special use properties** (e.g., houses
> of worship, schools, public buildings) **or newly constructed buildings that do not have comparable
> sales data available.**"*

The reason is the absence of comps. A church, a school, a courthouse — nothing comparable sold last
quarter. And a brand-new building has no sales history at all, but its cost is known precisely and its
depreciation is near zero, which is exactly the condition under which this approach is strongest.

#### D.2.b Estimate of land (lot) value

- *"Estimate using **the sales comparison method**."*
- ***"Land is never depreciated."***

Both halves matter. The Cost Approach does not escape the Sales Comparison Approach — it uses it for the
land component. And land never depreciates, which is why the two components must be estimated separately
before being added back together.

#### D.2.c Estimating building costs

- **Reproduction cost** — *"Frequently used to incorporate inherent value of historical property."*
- **Replacement cost** — by the **quantity survey method**, the **unit-in-place method**, or the
  **square foot method.**

**Reproduction** rebuilds it as it was, with the same materials and design. **Replacement** builds
something of equal utility using current materials and methods. On a Victorian with plaster mouldings,
reproduction and replacement are very different numbers, and the Commission's note says why reproduction
is chosen there. The three replacement methods run from most detailed to least: **quantity survey**
prices every material and labour item; **unit-in-place** prices installed components; **square foot**
applies a rate to area.

#### D.2.d Estimating depreciation

**Definitions:** **depreciation; effective age; economic life.**

**Effective age** is not chronological age. A 40-year-old house maintained impeccably may have an
effective age of 15. It is the age the condition suggests.

The Commission's warning, transcribed:

> ***"This is not the same concept as depreciation for tax purposes."***

Appraisal depreciation is loss in value from any cause. Tax depreciation is a cost-recovery schedule set
by tax law. The two share a word and nothing else.

**Depreciation methods:**

- **Age/life (straight-line) method** *(Level 2 — the section default)*
- **Market abstraction method** *(Level 1 — this subtopic)*
- **Breakdown method** *(Level 1 — this subtopic)*, which the Commission subdivides:
  - **Physical deterioration** — **curable and incurable**
  - **Functional obsolescence** — **curable and incurable**
  - **Economic (external or locational) obsolescence** — ***incurable only***

That last line is the examinable one. **External obsolescence is incurable, always** — and the reason is
structural, not a matter of expense. Physical deterioration and functional obsolescence are defects *in
the property*, so the owner can at least in principle fix them. External obsolescence comes from
*outside the boundary line* — a new industrial use across the road, a change in the neighbourhood, a
highway realignment. The owner does not own the cause and therefore cannot cure it at any price.

#### D.2.e Steps in formulating the opinion of value

1. **Estimate the value of the building and other improvements:**
   - **Estimate the current replacement or reproduction cost of the improvements**
   - **Estimate the depreciation accrued over the life of the improvement**
   - **Deduct the depreciation from the current cost estimate of the improvements**
2. **Estimate the value of the land as if vacant**
3. **Add the depreciated cost of improvements to the estimated land value** to determine the value
   estimate for the entire property

*As if vacant* — note the phrase. The land is valued as though nothing stood on it, because whatever
stands on it has already been counted in step 1.

### D.3 Income approaches — Direct Capitalization and GRM only

The Commission limits this part explicitly to **two methods.**

#### D.3.a General information

- *"Based on **present worth of rights to future income**."*
- *"Approach used for **income-producing or investment properties**."*
- **Capitalization (CAP) rate**, with the Commission's note:

> *"The CAP rate **measures the risk** of an investment; **the higher the CAP rate, the higher the risk.**
> **The CAP rate and the market value are inversely related; as one goes up, the other comes down.**"*

Both halves of that note are examinable and students routinely keep only the first. Hold the inverse
relationship: at a fixed income, a **higher** cap rate produces a **lower** value. That falls straight out
of the formula below — the cap rate is the divisor.

#### D.3.b Direct capitalization method

- *"Converts the estimate of projected future income into an estimate of current market value."*
- *"Used for **most income-producing property other than 1-4 unit residential properties**."*

**The formula, in the Commission's own sequence:**

> **Determine net operating income (NOI) estimate**
> - *Estimate **potential annual gross income***
> - *Deduct an allowance for **vacancy and rental loss** to reach **effective gross income***
> - *Deduct **annual operating expenses***
> - ***NOI = Gross income – losses – expenses***
>
> **Note:** ***"Mortgage payments of principal and interest are not operating expenses, and are not
> included in calculations."***
>
> **Derive capitalization rate** *from local market experts or by Market Abstraction Method*
>
> ***Value = Net operating income (NOI) ÷ CAP rate***

The debt-service exclusion is the classic examination trap and it has a reason. NOI describes **the
property's** earning power. Two buyers could purchase the same building with completely different
financing — one all cash, one heavily leveraged — and the building's NOI is identical either way. Debt
service belongs to the buyer, not to the property, so it never enters the calculation.

#### D.3.c Gross rent multiplier (GRM) method

- *"Relates the sales price to the property's expected rental income."*
- *"Used for **1-4 unit residential income-producing properties**."*

**The Commission's sequence, transcribed:**

> *"Obtain sales price and gross income (rent) data on recently-sold comparable properties"*
> ***"Derive GRM for each comparable: GRM = sales price ÷ income"***
> *"Reconcile data to determine appropriate GRM"*
> *"Derive estimate of subject property's value: **Value = Rental income ÷ GRM**"*
>
> **Note:** *"GRM can use **either monthly or yearly** rental income data, but **be sure to be consistent
> throughout calculations.**"*

> **Instructor note — a discrepancy in the source, stated openly.**
>
> The two formula lines above are transcribed exactly as they appear in the Syllabus text obtained by
> this project. **They are not algebraically consistent with one another.** If **GRM = sales price ÷
> income**, then solving that same relation for the subject gives **Value = rental income × GRM**, not
> divided by it.
>
> This module **teaches the form that follows from the Commission's own definition of GRM** —
> **Value = rental income × GRM** — because the Commission states that definition first and the second
> line cannot be reconciled with it. A worked check makes the point: comps selling at $240,000 on $2,000
> monthly rent give a GRM of **120**; a subject renting for $2,100 monthly is then worth
> **$2,100 × 120 = $252,000**, which is a sensible figure. Dividing would give $17.50.
>
> **This discrepancy is flagged for the Commission's reviewer as an item to confirm against the current
> official Syllabus PDF before this course is submitted.** It is possible the source text obtained here
> carries an extraction or typographical artefact. **No item in this module's bank turns on the divide
> form**, and the engagement items state the relationship in words — *the multiplier converts rent into
> value, and value is the larger number* — so that a student is never graded on the ambiguity.
>
> **Section XVI settles the practical consequence.** In its Required Activity for the income approach the
> Commission states, in its own words: ***"There will be no calculations using a GRM."*** GRM is therefore
> examinable as a **concept** — what it relates, and which property class it serves — and **no computation
> using it is required anywhere in this course.** See Module NC-16.

The consistency warning in the Commission's note is worth its own line: a GRM derived from **monthly**
rent must be applied to **monthly** rent. Mixing monthly and annual figures produces an answer wrong by a
factor of twelve, and it is a favourite examination distractor.

**Two methods, two property classes.** Direct capitalization for **most income property other than 1–4
unit residential**; GRM for **1–4 unit residential income property.** The Commission draws that line
itself, and it is examinable.

### D.4 Reconciliation of the three approaches

> *"**Weighted consideration is preferable to averaging** of the approaches; **greater weight given to the
> approach that is best for that particular type of property.**"*

The same principle as D.1's weighted averaging, one level up. For a single-family house, sales comparison
carries the weight — the Commission called it *"most reliable and most commonly used"* for exactly that
property type. For a church, the cost approach does. For an apartment building, the income approach.
Averaging three approaches equally would drag the best evidence toward the worst.

> **Required Activity, transcribed:** *"Show students on the appraisers' Uniform Residential Appraisal
> Report (URAR) form **where the appraiser reconciles the approaches used in the appraisal to arrive at a
> single opinion of value**."*
