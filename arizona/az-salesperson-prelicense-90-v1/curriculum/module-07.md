## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish value, cost, price and assessed value.
2. List the four elements of value and apply the economic principles.
3. Apply the sales comparison approach, including the two rules that govern adjustment.
4. Apply the cost approach and classify depreciation.
5. Apply the income approach by direct capitalization and by gross rent multiplier.
6. Distinguish an appraisal from a licensee's opinion of value, and state what a licensee may claim.

---

## A. Four figures that are not the same thing

**Value** is worth. **Cost** is what was spent to create it. **Price** is what someone actually paid.
**Assessed value** is what a taxing authority places on it, and it need not equal market value.

**A licensee who tells a seller "the county says it's worth $410,000" has confused two of the four.**

**The four elements of value: demand, utility, scarcity, transferability.** All four must be present.

**Principles:** **substitution** — the basis of the sales comparison approach; **contribution** — an
improvement adds what it **returns**, not what it **cost**; **highest and best use** — one at a time, and it
changes; **conformity**; **anticipation**; **progression and regression**; **supply and demand**;
**plottage**, the increment from merging adjacent parcels, produced by **assemblage**.

## B. The sales comparison approach

**Closed sales only.** A **current listing** is what a seller hopes for; a **property under contract** has a
price that has not yet survived appraisal, financing or inspection. **Neither is evidence of what a buyer
paid.**

Select for similarity in **location**, **physical characteristics and condition**, **terms and conditions of
sale**, and **date of sale**.

### B.1 The two rules

**Adjust the comparables, never the subject.** The subject is the unknown; adjusting it would be adjusting
the answer.

**Superior comparables adjust DOWN; inferior comparables adjust UP.** You are making each comparable into
the subject on paper.

**Worked example.** *Comparable A sold for $588,000 and has a casita the subject lacks, whose market
contribution derived from paired sales is $34,000. Comparable B sold for $551,000 and has a two-car garage
where the subject has three, the market contribution of the third bay being $9,000.*

Comparable A adjusted: 588,000 − 34,000 = **$554,000** *(superior, so down).*
Comparable B adjusted: 551,000 + 9,000 = **$560,000** *(inferior, so up).*

**A $6,000 spread is a tight range and good evidence.** If the adjusted figures were $70,000 apart, the
comparables were poorly chosen — **go back to selection rather than averaging the spread away.**

**Paired sales analysis** derives an adjustment honestly: two sales alike in everything but one feature, and
**the difference between them is what the market paid for that feature.** It is **contribution** applied to
one feature, and it yields **market value, usually less than cost.**

**Reconcile by weighted consideration, not simple averaging.** A comparable needing $5,000 of adjustment is
better evidence than one needing $60,000.

## C. The cost approach

Best where there are no comparables: **unique or special-use property**, and **new construction.**

1. **Estimate the land separately** by sales comparison. **Land is never depreciated.**
2. **Estimate replacement or reproduction cost** of the improvements. **Reproduction** rebuilds it as it
   was; **replacement** builds equal utility with current materials. Costing methods, most to least
   detailed: **quantity survey**, **unit-in-place**, **square foot.**
3. **Subtract accrued depreciation.**
4. **Add the depreciated improvement value to the land value.**

**Depreciation:** **physical deterioration** (curable or incurable); **functional obsolescence** (curable or
incurable); and **external (economic) obsolescence — incurable, always**, because its cause lies **outside
the property line** and the owner does not own it.

**Effective age is not chronological age.** A thirty-year-old house maintained impeccably may have an
effective age of twelve.

## D. The income approach

**Direct capitalization: Value = NOI ÷ capitalization rate.**

Potential gross income, **less vacancy and collection loss** → effective gross income, **less operating
expenses** → **net operating income.**

**Debt service is NOT an operating expense.** NOI describes **the property's** earning power; two buyers
could buy the same building with entirely different financing and the NOI is identical. **NOI less debt
service is before-tax cash flow — a different figure answering a different question.**

**Worked example, rate given.** *Potential gross income $312,000; vacancy and collection loss 8 percent;
operating expenses $107,500; annual debt service $128,000; market cap rate 6.25 percent.*

Effective gross income = 312,000 × 0.92 = **$287,040.**
NOI = 287,040 − 107,500 = **$179,540.**
Value = 179,540 ÷ 0.0625 = **$2,872,640.**
*(Before-tax cash flow = 179,540 − 128,000 = $51,540 — not the value.)*

**The cap rate measures risk and is inversely related to value:** at the same income a **higher** cap rate
gives a **lower** value, because it is the divisor.

**Gross rent multiplier.** **GRM = sale price ÷ gross rent**, derived from comparables; then **Value =
subject's gross rent × GRM.** **Be consistent about the period** — a GRM from monthly rent is applied to
monthly rent.

**Worked example.** *Comparables sell at $498,000 on $3,320 monthly gross rent: GRM = 498,000 ÷ 3,320 =
**150.** A subject renting for $3,450 is indicated at 3,450 × 150 = **$517,500.***

## E. What a licensee may claim about value

**A licensee is not an appraiser.** An appraisal is an appraiser's opinion produced under professional
standards; a licensee's market analysis is an opinion of probable sales price produced for a pricing or
offering decision.

**Three Arizona rules govern what you say about it.**

**R4-28-1101(H)** — do not undertake services **outside your field of competence** without engaging someone
competent, unless the lack of expertise is **disclosed to the client in writing first** and the client then
employs you.

**R4-28-1101(I)** — **obtain** information material to the client's interests, **communicate it
accurately**, and **help the client confirm** it.

**R4-28-502(C)** — advertising must contain **accurate claims and representations**, **fully state factual
material**, and must **not misrepresent the facts or create misleading or ambiguous impressions.**

**A defensible sentence:** *"This is my opinion of probable sales price, based on these three closed sales
and these adjustments. It is not an appraisal, and your lender will order one that may come in
differently."*

**Not defensible:** *"It'll appraise at six-fifty."* That is a prediction about a third party's future
opinion, and no rule shelters it.

**And the Arizona measurement rule from Module AZ-02 applies to a market analysis as much as to an
advertisement: R4-28-502(H)** — **never use "acre," alone or modified, for less than 43,560 square feet.**
