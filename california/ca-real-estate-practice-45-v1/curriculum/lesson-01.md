## 1. DRE authority and license categories

### 1.1 Why a license exists at all

Most commercial activity in California requires no occupational license. Real estate is different for a specific reason: the transactions are infrequent, the sums are large relative to the participants' net worth, the documents are technical, and the consumer usually cannot evaluate the quality of the service until long after it has been performed. A buyer who is sold a defective refrigerator learns quickly. A buyer who receives careless advice about a disclosure deadline may not learn for years, and by then the loss is unrecoverable.

Licensing responds to that asymmetry. It does not certify that a licensee is skilled, and it does not guarantee outcomes. What it does is create an identifiable, accountable person standing behind the advice, subject to standards of conduct, backed by a regulator with the authority to investigate and to remove that person from practice.

Everything in this course follows from that idea. When a rule seems burdensome, the productive question is not "is this required?" but "what consumer harm is this rule preventing?" A licensee who understands the second question usually gets the first one right without looking it up.

### 1.2 The Real Estate Law and the Commissioner's Regulations

Two bodies of authority govern licensed conduct in California, and they are not the same thing.

**The Real Estate Law** is statute — enacted by the Legislature, found in the Business and Professions Code. It establishes the Department, defines what activity requires a license, sets the grounds on which a license may be denied, suspended, or revoked, and creates the obligations that carry the most serious consequences.

**The Commissioner's Regulations** are administrative regulations adopted by the Real Estate Commissioner under authority the statute grants, found in the California Code of Regulations. They supply operational detail the statute leaves open: how trust funds must be handled, what records must be kept, what advertising must contain.

Three practical consequences follow:

1. **Both bind you.** A licensee who complies with the statute but violates a regulation has still violated the law.
2. **They change on different schedules.** Statutes change through legislation; regulations change through the rulemaking process. A licensee who learned a rule five years ago cannot assume it still reads the same way.
3. **Neither is the same as brokerage policy.** Your office manual may be stricter than the law — and often should be — but it can never be looser, and following it is not a defense if it permits something the law forbids.

Beyond these two sit **case law**, which interprets both, and **local ordinances**, which can impose additional requirements on transactions in a particular jurisdiction. A licensee is not expected to be a lawyer. A licensee is expected to know which category a question falls into and to recognize when the answer requires someone else's expertise.

> A useful habit: when you cannot immediately say whether a rule comes from statute, regulation, office policy, or local custom, treat that uncertainty itself as the signal to check before acting.

### 1.3 What activity requires a license

The licensing requirement turns on a combination of elements rather than on a job title. Broadly, a license is required when a person:

1. acts **for another person** — not for their own account;
2. **for compensation** or the expectation of compensation; and
3. performs one of the **activities the statute lists**, which include selling, buying, offering, soliciting, negotiating, or leasing real property or a business opportunity, and soliciting borrowers or lenders for loans secured by real property.

Each element does work. An investor buying property for their own account is acting for themselves and needs no license. A person who genuinely volunteers with no expectation of compensation is outside the compensation element — though "compensation" is read broadly and includes things of value other than cash. And an activity not on the statutory list is not licensed activity, however close to real estate it feels.

The exemptions are specific rather than general. An attorney performing legal services, a person acting under a properly executed power of attorney, and certain trustees and public officials operate under defined exemptions. What matters for practice is the habit of mind: exemptions are narrow, and "this seems like it should be exempt" is not an analysis.

#### Why this matters to a licensee

You will be asked to pay or receive referral fees, to let an unlicensed assistant handle "just the paperwork," and to help a friend market a property informally. Each of those is an unlicensed-activity question. Paying an unlicensed person for activity that requires a license is a violation by the licensee, regardless of whether the unlicensed person understood the rule.

### 1.4 The two license levels

California issues two individual real estate licenses, and the difference between them is not seniority. It is legal authority.

**A broker** holds an independent license. A broker may operate a brokerage, employ salespersons, hold trust funds, advertise under the brokerage's identity, and contract with clients in the brokerage's own name.

**A salesperson** holds a derivative license. It authorizes licensed activity only for and under the supervision of an employing broker. A salesperson cannot hold client trust funds in their own right, cannot contract with clients in their own name, cannot be compensated directly by anyone other than their employing broker, and cannot operate independently no matter how much experience they accumulate.

That single distinction resolves a large fraction of the questions new licensees ask:

| Question | Resolution |
|---|---|
| May a salesperson be paid directly by a lender for a referral? | No. Compensation for licensed activity flows through the employing broker. |
| May two salespersons at different brokerages split a fee between themselves? | No. The split is broker to broker. |
| May a salesperson open an office and advertise under a personal brand? | Not independently. Advertising must identify the employing brokerage. |
| May a salesperson hold a buyer's deposit in a personal account? | No. Trust funds are the broker's responsibility and belong in the broker's trust account. |

A **corporation** may also hold a real estate license, acting through a **designated officer** who must be a licensed broker and who bears the supervisory responsibility for the corporation's licensed activity. The corporate form changes who signs and who is liable in contract; it does not dilute the supervisory obligation, which attaches to the designated officer personally.

### 1.5 License status, renewal, and discipline

A license has a term and must be renewed, with continuing education required for renewal. A license that is not renewed does not merely become inconvenient — the person may not perform licensed activity while it is inactive or expired, and performing licensed activity without a current license is itself a violation.

The Commissioner's disciplinary authority extends to suspension, revocation, and restriction of a license. Two features of that authority regularly surprise licensees:

- **It reaches conduct outside licensed activity.** Conduct unrelated to a transaction can still bear on fitness to hold a license.
- **It does not require that a consumer suffered a loss.** A trust fund violation with no shortage, an advertisement that misled no one, a record destroyed inside the retention period — each is complete as a violation whether or not anyone was harmed.

The Department also maintains a **public license lookup**, which reports license status, expiration, employing broker, and any public disciplinary record. Consumers use it. So should licensees, before agreeing to work with an unfamiliar counterpart on the other side of a transaction.

### 1.6 Checkpoint

Before continuing, you should be able to state: which two bodies of authority govern licensed conduct and how they differ; the three elements that make an activity require a license; and four specific things a salesperson may not do that a broker may.

## 2. Broker supervision

### 2.1 The responsible broker

Every brokerage has a broker who is responsible for its licensed activity. That responsibility is the structural feature that makes the whole licensing system work: a salesperson's derivative authority only means something if someone is actually supervising it.

The obligation is best understood by what it covers rather than by a definition. Reasonable supervision extends at minimum to:

1. **Transactions and documents** — that agreements, disclosures, and notices are prepared, delivered, and retained correctly.
2. **Advertising** — that what goes out under the brokerage's identity is truthful and properly identified.
3. **Trust funds** — that money belonging to others is handled and accounted for correctly.
4. **Familiarity with the law** — that licensees know the requirements applicable to the work they are doing.
5. **Complaint and dispute handling** — that problems reach the broker rather than being managed quietly by the licensee who caused them.
6. **Records** — that the brokerage's files are complete and retained.

### 2.2 Delegation does not transfer responsibility

A broker with forty licensees cannot personally review every document, and no one expects that. Delegation is normal, necessary, and expected in any brokerage of size. What a broker cannot do is delegate the *responsibility*.

The distinction is concrete. A broker who assigns file review to an experienced office manager has delegated a task. If a disclosure is missed, the broker is still answerable — and the questions asked will be about the supervision *system*: Was there a written procedure? Was the person trained? Was the delegation documented? Did the broker monitor whether the review was actually happening, or simply assume it was?

That last question is where most supervision failures live. A procedure that no one checks is not supervision; it is a document about supervision.

#### A worked example

> A brokerage's policy requires broker approval of all advertising before publication. A salesperson posts a property video to social media without approval. The post is accurate, identifies the brokerage correctly, and generates two showings.
>
> **Is there a problem?** Yes, and it is not about this post. The post's accuracy is luck, not control. If approval is optional in practice, the broker cannot demonstrate supervision of the office's advertising at all — and the next post may be the one that misstates square footage or omits the brokerage identity. A policy enforced only after harm occurs is not a supervision system.
>
> **What should happen now?** The deviation is reported and reviewed, the process gap is addressed, and the review is documented. Quietly deleting the post would leave the gap in place for the next licensee.

### 2.3 Independent contractor status is not an exception

Many California salespersons work as independent contractors for tax and compensation purposes. That arrangement is common and lawful. It does not reduce the broker's supervisory obligation by one degree.

This confuses people because the two frameworks use similar words for different purposes. Tax law asks who controls the manner and means of the work in order to decide how income is reported. Real estate law asks who is responsible for supervising licensed activity in order to protect consumers. A licensee can be an independent contractor for the first purpose and a supervised licensee for the second at the same time, and routinely is.

A broker who responds to a compliance question with "they're an independent contractor" has misidentified the framework.

### 2.4 Branch offices and scale

As a brokerage grows, supervision must be designed rather than assumed. Branch offices, teams operating semi-autonomously, and licensees working remotely each create distance between the responsible broker and the work. The obligation does not shrink with distance; the systems have to grow to cover it.

Practical indicators that a supervision system is real:

- New licensees receive documented onboarding on the office's procedures before they take a file.
- Files are reviewed against a checklist at defined points, and the review is recorded.
- Advertising has a named approver and an archive of what was published.
- There is a stated escalation path, and licensees can name it without looking it up.
- Exceptions are logged and resolved rather than handled informally.

### 2.5 Checkpoint

You should be able to explain why a broker remains answerable for a task properly delegated to a competent person, and why independent contractor status does not change the supervisory analysis.

## 3. Professional competence and scope

### 3.1 Competence is an obligation, not a credential

A real estate license is not divided by property type. The same license permits a licensee to work on a suburban house, a commercial ground lease, a probate sale, and a mobile home in a rented park. The license permits it. Competence is a separate question, and it is a professional obligation the licensee owes the client.

The obligation does not mean refusing unfamiliar work — that would leave every licensee permanently confined to their first transaction type. It means being honest with the client about what you do and do not know, and arranging real support before you take on work at the edge of your experience.

A defensible approach to unfamiliar work has three parts:

1. **Disclose plainly** what you have and have not done before. Clients accept "I have not handled a ground lease assignment; my broker has, and she will be working the file with me" far better than they accept discovering it later.
2. **Arrange actual supervision or co-representation** — not an offer to answer questions if you have any, which puts the burden of knowing what to ask on the person who does not know.
3. **Identify the specialists** the transaction will need and involve them early.

What is not competence: relying on the other side's broker to catch what you miss. The opposing broker owes duties to their own client, not to yours.

### 3.2 The boundary with the practice of law

This boundary produces more discipline than almost any other issue in daily practice, and the line is not where most licensees assume.

**Within a licensee's role:**

- Completing the blanks of a standard form agreement in the ordinary course of a transaction.
- Explaining in general terms what a common provision — a financing contingency, an inspection period — is for and how it customarily operates.
- Describing the ordinary sequence of a transaction and what typically happens at each stage.
- Recommending that the client consult an attorney, and identifying the issue that prompts the recommendation.

**Outside it:**

- Drafting a custom provision to address a particular party's legal risk.
- Advising on the legal effect of an executed contract — whether a party is bound, whether a breach occurred, what remedies exist.
- Advising whether a client may cancel, rescind, or refuse to perform.
- Opining on how a boundary dispute, a title claim, or an estate matter would be resolved.
- Explaining the legal consequences of how a buyer takes title.

The distinction is not about difficulty and not about the licensee's experience. A licensee with twenty years of practice is no more permitted to advise on the legal effect of a contract than one with two weeks. The distinction is about whose professional judgment the client is relying on.

#### Two situations that look similar and are not

> **Situation A.** A buyer asks, "What does this inspection contingency do?" You explain that it gives him a defined period to investigate the property's condition and a path to respond within the contract's terms if what he finds is unacceptable. **This is within your role.**
>
> **Situation B.** A buyer asks, "The inspection period ended yesterday and I didn't send anything. Am I still able to cancel?" This asks about his legal position under an executed contract. **This is outside your role.** The correct response identifies the issue, states plainly that the answer depends on the contract's mechanism and on legal questions you are not able to resolve, refers him to an attorney, and records the referral in the file.

Note what the correct response in Situation B does *not* do: it does not refuse to engage. Saying nothing leaves the client without direction at exactly the moment direction matters.

### 3.3 Other scope boundaries

The law is not the only boundary a licensee must respect.

**Valuation.** A licensee may prepare a comparative market analysis and give an opinion of value to advise a client on pricing. A licensee may not hold that analysis out as an appraisal, and may not perform an appraisal where one is required. The difference is not the data used; it is who prepares it, under what standards, and for what purpose.

**Property condition.** A licensee conducts a reasonably competent and diligent visual inspection of accessible areas and discloses material facts it reveals. A licensee does not perform an inspection, does not evaluate the adequacy of a repair, and does not opine on whether a structural condition is serious. The correct response to reaching that boundary is a referral to the appropriate professional and a record of it.

**Tax and estate consequences.** How a buyer takes title, what a sale will cost an owner in taxes, and whether a transfer triggers reassessment are questions with consequences a licensee cannot evaluate and should not attempt.

### 3.4 The value of "I don't know"

New licensees often feel that admitting ignorance undermines their credibility. In practice the opposite is true, and the professional formulation has four parts:

1. Say plainly that you do not know.
2. Say where the answer comes from.
3. Say when you will have it.
4. Follow up in writing — with the answer, or with a note that it remains unresolved.

A licensee who does this consistently becomes the person clients trust with hard questions. A licensee who improvises becomes the person whose file contains a misrepresentation.

### 3.5 Checkpoint

You should be able to classify a given question as within scope, legal advice, appraisal, inspection, or tax advice — and to state the correct response for each.

## 4. Office policies and delegation

### 4.1 What written policies are for

A brokerage's written policies serve two audiences. For licensees, they answer "how do we do this here" without requiring a conversation each time. For a regulator or a court, they are evidence that the brokerage designed a system rather than improvised.

A policy that exists only on paper serves neither audience. The test of a policy is not whether it is written but whether it controls conduct — whether licensees know it, follow it, and are noticed when they do not.

### 4.2 What a working policy set covers

At minimum:

- **Intake** — what is collected and verified before a listing or representation begins, including authority to sell.
- **Disclosure handling** — who prepares, who reviews, how delivery is evidenced.
- **Advertising** — what must be included, who approves, where the archive lives.
- **Trust funds** — who may receive funds, where they go, how quickly, who reconciles.
- **Recordkeeping and retention** — what belongs in a file, where files live, how long they are kept.
- **Escalation** — what must reach the broker immediately, and how.
- **Complaints** — how a consumer complaint is received, recorded, and answered.
- **Departure** — what happens to files, clients, and pending transactions when a licensee leaves.

That last one is neglected and expensive. Transaction records belong to the brokerage. A departing licensee cannot decide unilaterally what leaves with them, and a brokerage without a written answer will discover this during an argument.

### 4.3 Delegation done properly

Delegation is not simply assignment. A delegation that will survive review has four components:

1. **A named person** — not a role that three people believe belongs to someone else.
2. **A defined scope** — what the person is deciding, and what must come back up.
3. **Training** — evidence the person was equipped for the task.
4. **Monitoring** — a way the broker learns whether it is actually being done.

Missing the fourth is the most common failure, and it is the one that converts an ordinary error into a supervision finding.

### 4.4 Stricter than the law is a legitimate choice

Brokerages frequently adopt policies stricter than the legal minimum: longer retention, mandatory second review on certain transactions, prohibitions on practices that are lawful but risky. This is sound, and consistency is what makes it valuable.

It also creates a trap worth naming. When a licensee discovers that office policy exceeds the legal requirement, the temptation is to follow the law instead. That is the wrong move for two reasons. The policy binds the licensee's conduct within the brokerage regardless of the legal floor, and the licensee's reading of the legal minimum may simply be wrong. Changing a policy is a policy-level decision that starts with confirming the current requirement — not a decision an individual licensee makes case by case.

### 4.5 Checkpoint

You should be able to name the four components of a defensible delegation and explain why "we have a written policy" is not by itself evidence of supervision.

## 5. Consumer-first communication

### 5.1 Three categories a consumer must be able to tell apart

Most communication failures in real estate are not lies. They are collapses of category — a consumer who cannot tell which of three things they have just been told:

1. **A verified fact** — something you know and can support.
2. **An unverified report** — something you were told, attributed to its source.
3. **A professional opinion** — your judgment, identified as judgment.

Consider three answers to "How old is the roof?"

- *"The permit record shows it was replaced in 2019."* — verified fact, with its source.
- *"The seller says it was replaced about six years ago; I have not confirmed that."* — unverified report, attributed and flagged.
- *"From the appearance and the material, it looks like a roof with some life left, but a roofer should evaluate it."* — opinion, identified as opinion, with a referral.

Each is proper. What is improper is the fourth answer — *"It's about six years old"* — which conveys a fact while resting on an unverified report, and which becomes a misrepresentation the moment it turns out to be wrong.

### 5.2 Timeliness and the written record

Consumers make decisions on deadlines. Information that arrives after a decision is worth almost nothing, and a correction that arrives after closing is worth less than that.

Two practices carry most of the weight:

- **Confirm materially important conversations in writing.** Not every exchange — a licensee who emails a summary of every phone call becomes noise. But when something was decided, when a deadline was discussed, when advice was given that the client may act on, the written confirmation is what makes it real.
- **Correct errors immediately, in writing, to everyone who received the original.** The instinct to wait and see whether an error matters is the instinct that turns a mistake into a misrepresentation. An error you know about and leave uncorrected is no longer a mistake.

### 5.3 Plain language and access

A consumer who does not understand an explanation has not been informed, whatever the file says. Practical obligations follow:

- Explain in plain language, then confirm understanding by asking what the client plans to do next rather than asking "does that make sense?"
- Recognize when a consumer's primary language is not English and address it properly — through appropriate translation or interpretation, not by simplifying to the point of inaccuracy.
- Accommodate disability-related needs in how information is delivered.
- Never let time pressure become the substance of a recommendation. "You need to decide today" is sometimes a true statement about a deadline and sometimes a sales tactic; a consumer cannot tell the difference, so the licensee must be scrupulous about which one they are doing.

### 5.4 What consumer-first does not mean

It does not mean telling the consumer whatever they want to hear, and it does not mean substituting your judgment for theirs. A client who wants to list above the supported range is entitled to do so; the licensee's obligation is to give the analysis and the advice in a form the client can act on, then follow the client's informed decision and record that it was informed.

Nor does it mean answering every question asked. Some questions — about the characteristics of a neighborhood's residents, about a prior occupant's illness, about whether a neighbor has a legal claim — must be declined, and declining them well is itself a professional skill: explain why you cannot answer, and point to where a proper answer comes from.

### 5.5 Checkpoint

You should be able to take a consumer question and produce an answer that is correctly categorized as fact, report, or opinion, and that identifies its source.

## 6. Transaction file accountability

### 6.1 The file is the record of your professional conduct

Every part of this lesson converges here. Supervision is demonstrated through files. Competence and scope decisions are evidenced by documented referrals. Communication is proven by what was confirmed in writing. When a transaction is questioned — by a client, a broker, a regulator, or a court — the file is what answers.

The governing standard is reconstructability: could a person who was not there determine what happened, when, and on what basis?

### 6.2 What belongs in it

- Agency disclosures and representation agreements, with dates.
- The purchase agreement, every counteroffer, and every signed amendment.
- All disclosures, **with evidence of delivery and its date** — not merely signatures.
- Notices, contingency removals, and demands, with delivery evidence.
- Material communications: advice given, decisions made, corrections issued.
- Referrals made and the client's response.
- Records of the licensee's own observations, including the visual inspection.
- Advertising published for the property.
- Escrow and closing documents.

### 6.3 Dates and delivery evidence

Two file defects account for a large share of disputes, and both concern timing rather than content.

**A signature without a delivery date.** The signature proves the consumer received the document. It does not prove *when*. Because rights and deadlines commonly run from delivery, a file that cannot establish the delivery date cannot establish that the consumer's response period was honored.

**An undated notice.** A contingency removal, a notice to perform, or a cancellation without a date is evidence of an event with no evidence of when it occurred — which is precisely the fact in dispute.

### 6.4 Version control, and why superseded drafts stay

The instinct when closing a file is to tidy it: discard the drafts, keep the executed versions. Resist it. The sequence of versions is frequently what shows when a term changed and who agreed to the change. A file containing only final documents can prove what was agreed but not how the parties got there.

Preserve the submitted version, the timestamp, the actor, and any later amendment, so that a correction does not erase the history it corrects.

### 6.5 The line that must never be crossed

A file gap discovered later is a problem. Filling it retroactively is a different category of problem entirely.

When an audit finds a missing document, the correct actions are: obtain it if it legitimately exists; record the gap if it does not; escalate to the broker. The incorrect actions — creating a document now and dating it to match the transaction, or removing surrounding records so the inconsistency does not show — are falsification. They convert a compliance failure that a brokerage can survive into conduct that ends careers.

The same principle governs correcting records generally. Corrections are made visibly, with the original preserved and the change attributable. A record that can be silently altered is not a record.

### 6.6 Post-closing audit

The most useful review of a file happens shortly after closing, while the participants still remember what happened. The audit asks:

1. Is every required document present?
2. Is each one dated, and is delivery evidenced where timing matters?
3. Are the documents internally consistent — do names, dates, amounts, and property descriptions reconcile?
4. Does the chronology make sense against the contract's deadlines?
5. Is anything present that should not be — an unexecuted draft mistaken for final, a superseded figure?

Findings are recorded, not quietly repaired. A brokerage that can show it audits its own files and acts on what it finds is in a materially different position from one that cannot.

### 6.7 Checkpoint

You should be able to state the reconstructability standard, explain why a signature without a delivery date is insufficient, and describe the correct response to a gap found in a closed file.
