# Real Estate Practice — Lesson Assignment 05

## Market analysis and pricing strategy

**Course version:** 1.0 development draft  
**Assignment time:** 180 instructional minutes  
**Regulatory status:** Not approved; no California credit is available.

A useful pricing recommendation is not the highest number a seller wants to hear and not the average of several nearby prices. It is a documented interpretation of a defined market, a verified subject property, current competition, recent transactions, and the limits of the available evidence. This assignment develops a repeatable comparative market analysis workflow and shows how to communicate pricing choices without promising a sale price or misrepresenting a brokerage analysis as an independent appraisal.

## Learning objectives

After completing this assignment, the student will be able to:

- define the subject property and its competitive market before collecting comparables;
- distinguish market value, listing price, contract price, cost, and seller proceeds;
- select comparable properties using objective physical, legal, locational, and transactional criteria;
- explain the different evidence supplied by closed, pending, active, expired, and withdrawn listings;
- apply a consistent, supportable adjustment process without inventing unsupported dollar amounts;
- calculate and interpret price per unit, sale-to-list ratio, days on market, absorption, and supply;
- prepare a dated seller net estimate that exposes assumptions and unknown amounts;
- explain the practical boundary between a comparative market analysis and an appraisal assignment;
- identify fair-housing and valuation-bias risks in data, language, comparable selection, and reconciliation; and
- preserve a pricing file that another professional can reconstruct.

## 1. Define what is being analyzed

### 1.1 Five numbers that are not interchangeable

Students must separate five common concepts:

- **Market value** is an opinion developed for a defined purpose and effective date under stated assumptions.
- **Listing price** is the seller’s authorized offering price and marketing position.
- **Contract price** is the price agreed by identified parties in a particular transaction, subject to its terms.
- **Cost** is the amount paid to acquire, build, repair, or replace something.
- **Net proceeds** are the seller’s estimated remaining funds after identified obligations and transaction charges.

These numbers may influence one another, but they do not automatically equal one another. A kitchen costing $140,000 does not necessarily add $140,000 to market value. An ambitious listing at $1,400,000 does not prove buyers will pay that amount. A contract at $1,250,000 may include credits, personal property, unusual financing, or other terms that affect comparison. A high sale price can still produce a disappointing seller net when liens, credits, repairs, or costs are substantial.

### 1.2 Purpose, user, and effective date

Before collecting data, state the analysis purpose. A listing consultation asks what pricing range and marketing strategy current evidence supports for the seller. A buyer comparison asks how an offered property relates to available alternatives and recent transactions. An investor analysis may emphasize income, condition, holding costs, and resale assumptions. A retrospective dispute or tax question may require a qualified appraiser or another professional rather than a brokerage CMA.

The analysis must identify an effective date because markets move. Interest rates, inventory, insurance availability, employment, seasonality, regulatory changes, property condition, and competing listings can change after a report is prepared. A report created sixty days ago should not be presented as current without confirming whether the subject and market evidence remain valid.

### 1.3 Verify the subject property

The subject profile is the anchor of the comparison. Collect and reconcile:

- property type and legal form of ownership;
- location, site, view, orientation, access, and external influences;
- living area and measurement source;
- bedroom and bathroom count;
- age, design, construction, and effective condition;
- permitted and unpermitted additions or conversions;
- parking, storage, accessory units, pools, solar, and other improvements;
- occupancy, tenancy, and possession constraints;
- association obligations and common amenities;
- zoning or use information from appropriate sources;
- known defects, repairs, deferred maintenance, and renovations; and
- personal property, concessions, or special terms expected in the sale.

Public records, MLS data, seller statements, prior listings, permits, inspection reports, title information, and a licensee’s observations may disagree. The analyst does not silently choose the fact that produces the preferred price. Each important fact should have a source, date, and confidence level. An unresolved discrepancy in living area, bedroom legality, access, or condition should appear as a limitation and may require professional investigation.

### 1.4 Scope and limitations

A CMA should state what the licensee inspected, what information was relied upon, what was not verified, and what the analysis is intended to accomplish. A drive-by analysis, automated estimate, desktop review, and interior inspection have different reliability. The licensee should not imply direct knowledge of a comparable’s interior when relying only on listing photographs and remarks.

Limitations are not an excuse for careless work. They are a truthful description of the evidence. If the property is unique or comparable information is thin, the conclusion should become less precise, not more confident.

## 2. Define the competitive market

### 2.1 Market area is not just a radius

The relevant market is the set of properties that a reasonably informed participant would consider as alternatives. Geography matters, but a circular one-mile search can cross school boundaries, freeways, view corridors, municipal lines, flood or fire areas, beach access, transit patterns, development types, or other features that affect buyer behavior.

Market segmentation may consider:

- detached home, condominium, cooperative, mobilehome, or multifamily property;
- owner-occupant, second-home, or investor demand;
- price range and likely financing;
- age and design;
- condition and renovation level;
- site size, view, parking, and accessory uses;
- association services and monthly obligations;
- tenancy or occupancy status; and
- legal use and transfer restrictions.

The licensee should document why a boundary or segment is relevant to market behavior. A statement such as “buyers usually compare these two tracts because their home sizes, school access, and commute are similar” is more defensible than “everyone knows this is the better area.”

### 2.2 Highest and best use as an issue-recognition tool

A residential salesperson need not produce a formal appraisal analysis, but should recognize when the existing use may not be the only relevant use. A large site with development potential, a mixed-use parcel, a nonconforming structure, a damaged building, or land with entitlement issues may require a different market and specialized analysis.

The licensee should ask whether a contemplated use appears physically possible, legally permissible, financially feasible, and productive, while avoiding conclusions outside competence. Zoning labels alone do not prove development rights. Utilities, access, environmental conditions, overlays, easements, subdivision rules, permits, cost, demand, and professional review may control the answer.

### 2.3 Search broad, select narrow

An effective process starts with a search broad enough to see the market and then applies stated selection criteria. Searching too narrowly can hide alternatives. Keeping every result can dilute the analysis with unlike properties.

A search log should preserve:

- data source and access date;
- property type and status;
- geographic boundary;
- sale or listing date range;
- size, age, site, condition, and feature ranges;
- price or financing filters;
- exclusions and their reasons; and
- any later change to the search criteria.

Do not change criteria solely to reach the seller’s desired number. If broader evidence is used because few close comparables exist, explain the tradeoff.

### 2.4 Fair-housing boundary

Market analysis describes property and transaction evidence, not the race, color, religion, sex, familial status, national origin, disability, or other protected characteristics of residents. A pricing report should not rank neighborhoods by who lives there, use coded phrases about “type of people,” or adjust value because of protected-class composition.

School, crime, demographic, cultural, religious, or disability-related questions can become steering or bias problems when the licensee selects information or language for the consumer. The professional may direct consumers to neutral third-party public sources and help them define objective property and location requirements without expressing a preference about protected groups.

Objective language also improves analytical quality. “0.3 mile from a specific transit station” is verifiable. “Best community for young professionals” is subjective and can imply a preferred occupant.

## 3. Select, verify, and compare market evidence

### 3.1 What each listing status contributes

**Closed sales** show transactions in which parties reached and completed an agreement. A recent arm’s-length closed sale that is physically, legally, and locationally similar often provides strong evidence, but the analyst must verify concessions, condition, financing, relationship of parties, and unusual circumstances.

**Pending sales** show that a seller accepted an offer, but the contract price and terms may not be public or verified until closing. A pending property can indicate current demand and competition, but its last asking price is not automatically its contract price.

**Active listings** show present alternatives and seller expectations. They define competition and can place practical pressure on a proposed price. They are not proof that buyers have accepted the asking price.

**Expired, canceled, and withdrawn listings** can show where the market rejected a price, condition, access plan, or marketing strategy, but status alone does not reveal the cause. A listing can end because the owner changed plans, access was restricted, the property had defects, or the broker relationship ended.

### 3.2 Arm’s-length and nonmarket influences

A comparable should be investigated for conditions that may have affected price:

- family or related-party transfer;
- foreclosure, probate, bankruptcy, or court supervision;
- seller distress or unusual urgency;
- tenant or possession problems;
- substantial concessions or repair credits;
- personal property included in price;
- favorable or unfavorable financing;
- assemblage, development, or business value; and
- a buyer or seller with a special motivation not typical of the market.

A sale with an unusual condition is not automatically discarded. It may still provide evidence if the condition is understood and the comparison accounts for it. The error is treating every recorded price as equally representative.

### 3.3 Comparable hierarchy

Select comparables using a hierarchy rather than one matching field. Consider:

1. same market segment and competing buyer pool;
2. similar property rights and legal use;
3. similar location and external influences;
4. recent transaction or listing date;
5. similar property type, design, age, size, site, and utility;
6. similar condition, quality, improvements, and amenities; and
7. reliable, verifiable transaction information.

The closest property is not always the best comparable. A condominium across the street may not compete with a detached home. A fully remodeled sale may require more interpretation than a slightly farther property with the same design and condition.

### 3.4 Adjustment logic

An adjustment represents a market-supported difference between a comparable and the subject. In a comparison grid, the common convention is to adjust the comparable toward the subject. If a comparable has a superior feature that buyers paid more for, a downward adjustment may be appropriate. If it has an inferior feature, an upward adjustment may be appropriate. The direction is easy; the amount requires evidence.

Support may come from paired or grouped sales, market extraction, cost information interpreted for contribution rather than cost alone, income evidence where relevant, prior resales, or other recognized analysis. The licensee should not use an office rule such as “every bedroom is $50,000” in every neighborhood. Feature contribution varies by market, property type, utility, and time.

### 3.5 Paired-data example

Suppose several similar homes suggest that properties with a functional two-car garage sold about $35,000 to $45,000 above otherwise similar one-car-garage properties during the same period. The analyst may use that evidence as one support for an adjustment, while explaining sample size and other differences.

One pair is rarely perfect. If the “garage pair” also differs in view, renovation, lot shape, and sale month, the apparent price difference cannot safely be assigned only to parking. Multiple observations and market knowledge are stronger than one convenient pair.

### 3.6 Condition and renovation

Condition adjustments require more than a seller’s renovation receipts. The market may not return every dollar of cost. Buyers may discount older finishes, unpermitted work, over-improvement, unusual design, or incomplete construction. The analysis should distinguish:

- ordinary maintenance;
- repair of deferred maintenance;
- functional modernization;
- quality upgrade;
- expansion or new utility;
- cure of an adverse condition; and
- personal taste with uncertain market contribution.

Photographs and remarks can be useful but may be selective or digitally altered. Verify when possible, describe uncertainty, and avoid claiming an interior condition never observed.

### 3.7 Price per square foot

Price per square foot is a screening and comparison metric, not a complete valuation method. It can be calculated as:

`sale price ÷ reported living area = price per square foot`

If a property sold for $1,080,000 and the reliable living area is 1,800 square feet, the indicated price is $600 per square foot. Applying that figure blindly to a 2,700-square-foot home ignores size economies, site, view, quality, condition, layout, parking, and the reliability of both area measurements.

When using the metric, keep the numerator and denominator consistent. Do not mix total price that includes substantial nonrealty items with building area, or compare tax-record area with another source without noting the difference.

### 3.8 Reconciliation, not averaging

After adjustments and qualitative analysis, reconcile the evidence. Reconciliation assigns weight based on similarity, data reliability, transaction timing, adjustment magnitude, and current market relevance. It is not a mechanical average.

A highly similar recent sale with reliable terms may deserve more weight than a geographically close sale requiring many unsupported adjustments. A range may be more honest than a single point. The final recommendation should state why the selected evidence is persuasive and what could cause the range to change.

## 4. Measure current market direction

### 4.1 Sale-to-list ratios

The sale-to-original-list ratio is:

`closed price ÷ original list price × 100`

The sale-to-final-list ratio uses the asking price immediately before contract. If a home originally listed at $1,250,000, reduced to $1,190,000, and closed at $1,170,000:

- sale-to-original-list ratio = $1,170,000 ÷ $1,250,000 = 93.6%;
- sale-to-final-list ratio = $1,170,000 ÷ $1,190,000 ≈ 98.3%.

The two ratios tell different stories. The final ratio alone can conceal a significant price correction. The original ratio alone can overstate the gap between the market and the seller’s revised expectation.

### 4.2 Days on market and exposure

Days on market can help evaluate demand, but data definitions vary. Relisting, status changes, private marketing, delayed entry, tenant restrictions, and MLS rules can affect the count. The analyst should identify the data field used and avoid presenting it as a perfect measure of exposure.

Median is often more useful than average when a few extremely long listings distort the data. Segment by price and property type. Combining entry-level condominiums with luxury estates can produce a number meaningful to neither.

### 4.3 Absorption and months of supply

An elementary monthly absorption rate can be calculated as the number of relevant sales per month over a selected period. Months of supply can then be estimated:

`current active inventory ÷ average monthly closed sales = months of supply`

If 24 comparable homes sold over six months, average absorption was four per month. If 10 comparable homes are active, the simple estimate is 2.5 months of supply.

The result depends on segment definition, season, period, and whether pending listings are considered separately. It is an indicator, not a promise. A market with low overall supply may still have oversupply in a narrow price tier or property type.

### 4.4 Change over time

Do not compare raw medians across periods without examining the mix of properties sold. If larger or more expensive homes dominated one month, the median can rise even if individual-property values did not. Stronger analysis may track matched segments, repeat sales, price per unit with controls, concession patterns, inventory, contract activity, and mortgage conditions.

The report should distinguish observation from inference. “Median closed price increased 6% in this sample” is an observation. “Every home appreciated 6%” is an unsupported inference.

### 4.5 Pricing strategy and feedback

The initial price is a decision, not the end of the analysis. A supervised marketing plan can define review points based on:

- online exposure and inquiries;
- showing volume and objective feedback;
- competing new listings, reductions, pending sales, and closings;
- changes in condition, access, financing, insurance, or seller goals; and
- offer terms and verified buyer responses.

Feedback should be organized, not manipulated. A licensee should not fabricate buyer comments to pressure a reduction. If the seller declines a supported adjustment, document the advice and seller decision and continue lawful service within the listing agreement.

## 5. Seller net, appraisal boundary, and valuation integrity

### 5.1 Seller net estimate

A seller net sheet converts a possible price into a preliminary proceeds estimate. Begin with the assumed sale price and itemize estimated deductions and credits. Depending on the transaction, items may include:

- loan and lien payoffs;
- accrued interest and authorized payoff charges;
- brokerage compensation;
- escrow, title, recording, transfer, association, or local charges;
- property-tax prorations and assessments;
- buyer credits and negotiated concessions;
- repairs, warranties, staging, moving, or preparation costs;
- tenant deposits or rent adjustments;
- judgments or other obligations; and
- reserves for unknown or disputed amounts.

Each item should show a source or be labeled as an estimate. The report should be dated and should state that escrow, title, lender, tax, association, and transaction figures can change.

### 5.2 Net calculation example

Assume:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Estimated sale price | $1,200,000 |
| Estimated loan payoff | ($612,000) |
| Negotiated brokerage compensation | ($60,000) |
| Seller-paid buyer credit | ($12,000) |
| Estimated escrow, title, transfer, and recording charges | ($8,500) |
| Estimated repairs and other seller costs | ($14,500) |
| **Estimated net before taxes and unlisted obligations** | **$493,000** |

The mathematics can be correct while the conclusion remains unreliable if the payoff is stale, a second lien is missing, or a tax liability is ignored. The document must show its limitations and be updated as verified information arrives.

### 5.3 CMA and appraisal are not interchangeable labels

A real estate licensee commonly prepares a CMA to counsel a client about listing or offering strategy within brokerage practice. A licensed or certified appraiser may perform an appraisal assignment for lending, litigation, tax, estate, or other purposes. The purpose, independence requirements, standards, scope, credential, and intended user may differ.

California’s Bureau of Real Estate Appraisers explains that when a state-licensed appraiser develops an opinion of value, the work is an appraisal regardless of whether the requested format is called a BPO or another preliminary valuation product; the appraiser must comply with USPAP and California requirements. A salesperson should not market a CMA as a “certified appraisal,” imitate an appraiser credential, or promise that a lender or court will accept it.

When the consumer needs an independent appraisal or an opinion for a specialized legal, tax, lending, estate, condemnation, or dispute purpose, the professional should recommend an appropriately qualified appraiser or other specialist.

### 5.4 Appraiser independence

Pricing a listing high to please a seller is poor brokerage practice. Pressuring an appraiser to “hit the number” is also improper. California Business and Professions Code section 11345.4 prohibits an appraisal management company from improperly influencing the development, reporting, result, or review of an appraisal through coercion, inducement, intimidation, compensation, instruction, and other listed conduct, including seeking a minimum or maximum value.

A licensee may provide relevant factual information and market evidence through an authorized process. The file should distinguish a legitimate correction request—such as pointing out a factual error or omitted comparable—from pressure to reach a desired result. The appraiser controls the appraisal conclusion.

### 5.5 Valuation bias controls

Valuation decisions can be distorted by protected-class information, neighborhood stereotypes, selective data, confirmation bias, or unsupported assumptions. California BREA identifies valuation bias and fair-housing education as part of current appraiser qualification and renewal requirements beginning in 2026. Brokerage pricing work should follow the same consumer-protection discipline even when it is not an appraisal assignment.

Controls include:

- define selection criteria before reviewing prices;
- use property and market characteristics rather than resident identity;
- document included and excluded comparables;
- review whether adjustments are consistently applied;
- avoid demographic descriptions and coded language;
- separate verified data from seller or agent expectations;
- have a supervisor review large or unusual adjustments;
- preserve the first analysis and any later revision; and
- explain contrary evidence rather than deleting it.

A seller’s desired conclusion should not determine which data survive the report.

### 5.6 Pricing-file integrity

The pricing file should preserve:

- subject profile and source reconciliation;
- purpose, effective date, intended use, and limitations;
- search criteria and data access date;
- original comparable reports or snapshots as permitted;
- verification notes and photographs;
- adjustment support and calculations;
- current market indicators;
- analysis drafts and broker review;
- final presentation and seller response;
- net sheets and later updates; and
- any correction, challenge, or appraisal-related communication.

Version integrity matters. A new CMA after a price change should not erase what the brokerage advised at listing.

## 6. Applied pricing case

### 6.1 Subject and market data

The subject is a 1,920-square-foot detached home with three bedrooms, two bathrooms, a two-car garage, an average-condition interior, and a 6,100-square-foot site. The owner installed a kitchen for $115,000 four years ago and wants to add the full cost to the price. Public records show 1,740 square feet; the seller says a 180-square-foot room was added, but no permit or measurement evidence is in the file.

The seller points to a neighbor actively listed at $1,425,000. That property is 2,250 square feet, has a permitted fourth bedroom, a panoramic view, and a renovated interior. It has been active for 74 days after a $75,000 reduction.

The analyst finds:

| Comparable | Status and price | Key facts |
|---|---|---|
| A | Closed 21 days ago at $1,218,000 | 1,880 sf; similar site; average condition; two-car garage |
| B | Closed 47 days ago at $1,265,000 | 1,960 sf; renovated; inferior busy-road influence; two-car garage |
| C | Closed 70 days ago at $1,175,000 | 1,850 sf; dated; superior site; one-car garage |
| D | Pending; last asking $1,239,000 | 1,905 sf; average condition; actual contract terms unknown |
| E | Active at $1,295,000 for 18 days | 1,940 sf; renovated; similar site; two-car garage |

The seller owes approximately $690,000 on a first loan, reports a possible $38,000 home-equity balance, and expects “about $700,000 cash” after sale. No current payoffs are available.

### 6.2 Required student work

Prepare a pricing memorandum that:

1. identifies the living-area discrepancy and explains why it must not be hidden;
2. defines the likely competitive segment and explains why the neighbor’s active listing is weak as the sole basis;
3. ranks Comparables A through E by usefulness and identifies verification questions;
4. explains the direction and support needed for condition, road, site, and garage adjustments;
5. calculates the price per square foot for Comparable A and states the metric’s limitation;
6. recommends a pricing range without guaranteeing a sale;
7. creates a preliminary seller net structure showing both reported debts as unresolved until payoffs arrive;
8. distinguishes the CMA from a lender appraisal; and
9. lists every record that belongs in the pricing file.

### 6.3 Model analysis path

A strong response begins with the subject, not the seller’s target. It preserves both living-area sources and obtains permit, measurement, and broker guidance before marketing the addition as lawful living area. Comparable A is highly useful because of recency and similarity, subject to verification. Comparable B offers size evidence but needs careful analysis of renovation and the busy-road influence. Comparable C may help bracket condition and parking but requires more adjustment. Pending D shows current demand but its unknown contract price limits precise use. Active E shows current competition, not an accepted market result.

The neighbor’s listing is not proof of value: it is larger, has a fourth bedroom, view, and renovation, and has already required a reduction during 74 days of exposure. The $115,000 kitchen cost is evidence of expenditure, not automatic market contribution.

Comparable A’s price per square foot is approximately $647.87 ($1,218,000 ÷ 1,880), but that figure does not independently account for site, condition, room legality, layout, or market changes. The student should reconcile several observations rather than multiply one metric by disputed subject area.

The net estimate must list the first loan, possible home-equity balance, compensation, concessions, escrow/title/transfer estimates, repair or preparation costs, prorations, and unknown obligations. It should not promise $700,000 cash. The seller makes the pricing decision after receiving the supported range, risks, and limitations.

### 6.4 Completion checklist

Before this assignment is complete, confirm that you can answer:

- What is the purpose and effective date of the analysis?
- Which subject facts are verified, reported, observed, or unresolved?
- What properties compete with the subject and why?
- What does each listing status prove—and not prove?
- Are comparable selections and exclusions documented?
- Is every dollar adjustment supported by market evidence?
- Have you reconciled the evidence rather than averaged it mechanically?
- Are market indicators calculated with consistent definitions?
- Does the seller net sheet expose assumptions and unknowns?
- Is the CMA clearly distinguished from an appraisal assignment?
- Are bias controls and version history visible?

## Course boundary

This assignment teaches comparative analysis and pricing workflow within real estate practice. It does not authorize the student to perform an appraisal requiring another credential, provide legal or tax advice, guarantee a sale price or net, or pressure an appraiser. Current official sources, responsible-broker supervision, reliable market data, and qualified specialists control the live transaction.
