## 1. Market selection

### 1.1 The question a market analysis answers

A comparative market analysis answers one question: **what would a willing, informed buyer most likely pay for this property, in this market, within a reasonable marketing period?**

Everything in the method follows from that sentence. "Most likely" means the analysis produces a range and a most-probable point, not a single certainty. "Willing and informed" excludes forced and uninformed transactions from the evidence. "This market" requires defining what the relevant market actually is. "Within a reasonable marketing period" ties the estimate to time — a price that would eventually be achieved after two years is not the answer to the question a seller is asking.

New licensees often skip straight to pulling comparables. The definition of the market is the step that determines whether those comparables mean anything.

### 1.2 Defining the market area

The relevant market is the area within which a buyer for this property would consider alternatives. That is a behavioral definition, not a geographic one, and it is why mechanical radius searches mislead.

Boundaries that genuinely separate markets:

- **School attendance areas**, where they materially affect buyer demand.
- **Physical barriers** — a freeway, a river, a ridgeline, a rail corridor — that separate neighborhoods a buyer treats as different places.
- **Jurisdictional lines** where they change taxation, services, or regulation.
- **Development patterns** — a tract of similar-vintage homes is a different market from the custom homes across the arterial, even at the same distance.
- **Common interest developments**, where governing documents and assessments create a distinct submarket.

Boundaries that usually do not: a fixed one-mile radius, a ZIP code, or a listing service's district, each of which is an administrative convenience rather than a description of buyer behavior.

#### A worked judgment

> The subject is a 1962 single-story ranch in a tract of about 200 similar homes. Half a mile away, across a six-lane arterial, is a newer development of two-story homes on smaller lots.
>
> **A radius search captures both.** A market definition does not: a buyer choosing among 1962 ranches on this side of the arterial is not weighing the two-story tract as an equivalent alternative, and vice versa. Sales from across the arterial can still be *used* — with a location adjustment and an explanation — but they are secondary evidence, and treating them as directly comparable overstates confidence.

### 1.3 Defining the competitive set

Within the market area, comparables should resemble the subject in the characteristics that drive buyer decisions:

| Characteristic | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Living area | The dominant driver in most residential markets |
| Lot size and usability | A large but unusable slope is not a large lot |
| Bedroom and bathroom count | Affects the pool of buyers who will consider it |
| Age and effective age | Effective age reflects condition and updating, not the year built |
| Style and stories | Single-story homes command different demand in many markets |
| Condition and updating | Frequently the largest single adjustment |
| Garage and parking | Especially where street parking is constrained |
| View, orientation, and exposure | Can dominate all other factors in some markets |
| Site influences | Corner lot, arterial frontage, adjacency to commercial or rail |

### 1.4 Recency and market movement

How recent is recent enough depends on how fast the market is moving. In a stable market, sales from the past six months may all be usable with little time adjustment. In a market that has moved appreciably in a quarter, a six-month-old sale requires a market conditions adjustment or it will mislead.

The test is not the age of the sale but whether the market has changed since it closed. That is why a licensee must have an independent view of market direction — from months of supply, days on market, and the ratio of sale price to list price — before pulling a single comparable.

### 1.5 Data sources and their limits

- **Listing service data** is the primary source and is generally reliable on price and dates, less reliable on square footage, lot size, and condition descriptions written to sell.
- **Public records** are useful for ownership, sale history, and characteristics, but assessor square footage and the licensee's measurement frequently disagree, and the assessor's parcel record is not a boundary determination.
- **The licensee's own observation** is the only source for current condition, which is often the largest adjustment.

Where sources conflict, say so in the analysis rather than silently choosing one. A note that "the listing service reports 1,840 square feet and the assessor reports 1,790; the analysis uses 1,840 with the discrepancy noted" is stronger than an unexplained number.

### 1.6 Property types where the standard method breaks down

The market-area and competitive-set method in Sections 1.2 and 1.3 assumes something that is true of most suburban resale housing and false of several common property types: **that enough genuinely similar properties sold recently nearby.**

Where that assumption fails, the method does not stop working — it starts producing a number with false precision, which is worse than producing none.

| Property type | Why the assumption fails | What the analysis does instead |
|---|---|---|
| **Attached housing in a common-interest development** | Two units with identical floor plans can differ materially by association dues, reserves, pending assessments, litigation, and rental restrictions. **The physical comparison is the easy part and the least important.** | Obtain the association's documents and compare on total monthly cost and restriction, not on price per square foot |
| **Acreage and rural property** | Sales are few, far apart, and driven by water, access, zoning, and usable versus total acreage | Widen the area deliberately and adjust for the drivers rather than the improvements; state the widening in the analysis |
| **A genuinely unique property** | No competitive set exists in any meaningful sense | Present a range with its reasoning rather than a point, and say plainly that the data does not support precision |
| **New construction in an active subdivision** | The builder's prices are list prices set by a single seller, and the incentives are frequently not in the recorded price | Treat builder pricing as active-listing evidence under Section 3.3, and pursue what the incentives actually were |
| **Deed-restricted or income-restricted housing** | Price is constrained by a governing instrument rather than by the market | **Read the restriction.** The analysis is bounded by it, and this is a legal document rather than a market fact |
| **Mixed residential and income use** | Value derives partly from income, which the residential method does not capture | Recognize the limit and say so; an income analysis is a different discipline |

**The professional obligation in every row is the same:** state what the data supports and what it does not.

> **The failure this prevents:** a single-page analysis with three comparables, a confident number, and no acknowledgment that the subject is a five-acre parcel with a shared well and the nearest similar sale is eleven months old and four miles away. **The number is not wrong because the licensee was careless. It is wrong because the method was applied where its assumptions did not hold**, and nothing on the page tells the seller that.

### 1.7 Checkpoint

You should be able to define a market area for a given subject and justify one comparable you included and one you excluded, in terms of buyer behavior rather than distance.

## 2. Comparable adjustment reasoning

### 2.1 The direction of adjustment

This is the most-missed mechanic in the entire method, and it is worth stating in a form that removes ambiguity.

> **Adjustments are made to the comparable, never to the subject.**
>
> The question each adjustment answers is: *what would this comparable have sold for if it had been like the subject?*

Therefore:

- If the comparable is **superior** to the subject in some respect, its price is adjusted **downward**.
- If the comparable is **inferior** to the subject, its price is adjusted **upward**.

A memory aid used widely: **CBS** — Comparable Better, Subtract. And its complement: comparable worse, add.

### 2.2 A worked adjustment grid

Subject: 1,800 square feet, 3 bedrooms, 2 baths, 6,000 square foot lot, 2-car garage, updated kitchen, no view.

| | Comp A | Comp B | Comp C |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Sale price | $845,000 | $902,000 | $810,000 |
| Closed | 2 months ago | 5 months ago | 1 month ago |
| Living area | 1,800 | 1,980 | 1,700 |
| Bedrooms / baths | 3 / 2 | 4 / 2 | 3 / 2 |
| Lot | 6,100 | 6,000 | 5,400 |
| Garage | 2-car | 2-car | 1-car |
| Kitchen | Updated | Updated | Original |
| **Adjustments** | | | |
| Market conditions | — | +$9,000 | — |
| Living area | — | −$27,000 | +$15,000 |
| Bedroom count | — | −$10,000 | — |
| Lot size | −$1,000 | — | +$4,000 |
| Garage | — | — | +$12,000 |
| Kitchen | — | — | +$20,000 |
| **Net adjustment** | −$1,000 | −$28,000 | +$51,000 |
| **Adjusted value** | **$844,000** | **$874,000** | **$861,000** |

Read the grid carefully and notice what each line is doing:

- **Comp B closed five months ago in a rising market**, so it is adjusted **up** — it would have sold for more today.
- **Comp B is larger**, so it is adjusted **down** toward the subject's size.
- **Comp C has an original kitchen and a one-car garage**, both inferior, so both are adjusted **up**.

### 2.3 Reconciling the indications

Three adjusted values — $844,000, $874,000, $861,000 — are not averaged. Averaging treats a comparable requiring $51,000 of adjustment as equally reliable as one requiring $1,000, which it is not.

Reconciliation weights the indications by reliability:

1. **Net adjustment size.** Comp A required almost none; it is the strongest indicator. Comp C required $51,000 gross; it is the weakest.
2. **Recency.** Comp C is the most recent, which partly offsets its adjustment burden.
3. **Similarity in the characteristics that drive this market.**

A defensible reconciliation here: *the supported range is approximately $845,000 to $870,000, with the most probable value near $850,000 to $855,000, weighted toward Comp A as the least-adjusted and most similar indicator.*

Note that the conclusion is a **range with a most-probable point and a stated reason for the weighting** — not a single number presented as fact.

### 2.4 Where adjustment amounts come from

Adjustment values are not invented. They are derived, in order of preference:

1. **Paired sales analysis** — comparing sales that differ in one characteristic to isolate its contribution.
2. **Market-derived rates** — an established price per square foot of difference within this submarket, applied to modest differences only.
3. **Cost with depreciation** — for a specific improvement, recognizing that contribution rarely equals cost.
4. **Reasoned judgment**, disclosed as such.

The last one is legitimate when the first three are unavailable, provided it is identified as judgment rather than presented as measurement.

### 2.5 Contribution is not cost

The most common seller misconception, and one the licensee must be able to explain without giving offense:

> **Money spent on a property does not become value in the property. The market decides what an improvement contributes, and for specialized improvements the contribution is usually well below the cost.**

A $150,000 recording studio built into a garage in a neighborhood of family homes may contribute a fraction of that, because the pool of buyers who want it — and who would pay for it — is small. This is **over-improvement relative to the neighborhood**, and it is a real and recognized phenomenon rather than a rhetorical device for arguing a seller down.

The complement is also true: some modest improvements contribute more than they cost, because they remove a specific buyer objection.

### 2.6 Common adjustment errors

| Error | Why it is wrong |
|---|---|
| Adjusting the subject instead of the comparable | Reverses the entire logic of the method |
| Averaging adjusted values | Treats unreliable indicators as equal to reliable ones |
| Discarding every comparable with a difference | Leaves nothing to analyze; differences are what adjustments exist for |
| Adjusting for price per square foot across large differences | Contribution per square foot is not linear across a large gap |
| Making a lot of small adjustments to reach a target | The grid becomes an argument for a predetermined number |
| No adjustment for a distressed sale, or automatic exclusion | Whether it reflects this market is an analytical question, not a preference |

### 2.7 Bracketing, and the grid that proves too much

Two disciplines separate a defensible grid from a persuasive one.

**Bracketing.** For each significant characteristic, the comparable set should contain properties both **above and below** the subject. A subject with four bedrooms compared only against three-bedroom sales produces an adjusted range built entirely on upward adjustments, and **an adjustment applied in only one direction cannot be checked.** Where the set brackets, an error in the adjustment amount tends to show itself: the grid's indications spread apart rather than converging.

**Net and gross adjustment.** Two figures, and they answer different questions.

- **Net adjustment** is the sum of the adjustments with their signs, and it tells you how far the comparable sits from the subject overall.
- **Gross adjustment** is the sum of their absolute values, and it tells you **how much work the grid is doing.**

A comparable with a small net and a large gross has been adjusted heavily in both directions — six thousand up for size, six thousand down for condition, and so on — and the small net conceals it. **A comparable requiring large gross adjustment is a weak comparable regardless of how close its net lands to the subject.**

> **The grid that proves too much.** Three comparables, each adjusted by amounts approaching a fifth of its sale price, all landing within a few thousand dollars of each other and of the number the seller mentioned on the phone. **Convergence produced by heavy adjustment is not evidence. It is the analyst's judgment restated three times**, and a reviewer will read it that way.

**What to do when the grid does not converge:**

1. **Re-examine the competitive set** before touching the adjustments. A wide spread usually means one comparable does not belong.
2. **Check for a characteristic not in the grid** — a corner lot on an arterial, a view, a functional obsolescence, a school attendance boundary the price data reflects and the property card does not.
3. **Report the range honestly** where the spread is real. Section 2.3 reconciles; it does not average. **A wide supported range is a more useful answer than a narrow unsupported one**, and Section 6.1 makes it defensible only if the reasoning is on the page.

## 3. Active, pending, and sold evidence

### 3.1 What each category proves

| Category | What it is | What it proves | What it does not prove |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Closed sales** | Completed transactions | What buyers actually paid | Current conditions, if the market has moved |
| **Pending sales** | Price agreed, not yet closed | Where the market is moving now | The final price, since terms can change |
| **Active listings** | Currently offered | What sellers are asking; the competition | Value — asking prices are hopes |
| **Expired and withdrawn** | Failed to sell | What the market rejected | Much on its own, but a useful ceiling indicator |

### 3.2 Why closed sales dominate

A closed sale is evidence of a completed exchange between a buyer and a seller — the only direct evidence of price the market produces. Everything else is intention.

That does not make closed sales sufficient. In a market that has moved, closed data is a picture of the recent past, which is why market conditions adjustments and pending evidence matter.

### 3.3 Why active listings mislead when misused

Active listings are the subject's competition and belong in the analysis for that reason. What they cannot do is establish value, because a seller can ask any number at all.

This is the core defect in the pricing dispute licensees encounter most often:

> **Seller:** "The other brokerage said $1.3 million. Three homes near here are listed at that."
>
> **The analysis:** Those three homes have been on the market 94, 112, and 130 days without an offer. That is not evidence that the market pays $1.3 million — it is evidence that the market has declined to pay $1.3 million three separate times.
>
> **The framing that works:** "Those listings tell us what our competition is asking. What they also tell us is that at that price, buyers have walked past three homes like yours for over three months. Here is what buyers actually paid, in the last four months, for homes like yours."

That version does not attack the competitor and does not argue with the seller. It reads the same data more carefully and lets the seller draw the conclusion.

### 3.4 Days on market and absorption

Two market indicators every licensee should be able to compute and explain:

**Months of supply** — the inventory currently available divided by the average monthly rate of sales. It answers: at the current pace, how long would it take to sell everything now listed? Lower supply indicates a market favoring sellers; higher supply, one favoring buyers.

**Days on market** — how long properties take to go under contract. Rising days on market is often the earliest visible sign that a market is turning, well before closed prices reflect it.

> **Worked example.** A submarket has 48 active listings. Over the past three months, 36 properties went under contract — an average of 12 per month.
>
> Months of supply = 48 ÷ 12 = **4 months.**
>
> If the same submarket had 96 actives and 8 sales per month, months of supply would be 12 — a materially different market calling for a materially different pricing strategy, even if recent closed prices looked similar.

### 3.5 The stale-listing effect

A property that sits generates its own headwind. Buyers and their agents read long market time as a signal that something is wrong, and offers that eventually arrive are frequently below where the property would have sold had it been priced correctly at launch.

This is the substantive argument against overpricing, and it is more persuasive to sellers than "the comps don't support it," because it describes a mechanism rather than asserting a conclusion:

> "If we launch above the market, the first three weeks — when buyer attention is highest and every agent with a matching buyer looks at it — go to a price the market won't meet. After that we are competing for attention with the next new listing, carrying the market time as a visible number. Sellers who reduce later frequently end up below where they would have started."

### 3.6 Concessions, and why the recorded price is not the price

A closed sale's recorded price is the number the analysis starts from. **It is frequently not the number the parties agreed to in economic terms**, and the difference is concessions.

**What moves value away from the recorded price:**

| Concession | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Seller credit toward buyer's closing costs** | The buyer paid a higher nominal price in exchange for cash back. The recorded price overstates the market price. |
| **Rate buydown paid by the seller** | Same mechanism, larger and less visible. A meaningful buydown can support a materially higher nominal price. |
| **Personal property included** | Furnishings, equipment, or a vehicle bundled into the price. |
| **Repairs credited rather than performed** | The price reflects a property that was never brought to the condition the price assumes. |
| **Seller-carried financing on favorable terms** | The price and the financing are one bargain. **A price achieved with seller financing at below-market terms is not a cash-equivalent price.** |
| **Leaseback or delayed possession at no cost** | Value transferred outside the price. |

**The analytical consequence.** A grid built on recorded prices in a market where credits are common systematically overstates value, and the overstatement is invisible on the page. **The licensee who does not pursue concession data is comparing nominal numbers in a market that trades in net ones.**

**Where the data comes from:** the listing service's concession fields where they are populated, the closing information the licensee has for his own transactions, and — most reliably and least often used — **asking the agent who closed the comparable.** That call takes two minutes and is the single highest-value verification step in market analysis.

**The same discipline applies to the subject.** A seller told "the house down the street sold for 812" is being given a number that may have carried a substantial credit. Section 4.4 establishes that price interacts with terms; **this section establishes that the interaction is already baked into the comparables**, and a net sheet built on a gross comparable is wrong before the arithmetic starts.

> **State it in the analysis.** Where concession data was available, say what was found. Where it was not, say that too. **"Concession information not available for comparables 2 and 3" is a professional statement**, and it tells the seller how much confidence the range deserves.

## 4. Seller net and pricing consequences

### 4.1 The seller's real question

Sellers ask about price. What they need in order to decide is **proceeds**. An estimated seller net sheet translates one into the other.

### 4.2 What a net sheet contains

| Line | Notes |
|---|---|
| Estimated sale price | The variable being tested |
| Less: existing loan payoff(s) | Balance **plus accrued interest** to the payoff date, plus any other amounts under the note — not the balance alone |
| Less: brokerage compensation | Per the listing agreement |
| Less: escrow and title charges | Allocation varies by local custom and by agreement |
| Less: transfer taxes and recording | Jurisdiction dependent |
| Less: county and association prorations | Taxes, assessments, dues to the ownership boundary |
| Less: agreed repairs or credits | Where known |
| Less: buyer closing cost credit | Where agreed |
| Less: other liens, judgments, or assessments | Discovered at intake, not at closing |
| **= Estimated net proceeds** | Labeled an estimate |

### 4.3 A worked comparison

A seller is weighing a $920,000 offer with a $15,000 closing cost credit against an $895,000 offer with no credit.

| | Offer A | Offer B |
|---:|---:|---:|
| Price | $920,000 | $895,000 |
| Buyer credit | −$15,000 | $0 |
| Compensation (assume 5%) | −$46,000 | −$44,750 |
| Escrow, title, transfer, recording (assume) | −$9,200 | −$9,000 |
| **Subtotal before payoff** | **$849,800** | **$841,250** |

Offer A nets about $8,550 more. That is the arithmetic — and it is not the whole answer. The licensee's job is to put the rest in front of the seller too:

- **Financing strength.** A credit-heavy offer sometimes signals a buyer stretched on cash.
- **Appraisal exposure.** The higher price carries more risk of an appraisal shortfall, and the credit does not reduce the price the appraisal must support.
- **Contingencies and timelines**, which affect the probability the transaction closes at all.
- **Occupancy and possession terms**, which may matter more to this seller than $8,550.

A net sheet that produces a number without that context invites the seller to decide on the number alone.

### 4.4 How price interacts with terms

Sellers frequently under-weight terms. Three worth explaining explicitly:

1. **A credit is not a price reduction for appraisal purposes.** The property must still appraise at the contract price for the lender to lend against it.
2. **A shorter contingency period has real value** because it shortens the window in which the transaction can fail, during which the property is off the market.
3. **A larger down payment reduces the risk of a financing failure**, which is worth something even at an identical price.

### 4.5 Updating the net sheet

A net sheet prepared at listing is an estimate built on assumptions. It should be updated when the price changes materially, when an offer with different terms is under consideration, and when the payoff figure is actually obtained — which is frequently higher than the seller expects, because it includes accrued interest and other amounts under the note rather than the principal balance alone.

### 4.6 The seller who may not have enough equity

A net sheet sometimes produces a negative number, or a number too small to cover the seller's next step. This changes the conversation from pricing to something else, and the licensee's obligation is to recognize the change rather than to keep discussing price.

**What the net sheet has revealed:** that the transaction as contemplated does not work, which is a fact the seller needs before he makes commitments — a deposit on a replacement property, a moving date, a school enrollment.

**The paths that exist**, none of which the licensee decides:

1. **Bring funds to closing.** Available to some sellers and not others, and its consequences are the seller's own financial question.
2. **Reduce the cost of sale.** Real but usually small, and it does not close a large gap.
3. **Do not sell now.** A legitimate outcome and often the right one. **A licensee who cannot say this to a seller has an incentive problem, not a knowledge problem.**
4. **A sale requiring lienholder approval.** A transaction of a different kind, with its own timeline, its own documentation, its own disclosure requirements, and consequences to the seller — credit, tax, and potentially deficiency — that are **legal and tax questions, not licensee questions.**
5. **Alternatives outside a sale**, which belong to the seller's lender, counsel, and tax advisor.

**Three obligations attach the moment the number comes back short.**

**Route the questions.** The tax consequences of debt forgiveness, whether a deficiency can be pursued, and what any of it does to the seller's credit are **not questions a licensee answers.** They are referred, and the referral is recorded.

**Do not promise an outcome that depends on a third party.** Whether a lienholder will approve anything is a lienholder's decision. "They'll approve it, they always do" is the assurance category from Lesson 02, and it is made to sellers under exactly this pressure.

**Watch for the schemes that target this seller.** A distressed seller is the target of arrangements involving up-front fees, title transfers with promised leasebacks, and rescue offers. **A licensee who encounters one raises it with the broker immediately**, and does not participate on the theory that the seller wanted to.

> **The net sheet's value here is that it produced the bad news early**, while the seller still has options. Section 4.5's requirement to update it is what keeps the news current — a gap that was manageable at one price is not at another, and the seller should learn that from the licensee rather than at closing.

## 5. Appraisal versus comparative market analysis

### 5.1 The boundary

| | Comparative market analysis | Appraisal |
|---|---|---|
| Prepared by | A real estate licensee | A licensed or certified appraiser |
| Purpose | To advise a client on pricing or offer strategy | An independent opinion of value for a defined use, commonly lending |
| Standards | Professional practice; not appraisal standards | Professional appraisal standards |
| Independence | Advises a client the licensee represents | Independent of the parties |
| May be called an appraisal | **No** | Yes |

The difference is not the data used. A licensee's analysis and an appraiser's report may rely on the same closed sales. The difference is **who prepares it, under what standards, with what independence, and for what purpose.**

### 5.2 What a licensee may and may not do

**May:** prepare a comparative market analysis; give an opinion of value to advise a client; explain how the opinion was formed; discuss what an appraisal is likely to consider.

**May not:** hold the analysis out as an appraisal; use it where an appraisal is required; represent that it satisfies a lender's valuation requirement; perform an appraisal without the required license or certification.

### 5.3 When the appraisal disagrees

A lender's appraisal below the contract price is, first, a **financing event** rather than a verdict on the licensee's analysis. It constrains what the lender will lend against, because lenders lend against the lower of appraised value and purchase price.

The options that follow are practical rather than automatic:

1. The buyer brings additional funds to cover the gap.
2. The parties renegotiate the price.
3. The parties negotiate some other resolution.
4. The transaction is cancelled, if a contingency permits and its procedure is followed.

What does **not** happen automatically: the price does not adjust to the appraised value by operation of any loan program, the loan is not automatically denied, and no one is entitled to a second appraisal as of right.

### 5.4 Appraiser independence and the licensee's conduct

A licensee may provide genuinely relevant information to an appraiser through the proper channel — a list of sales the licensee believes relevant, information about the property's condition or improvements, a copy of the contract.

A licensee may **not** tell an appraiser what value the transaction needs. The distinction is between supplying information and supplying a target, and a value that moves after the appraiser is told what it must be is unreliable for exactly the purpose the appraisal exists to serve.

Requests to "take another look" that arrive with a number attached are the pattern to recognize and refuse — including when the request comes from your own client.

### 5.5 Opinions prepared for purposes other than a sale

A licensee is asked for a value opinion in circumstances that have nothing to do with listing the property: an estate settling, a marital dissolution, a property tax matter, a relocation program, a lender's request, a partner buying out another partner.

**Each of these carries a use to which the opinion will be put**, and the use changes what the opinion must contain, who may prepare it, and what it may be called.

**Three questions come first, before the analysis.**

**1. Who will rely on this, and for what?** An opinion prepared for a seller deciding a list price and an opinion prepared for a court, a taxing authority, or a lender making a credit decision are not the same document, and the last three carry requirements that may place the assignment outside what a real estate license authorizes. **The answer determines whether the licensee may accept the engagement at all**, and Section 5.1's boundary is where that determination starts.

**2. What is the effective date?** A dissolution or an estate frequently requires value **as of a past date** — a date of death, a date of separation, a date of acquisition. A retrospective opinion uses the data available as of that date and excludes what came after, which is a different exercise from the analysis in Section 1.4 and easy to perform incorrectly by using current data out of habit.

**3. Is the licensee independent of the outcome?** Where a licensee expects to list the property if the value comes back a particular way, the interest is real and it must be disclosed. **An opinion prepared by a person who benefits from one answer is a conflict**, whatever the analysis says.

**What the licensee does not do:**

- Call an opinion an appraisal, format it to resemble one, or describe himself in terms that suggest a credential he does not hold. Section 5.1 governs.
- Accept a fee arranged so that it depends on the value reached.
- Accept an assignment where the intended use requires something the license does not authorize. **The correct response is a referral**, and the person asking is usually relieved rather than annoyed.
- Answer the legal question underneath — what a dissolution requires, what an estate must file, what a tax appeal needs — which belongs to counsel.

> **The pattern to recognize:** the request arrives casually, from a client or a friend of a client, and the consequences attach to a proceeding the licensee never sees. **"Just tell me what you think it's worth" is a different request when it is going to a court**, and the licensee's first obligation is to ask where it is going.

**Where the assignment is proper**, the file requirements in Section 6.2 apply with more force rather than less, because the opinion will be read by someone adverse to the person who asked for it.

## 6. Pricing presentation and file support

### 6.1 What makes an opinion defensible

The test is reconstructability as of the date given: **could someone reviewing this later see what data was used, what adjustments were made, what reasoning connected them, and when?**

Note what is not on that list. That the property eventually sold near the estimate is luck, not method. That the seller signed an acknowledgment of receipt proves delivery, not soundness. That the brokerage's standard template was used proves formatting.

### 6.2 What the file should contain

1. The comparables used, with their data and sources, **as of the date of the analysis**.
2. The adjustment grid, with the direction and basis of each adjustment.
3. The reconciliation, including which indicators were weighted and why.
4. Comparables **considered and rejected**, with the reason — this frequently matters more than the ones used.
5. The market conditions evidence: months of supply, days on market, the direction of prices.
6. The recommendation, as a range with a most-probable point.
7. The date, and the licensee who prepared it.
8. The written advice given, and the seller's decision.

### 6.3 Presenting to a seller

Structure that works:

1. **The market first, before any number.** What is selling, how fast, and in which direction. This establishes the frame before the seller has a number to react to.
2. **The comparables, with the adjustments visible.** Sellers respect shown work and distrust conclusions.
3. **The range**, not a single figure, with the reasoning for the weighting.
4. **The consequences of pricing above it**, described as a mechanism rather than a warning.
5. **The net sheet**, so the conversation moves to proceeds.
6. **The decision is theirs**, said plainly.

### 6.4 When the seller chooses a higher price

This is not a failure of the presentation, and it is not the licensee's decision to override. What the file needs is:

- The analysis and the range.
- The written advice about the likely consequences.
- The seller's decision.
- If a reduction strategy was discussed, what was agreed and when it would be revisited.

What must not happen: the seller's reasoning being adopted as the brokerage's opinion of value, or the analysis being quietly revised upward to match the list price. A revised analysis that follows the price rather than informing it is worse than no analysis.

### 6.5 Revisiting the price

A pricing recommendation has a shelf life. Where the market moves, or where the property has been exposed without generating offers, the analysis should be updated — with a new date, new data, and the reasoning that connects the market's response to the recommendation.

The most useful evidence in a price-reduction conversation is the property's own market feedback: showings without offers, feedback themes, and how the competition has moved. That is data the seller can evaluate, and it is more persuasive than repeating the original opinion.

### 6.6 The opinion, read two years later

Section 6.1 makes an opinion defensible and Section 6.2 lists what the file holds. This section supplies the reason, which is the only thing that makes either habit survive a busy week.

> **The situation.** Two years after a closing, the licensee receives a demand letter. The seller alleges the property was listed too low, that it sold in four days for materially less than it was worth, and that the licensee's analysis was careless.
>
> **The claim is not that the licensee was dishonest.** It is Lesson 03's reasonable-care claim: the work fell below the standard of a competent licensee. That is the claim licensees actually face, and it is decided by what the file shows about the work rather than by what the outcome was.
>
> **What the licensee needs, and either has or does not:**
>
> **The comparables as they existed then**, printed or exported at the time. A listing service re-queried today returns different data — records get corrected, concessions get added, and properties that were active then are sold now. **The current query cannot reproduce the analysis and will sometimes contradict it.**
>
> **The selection reasoning.** Why these comparables, and why the excluded ones were excluded. Section 1.3 makes this an analytical requirement; here it is the difference between a defensible judgment and an unexplained one.
>
> **The adjustments and their derivation.** Section 2.4 requires that the amounts come from somewhere. Two years later, "somewhere" must be on the page.
>
> **The market conditions statement.** Absorption and direction as of the date, which is what answers the allegation that the price ignored a rising market.
>
> **The presentation and the seller's decision.** Section 6.3 and Section 6.4 — what the seller was shown, what range was recommended, and what he chose. **If the seller listed below the recommendation, that document ends the claim.** If he listed at it, the claim is about the recommendation and the reasoning is the answer.
>
> **The four-day sale is not itself evidence of anything.** A property priced correctly in a market with low inventory sells quickly. **What makes it look like evidence is an empty file**, because in the absence of contemporaneous reasoning the only facts available are the price and the speed.
>
> **What must not happen** is the reconstruction: re-running the query today, building the grid now, and producing it as the analysis. Lesson 03's closing line and Lesson 02's close-out routine both govern. **A file with a thin analysis is a defensible weakness; a file with a reconstructed analysis presented as contemporaneous is not.**

**The practical instruction is one sentence.** When the pricing analysis is delivered, **save it as it was delivered, with its data as of that date**, and put it in the file the same day. It takes a minute and it is the difference between a claim that is answered and a claim that is argued.

### 6.7 Checkpoint

You should be able to take an adjustment grid, state which comparable is the strongest indicator and why, produce a range with a most-probable point, and list what the file must retain for the opinion to be reconstructable.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **Comparative market analysis** | The licensee's opinion of the price a willing, informed buyer would most likely pay within a reasonable marketing period. Produces a range, not a certainty. |
| **Market area** | The area within which a buyer for this property would consider alternatives. A behavioral definition, which is why a radius search is not one. |
| **Competitive set** | The comparables that resemble the subject in the characteristics driving buyer decisions in this market. |
| **Effective age** | Age as reflected by condition and updating, rather than the year the improvement was built. |
| **Market conditions adjustment** | The adjustment applied because the market has moved since the comparable closed. Driven by change, not by the calendar. |
| **Direction of adjustment** | Adjustments are made **to the comparable, never to the subject**. Comparable better, subtract; comparable worse, add. |
| **Paired sales analysis** | Deriving an adjustment amount by comparing sales that differ in a single characteristic. The preferred source. |
| **Contribution** | What the market pays for an improvement. Distinct from what the improvement cost. |
| **Over-improvement** | An improvement whose contribution falls well below its cost because the pool of buyers who want it is small. |
| **Reconciliation** | Weighting the indicated values by reliability to reach a range and a most-probable point. **Not** averaging. |
| **Gross versus net adjustment** | Gross is the total adjustment applied; net is the sum after offsets. A small net can conceal a large gross, and reliability tracks gross. |
| **Closed sale** | A completed transaction. The only direct evidence of price the market produces. |
| **Pending sale** | A price agreed but not closed. Evidence of where the market is moving, not of a final price. |
| **Active listing** | A property currently offered. Evidence of the competition and of asking prices — never evidence of value. |
| **Months of supply** | Active inventory divided by the average monthly rate of sales. Answers how long it would take to sell everything now listed. |
| **Days on market** | How long properties take to go under contract. Often the earliest visible sign a market is turning. |
| **Stale-listing effect** | The headwind an overpriced property generates for itself once buyer attention has passed and market time is visible. |
| **Seller net sheet** | An itemized estimate translating price into proceeds. Answers the question the seller is actually asking. |
| **Payoff demand** | The lender's statement of what satisfies the loan, including accrued interest and other amounts under the note — routinely above the principal balance. |
| **Appraisal** | An independent opinion of value prepared by a licensed or certified appraiser under appraisal standards, for a defined use. A licensee's analysis is not one and may not be called one. |
| **Appraiser independence** | The principle permitting a licensee to supply relevant **information** to an appraiser and prohibiting the supply of a **target**. |
| **Reconstructability** | The test of a defensible opinion: could a reviewer later see what data, what adjustments, what reasoning, and what date. |

## Practice problems

### Problem 1

A licensee prepares an analysis using a one-mile radius search. Four of the six comparables sit
across a six-lane arterial in a newer tract of two-story homes on smaller lots. The subject is a 1962
single-story ranch in a tract of about 200 similar homes.

**What is the defect, and are the four sales unusable?**

> **Solution.** The defect is that the market was never defined. A radius is an administrative
> convenience; the relevant market is the area within which a buyer for *this* property would consider
> alternatives, and a buyer choosing among 1962 single-story ranches is not weighing the two-story
> tract as an equivalent.
>
> The four sales are not unusable. They are **secondary evidence**, usable with a location adjustment
> and a stated explanation. What is wrong is treating them as directly comparable, because that
> overstates the confidence the conclusion deserves.
>
> **What transfers:** the market definition determines whether the comparables mean anything. It is
> the step new licensees skip and the step that decides the analysis.

### Problem 2

A comparable sold for $902,000 five months ago in a rising market. It is 180 square feet larger than
the subject and has a fourth bedroom the subject lacks.

**State the direction of each adjustment and why.**

> **Solution.** Three adjustments, in two directions.
>
> **Market conditions: upward.** The market has risen since it closed, so it would sell for more today.
>
> **Living area: downward.** The comparable is larger — superior — so its price is adjusted down toward
> the subject.
>
> **Bedroom count: downward.** Same logic; the extra bedroom is superior to the subject.
>
> Each answers the same question: *what would this comparable have sold for if it had been like the
> subject?* Comparable better, subtract. And every adjustment lands on the comparable — never on the
> subject.

### Problem 3

Three comparables produce adjusted values of $844,000 (net adjustment $1,000), $874,000 (net
$28,000), and $861,000 (net $51,000, and the most recent sale).

**A licensee averages them to $859,667. What is wrong, and what is the defensible conclusion?**

> **Solution.** Averaging treats the comparable requiring $51,000 of adjustment as exactly as reliable
> as the one requiring $1,000. It is not, and the average silently discards that information.
>
> Reconciliation weights by reliability: adjustment size first, then recency, then similarity in what
> drives this market. The first comparable is the strongest indicator; the third carries the heaviest
> adjustment burden, partly offset by being the most recent.
>
> A defensible conclusion: **a supported range of roughly $845,000 to $870,000, most probable near
> $850,000 to $855,000, weighted toward the least-adjusted and most similar indicator** — a range,
> a point, and a stated reason for the weighting.
>
> **What transfers:** a single number with no reasoning attached cannot be defended later, however
> close it turns out to be.

### Problem 4

A seller spent $150,000 building a recording studio into the garage of a home in a neighborhood of
family homes. He expects the analysis to reflect it.

**How should the licensee explain the result?**

> **Solution.** By explaining contribution rather than arguing about the number.
>
> Money spent on a property does not become value in the property; the market decides what an
> improvement contributes. Here the pool of buyers who want a recording studio — and would pay for
> one — is small, and the studio also consumed the garage, which most buyers in this neighborhood do
> want. That is over-improvement relative to the neighborhood: a recognized phenomenon, not a
> rhetorical device for arguing a seller down.
>
> Worth saying honestly in the same conversation: the relationship runs both ways, and some modest
> improvements contribute more than they cost because they remove a specific buyer objection.

### Problem 5

A seller points to three nearby homes listed at $1.3 million and asks why the recommendation is
lower. The three have been on the market 94, 112, and 130 days without an offer.

**Frame the response.**

> **Solution.** Read the same data more carefully rather than arguing with it.
>
> Active listings prove what sellers are asking — a seller can ask any number at all. What these
> three additionally prove is that **the market has declined to pay $1.3 million three separate
> times, over three months each.**
>
> The framing that works: "Those listings tell us what our competition is asking. They also tell us
> that at that price, buyers have walked past three homes like yours for over three months. Here is
> what buyers actually paid, in the last four months, for homes like yours."
>
> **What transfers:** it does not attack the competitor and does not argue with the seller. It puts
> the evidence in front of her and lets her draw the conclusion.

### Problem 6

Submarket X has 48 active listings and 36 properties went under contract over the past three months.
Submarket Y has 96 actives and 8 going under contract per month. Recent closed prices look similar.

**Compute months of supply for each and state the pricing consequence.**

> **Solution.** Submarket X: 36 ÷ 3 = 12 sales per month; 48 ÷ 12 = **4 months of supply.**
> Submarket Y: 96 ÷ 8 = **12 months of supply.**
>
> Same-looking closed prices, materially different markets. Four months of supply is a market where
> a correctly priced property meets buyers quickly; twelve is one where inventory sits, days on market
> climb, and closed prices are a lagging picture of a market that has already turned.
>
> **What transfers:** the licensee needs an independent view of market direction — supply, days on
> market, sale-to-list ratio — **before** pulling a single comparable, because that view is what
> decides whether a six-month-old sale needs a market conditions adjustment.

### Problem 7

A seller is weighing a $920,000 offer with a $15,000 buyer closing cost credit against an $895,000
offer with no credit. Compensation is 5%; closing costs are about $9,200 and $9,000 respectively.

**Which nets more, and what else belongs in the advice?**

> **Solution.** Offer A: $920,000 − $15,000 − $46,000 − $9,200 = **$849,800.**
> Offer B: $895,000 − $44,750 − $9,000 = **$841,250.** Offer A nets about **$8,550 more** before payoff.
>
> That is the arithmetic and not the whole answer. Also in front of the seller: financing strength,
> since a credit-heavy offer sometimes signals a buyer stretched on cash; **appraisal exposure**, since
> the higher price carries more shortfall risk and the credit does not reduce the price the appraisal
> must support; contingencies and timelines, which affect the probability of closing at all; and
> occupancy terms, which may matter more to this seller than $8,550.
>
> **What transfers:** a net sheet that produces a number without that context invites the seller to
> decide on the number alone.

### Problem 8

A lender's appraisal comes in $30,000 below the contract price. The listing agent emails the
appraiser a list of three sales she believes were overlooked and asks him to "take another look —
we need $920,000 to make this work."

**What is the appraisal event, and what is wrong with the email?**

> **Solution.** The appraisal is a **financing event**, not a verdict on anyone's analysis: lenders
> lend against the lower of appraised value and price. The paths are the buyer covering the gap,
> renegotiation, another negotiated resolution, or cancellation if a contingency permits and its
> procedure is followed. None happens automatically — the price does not adjust by operation of any
> loan program, the loan is not automatically denied, and no one is entitled to a second appraisal as
> of right.
>
> The email mixes something permitted with something prohibited. Sending relevant sales through the
> proper channel is **information** and is fine. "We need $920,000" is a **target**, and a value that
> moves after the appraiser is told what it must be is worthless for the purpose the appraisal exists
> to serve.
>
> **What transfers:** the request to reconsider with a number attached is the pattern to refuse —
> including when it comes from your own client.

## Chapter summary

**Define the market before pulling comparables.** The relevant market is where a buyer for this
property would consider alternatives — a behavioral boundary, which is why radius searches, ZIP
codes, and listing service districts mislead.

**Adjustments are made to the comparable, never to the subject.** Comparable better, subtract;
comparable worse, add. Every adjustment answers one question: what would this comparable have sold
for if it had been like the subject?

**Adjustment amounts are derived, not invented** — paired sales first, then market-derived rates,
then cost with depreciation, then reasoned judgment identified as judgment.

**Contribution is not cost.** The market decides what an improvement adds, and for specialized
improvements the contribution is usually well below what was spent.

**Reconcile, do not average.** Weight by adjustment size, recency, and similarity, and state the
weighting. The conclusion is a range with a most-probable point and a reason.

**Closed sales prove price; pending sales show direction; active listings are the competition.**
Active listings never establish value, and a long-unsold listing is evidence the market has refused
that price rather than evidence it will pay it.

**Months of supply and days on market give the licensee an independent view of direction**, which is
what decides whether a comparable needs a market conditions adjustment.

**A net sheet answers the seller's real question, which is proceeds.** Label it an estimate, update
it, and warn that the payoff includes accrued interest rather than the principal balance alone.

**A comparative market analysis is not an appraisal** — different preparer, standards, independence,
and purpose — and it may never be held out as one. Supply an appraiser information; never a target.

**Defensibility is reconstructability.** The file shows the data, the adjustments, the reasoning, the
comparables rejected and why, the range, the date, and the seller's decision. An analysis quietly
revised upward to match a list price is worse than no analysis.
