- Rewrite advertising copy that expresses a preference about people into copy that describes the property.
- Distinguish a reasonable accommodation from a reasonable modification and apply each to a request.
- Design open house and showing practices that are uniform, documented, and safe.
- Operate an advertising approval and archive process that can produce what ran, when, and with whose approval.

## 1. Truthful property advertising

### 1.1 The standard, stated precisely

Advertising must be truthful, and truthfulness is measured **against the property as it is at the time the advertisement runs** — not as it was when the photograph was taken, not as the seller described it, and not as the licensee assumed.

That framing resolves most questions in this section, because the common defects are not lies. They are statements that were true once, or true of something else, or true if an assumption held.

### 1.2 Every factual claim needs a source

A useful discipline: for each factual assertion in a piece of marketing, be able to name where it came from.

| Claim | Acceptable source | Common failure |
|---|---|---|
| Square footage | Measurement, appraisal, or public record — **with the source stated** | Repeating the seller's figure as fact |
| Lot size | Recorded map, survey, or assessor record, source stated | Estimating from a site visit |
| Bedroom count | Rooms that meet the local definition of a bedroom | Counting a converted space with no egress or closet |
| Year built | Public record | The seller's recollection |
| Permitted status of improvements | Permit records | "The seller says it was all permitted" |
| School assignment | The district, with a note that assignments change | The prior listing's copy |
| HOA dues and assessments | The association's current documents | Last year's figure |
| Zoning or permitted use | The jurisdiction | "There's a similar business down the street" |

Where the source is the seller and it has not been independently verified, the honest treatment is to attribute it — "per seller" — or to omit it. Copying a figure from a prior listing is not verification; it launders an unverified number into an apparently authoritative one.

#### The square footage trap

Square footage is the most litigated number in residential advertising because buyers price on it and because sources routinely disagree. The assessor's record, the listing service, an appraisal, and a measurement can produce four different figures for one house.

Sound practice: state the figure, state its source, and where sources conflict, say so and invite the buyer to verify. A licensee who writes "1,840 sq ft (per assessor; buyer to verify)" has given the buyer more useful information than one who writes "1,840 sq ft" and is also in a materially better position if it turns out to be wrong.

### 1.3 Photographs and virtual representation

Photographs are advertising and are held to the same truthfulness standard.

**Not acceptable:**

- Photographs showing a condition the property no longer has — after fire, storm, or removal of staging that included fixtures being sold with the home.
- Photographs of a different unit, however similar the floor plans. Sharing a floor plan does not equalize condition, finish, light, or outlook.
- Digital alteration that changes what the property is — removing a power line, a neighboring structure, or a visible defect.
- Wide-angle distortion so extreme that rooms are unrecognizable at viewing.

**Acceptable with disclosure:**

- Virtual staging, **labeled as virtual staging**.
- Renderings of proposed improvements, labeled as proposals and not as existing condition.
- Twilight or enhanced-sky images, where the property itself is unaltered.

The test is whether a consumer viewing the property would feel the images described a different place.

### 1.4 Opinion, puffery, and the line into misrepresentation

Not every statement in marketing is a factual claim. "Stunning," "charming," and "a rare opportunity" are opinion, and consumers understand them as such.

The line is crossed when an opinion is stated as a verifiable fact or when a prediction is stated as a certainty:

| Statement | Category |
|---|---|
| "Beautifully updated kitchen" | Opinion — acceptable |
| "The best value in the neighborhood" | Opinion — acceptable, though unsupported superlatives invite scrutiny |
| "Priced below market" | A factual claim about value; must be supportable |
| "Will appreciate significantly" | A prediction stated as certainty; not acceptable |
| "Approved for a second unit" | A factual claim about entitlement; requires the approval |
| "Room for a pool" | A factual claim about feasibility; requires setbacks, soils, and access to actually permit one |

### 1.5 Marketing a property that is not yet available

Two recurring problems:

- **Advertising a property before the listing agreement is signed.** The brokerage has no authority to market it. This includes "coming soon" posts made to gauge interest.
- **Continuing to advertise a property that is in contract or sold**, without accurately reflecting status. Misrepresenting a property's availability draws buyers to something that is not available and can mislead the market about inventory.

Listing status in a listing service must reflect reality. A property withdrawn while the listing continues, one in contract, and one sold each have a correct status, and using a status inaccurately — to keep exposure, to protect a negotiating position, to avoid resetting market time — is a misrepresentation to every participant relying on that data.

### 1.6 Advertising claims about the licensee

The truthfulness standard covers claims about the licensee as much as claims about the property:

- Production claims ("#1 agent in the area") must be supportable, and the basis should be stated — by what measure, over what period, in what area.
- Designations and certifications must be current and must not imply licensure or authority the licensee does not hold.
- Testimonials must be genuine and must not be presented in a way that implies a typical result where it is not.
- Claims about a course a licensee has taken must not imply approval, endorsement, or a credential that was not conferred.

### 1.8 Advertising a property that has a problem

The rules in this section are easy to apply to an ordinary property. They get difficult when the property carries something the seller would rather the advertising did not raise.

**Four categories, and they are not treated alike.**

**A physical condition.** Advertising does not have to catalogue every defect — that is what the disclosures do — but **it may not create a false impression of condition.** A property with a failed foundation may be advertised without describing the foundation; it may not be advertised with photographs and copy conveying a sound, move-in-ready home. Section 1.3's principle governs: the images must show the property as it is.

**A permitting or legal-use problem.** Unpermitted square footage, a converted space with no record, a use the zoning does not allow. **This is the category where advertising most often becomes a misrepresentation**, because the licensee repeats a bedroom count or a square footage figure that the records do not support. Section 1.2 requires a source, and the source here is the public record rather than the seller's description or the prior listing.

**A title or use constraint.** An easement, a shared facility, a restriction, a pending assessment, an association's rental limitation. These belong in the disclosures and frequently belong in the copy as well, because **a buyer attracted by an advertisement that omits a constraint fundamental to the use he is buying for has been misled by the omission.**

**A stigma or a history.** This is the category requiring the most care and the least improvisation. What must, may, or must not be said about a property's history — including deaths, alleged criminal events, and neighborhood matters — is **governed by specific California rules with specific boundaries**, and some inquiries touch protected characteristics where an answer is prohibited regardless of the questioner's intent. **This is a question for the responsible broker before anything is published or answered**, and the licensee's obligation is to know that the question exists rather than to answer it from instinct.

> **The seller's instruction is not the analysis.** "Don't mention the addition isn't permitted" is a request to omit; "describe it as a four-bedroom" is a request to state something the records contradict. **The first may or may not be permissible depending on what the advertisement conveys as a whole. The second is a misrepresentation** and Lesson 03's obedience limit governs.

**The practical procedure:** before publishing, list every factual claim the advertisement makes, name the source for each, and identify any claim whose source is the seller's recollection. **Those are the ones to verify or remove**, and they are where every advertising complaint in this category originates.

### 1.9 Checkpoint

You should be able to take a listing description, identify each factual claim in it, name the source each would require, and mark the claims that are opinion.

## 2. Identification and digital compliance

### 2.1 What identification is for

A consumer looking at an advertisement needs to know who is responsible and whether they are licensed. California requires a licensee's identification, including license identification, on solicitation materials, and requires advertising to identify the brokerage under which the licensee operates.

The purpose explains the shape of the rule: a salesperson may not advertise so as to appear to be an independent principal, because that would hide the supervising brokerage that stands behind the advertisement.

### 2.2 The common defects

| Defect | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| Personal brand name and mobile number only | The consumer cannot tell there is a brokerage, or which one |
| License identification omitted from solicitation material | The consumer cannot verify licensure |
| Brokerage name present but in unreadable type | Present is not the standard; legible and conspicuous is |
| Team name styled as an independent company | Obscures which licensed entity is accountable |
| A social profile that names no brokerage anywhere | Every point of contact is a point of identification |
| A property post shared by an unlicensed assistant from a personal account | The post is still the brokerage's advertising |

### 2.3 Team names

Teams are ordinary and useful, and the compliance question is narrow: does the team's presentation mislead a consumer about which licensed brokerage is responsible?

Working guidance:

1. The brokerage identity appears wherever the team identity appears, legibly.
2. The team name does not use terms suggesting it is itself a brokerage or a separate company.
3. The team name is not displayed so much more prominently than the brokerage that a reader would take the team for the firm.
4. Where the team uses a name other than the licensees' own, confirm what registration or approval that name requires.

The internal organizational chart is not the test. The impression created for a consumer is.

### 2.4 Social media

Social platforms create three specific hazards.

**The line between personal and professional collapses.** A licensee's personal account that posts listings is advertising. Treating it as personal expression does not change what it is.

**Content is fragmentary.** A post, a story, a short video — each is an advertisement in itself and each needs identification. A licensee who identifies the brokerage in the profile and omits it from the post has identified the profile, not the advertisement.

**Content is durable and shareable.** A post remains discoverable after the property sells, after the price changes, and after the condition changes. Reshared content carries the original claims into a new context where they may no longer be true.

Practical controls:

- Identification in the post itself, not only in the profile.
- A defined practice for updating or removing posts when status or facts change.
- The same approval process as any other advertising.
- Awareness that comments and replies are also communications with consumers, subject to the same truthfulness standards.

### 2.5 Audience targeting is part of the advertisement

This is the most consequential digital compliance point in the lesson, and it is easy to miss because it does not appear in the copy.

Platforms permit advertisers to select who sees an advertisement — by age, by location, by inferred interests, by proxies for demographic characteristics. **Restricting who may see a housing advertisement can deny groups access to the housing opportunity, and that is the harm advertising rules exist to prevent.**

Neutral copy delivered selectively can exclude just as effectively as discriminatory wording. Two consequences:

1. Targeting choices for housing advertising must be examined with the same care as the words.
2. Using a platform's tool does not shift responsibility away from the advertiser. "The platform offered that option" is not an answer.

### 2.6 Email, text, and lead follow-up

Marketing that arrives in a consumer's inbox or phone carries consent obligations addressed in Lesson 02, and it carries identification obligations here. Automated follow-up sequences are advertising: they must identify the licensee and brokerage, must be truthful, and must honor opt-outs promptly.

An automated sequence that continues after a consumer has opted out is a violation that runs unattended, which is why sequences need periodic review rather than one-time setup.

### 2.7 Checkpoint

You should be able to look at a social post promoting a listing and say what is missing, and explain why audience targeting is part of the advertisement rather than separate from it.

## 3. Fair housing advertising

### 3.1 The principle

Advertising may not indicate a preference, limitation, or discrimination based on protected characteristics — and it may not express an intention to make any such preference.

The workable form of that rule, and the one to carry into every piece of copy written:

> **Describe the property. Do not describe the people who should live in it.**

Copy that sorts people, however warmly intended, signals who is and is not welcome. That is the harm, and intent is not a defense.

### 3.2 California protects more than the federal baseline

The federal protected classes are race, color, religion, sex, national origin, familial status, and disability. **California protects those and adds further characteristics.** A licensee who knows only the federal list will write copy that is lawful federally and unlawful in California.

The practical consequence for advertising: do not treat "it's not one of the seven" as clearance. The safe practice is to describe the property, which never requires reference to any protected characteristic at all.

### 3.3 Rewriting copy that sorts people

| Problem copy | Why it fails | Property-focused rewrite |
|---|---|---|
| "Family-friendly neighborhood" | Speaks to familial status | "Cul-de-sac location; park two blocks away" |
| "Perfect for a young couple" | Age and familial status | "Two bedrooms, one bath, 940 square feet" |
| "Walking distance to St. Anne's parish" | Signals religion | "Walking distance to the town center" |
| "Not suitable for anyone with mobility issues" | Disability | "Second-floor unit; access by stairs, no elevator" |
| "Ideal for professionals, no students" | Sorts occupants | "Quiet building; on-site laundry; assigned parking" |
| "Mature adults only" | Age | Omit; senior-housing exemptions are narrow and must actually apply |
| "English-speaking building" | National origin | Omit entirely |
| "Great for empty nesters" | Familial status and age, softened | "Single-story, low-maintenance yard" |

Notice that every rewrite is **more informative** than the original. "Second-floor unit; access by stairs, no elevator" tells a buyer with a mobility concern exactly what they need to know, without deciding for them whether the property will work.

### 3.4 Accessibility information belongs in the copy

A common overcorrection is to omit accessibility features entirely on the theory that any mention of disability is prohibited. That withholds information consumers need.

The rule is about who the property is *for*, not about what the property *has*. So:

- **Do** describe: single-level layout, step-free entry, wide doorways, roll-in shower, elevator, grab bars, accessible parking.
- **Do not** characterize: who the property is or is not suitable for.

### 3.5 Photography and imagery

Imagery communicates preference as effectively as words. Marketing that consistently depicts one group of people — in photographs, in illustrations, in video — can signal who is welcome. Where people appear in marketing, the practice should not suggest that the housing is directed at or away from any group.

### 3.6 The equal housing statement is not a cure

Including an equal housing opportunity statement or logo is appropriate and expected. It does not cure discriminatory content. A discriminatory advertisement with a fair housing logo is still a discriminatory advertisement, and the same is true of a disclaimer stating that all are welcome.

### 3.7 The seller's instruction

A seller may direct copy the licensee cannot publish. The instruction does not authorize it, documenting that it came from the seller does not transfer responsibility, and publishing a softened version that conveys the same preference is the same violation in gentler words.

The professional response is to explain why the copy cannot run, offer the property-focused rewrite, and escalate to the broker if the seller insists.

### 3.8 Checkpoint

You should be able to take a paragraph of listing copy, identify each phrase that sorts people rather than describing the property, and rewrite it so the resulting copy is both compliant and more useful to a buyer.

## 4. Accessibility and accommodations

### 4.1 Two different requests

Licensees and property managers routinely encounter two related but distinct requests, and using the wrong framework produces the wrong answer.

| | Reasonable accommodation | Reasonable modification |
|---|---|---|
| What it changes | A rule, policy, practice, or service | The physical premises |
| Examples | Waiving a no-pet policy for an assistance animal; a reserved accessible parking space; permitting a live-in aide; accepting a rent payment on a different date tied to benefit timing | Installing a grab bar; widening a doorway; adding a ramp; lowering a countertop |
| Who requests | The person with the disability, or someone on their behalf | The same |
| Analysis | Is it reasonable, and is it necessary because of the disability? | The same |

The common error is to evaluate an accommodation request under the ordinary policy — treating a request to waive a no-pet rule as a pet question — which produces a denial that never engaged the actual legal standard.

### 4.2 Assistance animals are not pets

For fair housing purposes, an assistance animal is not a pet. Three consequences that are frequently mishandled:

1. A no-pet policy does not, by itself, justify denying the request.
2. **A pet deposit or pet rent may not be charged** for an assistance animal. Charging one imposes a condition on a protected group that others do not bear.
3. A request cannot be refused merely because the animal is not "certified." Reliance on a certification requirement is a common and serious error.

The housing provider may still evaluate reasonableness, and the resident remains responsible for damage the animal causes and for the animal's behavior.

### 4.3 What "reasonable" means

Reasonableness is an actual analysis, not a formality, and it can produce a denial. Factors that legitimately bear on it include whether the request would impose an undue financial and administrative burden, whether it would fundamentally alter the nature of the provider's operations, and whether an alternative would equally meet the need.

Two points about process:

- **The interactive process matters.** Where a request as made is not workable, the provider should engage about alternatives rather than simply refusing.
- **Delay functions as denial.** A request that receives no response is effectively refused, and the absence of a decision is not a defense.

### 4.4 Modifications: who pays and what conditions apply

In private housing, a reasonable modification is generally made **at the requester's expense**. The provider may impose reasonable conditions — that the work be done in a workmanlike manner, that permits be obtained, and in some circumstances that the premises be restored when the tenancy ends.

What the provider may not do is treat the request as an unauthorized alteration and refuse it on that basis. That is applying the lease framework to a fair housing question.

### 4.5 Verification, and its limits

Where a disability or the disability-related need is not obvious, a provider may generally seek information sufficient to establish that the person has a disability and that the requested accommodation is necessary because of it.

What may not be sought is a diagnosis, medical records, or detail about the nature or severity of the condition. The question is whether the need exists, not what the condition is.

### 4.6 New construction and design requirements

Certain multifamily housing is subject to accessible design and construction requirements, and California standards can exceed the federal baseline. A licensee marketing new construction should not represent that a property meets accessibility requirements without confirming it, and should direct specific questions to the developer, the architect, or the jurisdiction.

### 4.7 Accessibility in how the licensee delivers service

The obligation is not limited to the property. It extends to how a licensee provides service:

- Offering information in a format a consumer can use.
- Not assuming a consumer's needs or capacities from an observation.
- Not steering a consumer toward or away from properties based on an assumption about what would suit their disability. **The consumer decides what works for them**; the licensee provides accurate information about the property.

That last point is the one licensees most often get wrong with the best intentions. Deciding on a buyer's behalf that a two-story house "won't work for him" and not showing it is steering, however kindly meant.

### 4.9 A worked accommodation request

> **The request.** A licensee is showing a rental property he has listed for an owner. An applicant emails: "I have a service dog. The listing says no pets. Also, I use a wheelchair, and I'd need the bathroom doorway widened and a ramp at the back entrance. Is that possible?"
>
> **Section 4.1's first move: separate the requests**, because they are governed differently.
>
> **1. The animal.** Section 4.2 governs. **An assistance animal is not a pet**, and a no-pets policy is not the answer to the request. Waiving the policy for an assistance animal is a **reasonable accommodation** — a change to a rule — and the ordinary pet deposit and pet rent do not attach to it, though the tenant remains responsible for damage the animal actually causes.
>
> **What may be asked is limited**, and Section 4.5 sets the boundary: where the disability and the need are not obvious, reliable information that the person has a disability-related need may be requested. **What may not be asked** is the diagnosis, the medical records, or a demonstration of the animal's training. The licensee does not improvise this conversation — he follows the brokerage's procedure and asks the broker where anything is unclear.
>
> **2. The ramp and the doorway.** Section 4.4 governs. These are **modifications** — physical changes to the property — and modifications are analyzed differently from accommodations, including on the question of who pays and what conditions may attach. **The analysis is fact-specific and it belongs to the owner with the broker's guidance**, not to the licensee at the keyboard.
>
> **What the licensee does immediately, before any of that is resolved:** he does not treat the email as a reason to stop processing the application. **The most common violation in this scenario is not a wrong answer about the ramp. It is the applicant quietly not hearing back**, which Lesson 14 of the mortgage course names as discouragement and which produces a denial with no record of one.
>
> **What the licensee says to the owner.** Owners react to these requests, and some react badly. "We don't allow dogs" and "I'm not remodeling a bathroom for a tenant" are the two responses to expect. **The licensee's job is to tell the owner what the framework actually requires** — that the animal is not a pet question, and that the modification question has an analysis rather than a preference — and to escalate to the broker rather than to relay the owner's instruction.
>
> **What the file shows afterward:** the request with its date, the response with its date, what was asked and on what basis, what the owner was told, what was escalated, and the disposition of the application on the same criteria applied to every other applicant.
>
> **And what the licensee must never do:** ask the applicant what her disability is, tell her the property "probably isn't a good fit," suggest she look at a different property, or answer the modification question with an off-the-cuff no. **Each of those is a steering or discouragement problem in a matter that already involves two protected characteristics.**

### 4.10 Checkpoint

You should be able to classify a request as an accommodation or a modification, state the standard applied to it, and identify what may and may not be asked in verification.

## 5. Open house and showing practices

### 5.1 Three obligations at once

Every showing carries three obligations simultaneously, and a practice designed for only one of them fails the others:

1. **Security** — protecting the property and its occupants.
2. **Fair access** — treating every visitor the same way.
3. **Personal safety** — protecting the licensee.

### 5.2 Uniformity is the fair access control

The single most important principle: **the protocol is the same for every visitor.**

The reason is not only fairness in the abstract. Judgments about which visitors seem serious, trustworthy, or "right for the property" are precisely where bias operates, and they are indefensible afterward because the licensee cannot articulate a neutral basis for the distinction.

Concretely:

| Practice | Uniform version |
|---|---|
| Accompanying visitors | Everyone is accompanied, or the same supervision applies to everyone |
| Requesting identification | Requested of everyone, or of no one |
| Registration | Everyone registers, with the same disclosure about future contact |
| Qualifying questions | The same questions asked of everyone, in the same way |
| Access to all areas | The same areas shown to everyone |

A licensee who greets one visitor with coffee and asks the next whether he is preapproved has created a fair housing exposure at the first point of contact — before any property is discussed.

### 5.3 Registration and consent

An open house register serves security and follow-up. It also creates the consent question addressed in Lesson 02: what a visitor agreed to receive is defined by what the register told them.

A register that captures a phone number under a heading saying nothing about future contact has not obtained consent for a marketing text campaign. A register that says plainly what the visitor is agreeing to has. Write the register accordingly, and apply it to everyone.

### 5.4 Property security

Before the showing:

- Secure or remove valuables, medications, personal documents, and firearms — raised with the seller at intake, not at the door.
- Identify areas that will not be shown and secure them.
- Confirm what the seller has authorized: signage, lockbox, photography by visitors.

During:

- Maintain the accompaniment protocol.
- Do not leave the property unattended with visitors inside.
- Note who visits and with what brokerage.

After:

- Check windows, doors, and secondary entrances against a defined list.
- Confirm the property is secured before leaving.
- Report anything unusual to the seller and the broker.

### 5.5 Licensee safety

Real estate practice puts licensees alone in unoccupied properties with strangers. Sound practice:

- Avoid working an open house alone where that is possible.
- Tell someone where you are and when you expect to finish, with a check-in.
- Walk behind visitors rather than ahead of them, and keep the path to the exit clear.
- Know the layout, including secondary exits, before visitors arrive.
- Meet unfamiliar prospects first at the office rather than at a vacant property, applied as a uniform practice rather than selectively.
- Trust the instinct to end a showing. No transaction justifies remaining somewhere that feels unsafe.

That last practice must be **uniform** to be defensible. Meeting some prospects at the office and others at the property, based on impression, reintroduces the fair access problem the uniformity principle solves.

### 5.6 Tenant-occupied property

Where a tenant occupies the property, the tenant's possessory rights govern access: proper notice, permitted purpose, reasonable time. The seller's desire to show does not override it, and a licensee who enters without proper notice exposes the seller as well as themselves.

Agree a protocol with the tenant in advance, honor it, and document the notices given.

### 5.7 Virtual showings

Video tours and live virtual showings are advertising and are subject to the same truthfulness standard: they must depict the property as it is. They also raise their own questions — whether the seller consented to a live broadcast of the interior, whether recording devices in the home are disclosed, and whether virtual access is offered on the same terms to everyone.

### 5.8 Checkpoint

You should be able to write an open house protocol that satisfies security, fair access, and personal safety at once, and explain why selective application of a safety practice creates a fair housing problem.

## 6. Marketing approval and archive

### 6.1 Why approval exists

Advertising goes out under the brokerage's identity, and the broker is accountable for it. Pre-publication approval is how the broker exercises that accountability rather than discovering problems afterward.

An approval requirement that is optional in practice is not a control. The measure of whether it is real is whether a licensee who publishes without approval is noticed.

### 6.2 A working approval process

1. **A named approver** and a named backup, so approval does not stall.
2. **A defined turnaround**, short enough that licensees do not route around it. Most non-compliance with approval processes is caused by the process being slow.
3. **A checklist** covering identification, factual claims and their sources, fair housing language, imagery, and status accuracy.
4. **A record of the approval** — who approved what, and when.
5. **A defined exception path** for time-sensitive material, which is logged rather than informal.

### 6.3 The archive

An advertisement that cannot be produced cannot be reviewed. The archive should hold:

- What ran — the actual creative, not a description of it.
- Where it ran and on what dates.
- Who approved it and when.
- Any revision history, including corrections and takedowns.

This matters most for ephemeral formats. A printed flyer survives in a file; a story post that expired in 24 hours does not, unless it was captured. Social content needs deliberate archiving precisely because the platform does not keep it for you.

### 6.4 Retention and its purpose

Advertising records are retained because a complaint about an advertisement can arrive long after it ran, and because a regulator's inquiry will ask what was published rather than what the policy said. Retention length follows the brokerage's records policy and the applicable requirement, and — as with transaction records — a policy stricter than the legal minimum governs the licensee's conduct.

### 6.5 Corrections and takedowns

When advertising is found to be inaccurate:

1. **Correct or remove it promptly**, across every platform where it appeared — which requires knowing where it appeared, which requires the archive.
2. **Record what was wrong, when it was corrected, and by whom.**
3. **Notify the client** where the error concerned their property.
4. **Address the process gap**, not only the instance. A wrong square footage that came from an unverified source is a sourcing problem, and correcting one listing does not fix it.

Note what is not on the list: quietly deleting the post and moving on. Silent removal leaves no record that the problem existed, no evidence that it was addressed, and no correction of the process that produced it.

### 6.6 A worked audit

> A brokerage reviews its advertising for one quarter and finds:
>
> - Eleven social posts promoting listings with no brokerage identification.
> - One flyer describing a property as "walking distance to excellent family schools."
> - One listing still advertised as available three weeks after it went into contract.
> - No archive of any social content; the posts were found by scrolling the accounts.
>
> **Assessment.** The identification failures are the most numerous and the easiest to fix. The flyer is the most serious in kind, because it is fair housing copy that sorts people. The status misrepresentation misled every consumer and agent who saw it. And the absence of an archive is the finding that makes the others worse: the brokerage cannot show what else ran, so the quarter cannot actually be audited — only sampled.
>
> **Response.** Correct and record each instance; institute identification in the post rather than the profile; add fair housing language to the approval checklist; define who updates status and when; and begin archiving social content at publication. The response is documented, because how a brokerage answers its own findings is itself evidence about the supervision system.

### 6.8 The advertisement you did not make

A licensee's advertising footprint includes material he never wrote and cannot edit. It is still his footprint.

| The source | What the licensee is responsible for |
|---|---|
| **Syndication to portals** | Content reformatted by systems he does not control, where identification can be stripped and photographs re-cropped. **He is responsible for what appears about his listing**, which means checking what it looks like on the portals rather than only in the listing service. |
| **Aggregator profiles** | Assembled from scraped data, sometimes with stale listings, wrong contact details, or claims he never made. **The obligation to correct is his**, and most platforms provide a claim-and-correct process. |
| **A colleague's post about his listing** | Advertising of the property, subject to the same requirements. Where it is wrong, he raises it — with the colleague and, if needed, the broker. |
| **A seller's own posts** | Not the licensee's advertisement, but a licensee who sees a seller advertising the property with a claim he knows is false has a problem he cannot ignore. |
| **A vendor's marketing naming him** | A lender, warranty company, or photographer promoting "our partner agent." **Value received and a representation made** — both of which have appeared earlier in this course. |
| **Automated listing alerts and drip campaigns** | Written once, sending for years, often containing claims and identification that have gone stale. |

**Two of these deserve emphasis.**

**Stale automation is the largest silent exposure in this list.** A campaign built two years ago with a market claim that was true then, sending weekly, is making a false statement every week — and nobody is reading it, which is exactly why it persists. **Automated content is reviewed on a schedule**, not when someone happens to notice.

**Portal appearance is not listing service appearance.** The identification that satisfies the requirement in the listing service field may not survive syndication into a portal's template. **Check the destination**, because the consumer sees the destination.

> **The archive obligation extends to all of it.** Section 6.3 requires capturing what was published as published. For syndicated and third-party material, that means periodic captures of how the listing actually appears where consumers find it — which is also how a licensee discovers that a portal has been showing a photograph he removed six weeks ago.

### 6.9 Checkpoint

You should be able to describe an approval and archive process that would allow a brokerage to answer, six months later, exactly what ran, where, when, and with whose approval.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **Truthfulness standard** | Advertising is measured against the property **as it is when the advertisement runs** — not as photographed, not as the seller described it, not as assumed. |
| **Sourcing** | The discipline of being able to name where each factual claim in marketing came from. |
| **Per seller** | The honest attribution for an unverified figure supplied by the seller. The alternative is to omit it. |
| **Laundering a figure** | Copying an unverified number from a prior listing so it acquires apparent authority. Not verification. |
| **Virtual staging** | Digitally added furnishings. Acceptable **when labeled**; unacceptable when presented as existing condition. |
| **Puffery** | Opinion a consumer understands as opinion — "charming," "stunning." Distinct from a factual claim about value, entitlement, or feasibility. |
| **Status accuracy** | The requirement that a listing's advertised availability reflect reality. Misusing status to preserve exposure or market time misrepresents to everyone relying on the data. |
| **Identification** | The requirement that advertising and solicitation identify the licensee, license identification, and the brokerage, so a consumer knows who is responsible and can verify licensure. |
| **Conspicuousness** | The standard for identification: legible and prominent. Merely present is not the standard. |
| **Team name** | A brand used by a group within a brokerage. Compliant when it does not create the impression of a separate licensed firm. |
| **Audience targeting** | Selecting who sees an advertisement. Part of the advertisement — neutral copy delivered selectively can exclude as effectively as discriminatory wording. |
| **Protected characteristics** | The categories advertising may not express a preference about. **California protects more than the federal seven**, so "not one of the seven" is not clearance. |
| **Describe the property, not the people** | The workable form of the fair housing advertising rule. Property-focused copy is also more informative. |
| **Equal housing statement** | An appropriate and expected element of advertising. **Not a cure** for discriminatory content. |
| **Reasonable accommodation** | A change to a **rule, policy, practice, or service** made because of a disability. |
| **Reasonable modification** | A change to the **physical premises**, generally at the requester's expense in private housing, subject to reasonable conditions. |
| **Assistance animal** | Not a pet for fair housing purposes. No pet deposit or pet rent may be charged, and no certification may be required. |
| **Interactive process** | Engaging about workable alternatives where a request as made is not. Delay functions as denial. |
| **Verification limits** | A provider may seek enough to establish the disability and the disability-related need — **not** a diagnosis, medical records, or the nature or severity of the condition. |
| **Steering** | Directing a consumer toward or away from properties based on an assumption. Includes deciding on a buyer's behalf what will "work for them." |
| **Uniformity** | The control that satisfies fair access at showings: the same protocol for every visitor, including safety practices. |
| **Archive** | The record of what actually ran, where, on what dates, and with whose approval. Ephemeral formats require deliberate capture. |

## Practice problems

### Problem 1

A listing description reads: "1,840 sq ft, 4 bedrooms, all improvements permitted, walking distance
to excellent schools, room for a pool." The square footage came from the prior listing, the fourth
bedroom is a converted garage space with no egress window, and the seller told the licensee the
work was permitted.

**Identify each defect and state what each claim required.**

> **Solution.** Four defects.
>
> **Square footage:** copying from the prior listing is not verification — it launders an unverified
> number into an apparently authoritative one. Required: measurement, appraisal, or public record,
> **with the source stated**. "1,840 sq ft (per assessor; buyer to verify)" is both more useful and a
> materially better position if it turns out wrong.
>
> **Bedroom count:** a room counts if it meets the local definition. A converted space with no egress
> does not become a bedroom because it has a bed in it.
>
> **Permitted status:** "the seller says it was permitted" is not a source. Permit records are.
>
> **"Room for a pool":** this reads as opinion and is a factual claim about feasibility — it requires
> setbacks, soils, and access that would actually permit one.
>
> "Walking distance to excellent schools" is separately a fair housing problem, addressed in
> Problem 5.

### Problem 2

A listing's photographs were taken before a storm damaged the rear deck. The licensee digitally
removes a power line from the front elevation and adds furniture to the empty living room.

**Classify each of the three and state the test.**

> **Solution.** **Stale photographs: not acceptable.** Truthfulness is measured against the property
> as it is when the advertisement runs, and the deck is not what the images show.
>
> **Removing the power line: not acceptable.** Digital alteration that changes what the property is
> — removing a power line, a neighboring structure, or a visible defect — misrepresents it.
>
> **Adding furniture: acceptable, labeled as virtual staging.** Unlabeled, it is not.
>
> **The test:** whether a consumer viewing the property in person would feel the images described a
> different place.

### Problem 3

A licensee posts a listing to her personal Instagram account. Her brokerage is named in her profile
bio but not in the post. She boosts it with targeting set to homeowners aged 25–45 within three ZIP
codes.

**Identify the two problems and rank them.**

> **Solution.** **The targeting is the more serious**, and it is easy to miss because it does not
> appear in the copy at all. Restricting who may see a housing advertisement can deny groups access
> to the housing opportunity — that is the harm the rules exist to prevent — and age-based selection
> plus geographic proxies is exactly that. Neutral copy delivered selectively excludes as effectively
> as discriminatory wording, and "the platform offered that option" is not an answer.
>
> **The identification defect is second.** Each post is an advertisement in itself. Identifying the
> profile is not identifying the advertisement — and a personal account posting listings is
> advertising regardless of what the licensee considers it.
>
> **What transfers:** examine targeting choices with the same care as the words.

### Problem 4

A team advertises as "Summit Realty Group" with its own logo, displayed far more prominently than the
brokerage name in small type at the bottom. Every property fact in the advertisements is accurate.

**Is there a defect?**

> **Solution.** Yes, and the accuracy of the property facts is a different requirement that does not
> cure it. The test is the impression created for a consumer, not the internal organizational chart:
> a team identity presented as a separate firm obscures which licensed brokerage is accountable.
>
> "Group" and similar terms compound it by suggesting the team is itself a company. And "present" is
> not the standard — legible and conspicuous is.
>
> **The fix:** brokerage identity legible wherever the team identity appears, a team name that does
> not suggest a brokerage, and relative prominence that does not let a reader take the team for the
> firm.

### Problem 5

Rewrite each phrase so it describes the property: (a) "family-friendly neighborhood"; (b) "not
suitable for anyone with mobility issues"; (c) "walking distance to St. Anne's parish."

**Then state what makes each rewrite better than an omission.**

> **Solution.** (a) "Cul-de-sac location; park two blocks away." (b) "Second-floor unit; access by
> stairs, no elevator." (c) "Walking distance to the town center."
>
> Each rewrite is **more informative than the original**. That is the point worth carrying: (b)
> especially tells a buyer with a mobility concern exactly what they need to know, without deciding
> on their behalf whether the property will work for them.
>
> This is also the answer to the common overcorrection of omitting accessibility features entirely.
> The rule governs who the property is *for*, not what the property *has*. Describe the step-free
> entry, the roll-in shower, the elevator. Do not characterize who it suits.
>
> A seller who insists on the original copy does not authorize it, documenting that it came from him
> does not transfer responsibility, and a softened version conveying the same preference is the same
> violation in gentler words.

### Problem 6

A tenant with a disability asks the property manager to waive the no-pet policy for her assistance
animal and to install grab bars in the bathroom. The manager replies that the building does not
allow pets, that certification would be required in any event, and that a pet deposit would apply.

**Classify each request and identify every error in the reply.**

> **Solution.** The animal is a request for a **reasonable accommodation** — a change to a rule or
> policy. The grab bars are a **reasonable modification** — a change to the physical premises,
> generally at the requester's expense, subject to reasonable conditions such as workmanlike
> installation and permits.
>
> Four errors in the reply. It **evaluated the accommodation under the ordinary pet policy**, which
> is the classic mistake: it produced a denial that never engaged the actual standard. An assistance
> animal **is not a pet**, so the no-pet policy does not by itself justify refusal. **Certification
> may not be required.** And a **pet deposit or pet rent may not be charged**, because it imposes a
> condition on a protected group others do not bear.
>
> What the provider may still do: conduct a genuine reasonableness analysis, engage about
> alternatives, and hold the resident responsible for damage and for the animal's behavior. What it
> may not do is let the request sit — delay functions as denial.

### Problem 7

A licensee working an open house greets one couple warmly and walks them through the property, lets
a second visitor tour alone, and asks a third whether he is preapproved before showing him the back
bedrooms. She also meets some unfamiliar prospects at the office and others at vacant properties,
depending on her impression of them.

**Identify the exposures.**

> **Solution.** Every distinction here is drawn on impression, and impression is precisely where bias
> operates — none of it can be explained afterwards in neutral terms.
>
> **Accompaniment** must be uniform: everyone accompanied, or the same supervision for everyone. The
> unaccompanied visitor is separately a property security failure.
>
> **Qualifying questions** must be the same questions asked of everyone, in the same way. Asking one
> visitor about preapproval before showing him areas others saw freely is a fair housing exposure at
> the first point of contact, before any property was discussed.
>
> **The safety practice is the subtle one.** Meeting unfamiliar prospects at the office first is
> sound practice — but applied selectively by impression it reintroduces exactly the problem
> uniformity solves. Apply it to everyone or to no one.
>
> **What transfers:** three obligations run at every showing — security, fair access, and personal
> safety — and a practice designed for one of them fails the others unless it is uniform.

### Problem 8

A quarterly review finds eleven social posts with no brokerage identification, one flyer reading
"walking distance to excellent family schools," one listing still advertised as available three
weeks after going into contract, and no archive of any social content.

**Rank the findings and state the response.**

> **Solution.** The **flyer is the most serious in kind** — fair housing copy that sorts people. The
> **status misrepresentation** misled every consumer and agent relying on that data. The
> **identification failures** are the most numerous and the easiest to fix.
>
> But the **absence of an archive is the finding that makes the others worse**: the brokerage cannot
> show what else ran, so the quarter cannot actually be audited, only sampled.
>
> **Response:** correct and record each instance; identification in the post rather than the profile;
> fair housing language on the approval checklist; a named owner and timing for status updates; and
> archiving social content **at publication**, because the platform does not keep ephemeral formats
> for you.
>
> What must not happen is quietly deleting the posts. Silent removal leaves no record the problem
> existed, no evidence it was addressed, and no correction of the process that produced it — and how
> a brokerage answers its own findings is itself evidence about the supervision system.

## Chapter summary

**Truthfulness is measured against the property as it is when the advertisement runs.** Most defects
are not lies; they are statements that were true once, true of something else, or true if an
assumption held.

**Every factual claim needs a nameable source.** Where the source is the seller and it has not been
verified, attribute it or omit it. Copying from a prior listing is not verification.

**Photographs are advertising.** Virtual staging and renderings are acceptable when labeled;
alteration that changes what the property is, is not.

**Opinion is permitted; opinion stated as verifiable fact is not.** Claims about value, entitlement,
and feasibility are factual claims and must be supportable.

**Identification exists so a consumer can find who is responsible and verify licensure.** It belongs
in the post, not only in the profile, and legibility — not mere presence — is the standard.

**Audience targeting is part of the advertisement.** Restricting who sees a housing advertisement can
deny access as effectively as the words, and using a platform's tool does not shift responsibility.

**Describe the property; do not describe the people who should live in it.** California protects more
than the federal seven, intent is not a defense, and an equal housing statement does not cure
discriminatory content. Property-focused copy is consistently more informative.

**Accommodations change rules; modifications change premises.** Evaluating an accommodation under the
ordinary policy is the classic error. Assistance animals are not pets, no certification may be
required, no pet charge may be imposed, and delay functions as denial.

**Verification asks whether the need exists, not what the condition is.**

**Uniformity is the fair access control at showings** — including safety practices, which must be
applied to everyone to be defensible.

**Approval and archive are how a broker's accountability becomes real.** The measure of an approval
process is whether publishing without it is noticed; the measure of an archive is whether the
brokerage can say, six months later, exactly what ran, where, when, and with whose approval.
