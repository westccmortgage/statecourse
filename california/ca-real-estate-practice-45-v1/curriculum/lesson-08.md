## 1. California purchase agreement architecture

### 1.1 What the agreement is

The residential purchase agreement is the operative contract of the transaction. Everything that follows — escrow instructions, lender documents, disclosures, notices — implements it or reports on it. Where escrow's instructions and the purchase agreement conflict, **the parties' agreement governs**, and the discrepancy is reconciled by the parties in writing rather than by escrow deciding.

Licensees who treat the agreement as a form to be filled rather than a contract to be understood cannot answer their client's questions about their own transaction, and cannot recognize when a proposed change matters.

### 1.2 The functional components

Rather than memorizing a form's paragraph numbers, learn what any residential purchase agreement must address:

| Component | What it governs |
|---|---|
| **Parties and property** | Who is bound, and what is being conveyed, including personal property |
| **Price and deposit** | The consideration, and what the deposit secures |
| **Financing terms** | The loan the buyer will obtain, and the down payment |
| **Escrow and title** | Who holds, who insures, and the form of title policy |
| **Allocation of costs** | Which party pays which closing costs |
| **Contingencies** | The conditions qualifying the buyer's obligation, and their periods |
| **Disclosure obligations** | What the seller must deliver, and when |
| **Investigation rights** | The buyer's right to inspect, and the seller's obligation to provide access |
| **Repairs and credits** | Whether any are agreed, and how they are performed |
| **Closing and possession** | When title transfers, and when the buyer takes possession |
| **Notices and delivery** | How communications that carry legal effect must be made |
| **Default and remedies** | What happens if a party fails to perform, including liquidated damages |
| **Dispute resolution** | Mediation and arbitration provisions |
| **Agency confirmation** | The relationships elected |

### 1.3 The deposit

The deposit — earnest money — performs two functions: it signals commitment, and it is the fund from which a seller's remedy is typically drawn if the buyer defaults.

Points the buyer must understand before he offers it:

- **Where it goes.** To escrow, per the contract's terms, within the time the contract specifies.
- **When it is at risk.** Not on any change of mind — a buyer acting within a contingency generally has a path to recover it. Once contingencies are removed, the exposure changes materially.
- **Liquidated damages.** Where the parties agree in advance that a specified amount will be the seller's recovery on default, that provision must be properly agreed, and its enforceability is constrained by law. It is not a penalty the seller may simply impose.

### 1.4 What conveys

Fixtures generally convey; personal property generally does not. Because the fixture tests are difficult to apply after the fact, the professional practice is to make them unnecessary by naming the disputed items.

Items worth naming explicitly in most transactions: appliances, window coverings, mounted televisions and their brackets, chandeliers and light fixtures, above-ground spas, storage sheds and outbuildings, security and smart-home equipment, water treatment systems, and anything the seller has said they intend to take.

### 1.5 Time is a term

Nearly every obligation in the agreement carries a date or a period, and those dates are terms of the contract rather than administrative targets. A licensee who treats a closing date as a scheduling preference will eventually explain to a client why the other party was entitled to act.

### 1.6 What the agreement does not do

A purchase agreement is comprehensive about the things it addresses and silent about a great many things the parties assume it covers. **Most transaction disputes live in that silence**, and a licensee who knows where it falls can close it before it becomes an argument.

| The parties assume | The agreement's actual position |
|---|---|
| "The seller will fix everything on the inspection." | It says nothing of the kind unless a repair term was negotiated and written. **An inspection contingency is a right to investigate and respond, not a promise of repair.** |
| "Obviously the appliances stay." | What conveys is what the agreement says conveys, plus what the law treats as part of the real property. **A washer and dryer are personal property until an agreement makes them otherwise.** |
| "We agreed on the closing date in the email." | The operative date is the one in the agreement, amended in writing. Section 5.5 governs. |
| "The seller said he'd leave the patio furniture." | An oral side agreement about personal property, unrecorded anywhere, discovered at walkthrough. |
| "Possession is at closing." | Only if the agreement says so. Delayed possession and rent-back are terms, and each carries insurance, liability, and condition questions. |
| "The warranty is included." | A negotiated term, and one that is frequently discussed and not written. |
| "They're taking the solar with them." | Depends on ownership, lease, or financing arrangement — **a question with a documentary answer that must be obtained rather than assumed.** |

**Three of these produce the most walkthrough failures:** what conveys, possession, and anything the parties handled in conversation.

**The licensee's practice is a habit rather than a rule.** At the point the offer is written, run the list of assumptions out loud with the client: what stays, when possession happens, what the repair expectation is, and whether anything was discussed that is not on the page. **Whatever comes out of that conversation goes into the offer or into a written amendment**, and whatever does not go in does not exist.

> **The sentence that prevents most of them:** "If it matters to you and it isn't written down, it isn't part of the deal." It is not a legal opinion, it is accurate, and clients understand it immediately.

## 2. Offer presentation and formation

### 2.1 The formation rules

Three rules resolve most questions:

1. **An offer is a proposal that, if accepted without change, forms a contract.**
2. **An acceptance must match the offer.** Any change to a material term is not an acceptance.
3. **Acceptance must be communicated** to the offeror in the manner the offer or the law contemplates. A signed acceptance sitting in a drawer has not been communicated.

### 2.2 The counteroffer

When a seller changes the price and the closing date and signs, the effect is precise and is worth stating in full:

> The counteroffer **rejects** the original offer and **substitutes a new one**. The original offer can no longer be accepted unless it is renewed. The buyer may accept, reject, or counter again.

Two consequences licensees regularly get wrong:

- It is **not** an "acceptance with modifications" that forms a contract on the seller's terms.
- It does **not** suspend the original offer, leaving it available if the counteroffer is rejected. The original is gone.

The same applies in the other direction. A buyer who signs a counteroffer but writes a change on it before returning it has made a further counteroffer, however small the change. Signing does not convert a modification into an acceptance.

### 2.3 Revocation and expiration

An offer may generally be **revoked before acceptance**, even where it states a deadline — unless it is supported by consideration as an option. A stated expiration sets an outer limit; it does not make the offer irrevocable in the meantime.

Practical consequence for a buyer's licensee: an offer stated to be open until Friday can be withdrawn Wednesday, and a buyer who is relying on the deadline should know that. Practical consequence for a seller's licensee: a seller intending to accept should not assume the offer will still be there.

### 2.4 Presenting offers

The seller's licensee presents offers; the seller decides. Within that:

- **All offers are presented** unless the seller has lawfully instructed otherwise, and the licensee should understand and document any such instruction.
- **The presentation is accurate.** Terms are described as they are, including terms unfavorable to a quick decision.
- **The licensee advises**, including on the terms that make one offer more likely to close than another, rather than presenting price alone.
- **The seller's decision is the seller's**, including a decision the licensee considers unwise.

### 2.5 Electronic signature and delivery

Electronic signature is ordinary in California residential practice. Two points matter:

1. **Delivery still has to happen and still has to be evidenced.** An executed document in a signing platform that no one delivered has not been delivered, and the platform's audit trail is frequently the best evidence of when it was.
2. **The contract's own notice provisions govern** what constitutes delivery for a notice that carries legal effect. Delivery by a method the contract does not authorize may not count.

### 2.6 A worked formation problem

> **The sequence.**
>
> - **Monday 9:00 a.m.** Buyer signs an offer with an expiration of Wednesday at 5:00 p.m. It is delivered to the listing agent at 9:20.
> - **Tuesday 11:00 a.m.** The seller signs a counteroffer changing price and closing date, with its own expiration Thursday at 5:00 p.m. The listing agent puts it in her outbox and does not send it.
> - **Tuesday 3:00 p.m.** A second buyer's offer arrives, better on price.
> - **Tuesday 4:00 p.m.** The seller tells the listing agent he wants to withdraw the counteroffer and work with the second buyer.
> - **Tuesday 4:30 p.m.** The listing agent sends the first counteroffer by mistake — a scheduled send she forgot to cancel.
> - **Tuesday 4:45 p.m.** The first buyer signs the counteroffer and returns it immediately.
>
> **The questions this raises**, and they are contract-law questions on which the parties will need counsel — **the licensee's job is to recognize them, preserve the record, and escalate, not to declare who has a deal.**
>
> **1. Was the counteroffer effective before it was sent?** A counteroffer signed and sitting in an outbox has not been communicated. Section 2.1's formation rules turn on communication rather than on signature, which is why the 4:30 transmission matters more than the 11:00 signature.
>
> **2. Did the seller's 4:00 instruction revoke it?** Revocation generally must be communicated to be effective, and at 4:00 the counteroffer had not been communicated to anyone. **Whether an instruction to withdraw something that was never sent has any operative effect is precisely the kind of question that goes to counsel.**
>
> **3. What did the 4:30 send do?** The listing agent transmitted a document her principal had told her thirty minutes earlier not to send. **That is a serious problem for the listing agent independent of the contract question** — Lesson 03's obedience duty is engaged, and this is a broker matter immediately.
>
> **4. What is the first buyer's position?** He received a counteroffer, within its stated period, and accepted it. From his side the sequence looks ordinary. **Nothing about the listing brokerage's internal failure is visible to him**, which is why the situation is dangerous rather than merely awkward.
>
> **What both licensees do now.**
>
> **Preserve everything immediately** — the send record with its timestamp, the 4:00 instruction and how it was given, the scheduling configuration that produced the send, and every message in both directions. **Nothing is deleted, unsent, or recalled.** An attempted recall is itself an act on the record.
>
> **Notify the brokers.** Both of them, now.
>
> **Say nothing to either buyer about whether a contract exists.** "I don't know, and my broker and counsel are addressing it" is the accurate answer, and the buyer is better served by it than by a confident wrong one.
>
> **Do not present the second offer to the seller as though the first sequence had not happened**, and do not accept it, until the brokerage knows where it stands.
>
> **The preventable failure is not the mistake. It is the automation.** A scheduled transmission of a document whose authorization can change is a system that acts without a person, and the first thing the broker should change is that documents go out when someone sends them.

### 2.7 Checkpoint

You should be able to take a sequence of signed documents and say at which point, if any, a contract was formed and on whose terms.

## 3. Counteroffers and multiple offers

### 3.1 How multiple offers actually work

Three rules that dispose of most buyer and seller misconceptions:

1. **The seller chooses.** There is no obligation to accept the highest offer.
2. **There is no priority by order of receipt.** An earlier offer has no claim over a later one.
3. **The seller decides whether competing terms are disclosed.** Disclosure is the seller's call, made with their agent, not a right the competing buyers hold.

### 3.2 Countering multiple offers

A seller may counter more than one offer at once. Doing so creates a real hazard the licensee must manage: if two counteroffers are accepted, the seller may be exposed on both.

The customary protection is a provision making the seller's obligation contingent on the seller's subsequent written acceptance of the buyer's acceptance — so the seller retains a final election. A licensee who counters multiple offers without that protection has exposed the client.

### 3.3 The line between confidentiality and misrepresentation

This is the most consequential distinction in the section.

| Conduct | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Declining to disclose competing offer terms | **Permissible.** The seller decides. |
| Saying "there are multiple offers" when there are | Permissible and accurate |
| Saying "there are multiple offers" when there are not | **Misrepresentation** |
| Describing a competing offer's terms accurately, with the seller's authorization | Permissible |
| Inventing a competing offer's terms to raise the price | **Misrepresentation** |
| Describing an anticipated offer as an existing one | **Misrepresentation** |
| Disclosing terms without the seller's authorization | Breach of duty to the seller |

Note that two of these fail in opposite directions: inventing competition breaches the duty of honesty owed to the buyer; disclosing without authority breaches the duty of confidentiality owed to the seller. A licensee must satisfy both at once.

A client's instruction cannot authorize a misrepresentation. A seller who directs the agent to say there are three offers when there are none has asked for something the agent must refuse.

### 3.4 Backup offers

A backup offer is a contract that becomes primary if the first transaction fails, in the position and on the terms agreed. It must be documented as such — a buyer who believes he is "next in line" without a written backup agreement has nothing.

The seller's licensee should ensure the primary buyer's rights are not compromised and that the backup buyer understands his position, including whether and when his deposit is at risk.

### 3.5 Advising a buyer in competition

Useful advice competes on terms rather than only on price — financing strength, timeline, contingency periods, occupancy — and is honest about the exposures each concession creates. Advice that is not useful: encouraging a buyer to waive protections without explaining what he is giving up, or presenting an escalation clause without ensuring he understands its mechanics and that the escalated price must still appraise.

## 4. Contingency mechanics

### 4.1 What a contingency is

A contingency **conditions a party's obligation** on a defined event or approval, inside a contract that has already been formed. Three things it is not:

- It does not delay formation. The contract exists.
- It is not a general right to cancel for any reason.
- It does not transfer the underlying responsibility to the other party.

### 4.2 The common residential contingencies

| Contingency | What it protects | Typical resolution |
|---|---|---|
| **Investigation / inspection** | The buyer's ability to evaluate condition | Removed after investigation, or used to request repairs or cancel |
| **Loan** | The buyer's ability to obtain the financing described | Removed on loan approval, or used to cancel if denied |
| **Appraisal** | The buyer's exposure if value does not support the price | Removed, or used to renegotiate or cancel |
| **Sale of buyer's property** | The buyer's dependence on another closing | Removed on that closing, subject to the agreed terms |
| **Review of documents** | Time to review disclosures, reports, HOA documents | Removed after review |
| **Title review** | The buyer's ability to evaluate title exceptions | Removed after review of the preliminary report |

### 4.3 How removal actually works

This is the single most misunderstood mechanic in California residential practice, and getting it wrong produces confident, wrong advice on both sides.

> **Under the standard California residential purchase agreement form, contingencies remain in effect until the buyer removes them in writing. They do not expire automatically when the date passes.**

Consequences:

- A buyer who lets the date pass without delivering a removal has **not** removed the contingency.
- A seller cannot treat the transaction as firm merely because the date has passed.
- The seller's route is to deliver the **notice the contract requires**, giving the buyer a defined period to act, after which the seller may cancel per the contract.

The habit to build is more durable than the rule: **read the contract's own removal mechanism** rather than assuming deadlines are self-executing. Different forms and negotiated terms can operate differently, and the contract in front of you governs.

### 4.4 A worked sequence

> The buyer's investigation contingency period ends on the 17th. Nothing is delivered.
>
> **On the 18th, what is true?** The contingency remains in effect. The buyer has not removed it. The seller is not free to sell to someone else.
>
> **What may the seller do?** Deliver the contractual notice requiring the buyer to act, which starts the period the contract specifies.
>
> **What if the buyer removes the contingency during that period?** The notice is satisfied and the transaction continues.
>
> **What if the buyer does not?** The seller may cancel per the contract's procedure.
>
> **What is not true at any point?** That the contract became void, that the buyer automatically became committed, or that the seller could cancel on the 18th without notice.

### 4.5 Cancelling under a contingency

A contingency provides a path, and the path has requirements. A buyer whose loan is denied while the loan contingency is in effect must **follow the contract's cancellation procedure** — the written notice it requires, within its terms, with any supporting documentation.

What forfeits the protection: notifying the other agent verbally, instructing escrow unilaterally to return the deposit, or simply ceasing to perform and letting the seller cancel for non-performance.

### 4.6 Waiver and its consequences

A buyer may waive a contingency, and in competitive markets many do. Two points the buyer must understand:

1. **He may still investigate.** Waiving the contingency removes the contractual path to act on what he finds; it does not prevent him from looking.
2. **The exposure is concrete.** Where the appraisal contingency is waived and the appraisal comes in low, the buyer's options narrow to bringing funds, renegotiating without leverage, or defaulting — with his deposit exposed.

The licensee's obligation is to put that in writing before the buyer decides, then follow his decision.

### 4.7 Contingencies under competitive pressure

In a market where sellers receive several offers, buyers are advised — often by other buyers, sometimes by licensees — to shorten or remove contingencies to compete. **This is a real strategy with real consequences, and the licensee's obligation is that the buyer understands the consequence before he chooses it.**

**What each shortening actually costs:**

| The move | What the buyer gives up |
|---|---|
| **Shortening the investigation period** | Time to obtain the specialist evaluations a general inspection recommends. **The evaluations do not get faster because the period got shorter.** |
| **Waiving investigation entirely** | The right to respond to what he finds — and Section 4.6 establishes that the property's condition is unchanged by the waiver. |
| **Waiving the appraisal contingency** | The obligation to cover a shortfall in cash, in an amount he cannot know in advance. **This is the waiver whose cost is most often misunderstood**, because it is stated as a risk and experienced as a bill. |
| **Waiving the loan contingency** | Exposure of the deposit — and potentially more — if financing fails for a reason outside his control. |
| **Shortening the closing period** | A deadline his own lender may not be able to meet, converting his aggressiveness into his default. |
| **Increasing the deposit** | More money at risk under every scenario above. |

**The licensee's role here has three parts and one prohibition.**

**Explain the mechanism with numbers.** "Waiving the appraisal contingency means if it appraises at 40,000 below your price, you bring 40,000 more in cash or you are in breach." **The number makes it real in a way the word "risk" does not.**

**Confirm the capacity is real.** A buyer waiving an appraisal contingency should have the funds, and the licensee should know he has told his lender what he is doing. **A waiver the buyer cannot perform is a default with a delay built in.**

**Document the decision.** Section 6.4 and Lesson 03's file requirements govern. The advice given, the consequence explained, and the buyer's decision — in writing, before the offer goes out.

**The prohibition:** the licensee does not recommend a waiver. **The strategy belongs to the buyer**, and a licensee whose file shows he advised waiving an appraisal contingency for a buyer who then could not perform has advised on a matter with financial consequences he does not carry.

> **One further point, easily missed.** The listing side has an interest in these waivers, and a listing agent who tells buyers' agents that "non-contingent offers only" will be considered is communicating the seller's preference — which is legitimate — while **also creating pressure on buyers to waive protections without advice.** Where a licensee sees a buyer waiving under that pressure with no apparent understanding, the professional response is to make sure the buyer's own agent has explained it, not to assume someone did.

## 5. Deadlines, notices, and removal

### 5.1 Build the calendar from the contract

The first act after acceptance is to build a deadline calendar **from the contract's own terms**, not from habit or from what is usual. Every date, every period, and what triggers each one.

Then share it with the client in writing. A buyer who knows his investigation period ends on the 17th behaves differently from one who is told on the 16th.

### 5.2 How periods are counted

Contracts define how time is counted, and the definitions vary: calendar days versus business days, whether the triggering day counts, when a period ending on a weekend or holiday rolls, and what time of day a deadline falls.

**Read the definitions in the contract in front of you.** A licensee who assumes and is wrong by one day has cost a client a right.

### 5.3 Notices carry legal effect

The contract specifies how notices must be delivered because rights depend on whether and when delivery occurred. Consequences:

- **Use the method the contract authorizes.** A text message is not delivery unless the contract makes it so.
- **Preserve the evidence.** Delivery confirmation, platform audit trail, signed receipt — whatever the method produces.
- **An undated notice is a notice with no proof of timing**, and timing is what disputes are about.

### 5.4 The common notices

| Notice | Function |
|---|---|
| **Contingency removal** | The buyer removes a specified contingency in writing |
| **Notice to buyer to perform** | The seller requires the buyer to act within the contract's period |
| **Notice to seller to perform** | The buyer requires the seller to act |
| **Request for repair** | The buyer requests repairs or credits following investigation |
| **Cancellation** | A party cancels under a right the contract provides |
| **Demand to close** | A party requires the other to close |

Each has a form and a timing requirement in the contract. Sending the wrong one, or the right one by the wrong method, is a common and expensive error.

### 5.5 Amendments

Any change to a term of the contract — the closing date, the price, what conveys, an agreed repair — is made by **written amendment signed by both parties** and delivered to escrow so the instructions match the agreement.

What does not change the contract: an email between the agents, a verbal agreement between the parties, an instruction to escrow alone, or an informal understanding that a short extension "won't matter."

> The parties agree during escrow to move the closing by a week. The agents confirm by email. Escrow is told. No amendment is signed.
>
> If the transaction later fails, the parties are litigating on a contract whose closing date is the original one, with an email chain between non-parties as the evidence of a change.

### 5.6 Tracking and confirming

Two habits prevent most deadline disputes:

1. **A written timeline** with each contractual date, the notice each requires, and the evidence of what was delivered.
2. **Written status confirmation at each milestone** — a short message confirming what has been delivered, what remains, and what the next date is.

Relying on escrow to track dates, on software reminders, or on verbal confirmation with the other agent may help the work happen, but none of them creates the record.

### 5.7 A worked calendar

> **The contract.** Acceptance is communicated on a Friday. The agreement provides an investigation period of seventeen days, a loan contingency of twenty-one days, an appraisal contingency of seventeen days, delivery of association documents "within seven days," a seller disclosure delivery obligation with a response period running from delivery, and a closing thirty days from acceptance.
>
> **Building the calendar — the questions that must be answered before a single date is written.**
>
> **1. What is day zero?** Periods run from a defined starting event, and the agreement defines it. **Assuming acceptance day is day one is the most common error**, and it shifts every deadline in the file by one.
>
> **2. Are the periods in calendar days or business days?** The agreement says which, and different periods within the same agreement can be counted differently.
>
> **3. What happens when a deadline lands on a weekend or holiday?** The agreement addresses it, and the rule is not the same in every agreement.
>
> **4. Which periods run from delivery rather than from acceptance?** The disclosure response period is the important one, and **it cannot be calendared until delivery actually occurs.** Section 5.2 governs, and this is why a file with an undated delivery has a deadline nobody can compute.
>
> **5. What is the time of day?** A deadline at 5:00 p.m. and a deadline at 11:59 p.m. are different deadlines, and a removal delivered at 8:00 p.m. on the correct date may be late.
>
> **The calendar that results contains more entries than the contract has deadlines**, because each deadline generates work that must happen before it:
>
> - **Association documents ordered** — the day after acceptance, not on the seventh day. The seven-day delivery obligation is the seller's; the buyer's review time is what remains after it.
> - **Inspections scheduled** — within the first three days, because Section 5.7 of Lesson 07 establishes that further-evaluation items need time inside the same period.
> - **Specialist evaluations ordered** — the moment the general report recommends them.
> - **Appraisal ordered** through the lender, with a follow-up date, because it is the item over which the buyer's side has the least control.
> - **A check-in three days before each removal deadline**, which is the entry that actually prevents the failure.
> - **Delivery confirmations** for every notice, recorded on the calendar as tasks rather than assumed.
>
> **The two failures this structure prevents.** First, the deadline that arrives with the work not started — the association documents ordered on day six, the specialist called on day fifteen. Second, **the removal delivered on time to the wrong place or with no proof**, which Section 5.3 identifies as carrying legal effect and Section 5.6 requires be confirmed.
>
> **And the calendar is built the day acceptance is communicated**, not later in the week. **A calendar built on day four has already lost the three days it would have protected.**

## 6. Amendment, cancellation, and dispute prevention

### 6.1 Cancellation is a procedure

A party cancels by exercising a right the contract provides, following the procedure the contract specifies. Cancellation is not accomplished by announcing an intention, by ceasing to perform, or by instructing escrow.

Where a cancellation is disputed, escrow's position is instructive: escrow may act only on **mutual** written instructions. Where the parties disagree, escrow stops. It does not decide between them, and it cannot release the deposit on one party's say-so.

### 6.2 The deposit in a disputed cancellation

Buyers and sellers both assume the deposit follows automatically from who was right. It does not. Escrow holds pending mutual instruction or a resolution through the contract's dispute resolution provisions or the courts.

The licensee's role is to explain the mechanism, not to predict the outcome or to advise on entitlement — that is a legal question.

### 6.3 Mediation and arbitration

The contract's dispute resolution provisions are substantive rights, and they are frequently initialed without explanation. A licensee should ensure the client understands, at minimum, that mediation is generally a required first step before other remedies, and that an arbitration provision, where agreed, affects how disputes will be resolved and what review is available.

Where a client asks what the provisions mean for their position, that is a question for counsel.

### 6.4 What actually prevents disputes

Most transaction disputes are not about substance. They are about **whether something was delivered, to whom, and when** — which is why the practices that prevent them are documentary rather than interpersonal:

1. Build the deadline calendar from the contract and share it.
2. Deliver notices by the authorized method and keep the evidence.
3. Confirm materially important conversations in writing.
4. Amend in writing, signed, and deliver to escrow.
5. Correct errors immediately, in writing, to everyone who received the original.
6. Keep superseded drafts, so the sequence of versions shows how terms changed.
7. Audit the file at closing while memories are fresh.

### 6.5 A worked file audit

> A closed file contains: an email agreeing to move the closing date, with no signed amendment; a contingency removal that is signed but undated; a transfer disclosure with two items left blank; and an agency confirmation naming a relationship different from the disclosure earlier in the file.
>
> **Assessment.** Four defects, and they are not equivalent. The agency contradiction is the most serious, because it suggests the brokerage acted on both sides without documented consent. The undated removal is next: rights ran from it and the file cannot show when. The unsigned date change means the operative contract still says the original date. The blanks make the disclosure ambiguous about whether the items were addressed.
>
> **The correct response.** Record each finding, escalate to the broker, and obtain what legitimately exists. **What must not happen** is dating the removal now, completing the blanks now, or producing an amendment now with a matching date. A documented gap is survivable. A backdated document is falsification, and it converts a compliance problem into conduct that ends a career.

### 6.6 A second audit — the transaction that did not close

> **The file.** A purchase cancelled twenty-two days after acceptance. The buyer wants his deposit. The seller says the buyer defaulted. The file contains:
>
> - **(a)** The purchase agreement, fully executed, acceptance communicated by email on a Friday afternoon.
> - **(b)** An inspection report dated day twelve.
> - **(c)** A signed investigation contingency removal, **undated**, in the file with no transmittal.
> - **(d)** A text from the buyer's agent to the listing agent on day sixteen: "we're good on inspection."
> - **(e)** A request for repairs sent on day eighteen — **after** items (c) and (d).
> - **(f)** A cancellation signed by the buyer on day twenty-two, citing the loan contingency, with the loan contingency period stated in the agreement as twenty-one days.
> - **(g)** No delivery evidence for the seller's disclosures.
>
> **Ranked by consequence to the deposit:**
>
> **1. (g) The missing delivery evidence.** Rights run from delivery, and a file that cannot show when the disclosures reached the buyer cannot show that the buyer's response period ever started — **which may mean it never closed.** This item is first because it potentially changes the answer to everything below it, and it is the item nobody looks at because it is not about the cancellation.
>
> **2. (c) The undated removal.** The single most consequential defect in the file's own terms. **The contingency either was or was not removed before the events that followed, and the document cannot say.** Section 5.3 and Lesson 03's first worked audit both rank an undated removal near the top for exactly this reason.
>
> **3. (f) The cancellation timing.** Cited on day twenty-two under a contingency stated as twenty-one days. **Whether the period had expired depends on the day-zero and counting questions in Section 5.7**, which is why those questions are not clerical.
>
> **4. (e) The repair request after the removal.** A request made after the contingency was apparently removed. It may have been a negotiation the seller was free to decline, or it may indicate the parties did not believe the contingency had been removed. **It is evidence about what everyone understood**, and it points in a different direction from item (c).
>
> **5. (d) The text.** Informal, ambiguous, and now evidence. "We're good on inspection" is not a removal and was probably not intended as one, **but it is dated, which is more than the removal is.**
>
> **6. (b) The inspection at day twelve.** Not a defect. It is here because it establishes the timeline the other items sit on.
>
> **What the licensees do now.** Preserve everything including the texts. Escalate to both brokers immediately. **Deposit disposition is a contractual and legal question with a defined procedure**, and it is not resolved by the licensees' view of who was right. Section 6.2 governs and counsel decides.
>
> **What must not happen:** dating the removal now, producing a delivery receipt now, or characterizing the text as a removal in a communication to the other side. Each is Lesson 03's manufactured document.
>
> **The lesson the brokerage should take.** Four of these six items are the same failure: **a document exists and its date does not.** A transaction management process that refuses to accept an undated document — and that records delivery as an event rather than assuming it — would have prevented all four.

### 6.7 Checkpoint

You should be able to state how a term of the contract is changed, what escrow may do when the parties disagree, and why an undated notice is a defect even when everyone remembers what happened.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **Purchase agreement** | The operative contract of the transaction. Everything after it — escrow instructions, lender documents, disclosures, notices — implements it or reports on it. |
| **Conflict with escrow instructions** | Resolved in favor of **the parties' agreement**, reconciled by the parties in writing. Escrow does not decide. |
| **Deposit (earnest money)** | The fund signaling commitment and from which a seller's remedy is typically drawn on buyer default. |
| **Liquidated damages** | An amount agreed in advance as the seller's recovery on default. Must be properly agreed, and its enforceability is constrained by law. Not a penalty the seller may simply impose. |
| **Naming what conveys** | Listing appliances, window coverings, mounted televisions, chandeliers, sheds, and smart-home equipment expressly, so the fixture tests never have to be applied after the fact. |
| **Offer** | A proposal that, if accepted without change, forms a contract. |
| **Mirror-image acceptance** | An acceptance must match the offer. Any change to a material term is not an acceptance. |
| **Communication of acceptance** | Acceptance must reach the offeror in the manner contemplated. A signed acceptance in a drawer has not been communicated. |
| **Counteroffer** | A **rejection** of the original offer and the substitution of a new one. The original is gone and cannot be accepted unless renewed. |
| **Revocation** | Withdrawal of an offer before acceptance, generally permitted even where the offer states a deadline — unless supported by consideration as an option. |
| **Seller's final election** | The provision making a seller's obligation contingent on subsequent written acceptance of the buyer's acceptance. The protection when countering multiple offers. |
| **Backup offer** | A contract that becomes primary if the first transaction fails, in the position and on the terms agreed. Must be documented; "next in line" without one is nothing. |
| **Contingency** | A condition on a party's obligation **inside a formed contract**. Not a delay of formation, not a general right to cancel, not a transfer of responsibility. |
| **Removal in writing** | Under the standard California residential form, how a contingency ends. Contingencies **do not expire automatically** when the date passes. |
| **Notice to buyer to perform** | The seller's route when a date passes without removal: a contractual notice starting a defined period, after which the seller may cancel per the contract. |
| **Waiver** | Giving up a contingency. Removes the contractual path to act on what is found; does not prevent the buyer from investigating. |
| **Counting of periods** | How the contract defines time — calendar or business days, whether the triggering day counts, weekend and holiday rolls, and the time of day a deadline falls. |
| **Authorized delivery method** | The method the contract specifies for notices. A text message is not delivery unless the contract makes it so. |
| **Undated notice** | A notice with no proof of timing — and timing is what disputes are about. |
| **Amendment** | A change to a contract term, made by **writing signed by both parties** and delivered to escrow. Agent emails, verbal agreements, and escrow instructions alone do not amend. |
| **Cancellation** | Exercising a right the contract provides, by the procedure it specifies. Not accomplished by announcing an intention, ceasing to perform, or instructing escrow. |
| **Mutual instruction** | The only basis on which escrow may act where parties disagree. Escrow stops; it does not decide, and it cannot release a deposit on one party's say-so. |

## Practice problems

### Problem 1

A buyer offers $880,000 with a 21-day investigation period and a March 30 closing. The seller strikes
the price and writes $895,000, changes closing to March 20, signs, and returns it. The buyer signs
it, but crosses out March 20 and writes March 25 before sending it back. The seller then says he no
longer wants to sell at $895,000 and would accept the original $880,000 offer instead.

**Trace the formation. Is there a contract, and can the seller accept the original offer?**

> **Solution.** No contract, and no — the original offer is gone.
>
> The seller's changes were a **counteroffer**, which rejected the $880,000 offer and substituted a
> new one. That is not an "acceptance with modifications" forming a contract on the seller's terms,
> and it does not suspend the original offer leaving it available later. The original cannot be
> accepted unless the buyer renews it.
>
> The buyer's signature with March 25 written on it is a **further counteroffer**, however small the
> change. Signing does not convert a modification into an acceptance.
>
> Where the parties stand: the buyer's counteroffer at $895,000 / March 25 is outstanding. The seller
> may accept it, reject it, or counter again — or ask the buyer to renew the original $880,000 terms,
> which the buyer is free to decline.

### Problem 2

A buyer's offer states it is open for acceptance until 5:00 p.m. Friday. On Wednesday the buyer finds
another property and withdraws it. On Thursday the seller signs the original offer and puts it in a
file; his agent emails the signed copy to the buyer's agent on Friday morning.

**Was a contract formed?**

> **Solution.** No, on two independent grounds.
>
> **The offer was revoked before acceptance.** An offer may generally be revoked before acceptance
> even where it states a deadline, unless supported by consideration as an option. A stated expiration
> sets an outer limit; it does not make the offer irrevocable in the meantime.
>
> **And acceptance was not communicated on Thursday.** A signed acceptance sitting in a file has not
> been communicated to the offeror — by Friday there was nothing left to accept.
>
> **What transfers, in both directions:** a buyer relying on "open until Friday" should know it can be
> withdrawn Wednesday, and a seller intending to accept should not assume the offer will still be
> there.

### Problem 3

A seller receives four offers. His agent counters three of them at $940,000 without any further
provision. Two buyers accept.

**What has happened, and what should have been done?**

> **Solution.** The seller may now be **exposed on two contracts for one property**, which is the
> hazard countering multiple offers creates.
>
> The customary protection is a provision making the seller's obligation contingent on the seller's
> **subsequent written acceptance of the buyer's acceptance** — preserving a final election. A
> licensee who counters multiple offers without it has exposed the client.
>
> Worth stating alongside it, because buyers assume otherwise: the seller chooses, there is no
> obligation to accept the highest offer, and there is no priority by order of receipt.

### Problem 4

A seller instructs his agent to tell competing buyers there are three offers on the table. There is
one. Separately, a buyer's agent asks the listing agent to reveal the competing offer's price, and
the listing agent declines without asking the seller.

**Assess both.**

> **Solution.** These fail in **opposite directions**, and the licensee must satisfy both duties at
> once.
>
> **The seller's instruction must be refused.** Saying there are multiple offers when there are not is
> a misrepresentation, and a client's instruction cannot authorize one. Inventing competition breaches
> the duty of honesty owed to the buyer. The same is true of describing an anticipated offer as an
> existing one.
>
> **Declining to disclose the competing price is permissible** — whether competing terms are disclosed
> is the seller's decision, not a right the competing buyers hold. But the agent should route it: the
> call belongs to the seller. Disclosing terms **without** the seller's authorization would breach
> confidentiality owed to the seller.
>
> **What transfers:** permissible silence and affirmative falsehood are not near neighbors. One is the
> seller's prerogative; the other ends careers.

### Problem 5

The buyer's investigation contingency period ends on the 17th. Nothing is delivered. On the 18th the
seller's agent tells the seller the contingency expired, the buyer is now committed, and the seller
may accept a better offer that arrived overnight.

**Correct each statement.**

> **Solution.** All three are wrong, and they are the most confidently repeated errors in California
> residential practice.
>
> **The contingency did not expire.** Under the standard form, contingencies remain in effect until
> the buyer **removes them in writing**. They are not self-executing on a date.
>
> **The buyer is not committed.** He has not removed anything, so the protection is intact.
>
> **The seller is not free to sell elsewhere.** The seller's route is to deliver the **notice the
> contract requires**, which starts the period the contract specifies. If the buyer removes during
> that period, the notice is satisfied and the transaction continues; if he does not, the seller may
> cancel per the contract's procedure.
>
> What is not true at any point: that the contract became void, that the buyer automatically became
> committed, or that the seller could cancel on the 18th without notice.
>
> **The durable habit, more useful than the rule:** read the contract's own removal mechanism.
> Different forms and negotiated terms operate differently, and the contract in front of you governs.

### Problem 6

A buyer's loan is denied while the loan contingency is in effect. He tells the listing agent by phone,
emails escrow asking for his deposit back, and stops responding to requests for documents.

**What has he done wrong, and what does the deposit dispute look like?**

> **Solution.** He had a **path** and did not follow it. A contingency provides a route with
> requirements: the written notice the contract specifies, within its terms, with any supporting
> documentation.
>
> Each of his three acts forfeits protection rather than exercising it. A verbal notification to the
> other agent is not the contractual notice. A unilateral instruction to escrow is not cancellation —
> escrow may act only on **mutual** written instructions, and where the parties disagree it stops. And
> simply ceasing to perform invites the seller to cancel for non-performance instead, which is a
> materially worse position.
>
> On the deposit: it does not follow automatically from who was right. Escrow holds pending mutual
> instruction or resolution through the contract's dispute resolution provisions or the courts. The
> licensee explains that mechanism and does **not** predict the outcome or advise on entitlement,
> which is a legal question.

### Problem 7

The parties agree during escrow to move closing back one week. The agents confirm by email, escrow is
told, and everyone proceeds. The transaction later fails and the parties dispute who breached.

**What is the operative closing date?**

> **Solution.** **The original one.** A change to a contract term is made by written amendment signed
> by both parties and delivered to escrow so the instructions match the agreement.
>
> An email between the agents does not amend the contract — the agents are not the parties. Neither
> does a verbal agreement between the parties, an instruction to escrow alone, or a shared
> understanding that a short extension "won't matter."
>
> The parties are now litigating on a contract whose closing date is the original one, with an email
> chain between non-parties as the evidence of a change.
>
> **What transfers:** time is a term. A licensee who treats a closing date as a scheduling preference
> will eventually explain to a client why the other party was entitled to act.

### Problem 8

A closed file contains: an email agreeing to move the closing date with no signed amendment; a
contingency removal that is signed but undated; a transfer disclosure with two items blank; and an
agency confirmation naming a relationship different from the disclosure earlier in the file.

**Rank the defects and state the correct response.**

> **Solution.** **The agency contradiction is the most serious**, and different in kind: it suggests
> the brokerage acted on both sides without documented consent — a breach of duties actually owed
> rather than a paperwork gap.
>
> **The undated removal is next.** Rights ran from it, and the file cannot show when. An undated
> notice is a notice with no proof of timing, and timing is what disputes are about — even when
> everyone remembers what happened.
>
> **The unsigned date change** means the operative contract still says the original date. **The blanks**
> leave the disclosure ambiguous about whether those items were addressed at all.
>
> **The response:** record each finding, escalate to the broker, and obtain what legitimately exists.
> What must **not** happen is dating the removal now, completing the blanks now, or producing an
> amendment now with a matching date. A documented gap is survivable; a backdated document is
> falsification, and it converts a compliance problem into conduct that ends a career.

## Chapter summary

**The purchase agreement is a contract to be understood, not a form to be filled.** Where escrow's
instructions conflict with it, the parties' agreement governs, reconciled by the parties in writing.

**Learn the functional components** — parties and property, price and deposit, financing, escrow and
title, cost allocation, contingencies, disclosures, investigation rights, repairs, closing and
possession, notices, default and remedies, dispute resolution, agency confirmation — rather than a
form's paragraph numbers.

**Name what conveys** so the fixture tests never have to be applied after the fact.

**Three formation rules dispose of most questions:** an offer accepted without change forms a
contract; an acceptance must match the offer; acceptance must be communicated.

**A counteroffer rejects and replaces.** The original offer is gone, not suspended, and a signature
with a change written on it is a further counteroffer however small the change.

**An offer may generally be revoked before acceptance**, even one stating a deadline.

**Counter multiple offers only with the seller's final election preserved**, or the seller may end up
bound twice.

**Declining to disclose competing terms is the seller's prerogative; inventing them is a
misrepresentation** — and a client's instruction cannot authorize one. Disclosing without the
seller's authority breaches the other duty. Both must be satisfied at once.

**A contingency conditions an obligation inside a formed contract**, and under the standard
California form it ends only by **written removal**. Dates are not self-executing; the seller's route
is the contractual notice to perform.

**Waiver removes the path, not the ability to investigate**, and the exposure should be put in writing
before the buyer decides.

**Build the deadline calendar from the contract**, read its own definitions of how time is counted,
deliver notices by the authorized method, and keep the evidence.

**Amend in writing, signed, and deliver to escrow.** Cancel by the contract's procedure. Escrow acts
only on mutual instruction and never decides between the parties.

**Most disputes are about whether something was delivered, to whom, and when** — which is why the
practices that prevent them are documentary rather than interpersonal.
