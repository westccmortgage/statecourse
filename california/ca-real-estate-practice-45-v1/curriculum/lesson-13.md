## 1. Honesty and fair dealing

### 1.1 The duty that runs to everyone

Fiduciary duties run to the principal. **Honesty and fair dealing run to everyone** — the other party, their agent, lenders, escrow, appraisers, and the public.

That is why "I don't represent them" answers the wrong question. It correctly identifies whose interests the licensee advances. It says nothing about whether the licensee may mislead them.

### 1.2 The three categories

| | Standard | Distinguishing feature |
|---|---|---|
| **Innocent error** | A mistake made with reasonable care | Reasonable basis existed; care was used |
| **Negligent misrepresentation** | A statement made without reasonable basis or reasonable care | **The care taken**, not the intent |
| **Fraud** | A knowing misstatement or concealment, intended to be relied on | **Intent to deceive** |

The distinction that matters daily is the first two. Most claims against licensees are **negligence** claims, and negligence does not require any dishonesty at all. A licensee who repeats a square footage figure from a prior listing without verifying or attributing it has not lied — and may still have made a negligent misrepresentation.

### 1.3 The three categories of statement

The practical discipline, introduced in Lesson 01 and applied throughout:

1. **Verified fact** — stated with its source.
2. **Unverified report** — attributed and flagged as unverified.
3. **Professional opinion** — identified as opinion.

Nearly every misrepresentation in residential practice is a category collapse: an unverified report delivered as a fact, or an opinion delivered as a certainty.

### 1.4 Concealment is a form of misrepresentation

Saying nothing about a known material fact is not neutral. Where a duty to disclose exists, silence is concealment, and careful silence — avoiding an affirmative false statement while ensuring the fact is not discovered — is worse rather than better, because it demonstrates awareness.

### 1.5 Predictions

A prediction stated as a certainty is actionable:

| Statement | Assessment |
|---|---|
| "This will definitely appreciate over the next two years." | A future outcome stated as fact, with no basis to guarantee it |
| "Property in this area has appreciated in recent years; here is the data. Past performance doesn't predict the future." | Accurate, sourced, and appropriately qualified |
| "The city will approve your second unit." | A prediction about a discretionary decision |
| "Here is the planning department's process and contact; they can tell you what applies." | Correct |

The defect is not optimism. It is stating as certain something the licensee cannot know, on which the client will rely.

### 1.6 The pressures that produce dishonesty

Almost nobody enters this business intending to misrepresent anything. **The misrepresentations that occur are produced by a small number of recurring pressures**, and a licensee who can name the pressure he is under is much better placed to resist it.

| The pressure | The statement it produces |
|---|---|
| **A transaction about to fail** | "It'll appraise." "The loan is fine." "They'll approve it." |
| **A client who wants reassurance** | "Don't worry, that's normal for a house this age." |
| **Not knowing the answer in front of a client** | A confident guess, offered because "I'll find out" feels like weakness |
| **A number needed to make a deal work** | A square footage, a tax figure, a fee "usually around" an amount |
| **A colleague or manager applying pressure** | Participation in something the licensee would not initiate |
| **Money the licensee needs this month** | Every item above, with the volume turned up |
| **An error already made** | The second statement, covering the first — **and this is the one that ends careers** |

**Two observations about this table.**

**The first six rows produce misstatements about the transaction. The last row produces misstatements about the licensee**, and Section 6.6 explains why that difference is the whole difference. A licensee who told a buyer something inaccurate has a correctable problem. A licensee who then adjusts a record so the inaccuracy is not visible has a different kind of problem entirely.

**The sixth row is the one Lesson 02's business plan addresses.** A licensee with three months of reserves and a licensee with none face the same client conversation, and they do not face it the same way. **Financial pressure is a compliance risk factor**, which is an unusual sentence in an ethics chapter and a true one.

**The defenses are not attitudes. They are sentences prepared in advance:**

- **"I don't know — let me find out and get back to you today."** Solves rows three and four permanently, and clients respond to it far better than licensees expect.
- **"That's a question for your inspector."** And for the lender, the tax advisor, the attorney, the title officer. Section 1.3's category discipline, in its practical form.
- **"I can't predict that, and here's what I can tell you."** Row one, answered without abandoning the client.
- **"I'm not comfortable with that — let's take it to the broker."** Row five, and it does not require confrontation.
- **"I made a mistake. Here's what happened."** Row seven, said early, when it is still a small sentence.

> **The pattern worth internalizing:** every one of those is shorter than the misstatement it replaces. **Honesty in this business is almost always the less effortful option in the moment and always the less effortful option afterward.**

## 2. Errors, omissions, and supervision

### 2.1 The correction obligation

A licensee who discovers an error in something they said or produced must correct it **immediately, in writing, to everyone who received the original** — and, critically, **before the recipient acts on it**, if that is still possible.

The instinct to wait and see whether the error matters is the instinct that converts a mistake into a misrepresentation. An error you know about and leave uncorrected is no longer an error.

### 2.2 The worked case

> A licensee realizes she transposed two digits reporting square footage. The buyer relied on it in setting his offer. Escrow closes in four days. Her manager suggests waiting: the buyer has an inspection report, the figure came from a public record, and raising it now may kill the transaction.
>
> **The correct action:** correct the figure in writing to both sides immediately, notify her broker, and let the buyer decide what to do with accurate information while he still can.
>
> **Why each alternative fails:**
>
> - *Say nothing because it came from a public record.* The source does not matter once she knows the figure she gave was wrong.
> - *Say nothing because the buyer had his own opportunity to verify.* The buyer's diligence does not discharge her duty.
> - *Wait and offer a credit if he raises it.* Arrives after the decision it should have informed.
> - *Correct it verbally to the other agent.* Not to the person relying on it, and no record.
>
> **The manager's advice is the problem, not the guidance.** A licensee told to conceal a known error should escalate above the person who told her.

### 2.3 What supervision looks like from below

Licensees encounter supervision failures from the receiving end. Three situations and their correct responses:

| Situation | Response |
|---|---|
| A manager tells you to conceal an error | Escalate to the responsible broker; the instruction does not protect you |
| Office practice deviates from the written policy | Raise it; a policy nobody follows is not a defense for anyone |
| You are asked to handle work beyond your competence with no support | Say so in writing and ask for supervision or reassignment |

Following an improper instruction and documenting that it came from someone else does **not** transfer responsibility for the act. It documents that you knew.

### 2.4 Errors and omissions coverage

E&O responds to claims arising from professional services, **within its terms and limits**, and is commonly conditioned on **prompt notice of a potential claim**.

Three consequences:

1. **Late reporting can forfeit coverage.** A licensee who handles a brewing problem quietly for three months may have destroyed the coverage that would have handled it.
2. **It does not transfer responsibility** for the underlying conduct.
3. **Intentional acts are generally excluded.** Coverage protects against mistakes, not against decisions to deceive.

E&O is also not a substitute for supervision and documented procedures — it is what responds when those fail.

### 2.5 What to report, and when

Report to the broker promptly:

- Any error affecting a client's decision or a transaction's terms.
- Any client expression of dissatisfaction that could become a claim.
- Any allegation of misrepresentation, discrimination, or mishandling.
- Any request from another party's counsel.
- Any regulatory contact.
- Any suspected fraud.
- Any wire fraud incident or near-miss.

The bias should be toward reporting. A report that turns out to be unnecessary costs a conversation. An unreported matter that becomes a claim costs the coverage and the credibility.

### 2.6 Errors made by other people

Section 2.1 addresses the licensee's own errors. A large share of what a licensee encounters is somebody else's — the other agent's, escrow's, the lender's, a vendor's, or his own client's — and the handling differs by whose it is.

**Another licensee's error.** Raise it directly and promptly, with the fact rather than a characterization: "the removal in the file is undated" rather than "you didn't do your job." **Most are genuine mistakes and are fixed in a phone call.** Where it is not fixed, or where it affects the licensee's own client's rights, it goes to the broker — **and the licensee documents that he raised it and what the response was.**

**A service provider's error.** Escrow's figure, the lender's disclosure, the title company's report, an inspector's report with the wrong address. Raise it in writing, to the provider, with the supporting document. **Do not correct it yourself on a document that is not yours**, and do not tell the client "it's fine, they'll fix it" until they have.

**The client's own error.** A client who has stated something inaccurate — about a repair, an income figure, an occupancy intention, the condition of the property. **This is the most delicate category and the most consequential.** The licensee's obligation is not to conceal it and not to accuse. He tells the client what the document says, asks, and — where the answer confirms a material inaccuracy in something already submitted — **tells the client it must be corrected and escalates if it is not.** Lesson 09's worked walkthrough and Lesson 14 of the mortgage course both address what happens when it is a contradiction rather than an ambiguity.

**An error nobody has noticed but you.** The hardest case, because raising it creates work and delay for people who are content. **It is raised anyway.** A licensee who sees a defect and says nothing has joined it, and "it wasn't mine" is not a position the record will support once it shows he knew.

**Three rules across all four categories:**

1. **In writing, promptly, factually.** Sections 6.1 and 6.3 supply the form.
2. **To your broker whenever it affects your client's rights**, and always when the response is inadequate.
3. **Never fix another party's document.** Not to be helpful, not to save a closing. **The correction belongs to whoever authored it**, and a licensee who edits somebody else's record has created the problem Section 6.6 describes.

> **What must not happen:** a private accommodation between two agents to "just not put that in the file." That is the beginning of the only category of conduct this course treats as unsurvivable, and it always begins as a favor.

## 3. Fraud warning signs

### 3.1 Fraud presents as a pattern

The most useful thing a licensee can learn about fraud is that it rarely presents as one obvious falsehood. It presents as **a pattern of small inconsistencies**, each individually explainable.

That is why the correct response to "something doesn't add up but no single item is clearly false" is to document the inconsistencies, resolve them with evidence, and escalate if they cannot be resolved. Proceeding because suspicion is not proof is how licensees end up as the documented participant in someone else's scheme.

### 3.2 Patterns worth recognizing

| Pattern | What it may indicate |
|---|---|
| Pressure to close unusually fast, combined with changing parties, funding sources, or documents | The urgency exists to defeat verification |
| A buyer or seller who never appears, acting only through an intermediary | Identity or authority problems |
| Identification that does not match other documents | Identity fraud |
| A sale price materially inconsistent with the property and the market, with no explanation | Value or flipping fraud |
| Funds arriving from parties unconnected to the transaction | Money laundering exposure |
| An occupancy statement contradicted by other documents in the same file | Occupancy fraud |
| Requests to alter, backdate, or omit documents | Direct participation request |
| A seller who cannot produce evidence of authority to convey | Title or authority fraud |
| Instructions to send funds somewhere unexpected, or changed instructions | Wire fraud |

### 3.3 What is not suspicious

Equally important, so that scrutiny is not applied selectively — which is its own problem:

- An aggressive negotiator.
- A seller who insists on attorney review before signing.
- A buyer and seller who already know each other.
- Income that is variable, commission-based, or self-employed.
- A buyer whose income has risen substantially.
- A consumer who asks a lot of questions.

Applying heightened scrutiny based on characteristics rather than on the pattern above is where fair housing problems begin.

### 3.4 The occupancy contradiction

> A file contains an application with owner-occupancy stated, **and a lease for the same property dated a month earlier.**
>
> This is different in kind from the other defects a file audit finds. It is not a missing document or a late delivery — the file itself contains the evidence contradicting a material representation to the lender.
>
> The correct response is escalation, not a judgment call about whether it matters or whether the borrower could afford the payment anyway.

### 3.5 Wire fraud

Covered mechanically in Lesson 02 and at closing in Lesson 11; the ethical point belongs here. The single most valuable protective act available to a licensee is a **written warning to the client at the start of escrow**: wire instructions will never be changed by email, and you must call to verify at a number you obtained independently.

Sent early, it costs nothing and prevents the largest single loss a residential client can suffer. Sent the day funds move, it arrives after the attacker has already established the thread.

### 3.6 What a licensee must not do

- Do not investigate suspected fraud alone.
- Do not confront a suspected participant.
- Do not warn a suspected participant that concerns have been raised.
- Do not withdraw quietly and let the next licensee face the same file blind.
- Do not participate, however small the requested act.

Escalate to the broker, who determines what reporting is required.

### 3.7 The escalation, written

Section 3.6 states what a licensee must not do when he suspects fraud. This section supplies what he does instead, in the form it must take.

**The escalation is written, prompt, factual, and addressed to the responsible broker.** Each of those four words is doing work.

**Written**, because an oral report leaves the licensee with nothing showing he made it, and because the broker needs the facts in a form that can be forwarded.

**Prompt**, because delay is itself a fact that will be examined, and because the transaction is moving.

**Factual**, which is the part licensees get wrong. **The memorandum states observations, not conclusions.**

| Do not write | Write instead |
|---|---|
| "The buyer is committing occupancy fraud." | "The application states owner occupancy. The file contains a lease for the property signed by the buyer as landlord, dated eleven days before the application." |
| "The seller is hiding something." | "The seller stated on [date] that the ceiling stain was from a repaired leak in 2019. The disclosure does not mention it. I recorded the statement in my note of that date." |
| "This vendor seems shady." | "The invoice is for work at a different address. The vendor's phone number matches the one on the buyer's employer verification." |
| "Something feels wrong." | The specific facts that produced the feeling, listed, with dates. |

**Why the distinction matters.** A conclusion is the licensee's opinion, and stating it can expose him — to a defamation claim, to an accusation of bad faith, and to being wrong. **A fact is a fact.** The broker, and whoever the broker involves, draws the conclusion.

**What the memorandum contains:**

1. **The transaction and the parties**, identified.
2. **What was observed**, each item with its date and its source — a document, a statement, an observation.
3. **What the licensee did** — what he asked, whom he told, what he declined to do.
4. **What he is asking the broker to decide**, stated plainly. Usually: whether to proceed, and what to report.
5. **Nothing else.** No speculation, no theory of the scheme, no characterization of anyone's character.

**And what happens after it is sent.** The licensee does not investigate, does not warn the party, does not tell the other side, and does not quietly withdraw hoping it resolves itself. Section 3.6 lists each of those and the reasons hold. **The decision about reporting belongs to the broker**, and the licensee's role is complete when the facts are delivered and he has declined to participate.

> **One sentence that appears in enforcement matters repeatedly:** "I told my manager and he said to keep going." **Documenting an improper instruction does not transfer responsibility for the act.** If the answer to a written escalation is an instruction to do the thing, the licensee is back to Section 6.5 — this is now a question for counsel, and the licensee still does not perform the act.

## 4. Conflicts and referral disclosure

### 4.1 What creates a conflict

Any interest that could reasonably influence the licensee's advice:

- A referral fee or thing of value from a service provider.
- An ownership or financial interest, including a family member's, in a recommended provider.
- A bonus contingent on a sale or a price threshold.
- The licensee's own interest, or a relative's, in a property.
- Representation of another party with a competing interest.
- Any personal relationship a client would want to know about.

### 4.2 Disclosure must reach the client in time to matter

The rule is short: **disclose before the client relies on the advice the conflict affects.**

| Inadequate approach | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| "The client is free to choose someone else" | The client cannot weigh a recommendation without knowing what the recommender receives |
| "I'll disclose it if they ask" | The client does not know to ask |
| "I won't let it influence me" | A private intention is not something the client can evaluate |
| "I'll disclose after the offer is accepted" | Information after the decision has no value |
| "The fee comes from the seller, not the buyer's pocket" | Who pays does not change that the advice carries an interest |

### 4.3 Settlement service referrals

A specific and serious federal rule: **a licensee may not give or accept a fee, kickback, or thing of value for the referral of settlement service business**, and may not split a fee where no services were actually performed.

This reaches arrangements that feel like ordinary business courtesy — an escrow company paying for each client sent, a lender covering marketing costs in exchange for referrals, a home warranty company paying per policy sold.

**Disclosure does not cure this one.** Unlike an ordinary conflict, which disclosure manages, a payment for referrals is prohibited whether or not it is disclosed.

Where the brokerage has an ownership interest in a provider it refers to, that is an **affiliated business arrangement**, and it carries its own disclosure and conditions.

### 4.4 The licensee as principal

Where a licensee buys or sells for their own account, or for a relative or entity they have an interest in, the licensee's status must be disclosed. A licensee who purchases from a client, or who has an interest in a buyer of a client's property, is in the highest-risk position in the business, and it requires disclosure, the client's informed consent, and the broker's involvement.

### 4.5 Personal relationships

A licensee representing a client in a transaction where the other party is a friend or relative should disclose it. The disclosure is inexpensive; discovering it later is expensive, because the client will reinterpret every recommendation in light of it.

### 4.6 A conflicts inventory

Conflicts are managed transaction by transaction and they accumulate practice-wide. **A licensee who has never listed his own is nearly certain to miss one**, because the ones that get missed are the arrangements that have been in place long enough to feel like background.

**The inventory, reviewed annually and whenever anything changes:**

1. **Every vendor from whom you receive anything** — a fee, a lead, an event sponsorship, marketing materials, a meal budget, a co-branded mailer. **Value is value**, and Section 5.6 of Lesson 03 establishes that its form does not change the analysis.
2. **Every vendor to whom you send business without receiving anything**, listed anyway — because the referral relationship exists and because next year's version of it may include something.
3. **Every entity in which you or a family member holds an interest** that touches real estate transactions: a title or escrow interest, a warranty company, a contracting business, an investment entity that buys property.
4. **Every affiliated business arrangement** your brokerage has, whether or not you personally use it.
5. **Your own property holdings**, and those of immediate family, in the markets you serve.
6. **Client relationships that could collide** — two clients interested in the same property, a client who is also a vendor, a client who is a relative.
7. **Compensation arrangements that vary** by product, by property, by builder, or by outcome — bonuses, incentives, and anything a third party offers contingent on a result.
8. **Personal relationships with parties on the other side** of transactions you handle. Section 4.5 covers these; the inventory is where you notice you have four of them.

**What the inventory produces.** For each item: **when it must be disclosed, to whom, and in what form** — and the disclosure language, written once and reused, so that it is delivered the same way every time rather than improvised under pressure.

**Two entries deserve a standing rule rather than a case-by-case judgment.**

**Anything received from a settlement service provider** is disclosed at the point of recommendation, every time, without the licensee deciding whether it is large enough to matter. **The consumer decides what matters to him**, and Section 4.2's timing requirement makes the point unanswerable: disclosure must reach him while he can still act.

**Anything involving the licensee as principal** — Section 4.4 — is disclosed in its own document rather than folded into a paragraph, because it is the disclosure most likely to be later characterized as buried.

> **The value of an inventory is not the list. It is that a licensee reviewing one every year notices the arrangement that grew.** A vendor who bought lunch twice in 2023 and now sponsors a monthly event is a different relationship than he was, and nobody made a decision to change it.

## 5. Complaint response

### 5.1 The first response determines the trajectory

When a client alleges mishandling, the first response frequently determines whether the matter resolves or escalates. The correct sequence:

1. **Acknowledge promptly.** Silence reads as evasion.
2. **Notify the broker immediately.**
3. **Preserve everything.** All records relating to the matter, including drafts, texts, and emails.
4. **Respond factually**, without admitting or denying liability before the file has been reviewed.
5. **Do not offer a settlement or a fee reduction** on your own initiative.
6. **Report to the carrier** where the matter could become a claim.

### 5.2 Why the wrong responses are wrong

| Response | Problem |
|---|---|
| An immediate detailed defense | Commits a position before anyone has reviewed the file; frequently wrong on details |
| An apology accepting fault | May be treated as an admission and can compromise coverage |
| A fee reduction to make it go away | Commits the brokerage, may be read as an admission, rarely ends the matter |
| Waiting for a formal written demand | The matter hardens; coverage notice may be late |
| Handling it quietly without telling the broker | The supervision failure now compounds the original problem |

### 5.3 Preservation

The moment a complaint is foreseeable, preservation obligations attach in practice. Nothing is deleted — not texts, not drafts, not the email that reads badly.

Deleting a record that reflects poorly is far worse than the record. It converts a defensible dispute about professional judgment into a question about integrity, and it is generally discoverable anyway.

### 5.4 When a regulator makes contact

Contact from the Department is a matter for the broker, and usually for counsel. A licensee's obligations are to notify the broker immediately, preserve records, and not respond independently or informally.

Cooperation is expected. Improvised cooperation without review is where licensees make statements they cannot support.

### 5.5 Complaints that are not about you

A consumer who reports that another brokerage discriminated against them should be taken seriously:

- **Record what they report**, without characterizing it as established.
- **Inform them that enforcement agencies accept complaints**, and how.
- **Notify your broker.**

What not to do: investigate the other brokerage, tell the consumer such complaints rarely succeed, or take no action because the conduct was someone else's.

### 5.6 A worked complaint

> **The message**, received eleven weeks after a closing, from a buyer: *"We found out the addition was never permitted. The listing said 4 bedrooms. Our contractor says the whole thing has to be redone to be legal. You knew or should have known. We want you to make this right or we're filing with the DRE and calling a lawyer."*
>
> **The first hour.**
>
> **Do not reply yet.** Section 5.1 establishes that the first response sets the trajectory, and the reply written in the first hour is the one written by adrenaline.
>
> **Notify the broker immediately.** This message contains a demand, a threat of a regulatory filing, and an allegation about the licensee's own conduct. **It is a potential claim from this moment**, and Section 2.5's reporting obligation attaches now rather than when something is filed.
>
> **Preserve everything.** Section 5.3. The file, the emails, the texts, the listing service history including prior versions, the photographs, the advertising archive, the seller's disclosures, the licensee's own visual inspection note, and the delivery records. **Nothing is deleted, tidied, or organized in a way that changes it.** A text thread is exported today.
>
> **The reconstruction, before any response.** What did the licensee actually know and when? What did the listing say and where did the bedroom count come from — the public record, the prior listing, or the seller? **Section 1.2 of Lesson 06 required a source for every factual claim, and this is the moment that requirement pays or does not.** What did the seller disclose? What did the licensee's own note record? What did the buyer receive, and when?
>
> **The four outcomes this reconstruction can produce**, and they are genuinely different:
>
> **1. The source was the public record and the record says four bedrooms.** The claim is about the permitting history, which is a different question, and the file shows where the number came from.
>
> **2. The source was the seller and the licensee repeated it.** Weaker, and the file should show it was attributed. **This is the scenario Lesson 03's reasonable-care section warns about.**
>
> **3. The licensee knew the addition was unpermitted.** Then Section 4.3 of Lesson 09 governs and the exposure is real. **The response is the broker's and counsel's, and it is not a denial.**
>
> **4. Nobody can tell**, because the file does not record the source. **The most common outcome**, and the reason the requirement exists.
>
> **The response itself**, whatever the outcome: prompt, acknowledging the message and saying it is being reviewed seriously; **factual, not defensive**; free of admissions of legal conclusions and equally free of denials the file cannot support; and **sent with the broker's involvement.**
>
> **What must not happen:** a fast reassuring reply ("we had no idea, the seller told us"), an offer to pay for something, contact with the seller to coordinate a story, an edit to the listing archive, or a note added to the file today and left undated. **The last is the only one on this list that would end a career**, and it is the one that will feel most reasonable at eleven o'clock that night.

## 6. Documentation, escalation, and legal referral

### 6.1 Contemporaneous records win

A record created **as events occurred**, with dates and sources, carries weight a later reconstruction cannot. This is not a technicality about admissibility — it is about credibility. A note written before anyone knew there would be a dispute reads differently from an account assembled once the outcome was known.

Two clarifications, because licensees repeat both errors:

- A reconstruction is **not inadmissible**; it is simply weaker.
- Contemporaneous notes are **not privileged** from disclosure. Write them as though they will be read, because they may be.

### 6.2 What to document as a matter of habit

- Advice given, particularly where the client chose differently.
- Referrals made and the client's response.
- Material conversations, confirmed in writing.
- Observations from the visual inspection.
- Delivery of documents, with dates and method.
- Corrections issued.
- Instructions received that you declined to follow, and why.
- Escalations, and their outcomes.

### 6.3 How to write it

| Write | Not |
|---|---|
| "Recommended buyer obtain structural evaluation of the foundation crack before the 17th; buyer declined by text on the 12th." | "Discussed inspection issues with buyer." |
| "Brown staining approx. 3 ft across on NW bedroom ceiling; paint appears newer in that area." | "Possible old roof leak, appears repaired." |
| "Seller instructed that the drainage issue not be disclosed. Advised it must be. Escalated to broker 6/14." | "Seller was difficult about disclosure." |

Specific, dated, factual, and distinguishing observation from inference. Characterizations of people age badly; recorded facts do not.

### 6.4 When to escalate to the broker

Immediately, for: any suspected fraud; any allegation against the licensee or the brokerage; any unlawful instruction from a client; any error affecting a client's decision; any regulatory contact; any wire fraud incident or near-miss; any request to alter or backdate a record; any conflict the licensee is unsure how to handle.

The cost of over-escalating is a conversation. The cost of under-escalating is everything the brokerage would have done had it known.

### 6.5 When to refer to counsel

The trigger is **the nature of the question**, not the client's request, the stage of the dispute, or whose interests are exposed:

- The legal effect of documents, rights, or remedies.
- Whether a party may cancel, rescind, or refuse to perform.
- Entitlement to a deposit.
- The consequences of how title is taken.
- Boundary, easement, and title claims.
- Estate, probate, trust, and entity authority questions.
- Anything where the licensee's answer would be a legal conclusion.

Experience does not extend the scope of the license. A licensee with twenty years of practice is no more entitled to advise on the legal effect of a contract than one with two weeks.

### 6.6 The line that ends careers

Everything in this lesson is survivable — errors, complaints, claims, even regulatory findings — provided the licensee's conduct in response was honest.

What is not survivable is **falsifying the record**: creating a document after the fact and dating it to match, altering an executed document, deleting records that reflect badly, or removing surrounding documents so an inconsistency does not show.

> A documented gap is a compliance problem. A manufactured document is a question about integrity, and it converts a defensible situation into one that ends a career.

Corrections are made visibly, with the original preserved and the change attributable. A record that can be silently altered is not a record.

### 6.7 Reading your own file as an adversary would

Every documentation habit in this lesson answers one question: **what will this file look like to someone whose job is to find its weakness?** The habit that keeps the other habits alive is periodically taking that view deliberately.

**Pick a closed file and read it as opposing counsel would.** The questions are always the same:

1. **What is undated?** Every undated document is an opportunity, because its timing can be argued to whatever the adversary needs it to be.
2. **What is signed but not evidenced as delivered?** Section 5.2 of Lesson 09 — the gap between execution and receipt is where rights are contested.
3. **What is asserted with no source?** Numbers, conditions, representations about the property. **A claim with no source in the file is the licensee's own statement**, whatever its actual origin.
4. **Where does the file characterize rather than record?** "Everything went smoothly" and "buyer was happy" are the licensee's conclusions. **Facts survive cross-examination; characterizations invite it.**
5. **What is missing that would exist if the process had been followed?** An absence is evidence too, and the adversary will say so.
6. **Where do two documents disagree?** Names, dates, amounts, property descriptions. **Any inconsistency becomes the theme of the questioning**, regardless of how immaterial it is.
7. **What is in the file that should not be?** An unexecuted draft, a superseded figure without its context, a note written in frustration, a message that reads badly out of context. **Section 6.3's guidance about how to write it exists because everything written is eventually read by someone else.**
8. **What decisions does the file not explain?** A price, a strategy, a waiver, a removal. **A decision with no recorded reasoning looks like it had none.**

**Do this twice a year on a file chosen at random**, and the findings will be about the process rather than the file — which is the point. **A defect found in one closed file is present in every file that process produced.**

> **The reframe worth carrying:** documentation is not paperwork performed for a regulator. **It is the only version of events that will exist when the people involved have forgotten, moved, retired, or become adverse.** Everything in this course about dating, evidencing, sourcing, and recording reduces to that one fact — and a licensee who has read one of his own files this way stops needing to be told.

### 6.8 Checkpoint

You should be able to classify a statement as innocent error, negligent misrepresentation, or fraud; state the correct first response to a client complaint; and name the trigger that requires referral to counsel.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **Honesty and fair dealing** | The duties owed to **everyone** — the other party, their agent, lenders, escrow, appraisers, the public. Distinct from fiduciary duties, which run only to the principal. |
| **"I don't represent them"** | A correct answer to the question of whose interests you advance, and no answer at all to whether you may mislead them. |
| **Innocent error** | A mistake made with reasonable care on a reasonable basis. |
| **Negligent misrepresentation** | A statement made without reasonable basis or reasonable care. Turns on **the care taken**, not on intent — which is why most claims against licensees involve no dishonesty at all. |
| **Fraud** | A knowing misstatement or concealment intended to be relied on. Turns on **intent to deceive**. |
| **The three categories of statement** | Verified fact stated with its source; unverified report attributed and flagged; professional opinion identified as opinion. |
| **Category collapse** | Nearly every misrepresentation in residential practice: an unverified report delivered as a fact, or an opinion delivered as a certainty. |
| **Concealment** | Silence about a known material fact where a duty to disclose exists. Careful silence is worse rather than better, because it demonstrates awareness. |
| **Correction obligation** | Correcting a known error immediately, in writing, to everyone who received the original — and before the recipient acts on it, if that is still possible. |
| **"An error you leave uncorrected"** | No longer an error. Waiting to see whether a mistake matters is what converts it into a misrepresentation. |
| **Escalating above the instruction** | The response to a manager who tells you to conceal an error. Following an improper instruction and documenting its source does not transfer responsibility — it documents that you knew. |
| **Errors and omissions coverage** | Responds to claims from professional services within its terms and limits, commonly conditioned on **prompt notice**. Late reporting can forfeit it; intentional acts are generally excluded. |
| **Fraud as a pattern** | Fraud rarely presents as one obvious falsehood. It presents as small inconsistencies, each individually explainable. |
| **Selective scrutiny** | Applying heightened suspicion based on characteristics rather than the pattern. Where fair housing problems begin. |
| **Occupancy contradiction** | A file containing both an owner-occupancy representation and a lease for the same property. Different in kind from a missing document: the file itself contains the evidence contradicting a material representation to the lender. |
| **Conflict of interest** | Any interest that could reasonably influence the licensee's advice — a referral arrangement, an ownership interest including a relative's, a threshold bonus, a personal relationship. |
| **Disclosure in time to matter** | Before the client relies on the advice the conflict affects. Freedom to choose elsewhere, an intention not to be influenced, disclosing on request, and disclosing after acceptance are each inadequate. |
| **Settlement service referral prohibition** | No fee, kickback, or thing of value for referring settlement service business, and no fee split where no services were performed. **Disclosure does not cure it.** |
| **Affiliated business arrangement** | Where the brokerage has an ownership interest in a provider it refers to. Carries its own disclosure and conditions. |
| **Licensee as principal** | Buying or selling for one's own account, or for a relative or entity. The highest-risk position in the business; requires disclosure, informed consent, and the broker's involvement. |
| **Preservation** | The practical obligation attaching the moment a complaint is foreseeable. Nothing deleted — not texts, not drafts, not the email that reads badly. |
| **Contemporaneous record** | A record created as events occurred, with dates and sources. Weightier not as a technicality but as **credibility**: a note written before anyone knew there would be a dispute reads differently from one assembled afterwards. |
| **The trigger for counsel** | **The nature of the question** — not the client's request, the stage of the dispute, or whose interests are exposed. |
| **Falsification** | Creating a document after the fact and dating it to match, altering an executed document, deleting unfavorable records, or removing surrounding documents so an inconsistency does not show. |

## Practice problems

### Problem 1

Three licensees make three statements. (a) One repeats a square footage figure from a prior listing
without verifying or attributing it; it is wrong. (b) One tells a buyer "this will definitely
appreciate over the next two years." (c) One knows the seller concealed a drainage problem, says
nothing affirmatively false, and steers the buyer's attention away from the side yard during the
walkthrough.

**Classify each.**

> **Solution.** (a) **Negligent misrepresentation.** No lie was told and no dishonesty is required —
> the standard is **the care taken**, and repeating an unverified figure as fact is a category
> collapse: an unverified report delivered as verified fact. Most claims against licensees look
> exactly like this.
>
> (b) **A prediction stated as a certainty**, and actionable. The defect is not optimism but stating
> as certain something the licensee cannot know, on which the client will rely. The compliant version
> is "property in this area has appreciated in recent years; here is the data — past performance
> doesn't predict the future."
>
> (c) **Fraud territory — concealment with intent.** Where a duty to disclose exists, silence is
> concealment, and careful silence is **worse** rather than better, because avoiding an affirmative
> false statement while ensuring the fact is not discovered demonstrates awareness.

### Problem 2

A licensee realizes she transposed two digits reporting square footage. The buyer relied on it in
setting his offer. Escrow closes in four days. Her manager tells her to say nothing: the buyer has
his own inspection report, the figure came from a public record, and raising it now may kill the
transaction.

**What is the correct action, and why does each alternative fail?**

> **Solution.** **Correct the figure in writing to both sides immediately, notify the broker, and let
> the buyer decide what to do with accurate information while he still can.**
>
> *Say nothing because it came from a public record* — the source is irrelevant once she knows the
> figure she gave was wrong. *Say nothing because the buyer could have verified* — the buyer's
> diligence does not discharge her duty. *Wait and offer a credit if he raises it* — arrives after the
> decision it should have informed. *Correct it verbally to the other agent* — not to the person
> relying on it, and no record.
>
> **The manager's advice is the problem, not the guidance.** A licensee told to conceal a known error
> escalates **above** the person who told her. Following an improper instruction and documenting that
> it came from someone else does not transfer responsibility for the act; it documents that she knew.

### Problem 3

A licensee handles a brewing client complaint quietly for three months, hoping it resolves. It becomes
a claim. She assumes the brokerage's E&O policy will handle it, and separately assumes it covers her
decision to omit a known defect from a marketing sheet.

**Assess both assumptions.**

> **Solution.** Both are wrong, and the first is the more expensive.
>
> **E&O is commonly conditioned on prompt notice of a potential claim**, so three months of quiet
> handling may have **forfeited the coverage** that would otherwise have responded. The bias must run
> toward reporting: a report that turns out unnecessary costs a conversation; an unreported matter that
> becomes a claim costs the coverage and the credibility.
>
> **Intentional acts are generally excluded.** Coverage protects against mistakes, not against
> decisions to deceive — and it does not transfer responsibility for the underlying conduct in any
> event. E&O is what responds when supervision and documented procedures fail; it is not a substitute
> for them.
>
> The report-promptly list is worth memorizing: any error affecting a client's decision or a
> transaction's terms; any dissatisfaction that could become a claim; any allegation of
> misrepresentation, discrimination, or mishandling; any contact from another party's counsel; any
> regulatory contact; any suspected fraud; any wire fraud incident **or near-miss**.

### Problem 4

A transaction features: pressure to close in nine days, a buyer who never appears and acts through an
intermediary, funds arriving from a party unconnected to the transaction, and a purchase price well
above anything the market supports with no explanation offered. The listing agent notes the buyer also
negotiates aggressively, is self-employed with variable income, and asks a lot of questions.

**Separate the signals from the noise, and state the correct response.**

> **Solution.** **The first four are the pattern.** Urgency combined with changing parties or funding
> can exist to defeat verification; a principal who never appears raises identity and authority
> questions; unconnected funds are money laundering exposure; and a price materially inconsistent with
> the property and market with no explanation suggests value or flipping fraud.
>
> **The last three are noise** — and treating them as signals is its own problem. An aggressive
> negotiator, variable or self-employed income, and a consumer who asks many questions are not
> suspicious. Applying heightened scrutiny based on characteristics rather than the pattern is where
> **fair housing problems begin.**
>
> **The correct response:** document the inconsistencies, resolve them with evidence, and escalate if
> they cannot be resolved. Proceeding because "suspicion is not proof" is how a licensee becomes the
> documented participant in someone else's scheme.
>
> **What must not happen:** investigating alone, confronting the suspected participant, warning them
> that concerns have been raised, withdrawing quietly so the next licensee faces the file blind, or
> participating in any requested act however small. Escalate to the broker, who determines what
> reporting is required.

### Problem 5

A licensee recommends an escrow company that pays her brokerage $300 per file, refers buyers to a
home warranty provider her brother owns, and stands to receive a builder bonus if a particular home
sells above a threshold. She plans to mention all three after the offers are accepted, and notes that
clients are always free to use someone else.

**Assess each arrangement and each justification.**

> **Solution.** **The $300 per file is prohibited, not merely undisclosed.** A licensee may not give or
> accept a fee, kickback, or thing of value for referring settlement service business, or split a fee
> where no services were performed. **Disclosure does not cure this one** — unlike an ordinary conflict,
> which disclosure manages, payment for referrals is prohibited whether disclosed or not. The
> arrangement's resemblance to ordinary business courtesy is exactly why it catches people.
>
> **The brother's company and the builder bonus are conflicts requiring disclosure before the client
> relies on the advice they affect** — and if the brokerage has an ownership interest in a provider it
> refers to, that is an affiliated business arrangement with its own disclosure and conditions.
>
> **Both justifications fail.** "Free to choose someone else" — a client cannot weigh a recommendation
> without knowing what the recommender receives. "Disclose after acceptance" — information after the
> decision has no value. Three more that fail the same way: *I'll disclose if they ask* (they don't
> know to), *I won't let it influence me* (a private intention is not something a client can evaluate),
> and *the fee comes from the seller* (who pays does not change that the advice carries an interest).

### Problem 6

A client emails alleging the licensee mishandled a disclosure. The licensee immediately sends a
detailed six-paragraph defense, apologizes for "dropping the ball," offers to reduce her commission
by $2,000, deletes a text thread that reads badly, and decides to tell her broker if the client
escalates.

**Identify each error and give the correct sequence.**

> **Solution.** Five errors, and the deletion is categorically the worst.
>
> **The immediate detailed defense** commits a position before anyone has reviewed the file, and is
> frequently wrong on details. **The apology accepting fault** may be treated as an admission and can
> compromise coverage. **The fee reduction** commits the brokerage, may read as an admission, and
> rarely ends the matter. **Waiting to tell the broker** turns the original problem into a supervision
> failure compounding it.
>
> **The deletion converts a defensible dispute about professional judgment into a question about
> integrity** — and it is generally discoverable anyway. The moment a complaint is foreseeable,
> preservation attaches in practice: nothing is deleted, not texts, not drafts, not the email that
> reads badly.
>
> **The correct sequence:** acknowledge promptly, because silence reads as evasion; notify the broker
> immediately; preserve everything; respond factually without admitting or denying liability before
> the file is reviewed; offer no settlement on your own initiative; and report to the carrier where the
> matter could become a claim.

### Problem 7

A file note reads: "Discussed inspection issues with buyer. Seller was difficult about disclosure.
Possible old roof leak, appears repaired." A colleague adds that notes like this are protected from
disclosure anyway, and that anything missing can be written up accurately after the fact if a dispute
arises.

**Rewrite the note and correct both claims.**

> **Solution.** **Rewritten:** "Recommended buyer obtain structural evaluation of the foundation crack
> before the 17th; buyer declined by text on the 12th." — "Seller instructed that the drainage issue
> not be disclosed. Advised it must be. Escalated to broker 6/14." — "Brown staining approx. 3 ft
> across on NW bedroom ceiling; paint appears newer in that area."
>
> Specific, dated, factual, and separating observation from inference. **Characterizations of people
> age badly; recorded facts do not** — "seller was difficult" tells a later reader nothing and reads as
> hostility.
>
> **Both claims are wrong.** Contemporaneous notes are **not privileged** from disclosure — write them
> as though they will be read, because they may be. And a later reconstruction is **not inadmissible**;
> it is simply **weaker**. The advantage of a contemporaneous record is credibility rather than
> admissibility: a note written before anyone knew there would be a dispute reads differently from an
> account assembled once the outcome was known.

### Problem 8

Preparing for a Department inquiry, a licensee with twenty years of experience decides to answer the
investigator's questions informally by phone, tells a buyer he is "definitely entitled" to his deposit
back, and recreates a missing contingency removal with a matching date so the file is complete.

**Assess each.**

> **Solution.** **The informal response is wrong.** Contact from the Department is a matter for the
> broker and usually for counsel. The licensee's obligations are to notify the broker immediately,
> preserve records, and not respond independently or informally. Cooperation is expected —
> **improvised** cooperation without review is where licensees make statements they cannot support.
>
> **The deposit statement is a legal conclusion.** Entitlement to a deposit is squarely on the referral
> list, alongside the legal effect of documents, whether a party may cancel or refuse to perform, the
> consequences of how title is taken, boundary and easement claims, and estate, probate, trust, and
> entity authority questions. The trigger is **the nature of the question**, and **experience does not
> extend the scope of the license** — twenty years confers no more entitlement to advise on legal
> effect than two weeks.
>
> **The recreated document is the one that ends the career.** Everything else in this lesson is
> survivable — errors, complaints, claims, even regulatory findings — provided the conduct in response
> was honest. A documented gap is a compliance problem; a manufactured document is a question about
> integrity. Corrections are made **visibly**, with the original preserved and the change attributable.
> A record that can be silently altered is not a record.

## Chapter summary

**Fiduciary duties run to the principal; honesty and fair dealing run to everyone.** "I don't
represent them" answers the wrong question.

**Innocent error, negligent misrepresentation, and fraud** are separated by care and by intent — and
most claims are negligence claims requiring no dishonesty at all.

**Keep the three categories of statement distinct:** verified fact with its source, unverified report
attributed, opinion identified as opinion. Nearly every misrepresentation is a collapse between them.

**Silence about a known material fact is concealment**, and careful silence is worse rather than
better.

**Correct known errors immediately, in writing, to everyone who received the original**, before they
act on it. An error you leave uncorrected is no longer an error — and an instruction to conceal one is
escalated above the person who gave it.

**E&O responds within its terms, on prompt notice, and not to intentional acts.** Late reporting can
forfeit it.

**Fraud presents as a pattern of small explainable inconsistencies.** Document, resolve with evidence,
escalate — and do not apply scrutiny to characteristics instead of the pattern.

**Do not investigate, confront, warn, quietly withdraw, or participate.** Escalate to the broker.

**Disclose conflicts before the client relies on the advice they affect** — and note that a payment for
settlement service referrals is prohibited outright, which disclosure does not cure.

**Acting as principal, or opposite a friend or relative, requires disclosure, consent, and the
broker's involvement.**

**The first response to a complaint sets the trajectory:** acknowledge, notify the broker, preserve
everything, respond factually, commit nothing, notify the carrier. Deleting an unfavorable record is
worse than the record.

**Contemporaneous, specific, dated records win on credibility.** Notes are not privileged;
reconstructions are not inadmissible, only weaker.

**Refer on the nature of the question**, and remember that experience does not enlarge the license.

**Falsifying the record is the one thing that is not survivable.** Corrections are visible, originals
are preserved, and changes are attributable.
