## Learning objectives

- Distinguish innocent error, negligent misrepresentation, and fraud by the standard each applies.
- Correct a known error at the point where correction still has value to the person relying on it.
- Recognize the fact patterns that indicate transaction fraud and respond through escalation rather than judgment.
- Disclose conflicts of interest so that a client can evaluate the advice they affect.
- Respond to a complaint or claim without committing the brokerage before the file has been reviewed.
- Build the contemporaneous record that determines the outcome of a dispute years later.

## 1. Honesty and fair dealing

### 1.1 The duty that runs to everyone

Fiduciary duties run to the principal. **Honesty and fair dealing run to everyone** — the other party, their agent, lenders, escrow, appraisers, and the public.

That is why "I don't represent them" answers the wrong question. It correctly identifies whose interests the licensee advances. It says nothing about whether the licensee may mislead them.

### 1.2 The three categories

| | Standard | Distinguishing feature |
|---|---|---|
| **Innocent error** | A mistake made with reasonable care | Reasonable basis existed; care was used |
| **Negligent misrepresentation** | A statement made without reasonable basis or reasonable care | **The care taken**, not the intent |
| **Fraud** | A knowing misstatement or concealment, intended to be relied on | **Intent to deceive** |

The distinction that matters daily is the first two. Most claims against licensees are **negligence** claims, and negligence does not require any dishonesty at all. A licensee who repeats a square footage figure from a prior listing without verifying or attributing it has not lied — and may still have made a negligent misrepresentation.

### 1.3 The three categories of statement

The practical discipline, introduced in Lesson 01 and applied throughout:

1. **Verified fact** — stated with its source.
2. **Unverified report** — attributed and flagged as unverified.
3. **Professional opinion** — identified as opinion.

Nearly every misrepresentation in residential practice is a category collapse: an unverified report delivered as a fact, or an opinion delivered as a certainty.

### 1.4 Concealment is a form of misrepresentation

Saying nothing about a known material fact is not neutral. Where a duty to disclose exists, silence is concealment, and careful silence — avoiding an affirmative false statement while ensuring the fact is not discovered — is worse rather than better, because it demonstrates awareness.

### 1.5 Predictions

A prediction stated as a certainty is actionable:

| Statement | Assessment |
|---|---|
| "This will definitely appreciate over the next two years." | A future outcome stated as fact, with no basis to guarantee it |
| "Property in this area has appreciated in recent years; here is the data. Past performance doesn't predict the future." | Accurate, sourced, and appropriately qualified |
| "The city will approve your second unit." | A prediction about a discretionary decision |
| "Here is the planning department's process and contact; they can tell you what applies." | Correct |

The defect is not optimism. It is stating as certain something the licensee cannot know, on which the client will rely.

## 2. Errors, omissions, and supervision

### 2.1 The correction obligation

A licensee who discovers an error in something they said or produced must correct it **immediately, in writing, to everyone who received the original** — and, critically, **before the recipient acts on it**, if that is still possible.

The instinct to wait and see whether the error matters is the instinct that converts a mistake into a misrepresentation. An error you know about and leave uncorrected is no longer an error.

### 2.2 The worked case

> A licensee realizes she transposed two digits reporting square footage. The buyer relied on it in setting his offer. Escrow closes in four days. Her manager suggests waiting: the buyer has an inspection report, the figure came from a public record, and raising it now may kill the transaction.
>
> **The correct action:** correct the figure in writing to both sides immediately, notify her broker, and let the buyer decide what to do with accurate information while he still can.
>
> **Why each alternative fails:**
>
> - *Say nothing because it came from a public record.* The source does not matter once she knows the figure she gave was wrong.
> - *Say nothing because the buyer had his own opportunity to verify.* The buyer's diligence does not discharge her duty.
> - *Wait and offer a credit if he raises it.* Arrives after the decision it should have informed.
> - *Correct it verbally to the other agent.* Not to the person relying on it, and no record.
>
> **The manager's advice is the problem, not the guidance.** A licensee told to conceal a known error should escalate above the person who told her.

### 2.3 What supervision looks like from below

Licensees encounter supervision failures from the receiving end. Three situations and their correct responses:

| Situation | Response |
|---|---|
| A manager tells you to conceal an error | Escalate to the responsible broker; the instruction does not protect you |
| Office practice deviates from the written policy | Raise it; a policy nobody follows is not a defense for anyone |
| You are asked to handle work beyond your competence with no support | Say so in writing and ask for supervision or reassignment |

Following an improper instruction and documenting that it came from someone else does **not** transfer responsibility for the act. It documents that you knew.

### 2.4 Errors and omissions coverage

E&O responds to claims arising from professional services, **within its terms and limits**, and is commonly conditioned on **prompt notice of a potential claim**.

Three consequences:

1. **Late reporting can forfeit coverage.** A licensee who handles a brewing problem quietly for three months may have destroyed the coverage that would have handled it.
2. **It does not transfer responsibility** for the underlying conduct.
3. **Intentional acts are generally excluded.** Coverage protects against mistakes, not against decisions to deceive.

E&O is also not a substitute for supervision and documented procedures — it is what responds when those fail.

### 2.5 What to report, and when

Report to the broker promptly:

- Any error affecting a client's decision or a transaction's terms.
- Any client expression of dissatisfaction that could become a claim.
- Any allegation of misrepresentation, discrimination, or mishandling.
- Any request from another party's counsel.
- Any regulatory contact.
- Any suspected fraud.
- Any wire fraud incident or near-miss.

The bias should be toward reporting. A report that turns out to be unnecessary costs a conversation. An unreported matter that becomes a claim costs the coverage and the credibility.

## 3. Fraud warning signs

### 3.1 Fraud presents as a pattern

The most useful thing a licensee can learn about fraud is that it rarely presents as one obvious falsehood. It presents as **a pattern of small inconsistencies**, each individually explainable.

That is why the correct response to "something doesn't add up but no single item is clearly false" is to document the inconsistencies, resolve them with evidence, and escalate if they cannot be resolved. Proceeding because suspicion is not proof is how licensees end up as the documented participant in someone else's scheme.

### 3.2 Patterns worth recognizing

| Pattern | What it may indicate |
|---|---|
| Pressure to close unusually fast, combined with changing parties, funding sources, or documents | The urgency exists to defeat verification |
| A buyer or seller who never appears, acting only through an intermediary | Identity or authority problems |
| Identification that does not match other documents | Identity fraud |
| A sale price materially inconsistent with the property and the market, with no explanation | Value or flipping fraud |
| Funds arriving from parties unconnected to the transaction | Money laundering exposure |
| An occupancy statement contradicted by other documents in the same file | Occupancy fraud |
| Requests to alter, backdate, or omit documents | Direct participation request |
| A seller who cannot produce evidence of authority to convey | Title or authority fraud |
| Instructions to send funds somewhere unexpected, or changed instructions | Wire fraud |

### 3.3 What is not suspicious

Equally important, so that scrutiny is not applied selectively — which is its own problem:

- An aggressive negotiator.
- A seller who insists on attorney review before signing.
- A buyer and seller who already know each other.
- Income that is variable, commission-based, or self-employed.
- A buyer whose income has risen substantially.
- A consumer who asks a lot of questions.

Applying heightened scrutiny based on characteristics rather than on the pattern above is where fair housing problems begin.

### 3.4 The occupancy contradiction

> A file contains an application with owner-occupancy stated, **and a lease for the same property dated a month earlier.**
>
> This is different in kind from the other defects a file audit finds. It is not a missing document or a late delivery — the file itself contains the evidence contradicting a material representation to the lender.
>
> The correct response is escalation, not a judgment call about whether it matters or whether the borrower could afford the payment anyway.

### 3.5 Wire fraud

Covered mechanically in Lesson 02 and at closing in Lesson 11; the ethical point belongs here. The single most valuable protective act available to a licensee is a **written warning to the client at the start of escrow**: wire instructions will never be changed by email, and you must call to verify at a number you obtained independently.

Sent early, it costs nothing and prevents the largest single loss a residential client can suffer. Sent the day funds move, it arrives after the attacker has already established the thread.

### 3.6 What a licensee must not do

- Do not investigate suspected fraud alone.
- Do not confront a suspected participant.
- Do not warn a suspected participant that concerns have been raised.
- Do not withdraw quietly and let the next licensee face the same file blind.
- Do not participate, however small the requested act.

Escalate to the broker, who determines what reporting is required.

## 4. Conflicts and referral disclosure

### 4.1 What creates a conflict

Any interest that could reasonably influence the licensee's advice:

- A referral fee or thing of value from a service provider.
- An ownership or financial interest, including a family member's, in a recommended provider.
- A bonus contingent on a sale or a price threshold.
- The licensee's own interest, or a relative's, in a property.
- Representation of another party with a competing interest.
- Any personal relationship a client would want to know about.

### 4.2 Disclosure must reach the client in time to matter

The rule is short: **disclose before the client relies on the advice the conflict affects.**

| Inadequate approach | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| "The client is free to choose someone else" | The client cannot weigh a recommendation without knowing what the recommender receives |
| "I'll disclose it if they ask" | The client does not know to ask |
| "I won't let it influence me" | A private intention is not something the client can evaluate |
| "I'll disclose after the offer is accepted" | Information after the decision has no value |
| "The fee comes from the seller, not the buyer's pocket" | Who pays does not change that the advice carries an interest |

### 4.3 Settlement service referrals

A specific and serious federal rule: **a licensee may not give or accept a fee, kickback, or thing of value for the referral of settlement service business**, and may not split a fee where no services were actually performed.

This reaches arrangements that feel like ordinary business courtesy — an escrow company paying for each client sent, a lender covering marketing costs in exchange for referrals, a home warranty company paying per policy sold.

**Disclosure does not cure this one.** Unlike an ordinary conflict, which disclosure manages, a payment for referrals is prohibited whether or not it is disclosed.

Where the brokerage has an ownership interest in a provider it refers to, that is an **affiliated business arrangement**, and it carries its own disclosure and conditions.

### 4.4 The licensee as principal

Where a licensee buys or sells for their own account, or for a relative or entity they have an interest in, the licensee's status must be disclosed. A licensee who purchases from a client, or who has an interest in a buyer of a client's property, is in the highest-risk position in the business, and it requires disclosure, the client's informed consent, and the broker's involvement.

### 4.5 Personal relationships

A licensee representing a client in a transaction where the other party is a friend or relative should disclose it. The disclosure is inexpensive; discovering it later is expensive, because the client will reinterpret every recommendation in light of it.

## 5. Complaint response

### 5.1 The first response determines the trajectory

When a client alleges mishandling, the first response frequently determines whether the matter resolves or escalates. The correct sequence:

1. **Acknowledge promptly.** Silence reads as evasion.
2. **Notify the broker immediately.**
3. **Preserve everything.** All records relating to the matter, including drafts, texts, and emails.
4. **Respond factually**, without admitting or denying liability before the file has been reviewed.
5. **Do not offer a settlement or a fee reduction** on your own initiative.
6. **Report to the carrier** where the matter could become a claim.

### 5.2 Why the wrong responses are wrong

| Response | Problem |
|---|---|
| An immediate detailed defense | Commits a position before anyone has reviewed the file; frequently wrong on details |
| An apology accepting fault | May be treated as an admission and can compromise coverage |
| A fee reduction to make it go away | Commits the brokerage, may be read as an admission, rarely ends the matter |
| Waiting for a formal written demand | The matter hardens; coverage notice may be late |
| Handling it quietly without telling the broker | The supervision failure now compounds the original problem |

### 5.3 Preservation

The moment a complaint is foreseeable, preservation obligations attach in practice. Nothing is deleted — not texts, not drafts, not the email that reads badly.

Deleting a record that reflects poorly is far worse than the record. It converts a defensible dispute about professional judgment into a question about integrity, and it is generally discoverable anyway.

### 5.4 When a regulator makes contact

Contact from the Department is a matter for the broker, and usually for counsel. A licensee's obligations are to notify the broker immediately, preserve records, and not respond independently or informally.

Cooperation is expected. Improvised cooperation without review is where licensees make statements they cannot support.

### 5.5 Complaints that are not about you

A consumer who reports that another brokerage discriminated against them should be taken seriously:

- **Record what they report**, without characterizing it as established.
- **Inform them that enforcement agencies accept complaints**, and how.
- **Notify your broker.**

What not to do: investigate the other brokerage, tell the consumer such complaints rarely succeed, or take no action because the conduct was someone else's.

## 6. Documentation, escalation, and legal referral

### 6.1 Contemporaneous records win

A record created **as events occurred**, with dates and sources, carries weight a later reconstruction cannot. This is not a technicality about admissibility — it is about credibility. A note written before anyone knew there would be a dispute reads differently from an account assembled once the outcome was known.

Two clarifications, because licensees repeat both errors:

- A reconstruction is **not inadmissible**; it is simply weaker.
- Contemporaneous notes are **not privileged** from disclosure. Write them as though they will be read, because they may be.

### 6.2 What to document as a matter of habit

- Advice given, particularly where the client chose differently.
- Referrals made and the client's response.
- Material conversations, confirmed in writing.
- Observations from the visual inspection.
- Delivery of documents, with dates and method.
- Corrections issued.
- Instructions received that you declined to follow, and why.
- Escalations, and their outcomes.

### 6.3 How to write it

| Write | Not |
|---|---|
| "Recommended buyer obtain structural evaluation of the foundation crack before the 17th; buyer declined by text on the 12th." | "Discussed inspection issues with buyer." |
| "Brown staining approx. 3 ft across on NW bedroom ceiling; paint appears newer in that area." | "Possible old roof leak, appears repaired." |
| "Seller instructed that the drainage issue not be disclosed. Advised it must be. Escalated to broker 6/14." | "Seller was difficult about disclosure." |

Specific, dated, factual, and distinguishing observation from inference. Characterizations of people age badly; recorded facts do not.

### 6.4 When to escalate to the broker

Immediately, for: any suspected fraud; any allegation against the licensee or the brokerage; any unlawful instruction from a client; any error affecting a client's decision; any regulatory contact; any wire fraud incident or near-miss; any request to alter or backdate a record; any conflict the licensee is unsure how to handle.

The cost of over-escalating is a conversation. The cost of under-escalating is everything the brokerage would have done had it known.

### 6.5 When to refer to counsel

The trigger is **the nature of the question**, not the client's request, the stage of the dispute, or whose interests are exposed:

- The legal effect of documents, rights, or remedies.
- Whether a party may cancel, rescind, or refuse to perform.
- Entitlement to a deposit.
- The consequences of how title is taken.
- Boundary, easement, and title claims.
- Estate, probate, trust, and entity authority questions.
- Anything where the licensee's answer would be a legal conclusion.

Experience does not extend the scope of the license. A licensee with twenty years of practice is no more entitled to advise on the legal effect of a contract than one with two weeks.

### 6.6 The line that ends careers

Everything in this lesson is survivable — errors, complaints, claims, even regulatory findings — provided the licensee's conduct in response was honest.

What is not survivable is **falsifying the record**: creating a document after the fact and dating it to match, altering an executed document, deleting records that reflect badly, or removing surrounding documents so an inconsistency does not show.

> A documented gap is a compliance problem. A manufactured document is a question about integrity, and it converts a defensible situation into one that ends a career.

Corrections are made visibly, with the original preserved and the change attributable. A record that can be silently altered is not a record.

### 6.7 Checkpoint

You should be able to classify a statement as innocent error, negligent misrepresentation, or fraud; state the correct first response to a client complaint; and name the trigger that requires referral to counsel.
