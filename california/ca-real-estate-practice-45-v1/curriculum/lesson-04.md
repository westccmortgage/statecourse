# Real Estate Practice — Lesson Assignment 04

## Seller counseling and listing agreements

**Course version:** 1.0 development draft  
**Assignment time:** 180 instructional minutes  
**Regulatory status:** Not approved; no California credit is available.

A listing appointment is not simply a presentation designed to “win” a seller. It is the beginning of a fiduciary relationship and a controlled property-marketing project. The broker must verify the seller’s authority, explain agency, define services and compensation, gather property facts, document lawful instructions, protect access, and establish a reliable path from marketing through expiration or closing.

## Learning objectives

After completing this assignment, the student will be able to:

- conduct a seller intake that verifies identity, ownership, capacity, occupancy, and decision authority;
- deliver the California agency disclosure in the required sequence before a covered listing agreement;
- distinguish an open, exclusive-agency, and exclusive-right-to-sell listing;
- identify the parties, property, authority, duration, compensation, services, and termination terms that require careful review;
- apply Business and Professions Code section 10176(f) to the definite termination date of an exclusive agreement;
- apply Civil Code section 1670.50 to covered single-family residential exclusive listing agreements;
- prepare an evidence-based pricing discussion without promising price or mischaracterizing an opinion as an appraisal;
- establish showing, open-house, key, occupancy, and incident controls; and
- preserve listing versions, approvals, disclosures, marketing evidence, offers, and closing or termination records.

## 1. Seller intake establishes the facts

### 1.1 Verify the person before accepting instructions

The first question is not “What price do you want?” It is “Who owns or controls this property, and who may employ the broker?” The licensee should verify government-issued identity using the brokerage’s secure procedure, compare names with reliable title or ownership information, and investigate inconsistencies. Remote owners, vacant land, rapid-sale requests, unusual email-only instructions, pressure to avoid ordinary verification, and directions to send proceeds to a third party are fraud indicators that require escalation.

Identity verification protects the true owner and the brokerage. It should be proportional and secure: collect only what the policy requires, restrict access, and do not scatter identity documents across personal phones and email accounts. A checklist marked “verified” without the source, date, and reviewer is weak evidence.

### 1.2 Ownership and capacity

Title may be held by one or more individuals, a trust, estate, corporation, limited liability company, partnership, or another form. A person who lives at the property is not necessarily the owner; a person named on a tax bill may not have current authority to sell. A spouse, family member, trustee, executor, officer, manager, attorney-in-fact, guardian, or conservator may have some authority but not every authority.

The licensee does not give a legal opinion on title or interpret contested authority. Instead, the licensee collects the current evidence specified by broker policy, identifies missing or conflicting signatures, and coordinates review by the broker, title professional, escrow holder, or attorney. The listing should not launch until the brokerage knows who may give instructions and who must sign.

### 1.3 Property, occupancy, and transaction profile

The intake file should identify the legal and common address, assessor information, property type, units, improvements, personal property, parking, storage, access, and any area or amenity the seller expects to include. The licensee should also learn whether the property is owner occupied, tenant occupied, vacant, subject to a lease, part of a common-interest development, used for short-term rental, or affected by another occupancy arrangement.

Occupancy affects showing notices, possession, tenant rights, safety, condition, and marketing language. A listing agent should not promise vacancy, removal of a tenant, or a particular possession date without verified facts and qualified guidance. The seller’s desired timing must be compared with loans, liens, taxes, leases, relocation, replacement property, and other constraints.

### 1.4 Seller goals and lawful instructions

Record the seller’s reasons and priorities without converting them into unverified advertising claims. Relevant goals may include price, certainty, closing date, possession, privacy, repairs, property included in the sale, or coordination with another purchase. The licensee should distinguish an objective selection criterion from an unlawful preference.

A seller may choose among offers based on price, financing, contingencies, timing, and other legitimate transaction terms. A seller may not direct the agent to exclude or favor purchasers because of protected characteristics. The agent must not implement discriminatory marketing, showing, or offer-presentation instructions. The proper response is broker escalation and a documented explanation of the lawful service boundary.

### 1.5 Intake evidence

The file should identify:

- verified seller and representative identities;
- preliminary ownership and authority evidence;
- property and included-item description;
- occupancy, access, and possession facts;
- known loans, liens, assessments, leases, litigation, probate, bankruptcy, or other issues disclosed by the seller;
- the seller’s lawful goals and communication authority;
- accessibility, language, or scheduling needs;
- conflicts, affiliated services, or licensee interests; and
- questions assigned to the broker or another qualified professional.

The intake is a living record. If the title report, seller questionnaire, inspection, or escrow information changes a fact, the exception must be resolved and the chronology updated.

## 2. Agency disclosure before the listing

### 2.1 Explain the relationship first

For a covered residential transaction, Civil Code section 2079.14 requires the seller’s agent to give the seller the statutory agency disclosure described in section 2079.16 before the listing agreement is signed. The sequence provides the seller an opportunity to understand the possible roles before employing the broker.

The statutory disclosure and the listing agreement are not interchangeable. The disclosure explains seller agency, buyer agency, and dual agency. The listing agreement employs the broker and defines authority, property, duration, services, and compensation. A signature on one does not substitute for the other.

### 2.2 A defensible meeting sequence

A controlled listing meeting follows this order:

1. verify identity and authority sufficiently to proceed;
2. identify the property and proposed brokerage relationship;
3. deliver and explain the statutory agency disclosure;
4. obtain acknowledgment or document refusal as Civil Code section 2079.15 directs;
5. disclose conflicts, material compensation terms, and any anticipated dual-agency possibility;
6. review every material listing-agreement term and attachment;
7. answer questions within the licensee’s competence and recommend independent advice when needed;
8. obtain signatures and dates without blank material terms;
9. provide the executed documents according to law and broker procedure; and
10. preserve delivery, acknowledgment, signature, and version evidence.

Electronic signatures do not eliminate sequencing. The platform audit should show when the disclosure became available, when it was acknowledged or refused, when the listing was signed, and which version each person received.

### 2.3 No pressure and no invented certainty

The seller should have time to read the agreement and ask questions. The licensee should not describe a long agreement as “just paperwork,” conceal a fee in an attachment, fill blanks after signature without authorization, or say that commission is fixed by law. Compensation is negotiable. Services and limits should be explained with the same care as marketing benefits.

If the seller asks whether a clause is legally enforceable in a disputed circumstance, the licensee can explain the transaction purpose and broker procedure but should not provide a legal conclusion outside the license. The seller may be directed to independent counsel.

## 3. Listing agreements define employment and authority

### 3.1 Common listing structures

An open listing allows the owner to employ more than one broker and generally compensates the broker who produces the result specified by the agreement. Because several brokers may compete without exclusive employment, records of prospecting and procuring cause can become important.

An exclusive-agency listing employs one broker but may reserve a seller’s right to sell without owing the contracted commission when the agreement so provides. An exclusive-right-to-sell listing generally gives the broker the exclusive right to compensation if the property is sold during the listing term, subject to the agreement’s language and applicable law. Students must read the actual agreement rather than infer compensation from the title.

A net listing states a seller’s desired net and permits the broker to retain an amount above it as compensation. This structure creates acute conflict and disciplinary risk if compensation is excessive, secret, or inconsistent with fiduciary duty. It requires specific broker control and should never be treated as a shortcut around transparent negotiated compensation.

### 3.2 Material terms to review

A listing agreement should be reviewed for at least these elements:

| Element | Practical question |
|---|---|
| Parties and capacity | Who employs the broker and who must sign? |
| Property | What real and personal property is covered? |
| Listing authority | What may the broker market, show, disclose, and negotiate? |
| Price and terms | What does the seller authorize the broker to offer? |
| Duration | What is the exact beginning and definite termination date? |
| Compensation | How is it calculated, earned, payable, and modified? |
| Services | What marketing, communication, and transaction work is included? |
| Access | Who may enter, when, and under what controls? |
| Offers | How and when will offers be presented? |
| Seller duties | What facts, documents, access, and cooperation must the seller provide? |
| Conflict and agency | What agency possibilities and interests require disclosure? |
| Termination | What occurs on expiration, cancellation, withdrawal, or transfer? |
| Protection period | Which later transaction, if any, may trigger compensation? |

No licensee should complete a blank through guesswork. If a material term is not decided, the agreement is not ready for signature.

### 3.3 Definite termination under Business and Professions Code section 10176(f)

Business and Professions Code section 10176(f) makes it disciplinary grounds to claim, demand, or receive compensation under an exclusive agreement authorizing licensed acts when the agreement lacks a definite, specified date of final and complete termination. “Until sold” is not a definite calendar termination. The date should be unambiguous and should be reviewed with the seller before signature.

The rule does not mean a salesperson may choose any duration that is convenient. Duration must also comply with other law and the parties’ informed agreement. Calendar controls should warn the team before expiration, but a software reminder does not extend the agreement.

### 3.4 Civil Code section 1670.50: covered residential exclusives

Civil Code section 1670.50 adds specific controls for exclusive listing agreements regarding single-family residential property, defined broadly to include real property improved with one to four dwelling units and specified residential interests. For a covered agreement, it is unlawful for the original exclusive term to last longer than **24 months** from the date the agreement was made, subject to the statute’s stated entity exception. The agreement may not renew automatically. A renewal must be in writing, dated, and signed by all parties, and a covered renewal may not last longer than **12 months** from the date it was made.

The statute also makes it unlawful to present an exclusive listing agreement of any duration, or a memorandum or notice of it, for recording or filing with a county recorder. A violating agreement is void and unenforceable under the statute, and a licensee violation is treated as a violation of the licensing law. Students should not summarize this as “all listings can be two years.” The statute imposes an outside limit on a defined category; the parties still select a reasonable definite term and other laws or policies may apply.

### 3.5 Compensation and written changes

Compensation is negotiated, not set by DRE, an association, an MLS, or “the market.” The agreement should state the amount or method, services, triggers, timing, and any conditions in clear terms. A listing presentation may compare service packages, but it must not falsely imply that a fee is legally required or that competing brokers have agreed on a price.

Every material change should be documented in an authorized written amendment. A text message saying “we’ll work it out later” should not be used to replace the compensation term. The file should preserve the original and amended versions, the parties’ consent, and the effective date. No undisclosed amount, rebate, referral payment, or profit should be hidden from the client.

### 3.6 Expiration, cancellation, and withdrawal

Expiration occurs when the definite term ends. Cancellation or termination before expiration depends on the agreement, the parties, and applicable law. Withdrawal from active marketing may not be identical to ending the broker’s employment. A licensee should not promise that a cancellation eliminates every compensation or protection-period issue.

When a seller asks to stop, the salesperson should document the request, pause unauthorized activity, notify the broker, and follow the approved written process. The resolution should address active advertising, MLS status, signs, lockboxes, keys, scheduled showings, pending offers, confidential information, documents, and any protection-period list or notice authorized by the agreement.

## 4. Pricing counsel and seller disclosure preparation

### 4.1 Price opinion as a reasoned process

The seller chooses the listing price after receiving competent advice. A comparative market analysis should identify its purpose, the subject’s relevant characteristics, data sources, comparable-selection criteria, adjustments or qualitative differences, current competition, pending activity where reliable, closed sales, market timing, and limitations.

The licensee should not cherry-pick only high sales to secure the listing or promise that a marketing price is guaranteed. A broker price opinion or comparative analysis performed within license authority is not automatically an appraisal. The communication should not imply a state-certified appraisal when none was performed.

### 4.2 From data to a range

A disciplined analysis begins with the subject property: location, property type, living area, site, age, condition, bedrooms and baths, parking, view, improvements, occupancy, restrictions, and material external influences. Comparable selection should be explainable. A nearby sale of a different property type may be less useful than a somewhat farther, more similar sale.

Adjustments must be supportable. The licensee should not assign a value to a pool, view, remodel, accessory unit, or defect merely because the seller expects one. Where the data does not support precision, a range and sensitivity analysis are more honest than a falsely exact figure.

The pricing discussion can show several strategies:

- a price near supported market evidence to invite broad exposure;
- a higher price with an explicit risk of fewer showings and longer market time;
- a preparation or repair plan before launch;
- a staged review based on measured activity; and
- seller-defined decision points for adjustment.

The seller’s decision and the advice should be documented without disparaging other neighborhoods or describing the likely occupants in protected-class terms.

### 4.3 Net-proceeds estimate

A seller often cares about net proceeds rather than headline price. A preliminary estimate may organize sale price, loan payoff, liens, taxes, escrow and title charges, negotiated compensation, concessions, repair credits, association charges, transfer charges, and other verified items. Each number should identify its source and whether it is an estimate.

The licensee should not give tax advice or guarantee a payoff. Unknown amounts should remain visible rather than be filled with optimistic assumptions. Updated information should produce an updated estimate, with the prior version preserved.

### 4.4 Seller facts and disclosure workflow

The listing stage should begin an organized seller-disclosure process. The agent should identify the current disclosures and advisories required for the property and transaction, deliver seller questionnaires without coaching the seller to minimize answers, and flag incomplete or inconsistent responses.

The seller is the source for many property-history facts, but the agent has independent professional duties. The licensee should conduct the required visual inspection for covered residential property, disclose known material facts, and recommend qualified investigation when an issue exceeds competence. “As is” marketing does not authorize concealment. A seller’s statement that a problem was “fixed” should be supported by available permits, invoices, reports, warranties, or other evidence and should not be converted into an agent guarantee.

### 4.5 Corrections without erasing history

If the seller remembers a prior leak after disclosure delivery, the correct action is not to replace the old file silently. Preserve the original, create a dated correction or supplemental disclosure, deliver it through the approved process, and document who received it and when. The broker should evaluate effects on offers, contingencies, and transaction rights.

## 5. Marketing, showing, and access controls

### 5.1 Marketing authorization

Before publication, verify the authorized price, property facts, included items, image permissions, occupancy instructions, showing availability, and seller-approved privacy boundaries. Every material statement and digitally altered image should be reviewed under current advertising requirements and broker policy.

The seller should understand where the property may appear: MLS, brokerage website, syndication portals, social media, print, signs, direct marketing, virtual tours, and open houses. A licensee should not copy another broker’s images or advertise another listing without authorization.

### 5.2 Showing plan

A showing plan identifies:

- approved days, hours, and notice;
- occupied-property and tenant procedures;
- identity or appointment verification;
- key and lockbox permissions;
- areas that are excluded or require supervision;
- pets, alarms, gates, pools, hazards, and accessibility;
- protection of valuables, documents, medication, and personal data;
- property-securement steps after every visit;
- feedback and incident reporting; and
- emergency contacts and broker escalation.

Convenience does not justify uncontrolled access. Lockbox data, codes, and keys should be limited to authorized users. A missing key, unlocked door, property damage, injury, suspected theft, or unauthorized entrant is an incident requiring immediate safety action and documentation.

### 5.3 Open houses and agency clarity

California DRE’s 2025 guidance explains that the seller’s broker, or the broker’s affiliated salesperson or broker associate, represents the seller at an open house. The representative markets the property, communicates selling points, observes issues raised by visitors, and reports to the seller within the agency relationship.

DRE cautions about allowing an unaffiliated brokerage to “sit” another broker’s open house. Questions include whether the listing agreement permits it, whether the seller expects and consents to it, what agency relationship is created, what buyers will understand, whether the person knows the property, liability and insurance, MLS rules, incident responsibility, and the listing broker’s fiduciary duty. A casual referral arrangement should not override those controls.

Visitors should be treated consistently. The host should not ask protected-class questions, steer visitors, make unsupported claims, disclose the seller’s confidential position, or imply representation that does not exist. Questions about an existing buyer relationship should be handled under broker policy without using the open house to interfere improperly with another agency.

### 5.4 Offer presentation and seller decision

The listing agent should establish how offers will be received, verified, logged, and presented. The seller may receive a comparison showing price, estimated net, financing, deposit, contingencies, credits, included items, closing, possession, and expiration. The analysis must not use names, photographs, family composition, disability, nationality, or other protected information as a ranking factor.

The agent reports facts and risks, answers process questions, and documents the seller’s decision. The agent must not suppress an offer because its compensation is lower, because the buyer uses a disfavored lawful lender, or because the agent prefers another transaction. If an offer is rejected, the file should preserve receipt, presentation, material advice, and the seller’s authorized response.

### 5.5 Listing record

A complete listing record may contain:

- identity and authority verification;
- agency disclosure delivery and acknowledgment or refusal evidence;
- signed listing and every addendum or amendment;
- property intake, seller questionnaires, visual-inspection and other disclosure records;
- pricing analysis and net estimates with sources;
- marketing authorization, approved content, versions, distribution, and corrections;
- access instructions, key and lockbox logs, showing and open-house records;
- material communications, offers, presentations, and seller decisions;
- broker reviews, exceptions, incidents, and referrals; and
- closing, expiration, cancellation, withdrawal, or transfer records.

The listing file is not merely an archive at the end. It is the operating record used to supervise the engagement while decisions are still being made.

## 6. Applied listing-file exercise

### 6.1 File facts

Rina meets a salesperson about listing her one-unit California home. Title information also shows the name of Rina’s former spouse. Rina says the divorce judgment gave her the property but has not provided the judgment or a recorded deed. The salesperson wants the listing immediately and sends the statutory agency disclosure and exclusive-right-to-sell agreement in one electronic envelope. The system shows both documents opened at 4:12 p.m. and the listing signed at 4:13 p.m., but no agency acknowledgment appears.

The agreement runs for 30 months, renews automatically for 18 months, and authorizes recording a memorandum of the listing. The salesperson says these provisions are useful because the brokerage will invest heavily in marketing. The proposed commission is described as “the California standard.” The seller’s preferred list price is 14 percent above the supportable range, and the salesperson promises that professional photography will produce that price.

Rina reports a repaired roof leak but wants it omitted because the work “looks perfect.” The property is tenant occupied. No tenant-access plan exists. The salesperson plans to have a friend from another brokerage host the first open house because the friend wants buyer leads.

### 6.2 Required response

Prepare a listing-readiness memorandum that:

1. identifies the unresolved ownership and authority issue;
2. evaluates the agency-disclosure sequence and missing acknowledgment;
3. identifies the definite-termination and Civil Code section 1670.50 issues;
4. corrects the commission and pricing representations;
5. states how the prior leak should enter the disclosure workflow;
6. identifies the tenant, showing, key, and notice questions;
7. applies DRE’s open-house broker-sitting concerns; and
8. lists the evidence required before marketing may begin.

Do not decide divorce-title rights, tenant-notice law, or contract enforceability. Flag those issues for broker, title, escrow, or legal review as appropriate.

### 6.3 Model decision path

A strong response pauses the launch. It verifies Rina’s authority and determines who must sign before accepting instructions. It reviews the delivery chronology because the agency disclosure must precede the covered listing agreement and the file lacks acknowledgment or a refusal declaration. It rejects “standard commission” and guaranteed-price claims; compensation is negotiable and price advice must be supported.

The response also flags the 30-month original term, automatic 18-month renewal, and recording authorization under Civil Code section 1670.50, as well as the need for a definite termination date under Business and Professions Code section 10176(f). It begins a documented disclosure process for the leak rather than concealing it. Finally, it creates an occupied-property access plan and does not authorize the unaffiliated open-house host until the broker has addressed seller consent, agency, insurance, liability, MLS, competence, and fiduciary concerns.

### 6.4 Completion checklist

Before this assignment is complete, confirm that you can answer:

- Who owns the property and who may employ the broker?
- Was the statutory agency disclosure delivered before the listing agreement?
- Does the exclusive agreement contain a definite termination date?
- Does a covered residential exclusive exceed 24 months, auto-renew, renew beyond 12 months, or authorize recording?
- Are compensation and services clear, written, and negotiable?
- Is pricing advice supported and free from guarantees?
- Are property disclosures complete, sourced, and corrected transparently?
- Are occupancy, access, keys, open houses, and incidents controlled?
- Can the brokerage reconstruct every material listing decision?
