# Real Estate Practice - Lesson Assignment 04

## Seller counseling and listing agreements

**Course version:** 1.0 development draft
**Assignment time:** 180 instructional minutes
**Regulatory status:** Not approved; no California credit is available.

## Learning objectives

- Conduct a listing intake that establishes who has authority to convey, what is being sold, and what constraints affect the transaction.
- Identify each operative provision of a listing agreement and explain what a change to it does.
- Deliver a pricing recommendation the seller can act on, and document that the seller's decision was informed.
- Separate the seller's negotiable preferences from the disclosure obligations that cannot be deferred.
- Design showing and access controls that protect the property and apply uniformly to every visitor.
- Apply cancellation, expiration, and protection-period provisions correctly, and route the decision to the broker.

## 1. Property and client intake

### 1.1 What intake is for

Intake is not a formality performed before the real work begins. It establishes three things without which nothing that follows can be relied on:

1. **Authority** — who has the legal power to sell this property.
2. **Subject matter** — what exactly is being sold, including its boundaries and what conveys with it.
3. **Constraints** — what encumbrances, conditions, obligations, or third-party rights will affect the transaction.

A listing that is beautifully marketed and priced correctly still fails if it was signed by someone without authority to convey, or if it describes property whose boundaries were never checked. Intake failures are expensive precisely because they surface late — usually in escrow, when the cost of unwinding is highest.

### 1.2 Establishing authority to sell

The single most common intake failure is assuming that the person in the house is the person who can sell it. Ownership and occupancy are different facts, and so are ownership and authority.

| Ownership situation | Who must sign, and what to verify |
|---|---|
| Sole owner | The owner, verified against title and identification |
| Married couple | Both spouses where the property is community property or both are on title; marital status is a transaction fact, not a personal question |
| Co-owners (tenancy in common or joint tenancy) | **All** owners whose interests must be conveyed |
| Property held in trust | The **trustee**, acting within the authority the trust document grants — confirm rather than assume |
| Entity ownership (LLC, corporation, partnership) | The person authorized under the entity's governing documents, whose authority must be verified |
| Estate or probate | Authority derives from court appointment; confirm what the appointment permits and whether court confirmation of a sale is required |
| Power of attorney | The attorney-in-fact, under a power that is currently valid and that actually authorizes real property conveyance |
| Divorcing co-owners | Both, or a court order — a listing from one co-owner cannot deliver the whole |

Three practical rules follow:

- **Verify against a source, not against a statement.** A preliminary title report early in the listing process is inexpensive relative to what it prevents.
- **The tax roll is not evidence of authority.** It reflects who is billed, which is often but not always the owner.
- **When authority is unclear, stop and involve the broker or counsel.** Postponing the question to escrow guarantees the conflict surfaces at the worst possible moment.

### 1.3 Establishing what is being sold

- **The legal description**, not the street address, defines the property. Obtain it and confirm it matches what the seller believes they own.
- **Boundaries.** Fences, hedges, and long-standing use frequently do not match the recorded description. Where the seller intends to represent lot size or where a buyer will build near a line, a survey is the answer and the licensee's role is to recommend it.
- **What conveys.** Fixtures versus personal property is a genuine source of post-closing disputes. Walk the property and name the items that could reasonably be argued either way — chandeliers, mounted televisions, appliances, window coverings, outbuildings, above-ground installations — and address each by name in the agreement.
- **Included and excluded items should be listed, not assumed.** The purpose of naming them is to make the fixture tests unnecessary.

### 1.4 Establishing constraints

Intake should surface, at minimum:

- Existing loans, liens, and whether any are in default.
- Homeowners association membership, governing documents, assessments, and any pending litigation or special assessment.
- Leases or occupancy by anyone other than the owner, including month-to-month arrangements.
- Easements, encroachments, and shared facilities such as driveways or wells.
- Known property conditions, prior repairs, insurance claims, and permit history — especially unpermitted work.
- Any pending legal matter affecting the property or the owner's ability to convey.

#### Unpermitted work deserves its own attention

Unpermitted additions and conversions are common and consequential. They can affect value, insurability, financing, and the buyer's ability to use the space, and they are a disclosure item. Discovering one at intake gives the seller options — obtain permits, price accordingly, disclose clearly. Discovering it during the buyer's investigation gives everyone a problem.

### 1.5 The intake conversation itself

Intake is also where the professional relationship is set. Two habits pay for themselves:

- **Ask open questions and listen for what is not said.** "Is there anything about the property that has surprised you since you bought it?" surfaces more than a checklist question about defects.
- **Record what you were told, and by whom.** Seller-provided information is unverified report, and the file should show that you treated it as such.

## 2. Listing agreement structure

### 2.1 What the agreement actually does

A listing agreement is a contract between the seller and the **brokerage** — not the individual licensee — that engages the brokerage to market the property and sets the terms of the engagement. Reading it as a form to be completed rather than as a contract to be understood is how licensees end up unable to answer a seller's question about their own agreement.

### 2.2 The operative provisions

| Provision | What it does | What a change to it means |
|---|---|---|
| **Parties** | Identifies the seller(s) and the brokerage | Must match everyone whose interest is being conveyed |
| **Property** | Legal description and address, with included and excluded items | Ambiguity here becomes a dispute at closing |
| **Price and terms** | The seller's asking price and terms | The seller's decision; the licensee advises |
| **Term** | Beginning and definite ending date | A definite end protects the seller from an indefinite engagement |
| **Compensation** | Amount and when it is earned and payable | **Negotiable between seller and brokerage; never "standard"** |
| **Cooperation and compensation to other brokerages** | Whether and how the brokerage shares with a cooperating brokerage | Affects how the property is marketed to other brokerages |
| **Authority granted** | Marketing, signage, lockbox, listing service submission, photography | Each authorization is separate; a seller may withhold some |
| **Seller representations** | What the seller states about ownership, condition, and encumbrances | The seller is making statements the brokerage relies on |
| **Protection or safety clause** | Compensation where a buyer produced during the term buys shortly after it ends | Typically depends on the buyer having been identified to the seller |
| **Dispute resolution** | Mediation and arbitration provisions | Substantive rights; must be explained, not initialed past |
| **Agency disclosure confirmation** | Records the relationship | Must follow the disclosure, not precede it |

### 2.3 Why the term must be definite

An engagement without a defined end binds the seller indefinitely. A definite termination date is what allows a seller to reassess, and it is why a listing does not simply continue by inertia.

Note what the term does **not** do: it does not eliminate the seller's ability to seek cancellation before expiry. Expiration and cancellation are different mechanisms, addressed in section 6.

### 2.4 The compensation conversation

When a seller asks about compensation, the accurate answer is short and must be given without hedging:

> "Compensation is negotiable between you and our brokerage. There is no standard rate, and no rate set by any association or listing service. Here is what our brokerage charges, and here is what it covers."

Describing a rate as standard, customary, or set by a listing service is both factually wrong and an antitrust exposure. It is worth being precise about this even when the seller is not pressing, because a casual "everyone charges about this" is the sentence that later gets quoted.

### 2.5 The protection clause, explained properly

Sellers frequently misunderstand this provision and react badly to a poor explanation. The purpose is straightforward: if the brokerage introduces a buyer to the property during the listing term, and that buyer purchases shortly after the listing ends, the brokerage's work produced the sale. The clause addresses compensation in that situation.

Its ordinary limits matter and should be explained:

- It typically applies only to buyers **identified to the seller**, usually in writing, within a defined period after the term ends.
- It is generally **not** an automatic claim on any sale in the following period.
- It commonly does not apply where the seller lists with another brokerage after expiration.

A seller who understands it accepts it. A seller who is told "it's standard, just initial here" is the seller who disputes it later.

## 3. Pricing discussion

### 3.1 Whose decision, and whose obligation

The list price is the **seller's decision**. The analysis and the advice are the **licensee's obligation**. Both halves matter, and licensees fail in both directions: some refuse listings priced above their recommendation, overstating their role; others take any price without comment, abandoning the advisory duty.

The professional position is: give the analysis, give the advice, in writing, and then follow the seller's informed decision — with the file showing it was informed.

### 3.2 Building the recommendation

A defensible pricing recommendation rests on:

1. **Closed sales** — evidence of what buyers actually paid, adjusted for differences from the subject.
2. **Pending sales** — where the market is moving now, since a price has been agreed but is not yet in the closed data.
3. **Active listings** — the competition, and evidence of what sellers are asking, which is not evidence of value.
4. **Adjustments** — made to the comparables to make them resemble the subject, in the direction that answers "what would this comparable have sold for if it were like the subject?"
5. **Market conditions** — a time adjustment where values have moved between the comparable sale date and today.

### 3.3 The conversation that actually happens

> **Seller:** "Another brokerage said it's worth $1.3 million. You're saying $1.15."
>
> **Weak response:** "They're just buying the listing." — attacks the competitor, gives the seller nothing to evaluate.
>
> **Weak response:** "I can do $1.3." — abandons the analysis to win the listing.
>
> **Professional response:** "Let's compare what each analysis rests on. Theirs uses three homes currently listed, two of which have been on the market over ninety days without an offer — those are asking prices, which tell us what sellers hope for. Mine uses four closed sales in the last four months, adjusted for your smaller lot and the original systems. Here are the adjustments and why. You set the price; I want you to set it knowing what each number is built on."

That version can be evaluated by the seller, and it can be defended later.

### 3.4 What a seller net sheet does

Sellers ask about price; what they actually want to know is proceeds. An estimated seller net sheet answers the real question by showing anticipated costs and payoffs against the sale price.

Three requirements:

- **Itemize.** A range without components invites the seller to distrust all of it.
- **Label it an estimate.** The final figures are set at closing.
- **Update it** when the price, the terms, or the payoff changes materially.

### 3.5 Documenting the pricing decision

Where the seller chooses a price above the supported range, the file should contain:

1. The analysis, with the data used and the adjustments made, dated.
2. The written advice about the likely consequences of pricing above the market — longer time on market, the risk of a stale listing, appraisal risk if a buyer needs financing.
3. The seller's decision.

What should **not** be in the file: the seller's reasoning adopted as the brokerage's own opinion of value. That misattributes whose opinion it is.

## 4. Seller disclosures

### 4.1 The line that is not negotiable

At a listing appointment a seller may want many things: a high price, weekend-only showings, a listing they can cancel at will, and to defer the disclosure paperwork until an offer arrives. Three of those four are negotiable business terms. The fourth is not.

Disclosure obligations attach to the transaction and cannot be scheduled around. Deferring them until an offer arrives compresses the buyer's review period, creates pressure at exactly the wrong moment, and puts both the seller and the brokerage at risk.

The professional move is to separate the two categories openly at the appointment rather than to postpone the conversation:

> "Your price, your showing schedule, and how the listing can be ended are all things we set together — tell me what you want. The disclosure work is different: it runs on its own schedule because the buyer's rights depend on when it is delivered. Let's start it now, while there's no deadline pressure."

### 4.2 What the seller discloses

The seller's disclosure obligation covers **known material facts** affecting the value or desirability of the property. The California residential framework includes a transfer disclosure completed by the seller, natural hazard disclosure, and other disclosures depending on the property, its age, and its location.

Three principles the licensee must be able to explain:

1. **Materiality is measured by effect on a reasonable buyer's decision** — not by the seller's opinion of importance, not by whether it is currently causing a problem, and not by how often it appears.
2. **A repaired condition is still disclosed.** The adequacy of the repair is exactly what a buyer wants to evaluate.
3. **Obviousness does not excuse disclosure.** "They could see it themselves" is the assumption that produces post-closing disputes.

### 4.3 Counseling a reluctant seller

Sellers resist disclosure for predictable reasons, and each has a professional answer:

| Seller's position | The counseling response |
|---|---|
| "It's fixed, so there's nothing to disclose." | The repair is what the buyer wants to evaluate. Disclosing it with the repair records is stronger than silence. |
| "It only happens in heavy rain." | Materiality is about whether a buyer would consider it important, not about frequency. |
| "It'll kill the sale." | An undisclosed condition discovered later is far more likely to kill the sale — or produce a claim after closing. |
| "The buyer's inspector will find it anyway." | Then it will be disclosed by someone; better that it comes from you, with the repair history. |
| "I don't actually know for certain." | Then say so. "Unknown" is an honest and available answer. |

### 4.4 Completing the disclosure properly

- **Every item answered.** A blank is ambiguous and invites a later argument about whether it was addressed. "Unknown" is a real answer; a blank is not.
- **The seller completes the seller's disclosure.** A licensee filling it in on the seller's behalf misattributes the statement.
- **Uncertain items are not answered "no."** Marking an uncertain item "no" asserts knowledge the seller does not have.
- **The agent's own observations are separate.** The licensee's visual inspection disclosure is the licensee's statement, not the seller's, and it is an independent obligation.

### 4.5 Delivery and timing

Content is only half of it. Delivery, and evidence of when delivery occurred, is what rights run from. A file with a signed disclosure and no delivery date cannot show that the buyer's response period was honored.

Where a disclosure is delivered late — after contingencies have been removed, for example — the correct response is to identify the issue immediately rather than proceeding as though the transaction were firm. Rights that run from delivery are not extinguished by the buyer having acted earlier without the information.

## 5. Showing and access controls

### 5.1 Two obligations at once

Access arrangements serve two purposes that pull against each other: making the property easy to show, and keeping the property and its occupants safe. The licensee's job is to raise the conflict before it produces a loss, not after.

### 5.2 The intake conversation about access

Before agreeing to any access method, address:

- **Valuables and firearms.** Secure or remove them. A seller who has been told this and declines has made an informed choice; a seller who was never asked has not.
- **Medications and personal documents.** Frequently overlooked and frequently taken.
- **Occupants.** Tenants have possessory rights and notice requirements; children, elderly residents, and pets each change the arrangements.
- **Pets and hazards.** Both a safety issue and a liability issue.
- **Security systems and cameras.** Recording devices in the home raise legal questions about recording conversations, and a seller who records buyer discussions may create problems for themselves. Disclose the presence of recording devices.

### 5.3 Uniformity is the control

The most important principle in access management is that the practice must be **the same for every visitor**.

This is not only about property security. Judgments about which visitors seem trustworthy or serious are precisely where bias operates. A licensee who accompanies some visitors and lets others wander alone has created a fair housing exposure alongside a security failure — and cannot explain the distinction afterward in terms that sound neutral.

Practical protocol:

1. Visitors are accompanied, or supervised, consistently.
2. Identification is requested consistently, or not at all.
3. Showings are logged: who, when, with what brokerage.
4. The property is secured after each showing, with a defined check.
5. The same protocol applies at open houses, where the exposure is highest.

### 5.4 Open houses

Open houses concentrate the risk: multiple strangers, an unsecured property, and often a single licensee. Working practices:

- Do not work an open house alone where that can be avoided.
- Have a plan for the property's layout, including where you position yourself and how you would leave.
- Secure the areas that do not need to be shown.
- Register visitors consistently, and be accurate with them about what registering means for future contact — that consent is what governs later texts and emails.

### 5.5 Tenant-occupied property

Where a tenant occupies the property, the tenant's rights govern access. Entry for showings requires the notice the law provides, for a permitted purpose, at a reasonable time. A seller's desire to show the property does not override it, and a licensee who enters without proper notice exposes the seller as well as themselves.

Practical approach: agree a showing protocol with the tenant in advance, honor it, and document notices given.

## 6. Listing cancellation, expiration, and protection

### 6.1 Three different things

Licensees conflate these, and they operate differently:

- **Expiration** — the term ends on its stated date. The engagement simply ends.
- **Cancellation** — the engagement is ended before the term expires, under the agreement's terms.
- **Withdrawal** — the property is taken off the market while the listing remains in force.

A seller who says "I want to cancel" may mean any of the three, and the first job is to find out which.

### 6.2 Whose decision cancellation is

The listing is a contract between the seller and the **brokerage**. Releasing the seller from it is therefore a **broker-level decision**, not one the individual licensee makes — however sympathetic the circumstances and however much the licensee wants to accommodate the client.

The licensee's role is to understand what the seller wants and why, explain what the agreement provides, and take it to the broker.

### 6.3 What governs the outcome

The terms of the agreement. Not the licensee's assessment of whether the seller has a good reason, not the number of showings so far, and not the marketing already spent — though those may inform a negotiation, they do not decide the question.

Where a seller is dissatisfied, a productive path is often to identify the actual problem. "I want to cancel" frequently means "nothing is happening and no one is telling me why." A cancellation request that follows a communication failure is sometimes resolved by fixing the communication.

### 6.4 Withdrawal is not cancellation

A seller who needs the property off the market temporarily — a family situation, a repair, a change of plan — may want withdrawal rather than release. The listing continues; the marketing pauses. This should be documented, and the listing service status updated accurately, since misrepresenting a property's status in a listing service is its own problem.

### 6.5 After expiration

Two obligations survive:

1. **Confidentiality continues.** The former listing agent may not use or disclose the seller's confidential information — including the lowest price the seller would have accepted — afterward, including for another client, and including after the property is relisted elsewhere.
2. **The protection clause may apply**, on its terms, to buyers identified to the seller during the term.

### 6.6 Documenting the ending

Whatever the mechanism, the file should show:

- The date and method by which the engagement ended.
- Who authorized it on the brokerage's side.
- Any agreement about compensation, protection-period buyers, or the return of materials.
- The listing service status change and its date.

An engagement that ends informally, by everyone ceasing to act, is the one that generates a dispute six weeks later when the property sells.

### 6.7 Checkpoint

You should be able to distinguish expiration, cancellation, and withdrawal; state who decides a cancellation and why; and name the two obligations that survive the end of a listing.
