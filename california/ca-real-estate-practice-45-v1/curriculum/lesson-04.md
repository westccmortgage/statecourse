## Seller counseling and listing agreements

**Course version:** 1.0 development draft

## Learning objectives

- Conduct a listing intake that establishes who has authority to convey, what is being sold, and what constraints affect the transaction.
- Identify each operative provision of a listing agreement and explain what a change to it does.
- Deliver a pricing recommendation the seller can act on, and document that the seller's decision was informed.
- Separate the seller's negotiable preferences from the disclosure obligations that cannot be deferred.
- Design showing and access controls that protect the property and apply uniformly to every visitor.
- Apply cancellation, expiration, and protection-period provisions correctly, and route the decision to the broker.

## 1. Property and client intake

### 1.1 What intake is for

Intake is not a formality performed before the real work begins. It establishes three things without which nothing that follows can be relied on:

1. **Authority** — who has the legal power to sell this property.
2. **Subject matter** — what exactly is being sold, including its boundaries and what conveys with it.
3. **Constraints** — what encumbrances, conditions, obligations, or third-party rights will affect the transaction.

A listing that is beautifully marketed and priced correctly still fails if it was signed by someone without authority to convey, or if it describes property whose boundaries were never checked. Intake failures are expensive precisely because they surface late — usually in escrow, when the cost of unwinding is highest.

### 1.2 Establishing authority to sell

The single most common intake failure is assuming that the person in the house is the person who can sell it. Ownership and occupancy are different facts, and so are ownership and authority.

| Ownership situation | Who must sign, and what to verify |
|---|---|
| Sole owner | The owner, verified against title and identification |
| Married couple | Both spouses where the property is community property or both are on title; marital status is a transaction fact, not a personal question |
| Co-owners (tenancy in common or joint tenancy) | **All** owners whose interests must be conveyed |
| Property held in trust | The **trustee**, acting within the authority the trust document grants — confirm rather than assume |
| Entity ownership (LLC, corporation, partnership) | The person authorized under the entity's governing documents, whose authority must be verified |
| Estate or probate | Authority derives from court appointment; confirm what the appointment permits and whether court confirmation of a sale is required |
| Power of attorney | The attorney-in-fact, under a power that is currently valid and that actually authorizes real property conveyance |
| Divorcing co-owners | Both, or a court order — a listing from one co-owner cannot deliver the whole |

Three practical rules follow:

- **Verify against a source, not against a statement.** A preliminary title report early in the listing process is inexpensive relative to what it prevents.
- **The tax roll is not evidence of authority.** It reflects who is billed, which is often but not always the owner.
- **When authority is unclear, stop and involve the broker or counsel.** Postponing the question to escrow guarantees the conflict surfaces at the worst possible moment.

### 1.3 Establishing what is being sold

- **The legal description**, not the street address, defines the property. Obtain it and confirm it matches what the seller believes they own.
- **Boundaries.** Fences, hedges, and long-standing use frequently do not match the recorded description. Where the seller intends to represent lot size or where a buyer will build near a line, a survey is the answer and the licensee's role is to recommend it.
- **What conveys.** Fixtures versus personal property is a genuine source of post-closing disputes. Walk the property and name the items that could reasonably be argued either way — chandeliers, mounted televisions, appliances, window coverings, outbuildings, above-ground installations — and address each by name in the agreement.
- **Included and excluded items should be listed, not assumed.** The purpose of naming them is to make the fixture tests unnecessary.

### 1.4 Establishing constraints

Intake should surface, at minimum:

- Existing loans, liens, and whether any are in default.
- Homeowners association membership, governing documents, assessments, and any pending litigation or special assessment.
- Leases or occupancy by anyone other than the owner, including month-to-month arrangements.
- Easements, encroachments, and shared facilities such as driveways or wells.
- Known property conditions, prior repairs, insurance claims, and permit history — especially unpermitted work.
- Any pending legal matter affecting the property or the owner's ability to convey.

#### Unpermitted work deserves its own attention

Unpermitted additions and conversions are common and consequential. They can affect value, insurability, financing, and the buyer's ability to use the space, and they are a disclosure item. Discovering one at intake gives the seller options — obtain permits, price accordingly, disclose clearly. Discovering it during the buyer's investigation gives everyone a problem.

### 1.5 The intake conversation itself

Intake is also where the professional relationship is set. Two habits pay for themselves:

- **Ask open questions and listen for what is not said.** "Is there anything about the property that has surprised you since you bought it?" surfaces more than a checklist question about defects.
- **Record what you were told, and by whom.** Seller-provided information is unverified report, and the file should show that you treated it as such.

### 1.6 A worked intake

> **The appointment.** A licensee arrives at a single-family home. The person who called is Diane. During the first twenty minutes the following emerges:
>
> - The home was her mother's. Her mother died fourteen months ago. Diane believes the house "is hers now."
> - She has a brother who lives out of state. She says he "doesn't care about the house."
> - The garage was converted to a bedroom "years ago, by my dad."
> - There is a shared driveway with the neighbor. "We've always just worked it out."
> - She wants to list next week because she has already put a deposit on a condominium.
> - Tenants have been living in the converted bedroom since March, paying cash, with no written lease.
>
> **What the intake has to establish, in order.**
>
> **1. Authority.** "It's hers now" is a belief, not a fact the file can rely on. The property may have passed by a trust, by a will requiring administration, by intestate succession, or by an instrument that has not been recorded. **Fourteen months and a brother are the two facts that make this urgent** — if title stands in more than one name, one owner cannot list alone. **The listing does not proceed on Diane's characterization.** The licensee asks how title is currently held and asks to see what document she has. Where the answer is unclear, **this is a legal question and it goes to counsel**, not to the licensee's best guess.
>
> **2. What is being sold.** The converted garage is now the central question of the file. Was it permitted? Does the county record it as living area? **A licensee who advertises the bedroom count without knowing has made a representation he cannot support**, and square footage is exactly the number Section 3.6 of Lesson 03 warns about. The answer comes from the records, not from Diane's recollection of her father's work.
>
> **3. Constraints.** The shared driveway is either a recorded easement, an unrecorded arrangement, or a boundary the parties have been wrong about for thirty years. All three are disclosable and the third is a dispute. "We've always just worked it out" describes a relationship, not a right, and it does not transfer to a buyer.
>
> **4. Occupancy.** There are tenants. Their rights do not disappear because the tenancy is oral and paid in cash, and Section 5.5 governs access to their space. **Their existence changes the listing, the showing plan, and the disclosures.**
>
> **5. The seller's timeline.** Diane has committed money to a purchase before establishing she can sell. **This is the fact that will apply pressure to every decision in the transaction**, and naming it now is a service to her.
>
> **What the licensee does not do:** take the listing today and sort it out later. **Every one of these five is easier to resolve before the property is on the market** and nearly impossible to resolve once a buyer is in contract and Diane's condominium deposit is at risk.
>
> **What the licensee does:** write it all down, tell Diane plainly which items must be answered before listing and which can run in parallel, identify the two that require counsel, and set a date to reconvene. **An intake that ends without a listing is not a failed appointment.** It is the appointment doing its job.

## 2. Listing agreement structure

### 2.1 What the agreement actually does

A listing agreement is a contract between the seller and the **brokerage** — not the individual licensee — that engages the brokerage to market the property and sets the terms of the engagement. Reading it as a form to be completed rather than as a contract to be understood is how licensees end up unable to answer a seller's question about their own agreement.

### 2.2 The operative provisions

| Provision | What it does | What a change to it means |
|---|---|---|
| **Parties** | Identifies the seller(s) and the brokerage | Must match everyone whose interest is being conveyed |
| **Property** | Legal description and address, with included and excluded items | Ambiguity here becomes a dispute at closing |
| **Price and terms** | The seller's asking price and terms | The seller's decision; the licensee advises |
| **Term** | Beginning and definite ending date | A definite end protects the seller from an indefinite engagement |
| **Compensation** | Amount and when it is earned and payable | **Negotiable between seller and brokerage; never "standard"** |
| **Cooperation and compensation to other brokerages** | Whether and how the brokerage shares with a cooperating brokerage | Affects how the property is marketed to other brokerages |
| **Authority granted** | Marketing, signage, lockbox, listing service submission, photography | Each authorization is separate; a seller may withhold some |
| **Seller representations** | What the seller states about ownership, condition, and encumbrances | The seller is making statements the brokerage relies on |
| **Protection or safety clause** | Compensation where a buyer produced during the term buys shortly after it ends | Typically depends on the buyer having been identified to the seller |
| **Dispute resolution** | Mediation and arbitration provisions | Substantive rights; must be explained, not initialed past |
| **Agency disclosure confirmation** | Records the relationship | Must follow the disclosure, not precede it |

### 2.3 Why the term must be definite

An engagement without a defined end binds the seller indefinitely. A definite termination date is what allows a seller to reassess, and it is why a listing does not simply continue by inertia.

Note what the term does **not** do: it does not eliminate the seller's ability to seek cancellation before expiry. Expiration and cancellation are different mechanisms, addressed in section 6.

### 2.4 The compensation conversation

When a seller asks about compensation, the accurate answer is short and must be given without hedging:

> "Compensation is negotiable between you and our brokerage. There is no standard rate, and no rate set by any association or listing service. Here is what our brokerage charges, and here is what it covers."

Describing a rate as standard, customary, or set by a listing service is both factually wrong and an antitrust exposure. It is worth being precise about this even when the seller is not pressing, because a casual "everyone charges about this" is the sentence that later gets quoted.

### 2.5 The protection clause, explained properly

Sellers frequently misunderstand this provision and react badly to a poor explanation. The purpose is straightforward: if the brokerage introduces a buyer to the property during the listing term, and that buyer purchases shortly after the listing ends, the brokerage's work produced the sale. The clause addresses compensation in that situation.

Its ordinary limits matter and should be explained:

- It typically applies only to buyers **identified to the seller**, usually in writing, within a defined period after the term ends.
- It is generally **not** an automatic claim on any sale in the following period.
- It commonly does not apply where the seller lists with another brokerage after expiration.

A seller who understands it accepts it. A seller who is told "it's standard, just initial here" is the seller who disputes it later.

### 2.6 Changing a term after the agreement is signed

Listings get amended. A price changes, a term is extended, a compensation arrangement is restructured, a property is withdrawn and returned. Each of those is a change to a contract between the seller and the brokerage, and the discipline is the same one that governs the original.

**In writing, signed, dated, and retained — with the prior version kept.**

| The change | What it touches |
|---|---|
| **Price** | The marketing, the listing service record, and the seller's net analysis. **The reduction is documented as the seller's decision**, on the same basis as the original price in Section 3.5. |
| **Term extension** | The expiration date, and therefore what Section 6 does at the end. An extension agreed verbally leaves two parties with different beliefs about when the engagement ends. |
| **Compensation** | A term the brokerage is party to. **Not a licensee-level change.** |
| **Scope** | What is included in the sale — a change here changes what is being advertised. |
| **Withdrawal and return** | Listing status in the service, which Section 6.4 notes must be accurate. |

**Two failures recur.**

**The verbal amendment.** A seller says "go ahead and drop it to 699" in a phone call, the licensee changes the listing service, and no document is created. The marketing now reflects a price the file cannot show the seller authorized. **The correction is a confirming email the same day at minimum**, and a signed amendment as soon as practical.

**The overwritten record.** A change made by editing the original agreement, or by replacing the prior listing service entry, destroys the evidence of what the terms were before. **Prior versions are retained in sequence**, because the question a later dispute asks is almost always "when did this change, and who agreed?"

> **The test for whether a change needs a document:** would a person reading only the file know that this term is now different, when it changed, and that the seller agreed? If not, the change is undocumented regardless of how clearly both parties understood it at the time.

## 3. Pricing discussion

### 3.1 Whose decision, and whose obligation

The list price is the **seller's decision**. The analysis and the advice are the **licensee's obligation**. Both halves matter, and licensees fail in both directions: some refuse listings priced above their recommendation, overstating their role; others take any price without comment, abandoning the advisory duty.

The professional position is: give the analysis, give the advice, in writing, and then follow the seller's informed decision — with the file showing it was informed.

### 3.2 Building the recommendation

A defensible pricing recommendation rests on:

1. **Closed sales** — evidence of what buyers actually paid, adjusted for differences from the subject.
2. **Pending sales** — where the market is moving now, since a price has been agreed but is not yet in the closed data.
3. **Active listings** — the competition, and evidence of what sellers are asking, which is not evidence of value.
4. **Adjustments** — made to the comparables to make them resemble the subject, in the direction that answers "what would this comparable have sold for if it were like the subject?"
5. **Market conditions** — a time adjustment where values have moved between the comparable sale date and today.

### 3.3 The conversation that actually happens

> **Seller:** "Another brokerage said it's worth $1.3 million. You're saying $1.15."
>
> **Weak response:** "They're just buying the listing." — attacks the competitor, gives the seller nothing to evaluate.
>
> **Weak response:** "I can do $1.3." — abandons the analysis to win the listing.
>
> **Professional response:** "Let's compare what each analysis rests on. Theirs uses three homes currently listed, two of which have been on the market over ninety days without an offer — those are asking prices, which tell us what sellers hope for. Mine uses four closed sales in the last four months, adjusted for your smaller lot and the original systems. Here are the adjustments and why. You set the price; I want you to set it knowing what each number is built on."

That version can be evaluated by the seller, and it can be defended later.

### 3.4 What a seller net sheet does

Sellers ask about price; what they actually want to know is proceeds. An estimated seller net sheet answers the real question by showing anticipated costs and payoffs against the sale price.

Three requirements:

- **Itemize.** A range without components invites the seller to distrust all of it.
- **Label it an estimate.** The final figures are set at closing.
- **Update it** when the price, the terms, or the payoff changes materially.

### 3.5 Documenting the pricing decision

Where the seller chooses a price above the supported range, the file should contain:

1. The analysis, with the data used and the adjustments made, dated.
2. The written advice about the likely consequences of pricing above the market — longer time on market, the risk of a stale listing, appraisal risk if a buyer needs financing.
3. The seller's decision.

What should **not** be in the file: the seller's reasoning adopted as the brokerage's own opinion of value. That misattributes whose opinion it is.

### 3.6 When the seller's price is above your recommendation

This is the most common disagreement in listing practice, and it has a correct handling that is neither capitulation nor refusal.

**What the licensee owes:** an honest recommendation, the data behind it, and a clear statement of the consequences of pricing above it. **What the seller owns:** the decision.

**The consequences, stated plainly and in advance:**

1. **The first two weeks are the property's best exposure**, and an overpriced property spends them being seen and passed over by exactly the buyers most ready to act.
2. **Days on market accumulate and are visible.** A property that reduces after eight weeks is negotiated against differently than a property priced correctly at the start, because the market now has information about the seller.
3. **A reduction later usually lands below where the original recommendation was**, which is the outcome the seller was trying to avoid.
4. **Appraisal is a separate gate.** A buyer willing to pay a number does not end the question where financing is involved.

**Then the licensee documents the conversation** — Section 3.5's requirement — and, if the seller proceeds above the recommendation, prices it there and works it professionally. **Pricing at the seller's number and then marketing it half-heartedly is a breach of the duty of reasonable care**, and it is a common quiet response to being overruled.

**Two things the licensee should not do.**

**Do not adopt the seller's number as your own opinion.** If asked later — by the seller, by a buyer's agent, by a reviewer — the honest answer is that the recommendation was one number and the seller chose another, which is entirely his right. A licensee who has stated the seller's price as his own professional opinion has made a representation he cannot support.

**Do not agree to a price you believe is unsupportable in order to win the listing, planning to "get a reduction in three weeks."** That is a plan built on telling the seller something now and something different later, and Section 3.3 already named it: the strategy of buying a listing is the strategy of misleading a client at the outset.

> **A useful framing for the conversation:** "I'll list it at your number and work it hard. Here is what I expect to see in the first two weeks, and here is the point at which I'll come back and ask you to look at it again. Let's agree now on what we'll do if that happens." **The seller has made an informed decision and a review date exists**, which is what turns a disagreement into a plan.

## 4. Seller disclosures

### 4.1 The line that is not negotiable

At a listing appointment a seller may want many things: a high price, weekend-only showings, a listing they can cancel at will, and to defer the disclosure paperwork until an offer arrives. Three of those four are negotiable business terms. The fourth is not.

Disclosure obligations attach to the transaction and cannot be scheduled around. Deferring them until an offer arrives compresses the buyer's review period, creates pressure at exactly the wrong moment, and puts both the seller and the brokerage at risk.

The professional move is to separate the two categories openly at the appointment rather than to postpone the conversation:

> "Your price, your showing schedule, and how the listing can be ended are all things we set together — tell me what you want. The disclosure work is different: it runs on its own schedule because the buyer's rights depend on when it is delivered. Let's start it now, while there's no deadline pressure."

### 4.2 What the seller discloses

The seller's disclosure obligation covers **known material facts** affecting the value or desirability of the property. The California residential framework includes a transfer disclosure completed by the seller, natural hazard disclosure, and other disclosures depending on the property, its age, and its location.

Three principles the licensee must be able to explain:

1. **Materiality is measured by effect on a reasonable buyer's decision** — not by the seller's opinion of importance, not by whether it is currently causing a problem, and not by how often it appears.
2. **A repaired condition is still disclosed.** The adequacy of the repair is exactly what a buyer wants to evaluate.
3. **Obviousness does not excuse disclosure.** "They could see it themselves" is the assumption that produces post-closing disputes.

### 4.3 Counseling a reluctant seller

Sellers resist disclosure for predictable reasons, and each has a professional answer:

| Seller's position | The counseling response |
|---|---|
| "It's fixed, so there's nothing to disclose." | The repair is what the buyer wants to evaluate. Disclosing it with the repair records is stronger than silence. |
| "It only happens in heavy rain." | Materiality is about whether a buyer would consider it important, not about frequency. |
| "It'll kill the sale." | An undisclosed condition discovered later is far more likely to kill the sale — or produce a claim after closing. |
| "The buyer's inspector will find it anyway." | Then it will be disclosed by someone; better that it comes from you, with the repair history. |
| "I don't actually know for certain." | Then say so. "Unknown" is an honest and available answer. |

### 4.4 Completing the disclosure properly

- **Every item answered.** A blank is ambiguous and invites a later argument about whether it was addressed. "Unknown" is a real answer; a blank is not.
- **The seller completes the seller's disclosure.** A licensee filling it in on the seller's behalf misattributes the statement.
- **Uncertain items are not answered "no."** Marking an uncertain item "no" asserts knowledge the seller does not have.
- **The agent's own observations are separate.** The licensee's visual inspection disclosure is the licensee's statement, not the seller's, and it is an independent obligation.

### 4.5 Delivery and timing

Content is only half of it. Delivery, and evidence of when delivery occurred, is what rights run from. A file with a signed disclosure and no delivery date cannot show that the buyer's response period was honored.

Where a disclosure is delivered late — after contingencies have been removed, for example — the correct response is to identify the issue immediately rather than proceeding as though the transaction were firm. Rights that run from delivery are not extinguished by the buyer having acted earlier without the information.

### 4.6 The disclosure conversation that goes wrong

Section 4.3 supplies answers to the seller's predictable objections. This section addresses the harder case: the seller who has heard the answers and still instructs the licensee to leave something out.

> **The facts.** A seller discloses to his agent, privately, that the master bathroom shower leaked into the ceiling below "a few years ago," that he had a handyman open the ceiling and dry it out, and that there was "some black stuff" which the handyman "cleaned with bleach." No permit, no invoice, no professional remediation. He says the ceiling has been fine since. He does not want it on the disclosure, because "it will scare buyers into thinking there's mold."
>
> **What has just happened.** The seller has told the licensee about a **known material fact affecting the value or desirability of the property.** It concerns water intrusion, a concealed area, and a possible biological contaminant remediated informally. It is not close to the line.
>
> **What the licensee does not do.**
>
> - Leave it off because the seller directed it. Lesson 03, Section 3.3: **an instruction to conceal a material fact is not a lawful instruction**, and following it while documenting that the seller asked does not transfer responsibility.
> - Suggest phrasing that technically discloses while conveying nothing — "minor prior moisture, resolved." A disclosure that a reasonable buyer would not understand as describing what happened is not a disclosure.
> - Rely on the buyer's inspector to find it. Section 4.2's third principle governs, and the ceiling has been closed.
> - Say nothing and hope the seller reconsiders while the file moves forward.
>
> **What the licensee does.**
>
> **First, explain the exposure to the seller in his own terms** — not as a rule, but as the consequence: a buyer who discovers this after closing has a claim, and the fact that it was disclosed to his agent and left off the form is the worst possible version of the record.
>
> **Second, offer the stronger path.** Disclose it fully **with the remediation history and an invitation to inspect.** Section 4.3 already establishes why this is stronger than silence: a buyer evaluating a disclosed, documented condition negotiates; a buyer discovering a concealed one litigates. Where the seller is willing, a professional assessment before listing converts the unknown into a fact.
>
> **Third, involve the broker immediately** if the seller holds his position. This is not a conversation the licensee finishes alone, and the broker will decide whether the brokerage can continue with the listing.
>
> **Fourth, record it** — what the seller said, when, what the licensee advised, and what was escalated. **The note is written the day it happens**, because it is the only evidence that the licensee did not participate.
>
> **And the licensee's own disclosure is independent.** Section 4.4's last point governs: the licensee's observations are the licensee's statement. A seller's refusal does not make the licensee's own obligation disappear.

## 5. Showing and access controls

### 5.1 Two obligations at once

Access arrangements serve two purposes that pull against each other: making the property easy to show, and keeping the property and its occupants safe. The licensee's job is to raise the conflict before it produces a loss, not after.

### 5.2 The intake conversation about access

Before agreeing to any access method, address:

- **Valuables and firearms.** Secure or remove them. A seller who has been told this and declines has made an informed choice; a seller who was never asked has not.
- **Medications and personal documents.** Frequently overlooked and frequently taken.
- **Occupants.** Tenants have possessory rights and notice requirements; children, elderly residents, and pets each change the arrangements.
- **Pets and hazards.** Both a safety issue and a liability issue.
- **Security systems and cameras.** Recording devices in the home raise legal questions about recording conversations, and a seller who records buyer discussions may create problems for themselves. Disclose the presence of recording devices.

### 5.3 Uniformity is the control

The most important principle in access management is that the practice must be **the same for every visitor**.

This is not only about property security. Judgments about which visitors seem trustworthy or serious are precisely where bias operates. A licensee who accompanies some visitors and lets others wander alone has created a fair housing exposure alongside a security failure — and cannot explain the distinction afterward in terms that sound neutral.

Practical protocol:

1. Visitors are accompanied, or supervised, consistently.
2. Identification is requested consistently, or not at all.
3. Showings are logged: who, when, with what brokerage.
4. The property is secured after each showing, with a defined check.
5. The same protocol applies at open houses, where the exposure is highest.

### 5.4 Open houses

Open houses concentrate the risk: multiple strangers, an unsecured property, and often a single licensee. Working practices:

- Do not work an open house alone where that can be avoided.
- Have a plan for the property's layout, including where you position yourself and how you would leave.
- Secure the areas that do not need to be shown.
- Register visitors consistently, and be accurate with them about what registering means for future contact — that consent is what governs later texts and emails.

### 5.5 Tenant-occupied property

Where a tenant occupies the property, the tenant's rights govern access. Entry for showings requires the notice the law provides, for a permitted purpose, at a reasonable time. A seller's desire to show the property does not override it, and a licensee who enters without proper notice exposes the seller as well as themselves.

Practical approach: agree a showing protocol with the tenant in advance, honor it, and document notices given.

### 5.6 When something goes missing

Access controls exist because sometimes they fail. What a licensee does in the first hours after a report determines whether the situation is handled or compounded.

**The report arrives** — a seller says jewelry is gone, medication is missing, or a laptop that was on the desk is not there.

**What the licensee does, in order:**

1. **Take it seriously and say so.** The instinct to reassure ("it's probably just misplaced") reads to the seller as dismissal, and it is the response that turns an incident into a complaint about the licensee.
2. **Notify the broker immediately.** This is a brokerage matter from the first minute, and it may involve insurance, the listing service, and law enforcement.
3. **Tell the seller to contact law enforcement**, and do not discourage it. A licensee who steers a seller away from a report — for any reason, including protecting a colleague — has made himself part of the problem.
4. **Preserve the access record.** Section 5.3's uniformity requirement produces the log that answers who entered and when. **Print it, export it, and preserve it now**, before an electronic system rolls over its retention window.
5. **Do not conduct your own investigation.** Do not call the agents who showed the property to ask what happened, do not accuse anyone, and do not repeat a seller's suspicion about a named person to anyone other than the broker. **A statement about an identified person's honesty carries its own exposure.**
6. **Do not alter the log or the file.** Whatever it shows, it is the record.

**What the file should contain afterward:** the seller's report with its date and what was said, the access record, the notification to the broker with its time, the referral to law enforcement, and any report number.

> **The prevention is the log, and the log is only useful if it was uniform.** Section 5.3 makes the point in terms of fair treatment; this section makes it in terms of evidence. **An access process that was relaxed for people the licensee knew produces a record that cannot answer the question**, and the seller's loss becomes an argument about the licensee's diligence.

**One related situation.** Where a licensee is the one who discovers something — a door left unlocked, a window open, a lockbox missing — the same sequence applies, starting with the seller and the broker. **The instinct to quietly fix it and say nothing is the instinct to create an undocumented event**, and undocumented events are the ones that reappear as accusations.

## 6. Listing cancellation, expiration, and protection

### 6.1 Three different things

Licensees conflate these, and they operate differently:

- **Expiration** — the term ends on its stated date. The engagement simply ends.
- **Cancellation** — the engagement is ended before the term expires, under the agreement's terms.
- **Withdrawal** — the property is taken off the market while the listing remains in force.

A seller who says "I want to cancel" may mean any of the three, and the first job is to find out which.

### 6.2 Whose decision cancellation is

The listing is a contract between the seller and the **brokerage**. Releasing the seller from it is therefore a **broker-level decision**, not one the individual licensee makes — however sympathetic the circumstances and however much the licensee wants to accommodate the client.

The licensee's role is to understand what the seller wants and why, explain what the agreement provides, and take it to the broker.

### 6.3 What governs the outcome

The terms of the agreement. Not the licensee's assessment of whether the seller has a good reason, not the number of showings so far, and not the marketing already spent — though those may inform a negotiation, they do not decide the question.

Where a seller is dissatisfied, a productive path is often to identify the actual problem. "I want to cancel" frequently means "nothing is happening and no one is telling me why." A cancellation request that follows a communication failure is sometimes resolved by fixing the communication.

### 6.4 Withdrawal is not cancellation

A seller who needs the property off the market temporarily — a family situation, a repair, a change of plan — may want withdrawal rather than release. The listing continues; the marketing pauses. This should be documented, and the listing service status updated accurately, since misrepresenting a property's status in a listing service is its own problem.

### 6.5 After expiration

Two obligations survive:

1. **Confidentiality continues.** The former listing agent may not use or disclose the seller's confidential information — including the lowest price the seller would have accepted — afterward, including for another client, and including after the property is relisted elsewhere.
2. **The protection clause may apply**, on its terms, to buyers identified to the seller during the term.

### 6.6 Documenting the ending

Whatever the mechanism, the file should show:

- The date and method by which the engagement ended.
- Who authorized it on the brokerage's side.
- Any agreement about compensation, protection-period buyers, or the return of materials.
- The listing service status change and its date.

An engagement that ends informally, by everyone ceasing to act, is the one that generates a dispute six weeks later when the property sells.

### 6.7 Three listings that ended

> **Listing A — the seller who wants out.** Four weeks into a six-month term, the seller calls: "Nothing is happening. I want to cancel and list with someone else."
>
> **The first move is to find out which of the three things he means**, and Section 6.1 supplies the question. He means cancellation.
>
> **The second move is to find out what actually happened.** Four weeks with what activity? How many showings, what feedback, what has the licensee reported and how often? Section 6.3 notes that a cancellation request frequently follows a communication failure rather than a marketing failure, and this one has the shape of it — the seller does not say the price is wrong or the photographs are bad. He says nothing is happening, which is what a seller says when nobody has told him anything.
>
> **The third move is the broker.** Whatever the licensee concludes about the merits, the release is the brokerage's decision. **The licensee does not tell the seller "I'll let you out."**
>
> **The outcome is documented either way** — released on stated terms, or continued with an agreed communication schedule and a review date.

> **Listing B — the expiration nobody noticed.** A six-month listing expires on a Sunday. On Wednesday the licensee shows the property to a buyer, having not noticed. On Friday the buyer writes an offer.
>
> **The licensee is acting under no agreement.** The engagement ended Sunday, and there is no compensation agreement, no authority to market, and no current listing service authorization.
>
> **What must not happen is a listing agreement signed Friday and dated to the prior term.** Section 6.6's principle and Lesson 03's closing line both govern: a documented gap is survivable, a manufactured document is not.
>
> **What happens instead:** tell the seller immediately, present the offer — which the seller is entitled to see regardless — and address the engagement going forward with a new agreement dated today, on terms the broker approves. **The calendar failure is the real finding**, and it is a systems problem: expirations belong in a tracker, not in memory.

> **Listing C — the protection period.** A listing expires. Six weeks later the property sells to a buyer who toured it during the term, now working with a different brokerage.
>
> **The protection clause may apply, on its terms**, and the terms are the ones in the agreement rather than the ones the licensee remembers. Whether the buyer was identified to the seller in the manner the clause requires, and within the period it specifies, are factual questions answered by what was actually delivered during the term.
>
> **The file is what decides it.** A licensee who provided the seller a written list of protected buyers before expiration has an answer. One who did not is arguing from recollection against a seller who has already paid another brokerage.
>
> **And the claim is the brokerage's**, not the licensee's, which means the decision whether to assert it is the broker's.

### 6.8 The listing file, at a glance

The documents a listing file should hold, in the order they normally arise:

1. **The agency disclosure**, dated before the listing agreement.
2. **The listing agreement**, with every blank completed and the term definite.
3. **Evidence of authority** — how title is held, and the instrument or authority under which anyone signing on another's behalf acts.
4. **The pricing analysis and the net sheet**, with the seller's decision recorded, including where it departed from the recommendation.
5. **The seller's disclosures**, completed by the seller, with delivery evidenced.
6. **The licensee's own visual inspection disclosure**, as the licensee's separate statement.
7. **Advertising as published**, with dates, and any required approvals.
8. **The access record**, uniform and complete.
9. **Amendments**, each signed and dated, with prior versions retained in sequence.
10. **Vendor and conflict disclosures**, each dated before the seller acted on the recommendation.
11. **Correspondence evidencing decisions** — the price change, the showing instruction, the disclosure conversation.
12. **The ending** — expiration, cancellation, or withdrawal, with its date, authorization, and listing service status change.
13. **The protected-buyer list**, where the agreement provides for one, delivered before the term ended.

**Two of these are the ones missing from most files:** item 4, because the pricing conversation feels like a conversation rather than a decision, and item 13, because the protection clause is remembered only after it matters.

> **Run the list at the listing appointment and again at the ending.** At the appointment it tells you what to collect. At the ending it tells you what is still obtainable — which, as Lesson 02's close-out routine establishes, is a category that shrinks every week.

### 6.9 Checkpoint

You should be able to distinguish expiration, cancellation, and withdrawal; state who decides a cancellation and why; and name the two obligations that survive the end of a listing.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **Intake** | The process establishing who has authority to convey, what is being sold, and what constraints affect the transaction — before marketing begins. |
| **Authority to convey** | The legal power to sell the property, which rests with the owners, the trustee, the authorized entity representative, or the court-appointed personal representative — not with the occupant or the person on the tax bill. |
| **Trustee** | The person holding legal title under a trust, who may convey only within the authority the trust document grants. |
| **Attorney-in-fact** | A person acting under a power of attorney, whose authority to convey real property must be currently valid and expressly granted. |
| **Legal description** | The description that defines the property in law. The street address does not. |
| **Included and excluded items** | Personal property and disputable fixtures named expressly in the agreement, so the fixture tests never have to be applied. |
| **Listing agreement** | The contract between the seller and the **brokerage** engaging it to market the property, on negotiated terms. |
| **Definite termination date** | The stated end of the listing term, which protects the seller from an indefinite engagement. |
| **Protection (safety) clause** | The provision addressing compensation where a buyer the brokerage introduced during the term purchases shortly after it ends, ordinarily limited to buyers identified to the seller. |
| **Negotiable compensation** | Compensation set independently by the brokerage and negotiated with the seller. Never "standard," and never set by an association or listing service. |
| **Comparative market analysis** | The licensee's opinion of value, built from closed sales with adjustments, prepared to advise on pricing. |
| **Seller net sheet** | An itemized estimate of the seller's proceeds after costs and payoffs, which answers the question the seller is actually asking. |
| **Payoff demand** | The lender's statement of what satisfies the loan, including accrued interest and other amounts owed — routinely higher than the principal balance. |
| **Materiality** | The standard for disclosure: whether a fact would affect a reasonable buyer's decision or the price they would pay. |
| **Unknown** | The honest answer to a disclosure item the seller cannot answer. Distinct from a blank, which is ambiguous. |
| **Delivery evidence** | Proof of when a disclosure reached the buyer. Rights run from delivery, so a signature without a date proves the wrong thing. |
| **Access control** | The showing and access protocol, applied identically to every visitor for both security and fair access reasons. |
| **Expiration** | The listing ending on its stated date. |
| **Cancellation** | The listing being ended before its term expires, under the agreement's terms — a **broker-level decision**. |
| **Withdrawal** | Taking the property off the market while the listing remains in force. |

## Practice problems

### Problem 1

A woman calls to list the home she has lived in for twelve years. At the appointment she explains
that the house is held in her late mother's trust, that she is one of three children named as
beneficiaries, and that she has paid the taxes and upkeep since her mother died.

**Whose signature is required, and what must be verified?**

> **Solution.** The **trustee's**, acting within the authority the trust document grants. Not the
> beneficiaries', however many there are and however clearly they benefit.
>
> What must be verified: who the current trustee is, that the trust authorizes sale of real property,
> and what the trust requires of the trustee in doing so. Occupancy, payment of taxes, and beneficial
> interest are all evidence of connection to the property and none establishes authority to sell it.
>
> **What transfers:** intake failures surface in escrow, where the cost of unwinding is highest. Ten
> minutes with the trust document at the appointment prevents it.

### Problem 2

A seller shows the licensee a competing brokerage's estimate $150,000 above her supported range. It
uses three homes currently listed, two of which have sat for over ninety days, and makes no
adjustments.

**How should the licensee respond?**

> **Solution.** By making both analyses legible rather than competing on the number or attacking the
> competitor.
>
> Active listings show what sellers are asking; they are not evidence of value — and two of the three
> have been declined by the market for three months. Closed sales show what buyers actually paid.
> Present the closed evidence, the adjustments, and the reasoning, and let the seller decide with the
> basis of each number in front of her.
>
> **What transfers:** "they're just buying the listing" attacks a competitor and gives the seller
> nothing to evaluate. "I can do that number" abandons the analysis. Neither is advice.

### Problem 3

At the listing appointment a seller says he wants the highest possible price, weekend-only showings,
a listing he can cancel any time, and to leave the disclosure paperwork until an offer arrives.

**Which requests are negotiable?**

> **Solution.** The first three. Price, showing windows, and cancellation terms are business terms
> the parties set together.
>
> The fourth is not. Disclosure obligations run on their own schedule, and deferring them until an
> offer arrives compresses the buyer's review, creates pressure at the worst moment, and puts both
> the seller and the brokerage at risk.
>
> The professional move is to separate the categories openly at the appointment rather than defer the
> conversation — and to start the disclosure work while there is no deadline pressure.

### Problem 4

A seller's transfer disclosure comes back with four items left blank. He says he genuinely does not
know the answers.

**What should the licensee advise?**

> **Solution.** Complete every item, answering **"unknown"** where that is the truth.
>
> A blank is ambiguous: it invites a later dispute about whether the item was addressed at all.
> "Unknown" is a real, honest, and complete answer.
>
> Two things the licensee must not do: mark the uncertain items "no," which asserts knowledge the
> seller does not have; or complete the seller's disclosure on his behalf, which misattributes the
> statement.

### Problem 5

A seller wants a lockbox so any licensee can show while he is at work. He keeps firearms, prescription
medication, and a coin collection in the house.

**What must be addressed, and what if he declines?**

> **Solution.** Secure or remove the firearms, medications, and valuables; agree how showings are
> logged; confirm the access method the seller authorizes; and disclose any recording devices in the
> home.
>
> If he is told and declines, he has made an informed choice and the file should show the conversation
> happened. A seller who was never asked has not made a choice at all — and that is the licensee's
> failure rather than his.
>
> **What transfers:** convenient access and secure access are different problems. The licensee's job
> is to raise the conflict before it produces a loss.

### Problem 6

A licensee accompanies some open-house visitors through the property and lets others walk through
alone, based on her sense of who seems serious.

**What is wrong with this?**

> **Solution.** Two things, and the second is the more serious.
>
> First, the property is unsupervised for some visitors, which is the security failure.
>
> Second, and worse: judgments about which visitors seem serious or trustworthy are exactly where
> bias operates, and the distinction cannot be explained afterwards in neutral terms. Selective
> supervision is a fair housing exposure alongside a security one.
>
> **The control is uniformity.** Everyone is accompanied, or the same supervision applies to everyone.

### Problem 7

Two weeks into a ninety-day listing the seller calls and says she wants to cancel. The licensee has
spent $1,400 on photography and marketing and has held two open houses.

**Who decides, and what governs?**

> **Solution.** The **broker** decides, because the listing is the brokerage's contract with the
> seller — not the individual licensee's, however sympathetic he is or however much he wants to
> accommodate her.
>
> What governs is the agreement's cancellation and compensation terms. The marketing spend and the
> showings may inform a negotiation; they do not decide the question, and neither does the licensee's
> view of whether her reason is good.
>
> **A useful first step:** find out what she actually wants. "I want to cancel" often means "nothing
> is happening and no one is telling me why," which is a communication problem with a different fix.

### Problem 8

A listing expires unsold. Three weeks later the property sells to a buyer the brokerage introduced
and identified to the seller in writing during the term.

**What applies, and what else survives the expiration?**

> **Solution.** The **protection clause** may apply on its terms — typically to buyers identified to
> the seller in writing within a defined period after the term ends.
>
> Also surviving: **confidentiality**. The former listing agent may not use or disclose the seller's
> confidential information, including her lowest acceptable price, afterwards — including for another
> client and including after the property is relisted elsewhere.
>
> **What transfers:** explain the protection clause properly at signing. A seller who understands it
> accepts it; a seller told "it's standard, just initial here" disputes it later.

## Chapter summary

**Intake establishes three things:** who can convey, what is being sold, and what constrains the
transaction. Verify authority against a source rather than assuming it from occupancy or the tax
roll, and stop and escalate where it is unclear.

**Name the disputable items** in the agreement — chandeliers, mounted televisions, appliances,
outbuildings — so the fixture tests never have to be applied after the fact.

**The listing agreement is the brokerage's contract**, and the licensee should be able to explain
every operative provision. Compensation is negotiable and never described as standard. The definite
termination date protects the seller. The protection clause is explained rather than initialed past.

**Price is the seller's decision; the analysis and advice are the licensee's obligation.** Both halves
matter, and licensees fail in both directions. Where the seller prices above the supported range, the
file shows the analysis, the written advice, and the seller's decision — and does not adopt the
seller's reasoning as the brokerage's opinion of value.

**A net sheet answers the seller's real question**, which is proceeds rather than price. Itemize it,
label it an estimate, and update it — and warn about the payoff, which includes accrued interest.

**Disclosure obligations cannot be scheduled around.** Separate them from the negotiable preferences
openly at the appointment, complete every item, answer "unknown" honestly, and evidence delivery.

**Access protocols must be uniform**, because selective supervision is a fair housing exposure as
well as a security failure.

**Expiration, cancellation, and withdrawal are three different things.** Cancellation is a
broker-level decision governed by the agreement, and confidentiality survives whichever way the
engagement ends.
