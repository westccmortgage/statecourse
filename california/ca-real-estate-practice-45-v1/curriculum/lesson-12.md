## 1. Management agreement

### 1.1 Property management is licensed activity

Managing real property for another for compensation is licensed activity. It is performed for and under the employing broker, the funds handled are trust funds, and the records are the brokerage's records. Everything in this lesson follows from those three facts.

Property management also differs from sales in a way that shapes the risk: **it is continuous.** A sale ends. A tenancy runs for years, generating funds monthly, decisions weekly, and exposure continuously. A defect in a sales file is one transaction. A defect in a management practice repeats every month across every unit.

### 1.2 What the agreement establishes

| Term | What it governs |
|---|---|
| **Parties** | The owner and the **brokerage** |
| **Property** | Exactly which units are managed |
| **Term and termination** | Duration, notice to terminate, what happens on termination |
| **Scope of authority** | What the manager may do without asking, and what requires owner approval |
| **Spending authority** | The threshold above which owner approval is required, and emergency authority below it |
| **Compensation** | Management fee, leasing fee, renewal fee, and any other charges |
| **Trust funds** | Where funds are held and how they are accounted for |
| **Reporting** | What the owner receives and how often |
| **Owner obligations** | Funding reserves, maintaining insurance, providing disclosures and documents |
| **Insurance and indemnity** | Coverage each party maintains |
| **Fair housing** | The parties' obligations, and the manager's authority to refuse unlawful instructions |

### 1.3 Unsigned authority is a structural defect

A manager acting without a signed agreement is acting without established authority. That is not a paperwork gap:

- The scope of authority is undefined, so every decision is arguable.
- The compensation is unenforceable.
- The handling of funds is not authorized in writing.
- The owner can dispute any action taken.

An audit that finds two owners with no signed agreement has found a defect of the same order as commingling, not a filing oversight.

### 1.4 Spending authority and emergencies

The most practically important term is the threshold above which the manager must obtain owner approval, paired with **emergency authority** below or outside it.

Emergencies do not wait for approval. A burst pipe, a failed heater in cold weather, a security failure — these require action, and the agreement should say so, define what counts, and require prompt notice to the owner afterward.

A manager without written emergency authority faces a genuine dilemma: act and be second-guessed, or wait and let the damage compound while a habitability obligation runs. Settling it in the agreement removes the dilemma.

### 1.5 Owner disclosures the manager needs

Before managing, the manager should obtain from the owner: known property conditions and repair history; permit status of improvements; existing leases and their terms; existing deposits held and their amounts; association documents where applicable; insurance details; and any pending disputes or claims.

Deposits held by the owner from an existing tenancy deserve particular attention. Where deposits transfer to the manager, they are trust funds from the moment received, and the amounts must reconcile to the leases.

### 1.6 The management agreement's hardest terms

Section 1.2 lists what the agreement establishes. Four of its terms produce nearly all management disputes, and each is worth negotiating deliberately rather than accepting from a template.

**Scope of authority over money.** Section 1.4 sets spending authority; the harder question is what the manager may do when the authority is insufficient and the owner cannot be reached. **An emergency provision with no dollar figure and no definition of emergency is a provision that will be argued about**, and it will be argued about after the manager has already spent the money.

**Who decides on a tenant.** The agreement should say whether the manager approves applicants against the written criteria or forwards them to the owner. **The second arrangement is where fair housing exposure concentrates**, because an owner reviewing applicants is an owner making decisions on information the criteria do not include. Where an owner insists on approving, **the criteria still govern, the manager still applies them first, and the manager does not transmit an owner's improper reason to an applicant.**

**Term, termination, and what happens to the tenancies.** A management agreement ending does not end the leases. **The transfer of trust funds, deposits, records, and keys on termination is a term**, and its absence is why manager transitions produce deposit disputes years later.

**What the manager is not responsible for.** Habitability obligations run to the owner, and an agreement cannot make the manager the party who does not perform them. **What an agreement can do is state who does what**, and clarity here is protective for both sides. An agreement silent on who obtains required inspections, who maintains required systems, and who holds the compliance obligations produces a gap that shows up during an enforcement matter.

> **A term worth adding that most agreements lack:** what happens when the owner instructs the manager to do something the manager may not lawfully do. **Naming it in advance — the manager declines, states why in writing, and the instruction does not proceed — converts a future confrontation into a term both parties already accepted.**

**And one practical requirement.** The manager cannot perform an agreement he cannot find. **A signed original, in the file, with every exhibit** — the criteria, the fee schedule, the authority limits — is the baseline, and Section 1.3's point about unsigned authority is the reason.

## 2. Tenant selection and fair housing

### 2.1 Written criteria applied uniformly

The defensible practice, and the only one that survives review:

1. **Written criteria**, established before applications are taken.
2. **Objective standards** — income relative to rent, credit history, rental history, verifiable identity, lawful occupancy standards.
3. **Applied identically to every applicant.**
4. **Documented** — how each application was evaluated against each criterion.
5. **Disclosed** to applicants before they apply, along with any application fee.

Uniformity is not merely fair; it is what makes a decision explainable afterward in neutral terms.

### 2.2 Where bias operates

| Practice | Problem |
|---|---|
| "I get a feel for people at the showing" | Impression-based selection is where bias operates and cannot be defended |
| Choosing whoever the current tenants would get along with | Compatibility judgments reproduce the existing composition |
| Preferring applicants referred by existing tenants | Same effect, at scale |
| Applying criteria strictly to some applicants and flexibly to others | Differential application is discrimination even with neutral criteria |
| Discouraging an application rather than denying it | Steering, and harder to detect, which is why it is dangerous |

That last one deserves attention. Telling an applicant "you probably wouldn't qualify" or "the owner is looking for someone long-term" — instead of taking the application and evaluating it — is discouragement, and it produces no record of a denial while achieving one.

### 2.3 Occupancy standards

Occupancy limits must be reasonable and applied uniformly. A limit set so restrictively that it excludes families with children functions as familial status discrimination however it is phrased.

### 2.4 Source of income

California protects source of income. An owner instruction to refuse applicants using housing assistance is an unlawful instruction, and the manager's obligations are those covered in Lesson 03: refuse it, explain why, escalate to the broker, and address whether the engagement can continue.

### 2.5 Assistance animals

For fair housing purposes, an assistance animal is **not a pet**. Three consequences:

1. A no-pet policy does not by itself justify denial.
2. **No pet deposit or pet rent may be charged** for an assistance animal.
3. The request cannot be refused merely because the animal lacks "certification."

The provider may still evaluate reasonableness, and the resident remains responsible for damage and for the animal's behavior.

### 2.6 The worked case

> An owner instructs: "No housing assistance, no service animals because of my no-pet rule, and I'd prefer a quiet single person over a family with kids. Also that unit has two steps at the entry so it won't work for the applicant in a wheelchair anyway."
>
> **Every clause is a problem.** Source of income and familial status are protected in California. A service animal is not a pet. And deciding on the applicant's behalf that a unit will not work for him is not the manager's decision — accessibility is addressed through accommodation and modification, not exclusion.
>
> **The response:** refuse the instructions, explain each, evaluate the applicant on the same written criteria as everyone else, treat the entry steps as a possible modification question, and escalate to the broker on whether the brokerage can act for this owner.
>
> **What does not work:** applying only the "quiet tenant" preference by recasting familial status as a noise concern; documenting that the instructions came from the owner and following them anyway; or declining the engagement quietly without explaining the problem, which leaves the owner to repeat it with the next manager.

### 2.7 Advertising

Rental advertising is subject to the same rules covered in Lesson 06: describe the property, not the occupants. "Ideal for professionals, no students," "quiet building, adults preferred," and "perfect for a single person" all sort people. And the photographs must depict **the actual unit** — advertising a vacancy with images of a nicer unit in the building misrepresents what is offered, however similar the floor plans.

### 2.8 The application process, start to finish

Fair housing exposure in leasing is concentrated in a process that takes a week, and most of it is decided before any applicant applies.

**Before the property is advertised:**

1. **Written criteria exist** — income standard, credit standard, rental history, and whatever else the owner lawfully requires — with each element defined so that two people applying it reach the same result.
2. **The criteria have been reviewed** against what California prohibits, which Section 2.4 notes is broader than the federal baseline, and against how each element operates in practice rather than only in intent.
3. **The criteria are published or available to applicants**, so an applicant knows what he is being measured against.

**When inquiries arrive:**

4. **Every inquiry gets the same information** — availability, price, terms, criteria, and the application process. **The most common violation in leasing is not a denial. It is different information given to different callers**, and it leaves almost no trace.
5. **Every inquiry is logged** — date, contact, property, what was said. This is the record that answers a testing complaint, and there is no substitute for it.

**When applications arrive:**

6. **Applications are processed in a defined order**, and the order is documented. First-come-first-served is a rule; "whoever seems easiest" is not.
7. **The same verification steps run for every applicant.** Additional scrutiny applied to some applicants is the pattern Lesson 14 of the mortgage course calls selective scrutiny, and it produces a violation even where every decision was defensible.
8. **Accommodation requests are handled on their own track** — Section 2.5 and Section 4.9 of Lesson 06 — **and never as a reason the application slows down or stops.**

**When a decision is made:**

9. **The decision states the criterion it rests on**, recorded at the time.
10. **Adverse action requirements are met** where a consumer report contributed, including the disclosures the framework requires.
11. **The file is retained** — approved and denied alike. **Denied applications are the ones an examination is interested in**, for the same reason denied loan files are.

> **The test of the whole process:** hand the file to someone who was not there and ask them to explain why each applicant was approved or denied. **If the answer requires knowing what the manager thought, the process failed** — not necessarily unlawfully, but in the only way that can be proved either direction.

## 3. Rents, deposits, and accounting

### 3.1 What is a trust fund

Money received in the course of licensed activity that belongs to someone else is a trust fund. In property management that includes rents collected for the owner, security deposits, owner-provided reserves, and application fees held pending processing.

### 3.2 The three obligations

1. **Separation.** Trust funds go into a trust account or neutral depository, kept separate from the brokerage's own funds.
2. **Records.** A complete accounting of every receipt and disbursement, identifying whose funds they are.
3. **Timeliness.** Funds are placed as required, without being held personally in the interim.

### 3.3 Why internal ledgers do not cure commingling

The most common defense a manager offers is that funds in the operating account are tracked separately in the accounting system. It does not work, for a structural reason worth teaching:

> Funds in a brokerage operating account are exposed to the brokerage's creditors, to its operating losses, and to ordinary business risk. A ledger entry does not protect the money. **Separation is what protects it**, and the record is what proves the separation was maintained.

Commingling is complete when the funds are deposited in the wrong account, regardless of how carefully the total is tracked and regardless of whether anyone loses money.

### 3.4 Shortages and overages

| Finding | What it means | Correct response |
|---|---|---|
| **Shortage** | Someone else's money is missing | Cure immediately from the broker's own funds; document the cause; reconcile |
| **Overage** | The records do not accurately reflect whose funds are held | Investigate and document the source; the broker may not simply keep it |

Neither is a rounding matter. There is no tolerance threshold, and neither may be deferred to the next reconciliation.

> A manager discovers he deposited a tenant's rent into the operating account by mistake. The correct response is to correct it immediately, **document the error and the correction**, and notify the broker so the trust records reconcile and the cause is addressed. Correcting it quietly without a record is its own problem: trust accounting is judged by the record as much as by the balance.

### 3.5 Security deposits

Deposits are the tenant's funds held for defined purposes, and California regulates their handling and their return.

Practical obligations:

- **Hold them as trust funds**, separate from the brokerage's funds. Forwarding them to the owner is governed by the agreement and the law, not by convenience.
- **Document condition at move-in**, thoroughly, with dated photographs. A deposit dispute is decided on the difference between move-in and move-out condition, and a manager without move-in documentation has no case.
- **Distinguish damage from ordinary wear and tear.** Wear and tear is not deductible.
- **Account in writing** on termination, itemizing deductions with supporting documentation, within the required time.
- **Return the balance** within the required period.

Deposit accounting is the single most common source of tenant complaints, and nearly all of it is prevented by move-in documentation and an itemized written accounting.

### 3.6 Reconciliation

The trust account is reconciled on a regular cycle, and the reconciliation compares three things: the bank balance, the book balance, and the **total of the individual beneficiary ledgers**. All three must agree.

A reconciliation that balances the bank against the book but never against the beneficiary ledgers will not detect the error that matters most — money in the account belonging to a different beneficiary than the records say.

### 3.7 A worked trust account month

> **A manager holds funds for nine properties in one trust account. During March:**
>
> - Rent arrives for eight of nine properties; one tenant pays partially.
> - Two owners are due disbursements; one has asked that his be held for an upcoming repair.
> - A plumber is paid from property four's funds.
> - A tenant at property seven moves out; the deposit must be accounted for.
> - The manager's monthly fees are earned across all nine.
> - A tenant at property two pays with a check that is returned unpaid after the manager has already disbursed to that owner.
>
> **What each event requires.**
>
> **The partial rent** is recorded against that property's ledger for the amount actually received. **It is not rounded, netted, or recorded as full with a note.** Section 3.3 explains why the property-level ledger is what makes the account auditable.
>
> **The held disbursement** stays in trust and stays on the owner's ledger. **It is the owner's money being held on his instruction**, which is a different thing from money the manager has not gotten around to sending, and the instruction should be in writing.
>
> **The plumber's payment** comes from property four's funds and is recorded against property four. **A payment for one property made from another property's funds is a use of one owner's money for another's benefit**, and it is a violation regardless of whether the total balance was sufficient.
>
> **The move-out deposit** is accounted for on the terms the deposit framework requires, within the required period, with the itemization the framework prescribes. Section 3.5 governs. **The deposit was never the owner's money and it is not now the manager's**, and a deduction is documented by what was actually spent or reasonably estimated on stated terms.
>
> **The manager's fees** are earned when the agreement says they are earned, and **they are removed from the trust account** rather than left in it. Section 3.2's second obligation cuts both ways: broker funds do not stay in trust any more than trust funds go into the operating account.
>
> **The returned check is the month's real problem.** The manager disbursed money against funds that did not exist. **The account is now short**, and Section 3.4 governs: the shortage is corrected by the broker, immediately, from broker funds, and documented — **it is not carried until the tenant makes good, and it is not covered by the balances of the other eight properties**, which is exactly what "the account still has enough in it" means and exactly why that reasoning is wrong.
>
> **The reconciliation at month end** compares the bank balance to the sum of the individual ledgers, and Section 3.6 requires that both sides be examined. **A bank-to-book reconciliation alone would show this account balancing**, because the total is right; the property-level comparison is what reveals that property two's ledger is negative and property four paid property four's plumber with property four's money only because the total happened to cover it.
>
> **What the file shows afterward:** every receipt, every disbursement, the corrected shortage with its date and source, the owner's written hold instruction, the deposit accounting with its itemization, and a three-way reconciliation signed by the person who performed it.

## 4. Maintenance, habitability, and access

### 4.1 The habitability obligation

A residential landlord must maintain the property in a habitable condition. The obligation covers the elements that make a dwelling fit to live in — weather protection, plumbing, hot and cold water, heating, electrical, sanitary facilities, and structural integrity, among others.

Two features shape how a manager must respond:

1. **It is not waivable** by agreement. A lease clause purporting to relieve the landlord does not.
2. **It runs continuously**, not only at move-in.

### 4.2 Triage

Not every repair request is equal, and a manager who treats them uniformly will respond too slowly to the ones that matter.

| Category | Examples | Response |
|---|---|---|
| **Emergency** | No water, no heat in cold weather, sewage backup, gas odor, electrical hazard, security failure, flooding | Immediate; emergency authority applies |
| **Habitability** | Failed plumbing, non-functioning heat, pest infestation, mold, roof leak | Prompt, documented |
| **Ordinary** | Appliance malfunction, cosmetic damage, minor fixtures | Scheduled within a reasonable period |
| **Cosmetic or improvement** | Repainting by preference, upgrades | Owner decision |

A loss of water is not routine maintenance and does not wait for the next scheduled visit. Requiring a written request before responding, or referring the tenant to the owner, may be part of the process but neither justifies delaying the response.

### 4.3 Documentation

Every request and every response is recorded: when the request was received, its nature, when the manager responded, what was done, by whom, and what it cost. In a later dispute — with the tenant, with the owner, or with an insurer — that record is the entire case.

### 4.4 Lawful entry

A tenant has a possessory right, and entry requires:

1. **A permitted purpose** — necessary repairs, agreed services, showing the unit, inspection where permitted, or emergency.
2. **Proper notice**, in the manner and within the time the law requires.
3. **A reasonable time** of day.
4. **Documentation** that notice was given.

What does not substitute for notice: the tenant being home, the owner's authorization, the manager holding a key, or the convenience of the contractor's schedule.

### 4.5 Showing an occupied unit

Where a unit is shown while occupied — for re-rental or for sale — the entry rules apply to each showing. The practical approach is to agree a protocol with the tenant in advance, honor it, and document the notices.

A tenant who is cooperated with is far easier to show around than one whose rights are treated as an obstacle, and a manager who treats notice as optional will eventually meet a tenant who does not.

### 4.6 Habitability and the tenant's remedies

Where a habitability obligation goes unmet, a tenant may have remedies. A manager's practical concern is that the exposure grows with delay, and that a documented prompt response is the defense.

Where a tenant asserts a remedy, the matter should be escalated to the broker and, where appropriate, to counsel. It is not a matter for the manager to resolve by argument with the tenant.

### 4.7 A worked maintenance week

> **Monday.** A tenant reports no hot water. **Triage places this high** — Section 4.2 — because hot water is a habitability item rather than a convenience. The manager dispatches the same day, records the report time, the dispatch time, and the vendor.
>
> **Monday, later.** A different tenant reports a dripping faucet. Logged, scheduled, and communicated with a date. **The fact that it is minor does not mean it goes unanswered** — an unanswered minor request is how a tenant learns that reports do not produce responses, which is how the next urgent report arrives late.
>
> **Tuesday.** The hot water vendor reports the unit is failed and replacement exceeds the manager's spending authority. **Section 1.4 governs.** The manager reaches the owner, documents the contact and the authorization, and proceeds. **Where the owner cannot be reached and the condition is a habitability item, the emergency provision is what the manager relies on** — and this is why Section 1.6 argues for defining it in advance.
>
> **Wednesday.** The owner instructs the manager to install a used unit a friend is providing rather than the quoted replacement. The manager confirms in writing what was instructed, states what the requirement is for the work and the permit, and **routes the question rather than deciding it.** An owner's cost preference does not override the standard the work must meet.
>
> **Thursday.** A tenant at another property reports a broken window she says was broken by a neighbor's child. **Repair first, allocate later.** The security of the unit is a present condition; who pays is a separate question resolved afterward on the lease's terms and the facts.
>
> **Thursday, later.** The manager needs to enter a unit to inspect a reported leak. **Section 4.4 governs**, and the notice requirements apply to an inspection just as they apply to a repair. **A tenant's earlier report is not a standing invitation to enter**, and entering without the required notice converts a maintenance visit into a violation.
>
> **Friday.** A tenant who reported the hot water on Monday and has now had it fixed asks whether her rent will be reduced for the four days. **The manager does not answer this.** Whether any adjustment is owed is a legal question about remedies, and it goes to the broker and, where appropriate, counsel. **What the manager does is give the owner the facts and the dates**, which are exactly the facts the question will be decided on.
>
> **What the week produced in the file.** Every report with its date and time. Every dispatch with its vendor. Every authorization with who gave it. Every entry with its notice. Every invoice against the property it belongs to. **Section 4.3's documentation requirement is not a separate task performed at the end of the week — it is the week, written down as it happened.**
>
> **The failure this prevents.** Six months later a tenant alleges the hot water was out for three weeks and the manager entered her unit twice without notice. **The log answers both allegations in thirty seconds, or it answers neither.**

## 5. Lease enforcement and termination

### 5.1 Notice is where enforcement succeeds or fails

Termination and enforcement procedures are formal and unforgiving. **A good reason delivered by a defective notice fails**, and the landlord starts over — having lost weeks and given the tenant a stronger position.

The elements that must be right: the type of notice; its content; the period it gives; who is named; how it is served; and the proof of service. Each has requirements, and each is a common failure point.

### 5.2 What matters most, in order

1. **The correct notice type** for the circumstance.
2. **Correct content**, stating what the law requires it to state.
3. **Correct period**, calculated correctly.
4. **Correct service method**, with proof.
5. **Correct parties** named.

A manager who documents the tenant's conduct impeccably and then serves the wrong notice has accomplished nothing. Documentation matters — it just does not cure a defective notice.

### 5.3 Retaliation and its appearance

Enforcement action taken close in time to a tenant's complaint, repair request, or assertion of a right can look retaliatory, and California protects tenants against retaliation.

The manager's protection is a documented, independent basis for the action that predates or is unconnected to the tenant's protected activity. This is another reason the maintenance log matters: it establishes what happened and when.

### 5.4 Self-help is prohibited

Changing locks, removing doors, shutting off utilities, removing a tenant's belongings, or otherwise forcing a tenant out without process is unlawful and carries serious consequences.

There is no version of this that becomes acceptable because the tenant is badly behaved, has not paid, or has abandoned the unit in the manager's judgment. Where a unit appears abandoned, there is a legal process for that too.

### 5.5 Escalation

Termination and eviction are legal proceedings. The manager's role is to maintain the record, follow the process, and involve counsel — not to advise the owner on the strength of the case or predict the outcome.

An owner asking "can we just get them out by the first?" is asking a legal question, and the honest answer describes the process and its timeline rather than promising a date.

### 5.6 Move-out

At the end of a tenancy:

- **Document condition** as thoroughly as at move-in, dated, with photographs.
- **Compare against move-in documentation**, which is why move-in documentation exists.
- **Distinguish damage from ordinary wear and tear.**
- **Itemize deductions in writing** with supporting documentation.
- **Return the balance** within the required period.
- **Retain the record**, including the photographs.

## 6. Owner reporting and risk controls

### 6.1 Reporting discharges the accounting duty

The manager holds and handles the owner's money. Reporting is how the accounting duty is discharged, which is why it must be **regular and itemized** rather than summary.

An accounting that cannot be verified is not an accounting. Owner preference for more or less detail does not change the obligation.

### 6.2 What the owner receives

| Item | Frequency |
|---|---|
| Rent collected, by unit, with dates | Monthly |
| Delinquencies and their status | Monthly |
| Disbursements, itemized, with supporting documentation | Monthly |
| Management and leasing fees charged | Monthly |
| Reserve balance and trust funds held | Monthly |
| Security deposits held, by tenant | Monthly, and on any change |
| Maintenance activity, including requests and responses | Monthly |
| Vacancy and leasing activity | Monthly |
| Annual summary and tax reporting information | Annually |

### 6.3 An owner who has not been reported to for four months

That is not an administrative lapse. The owner has been unable to verify the handling of their own funds for a third of a year, the trust records have gone that long without external scrutiny, and if there is an error in them nobody has been in a position to find it.

An audit finding of that kind sits alongside commingling and unsigned agreements as a structural failure, not a filing backlog.

### 6.4 Risk controls that pay for themselves

1. **Move-in and move-out documentation**, dated, photographic, complete. Prevents most deposit disputes outright.
2. **A maintenance log** capturing request, response, action, and cost. Answers habitability and retaliation questions.
3. **Written screening criteria applied uniformly**, with documented evaluations. Answers fair housing questions.
4. **Monthly trust reconciliation** against beneficiary ledgers, not only bank against book.
5. **Written notices with proof of service**, retained.
6. **Insurance verification** — the owner's, and where required the tenant's.
7. **Vendor management** — licensing, insurance, and workmanship standards for contractors sent to the property.

### 6.5 The audit view

> A review of a management practice finds: security deposits in the operating account; one owner unreported for four months; two management agreements never signed; and a salesperson signing leases in her own name. The broker says nothing has gone wrong and no one has complained.
>
> **Each is an independent defect and none depends on harm having occurred.** Deposits are trust funds that may not be commingled. Owners are owed an accounting. Authority must rest on a signed agreement. And a salesperson may not contract in her own name for licensed activity — the lease is the brokerage's, executed for the broker.
>
> **The absence of complaints is not evidence of compliance.** It usually means the problems have not surfaced yet, and in a management practice — which repeats monthly across every unit — they will.

### 6.7 The owner who wants something the manager cannot do

Managers receive improper instructions more often than sales licensees do, because owners think of the property as theirs and of the manager as staff. **The instructions are usually not malicious. They are the owner solving a problem the only way he has thought of.**

| The instruction | Why it cannot be followed |
|---|---|
| **"Don't rent to [category]."** | Unlawful, whatever the phrasing, including where the owner offers a business reason. Lesson 03's obedience limit governs and the answer is refusal plus escalation. |
| **"Just change the locks, he hasn't paid in two months."** | Section 5.4. **Self-help is prohibited**, and the manager who performs it has done the unlawful act himself. |
| **"Shut off the water until she pays."** | The same category, and it compounds a habitability failure onto an unlawful eviction attempt. |
| **"Don't give the deposit back, he was a bad tenant."** | The deposit is governed by a framework with a procedure and a timeline. **"Bad tenant" is not a deduction category.** |
| **"Use this month's rent from the other building to cover my repair."** | One owner's funds for another's benefit. Section 3.7's plumber, in a more explicit form. |
| **"Skip the inspection this year."** | Where an inspection is required, it is required. **The manager does not certify or record that something happened when it did not.** |
| **"Tell the tenant the landlord is a management company"** when the owner wants to conceal his identity | Disclosure obligations about who the owner is exist for a reason, and misstating it is a misrepresentation. |
| **"Raise her rent, she keeps complaining."** | Section 5.3. **A rent increase following complaints has the appearance of retaliation** regardless of the owner's actual reason, and the appearance is itself the problem. |

**The response is the same in every row, and it has four parts:**

1. **Decline clearly.** Not "let me look into it" when the answer is already known.
2. **Say why**, in terms of what the manager may lawfully do rather than in terms of the owner's motives. **The owner is far more likely to accept a limit on the manager than an accusation about himself.**
3. **Offer the lawful path** where one exists — the notice procedure instead of the lockout, the deduction process instead of withholding the deposit, the documented business reason instead of the retaliatory-looking increase.
4. **Write it down the same day**, and send the confirmation to the owner. **The written record is what protects the manager if the owner does it himself**, and it is what the broker needs.

> **And the fifth step, when the owner insists:** this goes to the broker. **A manager who continues managing a property for an owner who has instructed him to break the law, without escalating, has made the brokerage's problem into his own.** Whether the engagement can continue is a brokerage decision, and Section 1.6's suggested term is what makes the conversation easier when it comes.

### 6.8 Checkpoint

You should be able to explain why an internal ledger does not cure commingling, name the three balances a trust reconciliation must agree, and state why a defective notice defeats a well-documented enforcement action.

| **Management agreement** | The contract between the owner and the **brokerage** establishing property, term, scope, spending authority, compensation, trust fund handling, reporting, owner obligations, insurance, and fair housing obligations. |
| **Unsigned authority** | A structural defect, not a filing gap. Scope is undefined, compensation is unenforceable, fund handling is unauthorized, and every action is disputable. |
| **Spending authority** | The threshold above which owner approval is required, paired with **emergency authority** below or outside it. |
| **Emergency authority** | Written authority to act without approval where waiting compounds damage while a habitability obligation runs. Its absence creates a genuine dilemma; the agreement removes it. |
| **Written screening criteria** | Objective standards established before applications are taken, disclosed to applicants, applied identically, and documented against each criterion. |
| **Differential application** | Applying neutral criteria strictly to some applicants and flexibly to others. Discrimination, even though the criteria are neutral. |
| **Discouragement** | Telling an applicant they probably would not qualify instead of taking and evaluating the application. Achieves a denial while producing no record of one. |
| **Occupancy standards** | Limits that must be reasonable and uniform. A limit restrictive enough to exclude families with children functions as familial status discrimination however phrased. |
| **Source of income** | A characteristic California protects. An instruction to refuse applicants using housing assistance is an unlawful instruction. |
| **Trust fund** | Money received in licensed activity belonging to someone else — rents, security deposits, owner reserves, application fees held pending processing. |
| **The three obligations** | Separation into a trust account or neutral depository; complete records identifying whose funds they are; and timely placement. |
| **Commingling** | Depositing trust funds into the wrong account. Complete on deposit, regardless of how carefully the total is tracked and regardless of whether anyone loses money. |
| **Why a ledger does not cure it** | Funds in an operating account are exposed to the brokerage's creditors, losses, and business risk. A ledger entry does not protect money; **separation** does, and the record proves it was maintained. |
| **Shortage** | Someone else's money missing. Cured immediately from the broker's own funds, with the cause documented. No tolerance threshold. |
| **Overage** | Records that do not accurately reflect whose funds are held. Investigated and documented; the broker may not simply keep it. |
| **Move-in documentation** | Dated, photographic, complete condition record. A deposit dispute turns on the difference between move-in and move-out condition, so a manager without it has no case. |
| **Ordinary wear and tear** | Not deductible from a security deposit. Distinguished from damage in the itemized written accounting. |
| **Three-way reconciliation** | Bank balance, book balance, and the **total of individual beneficiary ledgers** — all three must agree. Bank-to-book alone misses the error that matters most. |
| **Habitability** | The obligation to maintain the property fit to live in. **Not waivable** by agreement, and running continuously rather than only at move-in. |
| **Triage** | Sorting requests into emergency, habitability, ordinary, and cosmetic. A manager who treats them uniformly responds too slowly to the ones that matter. |
| **Lawful entry** | A permitted purpose, proper notice in the required manner and time, a reasonable hour, and documentation. Not substituted for by the tenant being home, the owner's authorization, holding a key, or a contractor's schedule. |
| **Retaliation** | Enforcement action close in time to a tenant's complaint or assertion of a right. The defense is a documented, independent basis predating or unconnected to the protected activity. |
| **Self-help** | Changing locks, removing doors, shutting off utilities, or removing belongings to force a tenant out. Unlawful, with no exception for a badly behaved or non-paying tenant. |
| **Defective notice** | A notice wrong in type, content, period, service, or parties. **A good reason delivered by a defective notice fails**, and documentation does not cure it. |

## Practice problems

### Problem 1

An audit of a management practice finds two owners with no signed management agreement. The broker
calls it a filing oversight — the properties have been managed for three years without complaint,
fees have been collected, and the owners are happy.

**Assess.**

> **Solution.** It is a defect of the same order as commingling, not a filing oversight.
>
> Acting without a signed agreement means acting **without established authority**: the scope is
> undefined so every decision is arguable, the compensation is unenforceable, the handling of funds is
> not authorized in writing, and the owner can dispute any action taken over three years.
>
> The absence of complaints is not evidence of compliance. In a management practice — which repeats
> monthly across every unit — it usually means the problems have not surfaced yet.
>
> **What transfers:** the same reasoning applies to the neighboring findings in a typical audit —
> deposits in the operating account, an owner unreported for months, a salesperson signing leases in
> her own name. Each is an independent defect and **none depends on harm having occurred.**

### Problem 2

An owner instructs: "No housing assistance, no service animals because of my no-pet rule, I'd prefer
a quiet single person over a family with kids, and that unit has two steps at the entry so it won't
work for the applicant in a wheelchair anyway."

**Address each clause and identify three responses that do not work.**

> **Solution.** Every clause is a problem. **Source of income** and **familial status** are protected in
> California. **A service animal is not a pet** — a no-pet policy does not by itself justify denial, no
> pet deposit or pet rent may be charged, and certification may not be required. And **deciding on the
> applicant's behalf** that a unit will not work for him is not the manager's decision; accessibility is
> addressed through accommodation and modification, not exclusion — here the entry steps are a possible
> modification question.
>
> **The response:** refuse the instructions, explain each, evaluate the applicant on the same written
> criteria as everyone else, treat the steps as a modification question, and escalate to the broker on
> whether the brokerage can act for this owner.
>
> **Three that do not work:** applying only the "quiet tenant" preference by recasting familial status
> as a noise concern; documenting that the instructions came from the owner and following them anyway;
> and declining the engagement quietly without explaining the problem, which leaves the owner free to
> repeat it with the next manager.

### Problem 3

A manager says she selects tenants by getting a feel for people at the showing, favors applicants
referred by existing tenants, and tells applicants she doubts will qualify that the owner is "looking
for someone long-term" rather than taking their applications.

**Identify the problem in each, and state the defensible practice.**

> **Solution.** **Impression-based selection** is exactly where bias operates and cannot be explained
> afterwards in neutral terms. **Referral preference** reproduces the existing composition of the
> building at scale — the same effect as choosing whoever the current tenants would get along with.
> **And the third is the most dangerous**: discouraging an application rather than denying it achieves
> a denial while producing no record of one, which is why it is harder to detect.
>
> **The defensible practice:** written criteria established before applications are taken; objective
> standards — income relative to rent, credit history, rental history, verifiable identity, lawful
> occupancy standards; applied identically to every applicant; documented as to how each application
> was evaluated against each criterion; and disclosed to applicants before they apply, along with any
> application fee.
>
> Uniformity is not merely fair. It is what makes a decision **explainable** afterwards.

### Problem 4

A manager deposits a month's rents and three security deposits into the brokerage operating account.
He points out that his accounting software tracks each beneficiary separately to the cent, that the
total is correct, and that no one has lost a dollar.

**Why does this fail, and what is the correct response?**

> **Solution.** **Commingling was complete on deposit.** It does not depend on the total being wrong or
> on anyone losing money.
>
> The structural reason the ledger does not cure it: funds in a brokerage operating account are exposed
> to the brokerage's **creditors, operating losses, and ordinary business risk.** A ledger entry does
> not protect the money. **Separation protects it**, and the record proves the separation was
> maintained.
>
> **The correct response** to the mistake: correct it immediately, **document the error and the
> correction**, and notify the broker so the trust records reconcile and the cause is addressed.
> Correcting it quietly without a record is its own problem — trust accounting is judged by the record
> as much as by the balance.
>
> Related: a **shortage** is cured immediately from the broker's own funds with the cause documented;
> an **overage** is investigated and documented and may not simply be kept. Neither is a rounding
> matter, neither has a tolerance threshold, and neither waits for the next reconciliation.

### Problem 5

A monthly reconciliation shows the bank balance agreeing with the book balance to the cent. The
manager reports the trust account as clean. Two months later a tenant's deposit turns out to have been
credited to a different tenant's ledger.

**What did the reconciliation miss, and why?**

> **Solution.** **The third balance.** A trust reconciliation compares the bank balance, the book
> balance, **and the total of the individual beneficiary ledgers** — and all three must agree.
>
> A reconciliation that balances bank against book alone cannot detect the error that matters most:
> money in the account belonging to a **different beneficiary** than the records say. The total was
> right the whole time, which is exactly why it went unnoticed for two months.
>
> **What transfers:** the same shape as the commingling defense in Problem 4. A correct total is not
> evidence that the right people's money is being held correctly.

### Problem 6

A tenant reports on Friday morning that the unit has had no water since Thursday night. The manager
tells him to submit a written request, that the plumber's next routine visit is Tuesday, and that he
should contact the owner directly. Separately, the manager lets the plumber in on Tuesday without
notice because the tenant is home.

**Assess the response and the entry.**

> **Solution.** **A loss of water is an emergency**, not routine maintenance, and it sits at the top of
> the triage list alongside no heat in cold weather, sewage backup, gas odor, electrical hazard,
> security failure, and flooding. Emergency authority exists precisely so this does not wait for
> approval.
>
> The habitability obligation is **not waivable** and runs **continuously** rather than only at
> move-in. Requiring a written request may be part of the process and referring to the owner may be
> part of the structure, but **neither justifies delaying the response** — and the exposure grows with
> the delay.
>
> **The entry is separately unlawful.** Entry requires a permitted purpose, proper notice in the manner
> and time the law requires, a reasonable hour, and documentation that notice was given. The tenant
> being home does not substitute for notice — nor does the owner's authorization, the manager holding a
> key, or the convenience of the contractor's schedule.
>
> **The record is the case.** Every request and response — when received, its nature, when answered,
> what was done, by whom, and at what cost — is what answers a later dispute with the tenant, the
> owner, or an insurer.

### Problem 7

A tenant files a complaint with the city about a persistent roof leak. Eleven days later the manager
serves a termination notice. The stated reason is a lease violation the manager has been documenting
for four months. The notice names only one of the two tenants on the lease and is served by leaving it
under the door. The owner then asks whether they can "just change the locks if she won't leave."

**Assess the retaliation exposure, the notice, and the owner's question.**

> **Solution.** **The timing creates a retaliation appearance**, and California protects tenants against
> retaliation. The manager's protection here is real but must be shown: a **documented, independent
> basis** predating or unconnected to the protected activity — which is what four months of
> contemporaneous records provide. This is another reason the maintenance and enforcement logs matter.
>
> **The notice is defective on at least two elements** — correct parties named, and correct service
> method with proof. Termination procedures are formal and unforgiving: type, content, period, parties,
> service, and proof of service each have requirements, and **a good reason delivered by a defective
> notice fails.** The landlord starts over, having lost weeks and given the tenant a stronger position.
> Impeccable documentation of the conduct does not cure it.
>
> **The owner's question is answered flatly: no.** Changing locks, removing doors, shutting off
> utilities, or removing belongings is unlawful self-help, with no exception for a badly behaved or
> non-paying tenant — and where a unit appears abandoned, there is a legal process for that too.
>
> "Can we get them out by the first?" is a **legal question**. The honest answer describes the process
> and its timeline rather than promising a date, and the matter goes to counsel. The manager maintains
> the record and follows the process; the manager does not assess the strength of the case.

### Problem 8

A tenancy ends. The manager withholds the full deposit for carpet replacement and repainting, sending
the tenant a one-line note four weeks later. There is no move-in condition record. Separately, one
owner has received no report for four months.

**Assess both.**

> **Solution.** **The deposit handling fails on every element.** A deposit dispute is decided on the
> difference between **move-in and move-out condition**, and a manager without move-in documentation
> has no case — which is the whole reason move-in documentation exists. **Ordinary wear and tear is not
> deductible**, and carpet wear and repainting after a tenancy are the standard examples. The
> termination accounting must be **itemized in writing with supporting documentation** and delivered
> within the required time, with the balance returned within the required period. A one-line note four
> weeks later is none of that.
>
> Nearly all deposit complaints — the single most common source of tenant complaints — are prevented by
> two practices: dated photographic documentation at move-in and move-out, and an itemized written
> accounting.
>
> **The unreported owner is not an administrative lapse.** The owner has been unable to verify the
> handling of their own money for a third of a year, the trust records have gone that long without
> external scrutiny, and if there is an error nobody has been positioned to find it. **Reporting is how
> the accounting duty is discharged** — regular and itemized, not summary — and an accounting that
> cannot be verified is not an accounting. Owner preference for less detail does not change the
> obligation.

## Chapter summary

**Management is licensed activity performed for the broker, with trust funds and brokerage records** —
and unlike a sale, it is continuous, so a defective practice repeats monthly across every unit.

**The agreement is with the brokerage**, and unsigned authority is a structural defect: undefined
scope, unenforceable compensation, unauthorized fund handling, and disputable actions.

**Settle spending and emergency authority in writing**, so no manager has to choose between acting
without authority and letting damage compound.

**Written criteria, objective standards, uniform application, documented evaluation, disclosed in
advance.** Impression, compatibility, referral preference, differential strictness, and discouragement
are each how bias enters — and the last leaves no record of the denial it accomplishes.

**Source of income and familial status are protected in California**; assistance animals are not pets;
and accessibility is addressed by accommodation and modification, never by exclusion.

**Trust funds require separation, records, and timeliness.** A ledger does not cure commingling,
because a ledger entry does not shield money from the brokerage's creditors and losses — separation
does.

**Reconcile three balances**, not two. Bank against book alone misses money held for the wrong
beneficiary.

**Move-in documentation is what wins deposit disputes**, wear and tear is not deductible, and the
termination accounting is itemized, supported, and timely.

**Habitability is not waivable and runs continuously.** Triage requests, respond promptly to
emergencies, and record request, response, action, and cost — that record is the entire case later.

**Entry requires purpose, notice, a reasonable hour, and documentation.** Nothing about the tenant
being home, the owner's say-so, or a contractor's schedule substitutes.

**A defective notice defeats a good reason.** Type, content, period, parties, service, proof — each
must be right, and documentation of the conduct does not cure a defect in the notice.

**Self-help is prohibited without exception**, and termination is a legal proceeding for counsel, not
a date to promise an owner.

**Reporting discharges the accounting duty.** Regular, itemized, verifiable — and an owner who has not
been reported to for months is a structural failure, not a backlog. The absence of complaints is not
evidence of compliance.
