## 1. Opening escrow

### 1.1 What escrow is

Escrow is a neutral third party holding funds and documents under the parties' conditional delivery, authorized to act **only according to their mutual written instructions**.

Two elements make it an escrow: a **binding contract between the parties**, and **conditional delivery** to a neutral holder. Without both, what exists is a stakeholder arrangement, not an escrow.

### 1.2 Neutrality, and what it forbids

Escrow's neutrality is not a courtesy. It is the structural feature that lets both parties rely on the same holder, and it produces limits licensees must be able to explain:

| Escrow may | Escrow may not |
|---|---|
| Follow mutual written instructions | Act on one party's instruction alone |
| Hold funds and documents | Advise either party |
| Prepare and circulate documents per the instructions | Decide a dispute between the parties |
| Record and disburse when conditions are met | Release funds because one party says they are entitled |
| Report status to both parties | Take a position on who is right |

Escrow is sometimes described as a **limited dual agent** — owing duties to both parties simultaneously, bounded strictly by the instructions. That description is useful precisely because it explains why escrow stops rather than deciding when the parties disagree.

### 1.3 A worked test of neutrality

> The day before recording, the buyer emails escrow: hold back $3,000 from the seller's proceeds for a repair the seller promised verbally. The seller knows nothing about it.
>
> **Escrow may not act on it.** A change to the disbursement requires mutual written instructions. One party cannot redirect funds.
>
> **Nor may escrow simply disburse in full**, which also acts on one side's position where a genuine dispute has been raised.
>
> **Nor does the buyer's agent confirming the request supply the missing consent.** An agent is not the other party.
>
> **The correct outcome:** escrow stops. The parties resolve it in writing, and the resolution reaches escrow as a mutual instruction. The agents' job is to get that done before the closing date, not to argue with escrow.

### 1.4 Who selects escrow

Selection is negotiable between the parties and is often addressed in the purchase agreement. Two points for the licensee:

- **A licensee must not accept anything of value for directing settlement business.** Referrals are ordinary; payment for them is a serious federal problem, and disclosure does not cure it.
- **Where the brokerage has a financial interest** in an escrow or title provider, that is an affiliated business arrangement, and it carries disclosure and other conditions.

### 1.5 Opening properly

What escrow needs at opening: the fully executed purchase agreement and all amendments; the parties' contact and identification details; the deposit per the contract; lender contact where financing is involved; and title order details.

The licensee's contribution is accuracy and completeness. An escrow opened on a partially executed contract, or without the amendment that changed a term, will produce instructions that do not match the agreement — which surfaces later, usually at the worst time.

### 1.6 Escrow instructions, and amending them

Escrow acts on instructions. That sentence explains most of what goes wrong.

**The instructions are not the purchase agreement.** They are a separate set of directions to the escrow holder, and where the two differ, escrow follows what it was instructed to do. **A term negotiated in an amendment that never reached escrow does not exist as far as escrow is concerned**, which is why Section 5.7 of Lesson 10 finds missing credits on closing statements.

**What this means in practice:**

1. **Every amendment goes to escrow when it is signed**, not in a batch before closing. The licensee's transmittal is dated and recorded.
2. **Instructions are amended in writing by the parties**, and the escrow holder will not act on an agent's phone call.
3. **Both parties must instruct.** A change one party wants is not a change until the other has instructed it too, and a licensee who tells his client "it's handled" after a one-sided call has misinformed him.
4. **Conflicting instructions stop escrow rather than being resolved by it.** Section 1.2's neutrality forbids the escrow holder from deciding between the parties, so a conflict produces a hold, not a ruling.
5. **The licensee does not draft instructions or interpret them for the client.** Their legal effect is a legal question.

**Two situations that recur.**

**The late verbal change.** A seller agrees on the phone to a credit two days before closing. Unless it becomes a signed amendment and reaches escrow, the closing statement will not show it, and the discovery happens at signing with everyone in a room. **The fix is the same day: reduce it to writing, get both signatures, send it to escrow, confirm receipt.**

**The instruction the client did not read.** Clients sign escrow instructions the way they sign everything, and then are surprised by a provision. **The licensee's contribution is to tell the client, before signing, that these are operative directions rather than formalities, and that questions about their effect go to counsel** — not to answer the questions himself.

> **A useful mental model:** the purchase agreement is what the parties owe each other. The escrow instructions are what a neutral third party has been told to do. **Keeping them aligned is a task somebody has to perform on every file, and on most files that somebody is the licensee.**

## 2. Title and vesting review

### 2.1 What a preliminary report is

A preliminary report reports the condition of title as shown in the records and states **the terms on which the title insurer would be willing to issue a policy**, including the exceptions it would take.

Three things it is not:

- **Not a guarantee** that title is clear.
- **Not the policy.** It is superseded by the policy, not replaced by it.
- **Not a boundary or condition survey.** It addresses record title, not the physical property.

### 2.2 Exceptions, and what they mean

An **exception** is an item the policy will not insure against. That is the entire significance, and it is why exceptions must be read rather than skimmed:

> If an item is excepted, the buyer bears that risk unless it is otherwise addressed.

Common exceptions include easements, covenants and restrictions, mineral rights reservations, assessments, and matters a survey would disclose.

### 2.3 Reading the report as a licensee

The licensee's role is to **ensure the buyer reviews the underlying documents and understands their effect on his intended use** — and to route legal questions to counsel.

| Item | Correct handling |
|---|---|
| Utility easement along the rear line | Obtain and review the document: width, location, permitted use. A rear-line easement can prevent structures the buyer intends to build. |
| Recorded CC&Rs | Obtain and review; use restrictions bind the buyer regardless of zoning |
| An unreleased deed of trust the seller says was paid | A recorded reconveyance or other clearing document must be obtained; the seller's recollection does not clear the record |
| A mechanic's lien | Route to escrow and title; note that its priority may relate back to when work commenced |
| A judgment lien against a similarly named person | Route to title for resolution; do not assume it is the seller |
| An item the buyer does not understand | Refer to counsel |

Three responses that are wrong in every case: treating an item as routine because most properties have one, asking the title company to "remove" an exception, and predicting the practical effect of an easement on the buyer's plans.

### 2.4 Vesting is not the licensee's question

How the buyer takes title — sole ownership, joint tenancy, tenancy in common, community property in its forms, a trust, an entity — carries **ownership, survivorship, tax, and estate consequences**.

A licensee may explain that the choice matters and that it has those consequences. A licensee may **not** advise which to choose. The correct action is a referral to legal and tax counsel **before escrow prepares the deed**, because changing it later is neither free nor simple.

> Two unmarried siblings buying together say they want the survivor to take the whole property automatically, **and** each to be able to leave her share to her own children.
>
> Those two goals point in different directions. Resolving that tension is estate planning, not a form selection, and it belongs to counsel.

### 2.5 Title insurance

The owner's policy insures the buyer's interest; the lender's policy insures the lender's lien and its priority. They insure different interests, which is why both commonly exist.

What a policy does: indemnifies the insured against covered losses from title defects, **within its terms and excluding what it excepts**. What it does not do: guarantee perfect title, insure physical condition, or oblige the insurer to clear what it excepted.

### 2.6 A worked preliminary report review

> **The report** on a suburban parcel lists, among the exceptions: general and special taxes for the current fiscal year; a supplemental assessment; a deed of trust in favor of a lender; an easement for public utilities recorded in 1962; covenants, conditions and restrictions recorded in 1958; an easement for ingress and egress in favor of the adjoining parcel recorded in 1979; a mechanic's lien recorded four months ago; a notice of a special assessment district; and an abstract of judgment against a person with the same surname as the seller.
>
> **How a licensee reads this — and Section 2.3's boundary applies throughout: he identifies items that require attention and routes the legal questions.**
>
> **The mechanic's lien is the first item.** It is recent, it is a monetary encumbrance, and it must be resolved or addressed before the buyer takes title. **It also tells a story:** work was done on the property recently and someone was not paid. **That is a fact about the property's condition and history**, and it belongs in the disclosure conversation as well as the title conversation.
>
> **The abstract of judgment against a similar name is the item most often mishandled.** It may be the seller. It may be an unrelated person with the same surname. **This is resolved by the title company through its own process**, and the licensee's role is to flag it early — because if it is the seller, it must be dealt with, and if it is not, clearing the record takes time nobody scheduled.
>
> **The 1979 ingress and egress easement is a use question the buyer needs to understand.** Someone has a recorded right to cross this property. **Where it runs, how wide it is, and what it permits are answered by the recorded document**, which the buyer should obtain and read. A buyer planning a fence, a structure, or a landscaping project across it will discover the problem later at greater cost.
>
> **The 1958 covenants and restrictions may constrain use** — setbacks, permitted structures, architectural approval, or provisions that are no longer enforceable. **Which provisions still operate is a legal question**, and it is one buyers ask licensees constantly.
>
> **The utility easement from 1962** is ordinary and still worth locating, for the same reason as the ingress easement.
>
> **The special assessment district and the supplemental assessment** are cost items the buyer's budget has probably not included, and the supplemental bill in particular surprises first-time buyers after closing. **Section 5.5 of Lesson 10 and this exception are the same subject arriving from two directions.**
>
> **The deed of trust** is expected and will be paid at closing from the seller's proceeds.
>
> **What the licensee does.** Deliver the report to the buyer promptly with a written recommendation to review it with the title officer and, for the recorded documents, with counsel; obtain the underlying documents for the easements and restrictions rather than relying on the one-line description; raise the lien and the judgment with escrow and the listing side immediately; and record the delivery and the recommendation.
>
> **What the licensee does not do.** Tell the buyer the restrictions are "old and unenforceable," that the easement is "just for the utility company," that the judgment is "probably a different person," or that the lien is "the seller's problem." **Four characterizations, none of which he has a basis for, each of which a buyer will rely on.**

## 3. Transaction coordination

### 3.1 Parallel tracks

A residential transaction runs several tracks at once, each with its own deadlines and its own failure modes:

| Track | Owner | Typical failure |
|---|---|---|
| **Contract deadlines** | The agents | A notice missed or delivered by the wrong method |
| **Investigation** | Buyer, with the agent's coordination | Started late, so findings arrive with no time to act |
| **Disclosure delivery** | Seller and both agents | Delivered late; delivery not evidenced |
| **Financing** | Buyer and lender | Conditions unsatisfied; a change restarts the timeline |
| **Appraisal** | Lender | Ordered late; value below price |
| **Title** | Escrow and title | An exception discovered late that takes time to clear |
| **Escrow** | Escrow | Instructions that do not match the agreement |
| **Insurance** | Buyer | Availability or cost, especially in mapped hazard areas |

Most transactions that fail do not fail because one track was impossible. They fail because a track started late.

### 3.2 What the licensee actually coordinates

1. **Build the deadline calendar from the contract** and share it in writing.
2. **Start the slow items first** — insurance in hazard areas, association documents, permit research, specialist inspections.
3. **Confirm status weekly in writing**, and record the answers.
4. **Escalate early.** A problem raised on day ten has options; the same problem on day twenty-eight has few.
5. **Reconcile escrow's instructions against the agreement**, including every amendment.

### 3.3 The insurance track

Insurance deserves its own attention in California. In mapped fire and flood areas, availability and cost have become a common cause of failed transactions, and the problem is discovered late because buyers treat insurance as a formality.

The advice: **obtain quotes early in the contingency period**, not at the end. A buyer who learns in week one that coverage will cost far more than assumed can act; a buyer who learns it two days before closing generally cannot.

### 3.4 Communication that prevents disputes

A short written status note at each milestone — what has been delivered, what remains, what the next date is — does more to prevent disputes than any other practice, because most disputes are about whether something was delivered and when.

### 3.5 How coordination fails

Section 3.1 describes the parallel tracks. Coordination failures have a small number of recognizable shapes, and each has a control.

| The failure | What it looks like | The control |
|---|---|---|
| **The unowned task** | Everyone assumed someone else ordered the association documents | A written task list with a name against each item, made at opening |
| **The silent dependency** | The appraisal cannot be scheduled until access is arranged with a tenant nobody has contacted | Map what each item waits on, not just when it is due |
| **The stale assumption** | Work continues on the belief that a condition cleared last week | Confirm status rather than assume continuity; verify at each check-in |
| **The one-way message** | An email sent, no response, no follow-up, and the recipient never saw it | Follow-up is scheduled, not remembered; no consequential item rests on an unacknowledged message |
| **The out-of-band change** | Parties agree to something in a phone call and no document follows | Same-day written confirmation of every agreement, without exception |
| **The vacation gap** | A participant is out and their work stops without anyone knowing | Ask who covers, at opening, for every party |
| **The last-week compression** | Four items all land in the final three days | Front-load: order everything orderable in week one |

**The two that cause the most damage are the unowned task and the silent dependency**, because both are invisible until the deadline. Nothing looks wrong — no one is behind on anything, because no one has started.

**The practical instrument is a single document**, made the day escrow opens, listing every item the transaction needs, who owns it, what it depends on, and when it must be complete. It is reviewed at each check-in, and the review takes minutes.

> **What it replaces** is a licensee holding the entire transaction in his head, which works until he has four transactions, and then fails on all four at once. Lesson 02's capacity analysis and this document are the same idea applied at two scales.

**One communication rule sits above all of it.** Section 3.4 explains why communication prevents disputes; the operational form is that **the client hears from the licensee on a schedule, including when nothing has happened.** A client who has to ask what is going on has already begun to lose confidence, and most cancellation requests — Section 6.3 of Lesson 04 — start there rather than with an actual failure.

## 4. Final verification and closing

### 4.1 What the final verification is

The buyer's final verification of condition — the walkthrough — confirms that the property is in the condition the contract requires: agreed repairs completed, included items present, and the property not materially changed since the buyer's investigation.

It is **not** an inspection, and it is not a new opportunity to renegotiate condition the buyer already accepted.

### 4.2 What to check

- **Agreed repairs**, against the amendment that agreed them, with any documentation required.
- **Included personal property** present — appliances, fixtures, window coverings, anything named in the contract.
- **Systems operating** as they were.
- **No new damage**, including damage from the seller's move-out.
- **Property vacant** where the contract requires it, and personal property removed.

### 4.3 Responding to a problem

> At the final verification the buyer finds an agreed repair was not completed.
>
> **The correct path:** document the finding — photographs, dated notes — and address it through the contract **before closing**, because the ordinary leverage to resolve it disappears once the transaction closes.
>
> **What does not work:** closing as scheduled and pursuing the seller afterward, which converts a solvable issue into a claim; instructing escrow to withhold funds, which escrow cannot do without mutual instruction; or treating the walkthrough as informational because it is not an inspection.

### 4.4 The closing sequence

Four events that licensees regularly conflate, in order:

1. **Signing.** The parties execute the closing documents. The transaction is not closed.
2. **Funding.** The lender releases loan proceeds to escrow, subject to its conditions.
3. **Recording.** The deed and security instrument are recorded, establishing the interests of record and giving constructive notice. **This is generally when the buyer becomes the owner of record.**
4. **Disbursement.** Escrow disburses once recording is confirmed.

Recording precedes disbursement deliberately: it prevents a gap in which money has moved but the interests are not of record.

### 4.5 Recording order matters

Where multiple instruments record, **the order establishes lien priority.** An out-of-sequence recording can place a new lender behind an interest it was to be ahead of. This is why closing is sequenced rather than simultaneous, and it is not a clerical preference.

### 4.6 Wire fraud at closing

The highest-risk moment in the transaction is when the buyer sends funds. The pattern is covered in Lesson 02; the closing-specific practices are:

1. **Warn the buyer in writing, early**, that wire instructions will never change by email and that he must verify by voice before sending.
2. **Verify by calling escrow at an independently obtained number** — never a number from the email carrying the instructions.
3. **Treat any change to instructions as presumptively fraudulent** until verified through that independent channel.

A licensee who sends that warning at the start of escrow, rather than the day funds move, has done the single most valuable thing available to protect a client's money.

### 4.7 A worked final verification

> **The walkthrough**, the afternoon before a scheduled closing. The buyer and his agent find:
>
> - **(a)** The refrigerator is gone. It appeared in the listing photographs and was not excluded in the agreement.
> - **(b)** A repair the seller agreed to make in a signed amendment — replacing a failed water heater — has been done, but the unit is not strapped and there is no permit record.
> - **(c)** Water staining on the garage ceiling that was not present at the inspection five weeks ago.
> - **(d)** The seller's belongings fill half the garage. He says the movers come "the day after close."
> - **(e)** A hole in a bedroom wall where a mounted television was removed.
> - **(f)** The rear slider does not lock; it did at the inspection.
>
> **Sorting these, because they are not the same kind of problem.**
>
> **(c) is the serious one.** New water intrusion since the inspection is **a change in the property's condition**, and its cause is unknown. It may be a roof failure, a plumbing failure, or the new water heater in item (b). **The buyer is being asked to close tomorrow on a property with an active unexplained condition.** This is escalated immediately — to both brokers — and it is a legitimate basis for the buyer to seek a delay while it is evaluated. **It is not resolved by a credit negotiated in a hallway.**
>
> **(b) is a performance question with a safety component.** The amendment required a replacement; what was delivered may not meet the requirements applicable to the work. **Whether the amendment was satisfied is a contract question**, and the permit and strapping issues are matters for the appropriate authority and a qualified professional rather than for the licensees' judgment.
>
> **(d) is a possession problem and it is bigger than it looks.** Belongings in the garage after closing means the seller occupies the property after title transfers, which Section 5.2 addresses. **A day is not nothing** — it raises insurance, liability, condition, and what happens if the movers do not come. **It requires a written agreement, not a handshake at the walkthrough.**
>
> **(a) and (e) are ordinary items** — a fixture-versus-personal-property question and damage from removing one. Section 1.6 of Lesson 08 explains why the refrigerator question exists at all. They are negotiated or credited, and the resolution is written.
>
> **(f) is small and belongs on the list anyway**, because a lock that no longer works is a security item on the property the buyer takes possession of tomorrow.
>
> **What the agents do, in order.**
>
> **Document everything now** — photographs, dated, with locations, before anyone moves anything or begins negotiating.
>
> **Notify both brokers and escrow the same day**, because a closing scheduled for tomorrow may not happen and every participant's schedule depends on knowing.
>
> **Take the items to the parties as facts rather than as demands.** The buyer decides what he wants; Section 4.3 governs the response and the options belong to him.
>
> **Reduce whatever is agreed to writing before closing**, and get it to escrow — Section 1.6 of this lesson.
>
> **What must not happen:** closing on a verbal promise to fix item (c) afterward, allowing the seller to remain in the garage without a written agreement, or a licensee reassuring his client that the staining is "probably from the old heater" — **a characterization of a building condition by a person not qualified to make it**, and one made under the pressure of a closing date.

## 5. Recording, disbursement, and possession

### 5.1 Possession is not ownership

Ownership transfers at recording. **Possession transfers when the contract says it does**, which is frequently the same day and frequently not.

Where they diverge, the arrangement must be documented.

### 5.2 Post-closing occupancy

Where the seller remains after closing, a written occupancy agreement should address:

| Term | Why |
|---|---|
| Duration and end date | The holdover risk is the whole problem |
| Payment | What the seller pays, if anything |
| Condition on delivery | The standard the property must meet |
| Insurance | Who insures what, in whose name |
| Utilities and maintenance | Responsibility during the period |
| Deposit or holdback | Security for damage or holdover |
| Remedy for failure to vacate | The point at which this becomes expensive |

A brief term does not reduce the need for terms. "They only need three days" is how a holdover with no agreed remedy begins, and the buyer now owns a property occupied by someone with no obligation he can enforce quickly.

### 5.3 Early possession before closing

Riskier still, and generally to be discouraged. A buyer occupying before closing who then fails to close leaves the seller with an occupant and a property that must be re-marketed. Where it happens at all, it should be documented at least as carefully as post-closing occupancy, with counsel involved.

### 5.4 Keys, access, and the transfer of control

Practical items that generate complaints when handled loosely: when keys transfer and from whom; garage and gate remotes, mailbox keys, association access devices; changing codes on smart locks and alarms; and confirming the seller's remote access to cameras and smart devices is removed.

That last one has become a real issue. A seller who retains app access to interior cameras after closing is a problem, and it should be addressed explicitly rather than assumed.

### 5.5 Utilities and services

Coordinate transfer dates so that services are not interrupted and neither party is billed for the other's period. This is administrative rather than legal, and it is a common source of avoidable client dissatisfaction.

### 5.6 Confirming closing to the client

When recording is confirmed, tell the client — in writing, with the date and what it means. Clients frequently do not know whether they own the property, and the licensee is the person they will ask.

### 5.7 Week one after closing

Most post-closing calls arrive in the first ten days, and they follow a short list. Knowing the list lets a licensee handle each correctly rather than defensively.

| The call | The correct handling |
|---|---|
| **"The [system] doesn't work."** | Sympathy, then process. What the contract provided, whether a warranty applies, and — where the buyer believes a condition was concealed — **a matter for the broker rather than an argument between agents.** The licensee does not diagnose and does not concede. |
| **"There's a bill I wasn't expecting."** | Frequently a supplemental tax assessment or an association charge. Explain what it is; **the amount and its calculation come from the assessing authority or the association.** |
| **"The seller left things behind."** | A cleanup and possession question. Where a post-closing occupancy agreement exists, it governs; where it does not, this is why Section 5.2 requires one. |
| **"The keys don't work for the side gate."** | Section 5.4's transfer of control was incomplete. Fix it, and note for future files that "keys" means every point of access. |
| **"Nobody told me about [X]."** | **The most important call on this list.** Determine what the file shows was delivered and when, before responding. **Do not answer from memory** — Section 5.2 of Lesson 09 exists for this moment. |
| **"Can you send me everything for my taxes?"** | Provide copies of the transaction documents. **The tax questions themselves go to the client's tax advisor**, without exception and without a "generally speaking." |
| **"My neighbor says the property line isn't where I think."** | A survey and legal question. Refer, and do not offer an opinion about the boundary. |

**Three disciplines apply to all of them.**

**Respond quickly, even when the answer is "I need to check."** Most post-closing complaints escalate because of silence rather than because of substance.

**Check the file before answering.** A licensee who says "you were definitely given that" and turns out to be wrong has converted a service problem into a credibility problem.

**Loop in the broker on anything that sounds like a claim.** Not later, and not only if it develops. **The first communication about a potential claim is the one that matters most**, and it should not be improvised by the person whose work is being questioned.

> **And one thing not to do:** offer to pay for something out of your own pocket to make a complaint go away. It feels generous, it is fast, and it is a decision with implications for the brokerage, for insurance, and for what the payment is later said to mean. **That decision belongs to the broker.**

## 6. Post-closing file audit

### 6.1 Audit while memories are fresh

The most useful review of a transaction file happens shortly after closing. Its purpose is to confirm the file can answer questions later, and to catch anything obtainable while the participants still respond to messages.

### 6.2 The audit questions

1. **Completeness.** Every required document present — agency disclosures, agreement, offer and counteroffers, disclosures, notices, amendments, closing documents.
2. **Dates.** Every document dated, and delivery evidenced where timing matters.
3. **Consistency.** Names, dates, amounts, and property descriptions reconcile across documents.
4. **Chronology.** The sequence makes sense against the contract's deadlines — the agency disclosure precedes the agreement, disclosures precede contingency removal, amendments precede the dates they change.
5. **Nothing improper present.** No unexecuted draft mistaken for final, no superseded figure presented as current.

### 6.3 Keep the superseded versions

The instinct to keep only final documents should be resisted. **The sequence of versions is frequently what shows when a term changed and who agreed to it.** A file containing only executed finals can prove what was agreed but not how the parties arrived there — which is the question a dispute usually asks.

### 6.4 What to do with findings

- **Obtain what legitimately exists** — a missing copy, an unrecorded acknowledgment.
- **Record what does not**, plainly.
- **Escalate to the broker.**
- **Address the process** that produced the gap, not only the instance.

### 6.5 The line

> Creating a document now and dating it to match the transaction, or removing surrounding records so an inconsistency does not show, is **falsification**.
>
> A documented gap is survivable. A manufactured document is not, and it converts a compliance failure into conduct that ends a career.

The same principle governs corrections generally: they are made visibly, with the original preserved and the change attributable. A record that can be silently altered is not a record.

### 6.6 Retention

The file is retained for the required period, which runs from closing. Cancelled and failed transactions are retained too — and are disproportionately the ones that later produce disputes, which is exactly why they must not be discarded as uninteresting.

### 6.7 What the client asks for later

The relationship does not end at closing, and the requests that arrive months or years afterward have their own rules.

**Copies of documents.** Ordinary and easily satisfied — **if the file is complete and findable.** This is the least dramatic argument for the retention discipline in Section 6.6 and the one that actually comes up: a client refinancing, appealing an assessment, settling an estate, or preparing a return needs a document from a transaction the licensee handled, and either it is in the file or it is not.

**Tax questions.** Referred, every time. Holding period, basis, exclusions, exchanges, and the treatment of credits and improvements are the tax advisor's work. **"I'm not a tax advisor, and here is why that question is worth asking one" is a complete answer**, and the client is better served by it than by a confident summary.

**A value opinion.** Section 5.5 of Lesson 05 governs. **Ask what it is for before preparing it**, because the answer determines whether the licensee may prepare it at all.

**Referrals to service providers.** Section 5.6 of Lesson 03 applies unchanged. **Time does not extinguish the disclosure obligation** — a referral fee received on a contractor referral two years after closing is the same conflict it would have been during escrow.

**Information about the other party.** A former client asking for the buyer's contact information, or for something the licensee learned about him during the transaction. **Confidentiality survives**, and information about a party who is not the licensee's client was never his to distribute.

**"Would you tell me what you'd do?"** The former client is asking for advice — about a refinance, a rental, an addition, a family transfer. **The categories that were outside the licensee's competence during the transaction are still outside it**, and the pressure to answer is higher because the relationship is warmer.

**A request to change something in the file.** It arrives innocently — a name spelled differently, a date the client remembers otherwise. **The file is a record.** Corrections are made visibly, dated as of today, with the original preserved. Section 6.5's line governs and does not soften with time.

> **The habit that makes all of this easy:** a complete, dated, retrievable file, and a standing willingness to say "that one goes to your attorney" or "that one goes to your tax advisor" without treating it as a failure to help. **A licensee who has said it a hundred times says it comfortably**, and clients read the comfort as competence rather than as evasion.

### 6.8 Checkpoint

You should be able to sequence signing, funding, recording, and disbursement; explain why escrow stops rather than deciding when the parties disagree; and state the correct response to a missing document found in a closed file.

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **Escrow** | A neutral third party holding funds and documents under conditional delivery, authorized to act **only on the parties' mutual written instructions**. |
| **The two elements** | A binding contract between the parties, and conditional delivery to a neutral holder. Without both, what exists is a stakeholder arrangement, not an escrow. |
| **Neutrality** | The structural feature letting both parties rely on the same holder. It is why escrow **stops** rather than deciding when the parties disagree. |
| **Limited dual agent** | The description of escrow that explains its shape: duties to both parties simultaneously, bounded strictly by the instructions. |
| **Mutual written instruction** | The only basis on which escrow may change disbursement, release funds, or act on a disputed matter. An agent's confirmation is not the other party's consent. |
| **Affiliated business arrangement** | Where the brokerage has a financial interest in a settlement provider. Carries disclosure and other conditions. Accepting anything of value for directing settlement business is separately prohibited. |
| **Preliminary report** | A report of record title stating **the terms on which the insurer would issue a policy**, including the exceptions it would take. Not a guarantee, not the policy, not a survey. |
| **Exception** | An item the policy **will not insure against**. The buyer bears that risk unless it is otherwise addressed. |
| **Reconveyance** | The recorded document clearing a paid deed of trust. The seller's recollection that a loan was paid does not clear the record. |
| **Mechanic's lien** | A lien for work performed whose priority may relate back to when work commenced. Routed to escrow and title, not evaluated by the licensee. |
| **Vesting** | How the buyer takes title. Carries ownership, survivorship, tax, and estate consequences — and is a question for legal and tax counsel **before escrow prepares the deed**. |
| **Owner's versus lender's policy** | The owner's policy insures the buyer's interest; the lender's insures the lien and its priority. Different interests, which is why both commonly exist. |
| **Parallel tracks** | Contract deadlines, investigation, disclosure, financing, appraisal, title, escrow, and insurance — each with its own deadlines and failure modes. |
| **Started late** | Why most failed transactions fail. Not that a track was impossible. |
| **Final verification of condition** | The walkthrough: confirming agreed repairs are done, included items are present, and the property is not materially changed. **Not** an inspection and not a renegotiation. |
| **Signing** | Execution of the closing documents. The transaction is not closed. |
| **Funding** | The lender's release of loan proceeds to escrow, subject to its conditions. |
| **Recording** | Recording of the deed and security instrument, establishing the interests of record and giving constructive notice. Generally when the buyer becomes owner of record. |
| **Disbursement** | Escrow's release of funds, **after** recording is confirmed — deliberately, to prevent a gap in which money has moved but interests are not of record. |
| **Recording order** | The sequence establishing lien priority. Out-of-sequence recording can place a lender behind an interest it was to precede. Not a clerical preference. |
| **Possession** | Transfers **when the contract says it does** — frequently the same day as recording and frequently not. Ownership and possession are separate questions. |
| **Post-closing occupancy** | The seller remaining after closing, under a written agreement addressing duration, payment, condition, insurance, utilities, deposit, and the remedy for failure to vacate. |
| **Superseded versions** | Earlier drafts kept in the file because the **sequence of versions** is what shows when a term changed and who agreed to it. |
| **Falsification** | Creating a document now and dating it to match the transaction, or removing records so an inconsistency does not show. Distinct from a documented gap, which is survivable. |

## Practice problems

### Problem 1

The day before recording, the buyer emails escrow asking it to hold back $3,000 from the seller's
proceeds for a repair the seller promised verbally. The seller knows nothing about it. The buyer's
agent emails escrow confirming the request is legitimate.

**What may escrow do?**

> **Solution.** **Escrow stops.** That is the whole answer, and each alternative fails for its own
> reason.
>
> It **may not act on the holdback**: a change to disbursement requires mutual written instructions,
> and one party cannot redirect funds.
>
> It **may not simply disburse in full** either — that also acts on one side's position once a genuine
> dispute has been raised.
>
> And **the agent's confirmation supplies nothing.** An agent is not the other party.
>
> **The correct outcome:** the parties resolve it in writing and the resolution reaches escrow as a
> mutual instruction. The agents' job is to get that done before the closing date, not to argue with
> escrow about its neutrality.

### Problem 2

A preliminary report shows a ten-foot utility easement along the rear property line, recorded CC&Rs,
an unreleased deed of trust the seller says was paid in 2019, and a judgment lien against a person
with a similar name. The buyer plans to build a workshop at the rear of the lot. The agent tells him
easements are routine, asks title to remove the judgment, and predicts the workshop will be fine.

**Route each item correctly and identify the three wrong responses.**

> **Solution.** **The easement:** obtain and review the document — width, location, permitted use. A
> rear-line easement can prevent exactly the structure this buyer intends, which makes it the most
> consequential item on the report for him. **The CC&Rs:** obtain and review; use restrictions bind
> him regardless of zoning. **The unreleased deed of trust:** a recorded reconveyance or other
> clearing document must be obtained — the seller's recollection does not clear the record. **The
> judgment lien:** route to title for resolution; do not assume it is or is not the seller.
>
> The three wrong responses, and the agent made all three: **treating an item as routine** because
> most properties have one; **asking title to "remove" an exception**; and **predicting the practical
> effect** of an easement on the buyer's plans, which is a legal question for counsel.
>
> **What an exception means, stated once:** the policy will not insure against it, so the buyer bears
> that risk unless it is otherwise addressed. That is why exceptions are read rather than skimmed.

### Problem 3

Two unmarried siblings buying together tell the licensee they want the survivor to take the whole
property automatically, **and** each to be able to leave her share to her own children. They ask
which vesting to use, and escrow needs the deed instruction tomorrow.

**How should the licensee respond?**

> **Solution.** By naming the tension and referring it — not by choosing.
>
> **Those two goals point in different directions.** Automatic survivorship and the ability to devise
> a share to one's own children are not both satisfied by a single form, and resolving that is
> **estate planning, not form selection.**
>
> The licensee may explain that the choice matters and carries ownership, survivorship, tax, and
> estate consequences. The licensee may **not** advise which to choose. The referral goes to legal and
> tax counsel **before escrow prepares the deed**, because changing it afterwards is neither free nor
> simple — and escrow's deadline is a scheduling pressure, not a reason to answer a question outside
> the license.

### Problem 4

At the final verification the buyer finds an agreed repair was never completed and a built-in
microwave named in the contract has been removed. The listing agent suggests closing on schedule and
sorting it out afterward; the buyer's agent suggests instructing escrow to hold back funds.

**Assess both suggestions.**

> **Solution.** Both fail, and the walkthrough's purpose explains why.
>
> It confirms that the property is in the condition the contract requires — agreed repairs completed
> against the amendment that agreed them, included items present, systems operating, no new damage
> including from the move-out, and vacancy where required. It is **not** an inspection and not a new
> chance to renegotiate condition already accepted — but it is not informational either.
>
> **Closing and pursuing afterward** converts a solvable issue into a claim: the ordinary leverage to
> resolve it disappears at closing. **Instructing escrow to hold back** cannot work — escrow acts only
> on mutual instruction.
>
> **Correct:** document the finding with photographs and dated notes, and address it through the
> contract **before closing**.

### Problem 5

A licensee tells his client the transaction "closed" the afternoon the parties signed. Funding
occurs the next morning; recording that afternoon; disbursement the following day. The client had
already given notice on her apartment based on the signing date.

**Sequence the four events and explain why recording precedes disbursement.**

> **Solution.** **Signing** — the parties execute the closing documents, and the transaction is **not**
> closed. **Funding** — the lender releases proceeds to escrow subject to its conditions.
> **Recording** — the deed and security instrument are recorded, establishing the interests of record
> and giving constructive notice; this is generally when the buyer becomes owner of record.
> **Disbursement** — escrow disburses once recording is confirmed.
>
> Recording precedes disbursement **deliberately**: it prevents a gap in which money has moved but the
> interests are not of record.
>
> Related, and not clerical: where multiple instruments record, **the order establishes lien
> priority**, and an out-of-sequence recording can place a new lender behind an interest it was meant
> to precede. That is why closing is sequenced rather than simultaneous.
>
> **The client-facing lesson:** when recording is confirmed, tell the client in writing, with the date
> and what it means. Clients frequently do not know whether they own the property, and the licensee is
> who they will ask.

### Problem 6

Two days before funding, the buyer receives an email in the existing escrow thread with updated wire
instructions. The reply-to address differs by one character. He forwards it to his agent asking
whether it looks right.

**What should the agent do, and what should have happened earlier?**

> **Solution.** **Treat the change as presumptively fraudulent until verified**, and verify by calling
> escrow at a number obtained **independently** — from the engagement documents or the company's
> published listing, never from the email carrying the instructions. Tell the buyer in writing not to
> send funds until that call is made.
>
> What should have happened earlier is the more important half: **the written warning at the start of
> escrow** that wire instructions will never change by email and that he must verify by voice before
> sending. A licensee who sends that at the start of escrow rather than the day funds move has done
> the single most valuable thing available to protect a client's money.
>
> This is the highest-risk moment in the transaction, and the moment a client is least able to
> recover from an error.

### Problem 7

The seller needs three extra days in the house after closing. Both agents agree it is too short to
need paperwork. The seller stays eleven days, leaves damage in the garage, and the buyer discovers
the seller still has app access to the interior cameras.

**What was missing, and what else belongs on the list?**

> **Solution.** **A written occupancy agreement**, whose absence is exactly what produced all three
> problems. "They only need three days" is how a holdover with no agreed remedy begins — and the buyer
> now owns a property occupied by someone with no obligation he can enforce quickly.
>
> The terms it should have carried: duration and end date; payment; condition on delivery; insurance
> and in whose name; utilities and maintenance; a deposit or holdback as security for damage or
> holdover; and **the remedy for failure to vacate**, which is the point at which this becomes
> expensive. A brief term does not reduce the need for terms.
>
> **The camera access belongs on the transfer-of-control list**, alongside when keys transfer and from
> whom, garage and gate remotes, mailbox keys, association access devices, and changing codes on smart
> locks and alarms. A seller retaining app access to interior cameras after closing is a real problem
> and must be addressed explicitly rather than assumed.
>
> Worth noting: **early possession before closing is riskier still** and generally to be discouraged —
> a buyer who occupies and then fails to close leaves the seller with an occupant and a property to
> re-market.

### Problem 8

A post-closing audit finds a missing signed copy of an amendment, an inconsistency between the
closing statement and an earlier figure, and only executed final documents — the drafts were
discarded as clutter. A colleague suggests recreating the amendment with a matching date and deleting
the superseded closing statement so nothing conflicts.

**State the correct response and the line.**

> **Solution.** **Obtain what legitimately exists** — the missing signed copy may still be
> retrievable, which is why the audit happens while participants still respond to messages. **Record
> what does not**, plainly. **Escalate to the broker.** And **address the process** that produced the
> gap, not only the instance.
>
> **The colleague's suggestion is falsification.** Creating a document now and dating it to match the
> transaction, or removing surrounding records so an inconsistency does not show, converts a
> compliance failure into conduct that ends a career. A documented gap is survivable.
>
> **Discarding the drafts was itself a mistake.** The sequence of versions is frequently what shows
> when a term changed and who agreed to it. A file of executed finals can prove what was agreed but
> not how the parties arrived there — which is the question a dispute usually asks.
>
> The same principle governs corrections generally: made visibly, with the original preserved and the
> change attributable. A record that can be silently altered is not a record. And the file is retained
> from closing — **including cancelled and failed transactions**, which are disproportionately the
> ones that later produce disputes.

## Chapter summary

**Escrow is neutral and acts only on mutual written instructions.** It holds, it does not decide;
where the parties disagree it stops, and no agent's confirmation substitutes for the other party's
consent.

**Never accept anything of value for directing settlement business**, and disclose affiliated
business arrangements where the brokerage has an interest.

**Open escrow on the complete, fully executed agreement including amendments**, or the instructions
will not match the contract — which surfaces later, at the worst time.

**A preliminary report states the terms on which a policy would issue.** An exception is a risk the
policy will not cover, so exceptions are read, the underlying documents obtained, and legal questions
routed to counsel. Never call an item routine, never ask title to "remove" one, never predict its
effect on the buyer's plans.

**Vesting is a legal and tax question**, referred before escrow prepares the deed.

**Transactions fail because a track started late.** Build the calendar from the contract, start the
slow items first — insurance in hazard areas, association documents, permit research, specialist
inspections — confirm status weekly in writing, escalate early, and reconcile escrow's instructions
against the agreement.

**The final verification confirms contract compliance, not condition generally** — and problems are
resolved before closing, while leverage still exists.

**Signing, funding, recording, disbursement** are four events in that order. Recording is generally
when ownership transfers; disbursement follows it deliberately; and recording order establishes lien
priority.

**Warn about wire fraud in writing at the start of escrow**, and verify any change through a channel
the attacker does not control.

**Possession transfers when the contract says**, and any divergence from recording is documented —
post-closing occupancy in full, early possession rarely and with counsel.

**Audit the file shortly after closing**, keep superseded versions, obtain what exists, record what
does not, and fix the process. A documented gap is survivable; a manufactured document is not.
