## 1. Transfer disclosure framework

### 1.1 The obligation

A seller of residential property must disclose known material facts affecting the value or desirability of the property. In California this is delivered principally through a statutory transfer disclosure statement completed by the seller, supplemented by other disclosures the property, its age, and its location require.

Two features define the obligation and resolve most questions about it:

1. **It is about what the seller knows.** The seller is not required to investigate or to become an expert. The seller is required to disclose what they actually know.
2. **It is measured by materiality to a reasonable buyer**, not by the seller's own view of importance.

### 1.2 The materiality standard

A fact is material if it would affect a reasonable buyer's decision to buy or the price they would pay.

Apply that standard and the recurring seller objections resolve themselves:

| Seller's position | Analysis |
|---|---|
| "It's fixed, so there's nothing to disclose." | The repair's adequacy is exactly what a buyer wants to evaluate. Disclose the condition **and** the repair. |
| "It only happens in heavy rain." | Frequency is not the test. A drainage problem that appears three times a year is material. |
| "The buyer could see it himself." | Obviousness is not the test. Assuming the buyer noticed is how disputes begin. |
| "Nobody asked." | Disclosure is not question-driven. |
| "It's not a problem for me." | The standard is a reasonable buyer, not this seller. |
| "It happened before I owned it." | If the seller knows it, the seller discloses it. |

### 1.3 Completing the disclosure

Four rules, each of which prevents a specific failure:

1. **The seller completes the seller's disclosure.** A licensee who fills it in on the seller's behalf misattributes the statement and may create a misrepresentation attributed to the seller.
2. **Every item is answered.** A blank is ambiguous; "unknown" is an honest, available, and complete answer.
3. **Uncertain items are not answered "no."** Marking an item "no" asserts knowledge the seller does not have.
4. **Amend when new information arrives.** A disclosure is a statement of what the seller knows, and knowledge acquired during the transaction updates it.

### 1.4 Counseling the reluctant seller

The counseling argument that works is not moral — it is practical, and it is true:

> "Undisclosed conditions are far more expensive than disclosed ones. Disclosed, this is a fact the buyer prices in and moves past. Undisclosed and discovered during inspection, it becomes a renegotiation with your credibility gone. Undisclosed and discovered after closing, it becomes a claim. The only version where this costs you least is the one where you disclose it now, with the repair records."

### 1.5 What the licensee does with the seller's disclosure

The licensee's obligations regarding the seller's disclosure are specific:

- **Ensure it is delivered** to the buyer, and record when.
- **Do not alter it** or complete it on the seller's behalf.
- **Do not suppress it**, and do not follow an instruction to conceal a material condition.
- **Where the licensee knows something the disclosure omits**, raise it with the seller and the broker. The licensee's own knowledge creates the licensee's own obligation.

That last point is the one that separates a compliant licensee from a complicit one. A licensee who knows of a material condition the seller has omitted, and delivers the disclosure without addressing it, has adopted the omission.

### 1.6 When the seller cannot complete the disclosure

The framework assumes a seller who has lived in the property and knows things about it. A significant share of transactions do not have one.

| The seller | The problem |
|---|---|
| **A trustee or personal representative** | Has authority to convey and may have never entered the property |
| **A successor who inherited** | Knows the house socially and not structurally, and may be confusing childhood memory with fact |
| **An institutional owner after foreclosure** | Has no occupancy knowledge at all |
| **An investor who bought, held, and never occupied** | Knows what he did to it and nothing about what preceded him |
| **An owner who has been away for years** | Knows the property as it was, not as it is |
| **A seller in a dissolution** | May have knowledge that is genuinely disputed between two people who each have to sign |

**What does not change.** The obligation is about **known** material facts, and a seller who genuinely does not know something answers accordingly. **"Unknown" is an honest and available answer**, as Section 1.3 establishes, and it is the correct one far more often in these transactions than in an ordinary owner-occupied sale.

**What changes is everything around it.**

**The exemption question is a legal one.** Certain transfers are treated differently under the disclosure framework, and **which exemptions apply to a given transfer is a question for counsel and the broker**, not a determination the licensee makes from the seller's description of how he acquired the property. A licensee who concludes "this is a trust sale so the disclosure isn't required" has answered a legal question.

**An exemption from one requirement is not an exemption from everything.** The most consequential error in this area is treating an exemption as a general release. **The obligation not to conceal known material facts, the obligation of honesty, and the licensee's own independent obligations do not disappear** because a particular form is not required of a particular seller.

**The licensee's own duty is unaffected and becomes more important.** Section 2.1 establishes it as independent. Where the seller's disclosure is thin because the seller genuinely knows little, **the licensee's own observations are a larger share of what the buyer receives** — and the buyer's need for independent investigation is correspondingly greater.

**The counseling move** is to say all of this to the buyer plainly and early: this seller has limited knowledge of the property, the disclosure will reflect that, and **the investigation contingency is carrying more weight in this transaction than it usually does.**

> **What must not happen:** a disclosure completed by a licensee, a family member, or anyone other than the seller, filling in from what "everybody knows about the house." Section 1.3 forbids it and the reason is that the resulting document misattributes a statement — it tells the buyer the seller said something the seller did not say.

## 2. Agent visual inspection duties

### 2.1 An independent obligation

In a residential transaction the licensee conducts a **reasonably competent and diligent visual inspection of the reasonably and normally accessible areas** and discloses material facts it reveals.

This is the licensee's own duty. It is not satisfied by:

- Reviewing the seller's disclosure.
- Recommending that the buyer hire an inspector.
- The buyer's own agent conducting one.
- The presence of a professional inspection report.

### 2.2 What the duty includes and excludes

| Included | Excluded |
|---|---|
| Areas reasonably and normally accessible | Areas requiring moving belongings or furniture |
| Visual observation | Opening walls, floors, or ceilings |
| Conditions apparent on inspection | Operating systems to test them |
| The interior and accessible exterior | Roofs, crawl spaces, or areas not reasonably accessible |
| What a competent licensee would notice | What only a specialist would recognize |

The licensee is not an inspector. The licensee is a professional expected to notice what a diligent professional would notice, and to say so.

### 2.3 What to record

Record observations rather than conclusions, and record them whether or not their significance is clear:

- **Observation:** "Brown staining approximately three feet across on the ceiling of the northwest bedroom; paint in that area appears newer than the surrounding ceiling."
- **Not a conclusion:** "There was a roof leak that has been repaired."

The first is what the licensee observed and is qualified to state. The second is an inference the licensee is not qualified to make, and it may be wrong in a way the buyer relies on.

### 2.4 The worked case

> Previewing a listing, the agent notices ceiling staining and fresh paint around it. The seller says a pipe leaked two years ago, was repaired, and "there's nothing to disclose because it's fixed." The draft transfer disclosure says nothing about it.
>
> **What is required:**
>
> 1. The **past leak and the repair are disclosed by the seller** — a repaired condition is still a fact a buyer would want, because the repair's adequacy is the question.
> 2. The **agent's visual observation is separately recorded and disclosed** — the staining is what the agent saw, and that is the agent's own duty regardless of what the seller discloses.
> 3. The **buyer is advised to have the repair evaluated** by an appropriate professional before his contingency expires.
>
> **What is not sufficient:** disclosing the staining but not the leak, because the agent observed one and the seller knows the other and both obligations exist. Nor is leaving it to the buyer's inspector, which discharges neither duty.

### 2.5 Where the duty meets the competence limit

When the visual inspection reveals something the licensee does not understand well enough to evaluate, the correct sequence is:

1. **Disclose the observation.**
2. **Recommend evaluation** by the appropriate specialist.
3. **Document** the recommendation and the buyer's response.

What is not appropriate: offering a general impression of how serious such conditions usually are, asking the listing agent whether the seller considers it significant, or waiting to see whether the inspector flags it.

### 2.6 A worked walkthrough

> **The property.** A single-story house built in 1968, owner-occupied, listed by the licensee. He conducts his visual inspection on a Tuesday afternoon.
>
> **What he observes, and what each observation obligates.**
>
> **A brown stain on the ceiling of the hall bathroom, roughly a foot across, dry to appearance.** **Recorded and disclosed.** He does not determine whether it is active, does not conclude it is old because it looks dry, and does not describe it as "prior water intrusion, repaired" — which asserts two facts he does not know. **He writes what he saw: a stain, its location, its approximate size, and that its cause and status were not determined.**
>
> **A hairline crack running diagonally from the corner of a doorway.** Recorded. He does not characterize it as settlement, cosmetic, or structural. Section 2.5's competence limit governs — **the characterization is exactly the part he is not qualified to supply.**
>
> **A door that does not latch and a window that sticks.** Recorded. Individually trivial; **noted because a pattern of doors and windows out of square is information, and the pattern is the sort of thing a professional evaluates.**
>
> **A subpanel in the garage with a hand-lettered label.** Recorded. Electrical work of unknown provenance in a house of this age is an observation, not a conclusion.
>
> **Fresh paint on one interior wall and nowhere else.** Recorded. He does not accuse anyone of concealment. **He writes what is observable**, which is that one wall was recently painted, and a reviewer can make of it what a reviewer makes of it.
>
> **A gate in the rear fence that opens onto a neighbor's yard.** Recorded, and it prompts a question to the seller about the boundary — which produces the answer that "the fence has always been about three feet inside the line." **That answer is a material fact and it belongs on the seller's disclosure**, and the licensee's note records that he asked and what he was told.
>
> **What he does not do.**
>
> **He does not move furniture, lift carpet, enter the crawlspace, or climb onto the roof.** Section 2.2's scope is a reasonably competent and diligent **visual** inspection of accessible areas, and exceeding it creates an expectation he cannot meet on every property.
>
> **He does not omit an item because the seller says it was fixed.** A repaired condition is disclosed, and the licensee records both the observation and the seller's statement about it as two separate things.
>
> **He does not omit an item because it will be obvious to the buyer.** Section 1.2 of Lesson 04 disposes of that.
>
> **He does not write "no issues observed" on a fifty-seven-year-old house.** That sentence is a statement about the property that the inspection did not support, and **it is the single most dangerous phrase in this document.**
>
> **What the record looks like.** Dated, specific, factual, in his own words, with locations. **Photographs where the description is hard.** And it is written the same day — Section 2.3's requirement — because the value of the note is that it is contemporaneous.

## 3. Natural hazard and environmental disclosure

### 3.1 Natural hazard disclosure

California requires disclosure of whether a property lies within certain mapped hazard zones — flood, fire, seismic, and related designations. The disclosure is commonly prepared by a third-party report provider.

What it does and does not do:

- **Does:** tell the buyer that the property is within a mapped zone, so the buyer can investigate what that means for insurance, construction requirements, and risk.
- **Does not:** certify the property is safe, predict whether an event will occur, or substitute for the buyer's own investigation.

It applies based on **mapping**, not on the property's event history. A property that has never flooded is disclosed as within a flood zone if it is mapped in one.

### 3.2 Why it matters more than buyers assume

Hazard zone status drives consequences buyers frequently discover late:

- **Insurance availability and cost.** In some areas this is now the determining factor in whether a transaction closes at all, and it should be investigated **early in the contingency period** rather than at the end.
- **Construction and improvement requirements**, which can materially affect a buyer's renovation plans.
- **Financing**, where lenders require particular coverage.
- **Disclosure obligations** the buyer will inherit as a future seller.

A licensee who ensures a buyer investigates insurance early has prevented a common and avoidable failure.

### 3.3 Environmental conditions

Depending on the property, environmental disclosures and investigations may include soil and groundwater contamination, proximity to industrial or agricultural use, radon, mold, methamphetamine contamination, and other conditions.

The licensee's role is consistent throughout: **disclose what is known, recommend appropriate investigation, and do not evaluate.** A licensee who tells a buyer that visible mold "is just surface mold, it'll clean off" has made an assessment they are not qualified to make.

### 3.4 Age-triggered disclosures

Certain obligations are triggered by the age of the housing rather than by any observed condition. For housing built before 1978, the federal lead-based paint requirements apply.

Those requirements are specific, and licensees routinely misstate them:

| The obligation is | The obligation is not |
|---|---|
| Disclose **known** lead-based paint and hazards | Test the property |
| Provide **available records and reports** | Create records that do not exist |
| Provide the required **pamphlet** | Certify the property is free of lead |
| Give the buyer the **opportunity to conduct an assessment** | Remediate before transfer |

It is triggered by the age of the housing, **not** by whether children will occupy it.

### 3.5 Water, drought, and California-specific conditions

California transactions increasingly involve water-related requirements: conservation obligations, fixture requirements, well and septic conditions on rural property, and supply questions that affect what may be built.

These are investigation items and, where known, disclosure items. A licensee marketing rural property who has not asked about water source, adequacy, and rights has skipped a question that determines whether the property is usable as intended.

### 3.6 Common interest developments

Where the property is in a common interest development, the buyer's investigation extends to the association: governing documents and use restrictions, budget and reserves, assessments including any special assessment, meeting minutes, and pending or threatened litigation.

Construction-defect litigation affecting the building is a clear material fact, and the buyer should have the documents in time to review them within the contingency period — which means requesting them promptly rather than late.

### 3.7 Reading a hazard report properly

A natural hazard disclosure arrives as a report from a third-party provider, and it is treated by most participants as a form to be delivered. **It is a document with content, and three habits separate a licensee who uses it from one who forwards it.**

**Read what it actually says.** The report states whether the property is within mapped zones, and its statements are qualified in ways that matter: mapping is done at a scale, boundaries are approximate, and maps are revised. **A property just outside a mapped boundary is not a property without the hazard**, and a buyer who reads "not in a zone" as "not at risk" has misread it.

**Understand what each determination does downstream.** A flood zone determination drives lender-required insurance. A fire severity zone determination can drive insurance availability, cost, and — increasingly in California — whether coverage is obtainable at all. A seismic or landslide zone can drive disclosure and investigation obligations. **Insurance availability is the item that most often surprises a buyer late**, and it is knowable early.

**Tell the buyer to price insurance before removing contingencies.** This is the practical instruction that flows from the report, and it is the one most often given too late. **A buyer who discovers at day nineteen that coverage will cost several times what he assumed, or that carriers will not write it, has lost the ability to respond** — and Section 5.4 of this lesson explains why the timing of information determines whether it protects anyone.

**Three things the licensee does not do:**

- **Interpret the maps.** The report is the disclosure; the licensee is not the source of the determination and does not offer an opinion about whether a boundary is "really" where it is drawn.
- **Reassure.** "It's technically in the zone but nothing's ever flooded there" is a prediction and a characterization, and it is offered to buyers constantly.
- **Answer the insurance question himself.** The carrier answers it. **The licensee's contribution is telling the buyer to ask, early, and recording that he did.**

> **What belongs in the file:** the report, its delivery with a date, and the licensee's written recommendation that the buyer obtain insurance quotes and any further evaluation the report suggests, delivered early enough for the buyer to act inside his periods.

**And where the seller knows something the report does not say** — that the street floods, that the property was evacuated in a fire season, that a slope moved — **that is a known material fact and the report does not cover it.** Section 4.3 governs: the seller's knowledge and the licensee's knowledge are separate obligations, and neither is satisfied by a third-party map.

## 4. Lead-based paint, material facts, and stigma limits

### 4.1 The limits on death and illness disclosure

California imposes specific limits on the disclosure of a prior occupant's death and on disclosure relating to a prior occupant's illness. These limits exist for a reason worth teaching: without them, disclosure practice would operate as a channel for discrimination against people with particular illnesses, and would attach a permanent penalty to properties for reasons unrelated to their condition.

The practical guidance for a licensee:

- **Do not volunteer** information about the manner of a prior occupant's death or about a prior occupant's illness.
- **Do not misrepresent.** Where a direct question arises and the answer is constrained, the licensee should not lie.
- **Escalate.** The precise boundary is a legal question with real consequences on both sides — disclose too much and there is one exposure, too little and another. This is a question for the broker and, where necessary, counsel, not one to resolve at a showing.

### 4.2 Material fact versus stigma

| Category | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **Physical condition of the property** | Material; disclose if known |
| **Legal condition** — liens, boundary disputes, litigation, permit status | Material; disclose if known |
| **Conditions affecting use** — zoning, association restrictions, easements | Material; disclose if known |
| **Neighborhood characteristics that are objective and verifiable** | Point the buyer to the source rather than characterizing |
| **Characteristics of neighbors or residents** | Do not characterize — this is steering territory |
| **Prior occupant's death or illness** | Subject to specific legal limits; escalate |
| **Rumor, reputation, and speculation** | Do not represent as fact |

### 4.3 The licensee's own knowledge

A licensee's disclosure obligation attaches to what the licensee knows, from any source. Two situations recur:

> **A neighbor tells the agent the home flooded three years ago. The seller has said nothing about it.**
>
> The report is neither ignorable nor assertable as fact. The correct path runs through the seller and the broker: raise it, ask the seller, and ensure the disclosure addresses what is known and what remains unverified. The file should show the issue was surfaced and how it was resolved.

> **The agent sold the same property four years ago and knows of a condition disclosed then.**
>
> The agent knows it. The obligation is the agent's, regardless of the current seller's disclosure.

### 4.4 Unverified information

The professional treatment of unverified information is to **attribute it and flag it**, never to launder it into apparent fact:

- Acceptable: "The seller reports the roof was replaced in approximately 2019; this has not been verified."
- Not acceptable: "Roof replaced 2019."
- Not acceptable: repeating a figure from a prior listing as though the licensee had verified it.

### 4.5 What must not be represented as fact

- A prediction — that values will rise, that a permit will issue, that a variance will be granted.
- A legal conclusion — that a use is permitted, that an encroachment is unimportant, that a neighbor has no claim.
- A specialist's assessment — that a crack is cosmetic, that a repair was adequate, that mold is harmless.
- Anything the licensee has not verified and cannot attribute.

### 4.6 The questions buyers ask about the surrounding area

Buyers ask about schools, crime, neighbors, demographics, "what kind of area is this," and whether a neighborhood is "good." **These questions are the most reliable way for a licensee to create a fair housing violation while trying to be helpful**, and the correct handling is a discipline rather than a script.

**The principle:** a licensee does not characterize the composition of a neighborhood or the desirability of an area in terms that sort people, whatever the question's phrasing and whatever the buyer's evident intent.

| The question | The correct handling |
|---|---|
| **"How are the schools?"** | Direct the buyer to the district's own published information and to the district itself for attendance boundaries. **Do not rate, rank, or compare.** Attendance boundaries change, and a boundary stated from memory is also a reasonable-care problem. |
| **"Is it safe?"** | Direct the buyer to law enforcement's published data and to his own investigation. **Do not characterize.** "Safe" and "bad area" are exactly the terms that carry composition. |
| **"What kind of people live here?"** | **Do not answer.** Not carefully, not vaguely, not with a compliment. Explain that you do not describe the composition of neighborhoods and that public sources exist for demographic data. |
| **"Would you buy here?"** | The buyer is inviting the characterization through a side door. **The answer is about the property and his criteria**, not about the area's desirability. |
| **"Are there a lot of families?"** | Familial status. **Do not answer**, including favorably. |
| **"Is it quiet?"** | An observable condition rather than a composition question, and it is answerable by direction to investigate — visit at different hours, check for a nearby flight path, arterial, rail line, or venue. |
| **"Any problems with the neighbors?"** | Section 4.3 governs the licensee's own knowledge of material facts. **A known material fact is disclosed; a characterization of people is not offered.** |

**Two clarifications that licensees find genuinely confusing.**

**Refusing to characterize is not refusing to help.** The buyer gets a better answer from the district, the police department, and his own visits than from the licensee's impression, and **saying so is a service rather than an evasion.** The professional version is: "I don't characterize neighborhoods — I'd be giving you my impression when you need facts. Here is where the actual data lives, and here is what I'd go look at myself."

**The prohibition does not depend on the buyer's intent or on the answer being positive.** A licensee who says "you'll love it, it's a great family area" to a buyer who asked innocently has still steered, and the violation does not require anyone to have been excluded on purpose.

> **The file consequence:** Section 4.5 of Lesson 07 requires documenting the search on the buyer's stated criteria. **A search record built on the buyer's criteria is the evidence that the search was not built on anything else**, which is why the documentation habit and the fair housing discipline are the same habit.

## 5. Delivery, timing, and rescission

### 5.1 Delivery is where rights attach

Content is half the obligation. **Delivery, and evidence of when it occurred, is the other half**, because disclosure rules attach consequences to delivery — including periods during which a buyer may respond.

A file with a signed disclosure and no delivery date proves that the buyer received it, and proves nothing about when. Timing is what disputes are about.

### 5.2 What the file must show

For each disclosure:

1. **What** was delivered — the specific document and version.
2. **To whom.**
3. **When** — the date, and where the rule is time-sensitive, the time.
4. **By what method**, and that the method was one the contract or the law authorizes.
5. **Evidence** — delivery receipt, signed acknowledgment with date, platform audit trail.

### 5.3 Early delivery is a professional advantage

Disclosures delivered early give the buyer time to review and investigate within the contingency period, which produces better decisions and fewer late renegotiations. Disclosures delivered late compress that review, create pressure, and generate the disputes that follow from pressure.

This is why the disclosure work belongs at the listing appointment rather than at the offer. A seller who resists — "let's wait until we have an offer" — is asking for the arrangement most likely to cost them.

### 5.4 Late delivery and its consequence

> The seller delivers a required disclosure after the buyer has already removed his contingencies.
>
> **What is true:** rights that run from delivery are not extinguished by the buyer having acted earlier without the information. The late delivery may revive or extend the buyer's ability to respond.
>
> **What the licensee must do:** identify the issue immediately and raise it, rather than proceeding as though the transaction were firm.
>
> **What is not a resolution:** treating it as cured because the buyer received it before closing, or extending the closing date, neither of which addresses the buyer's right to respond.

### 5.5 Amending a disclosure

Where information changes or new information arrives — a new leak, a notice from the association, a claim filed — the disclosure is amended and re-delivered, with a new delivery date. The buyer's rights run from the delivery of the new information.

An amendment does not erase the original. Both stay in the file, so the sequence shows what was known when.

### 5.6 The correction obligation

Where a licensee discovers that a disclosure or a statement was wrong, the obligation is to correct it **immediately, in writing, to everyone who received the original** — before the recipient acts on it if that is still possible.

Waiting to see whether the error matters is how an error becomes a misrepresentation. A correction that arrives after closing is worth almost nothing to the person who relied on it.

## 6. Disclosure risk management

### 6.1 The three-part test for any condition

When a licensee encounters something about a property and is unsure what to do, three questions resolve it:

1. **Do I know it?** If yes, the licensee's own obligation is engaged, whatever the seller does.
2. **Would a reasonable buyer consider it important?** If yes, it is material.
3. **Am I qualified to evaluate it?** If no, disclose the observation, recommend the specialist, and do not characterize.

### 6.2 The failure modes, and which is worse

| Failure | Consequence |
|---|---|
| **Not disclosing a known material fact** | The most serious. Concealment, and it survives closing as a claim |
| **Disclosing but not evidencing delivery** | The file cannot show the buyer's rights were honored |
| **Evaluating beyond competence** | A statement the buyer relies on that the licensee could not support |
| **Disclosing what the law limits** | A different exposure, and potentially a discrimination issue |
| **Over-disclosing rumor as fact** | Misrepresentation, and potential harm to the seller |

Note that the failures run in both directions. "Disclose everything" is not the rule; "disclose what is material and known, attribute what is unverified, decline what the law limits, and evaluate nothing" is.

### 6.3 Building the disclosure file

A defensible disclosure file contains:

- The seller's disclosures, completed with no blanks, signed and dated.
- The licensee's visual inspection disclosure, with observations recorded as observations.
- Hazard and third-party reports.
- Age-triggered disclosures with the required acknowledgments.
- Association documents where applicable, with the date requested and the date delivered.
- Delivery evidence for every item.
- Amendments, with their own delivery evidence, and the originals retained.
- A record of recommendations made to the buyer and the buyer's responses.

### 6.4 The audit question

The test to apply to any disclosure file: **could a reviewer, reading only this file, determine what the buyer knew and when he knew it?**

If the answer is no, the gap is not a paperwork problem. It is the specific gap that a dispute will be about.

### 6.5 A worked disclosure timeline

> **The transaction.** Acceptance communicated on a Monday. The agreement gives the buyer an investigation period and a defined period to respond to disclosures, running from delivery.
>
> **What the file records:**
>
> - **Day 1 (Monday).** Acceptance communicated.
> - **Day 4.** Seller's transfer disclosure signed by the seller. No delivery record.
> - **Day 6.** Natural hazard report ordered.
> - **Day 9.** Buyer's general inspection performed. Report recommends specialist evaluation of the subfloor.
> - **Day 11.** Natural hazard report received by the listing agent. Forwarded "to the buyer's agent" by email — **no record of transmission to the buyer.**
> - **Day 14.** Buyer signs a receipt acknowledging the transfer disclosure. The receipt is undated in the buyer's hand; the email transmitting the disclosure to him is dated Day 13.
> - **Day 16.** Seller amends the transfer disclosure to add a prior roof repair he had forgotten. The amendment is emailed to the buyer's agent.
> - **Day 17.** Buyer removes his investigation contingency. He has not obtained the subfloor evaluation.
> - **Day 19.** Buyer's agent forwards the Day 16 amendment to the buyer.
>
> **The findings, ranked.**
>
> **1. The amendment reached the buyer after he removed his contingency.** This is the defect with consequences, and Section 5.5 and Section 5.6 both govern. **An amended disclosure delivered after the buyer's decision has been delivered too late to inform the decision it was about**, and Section 5.4 establishes that rights running from delivery are not extinguished by the buyer having already acted without the information. **The correct response is to raise it immediately**, not to proceed as though the removal settled it.
>
> **2. Delivery of the transfer disclosure is documented only by inference.** The seller signed on Day 4; the file's evidence of the buyer receiving it is a Day 13 email and an undated receipt. **The response period cannot be computed from the file**, which is Section 5.2's requirement.
>
> **3. The hazard report's delivery to the buyer is unevidenced.** Forwarding to the agent is not, by itself, evidence the buyer received it, and the file has no record beyond the agent-to-agent email.
>
> **4. The nine-day gap between the seller's signature and transmission** is not itself a violation, but Section 5.3 explains what it costs: **the buyer's review time was consumed by the file rather than by the buyer.**
>
> **5. The contingency removed without the recommended evaluation** is a Lesson 07 issue rather than a disclosure issue, and it is recorded here because it interacts with finding 1 — **the buyer made an uninformed decision twice on the same day.**
>
> **What happens now.** Both agents raise it with their brokers immediately. The buyer is told what the amendment says and when it reached him. **Whether his rights are affected is a legal question**, and the licensees do not resolve it between themselves.
>
> **What must not happen:** dating the Day 14 receipt, re-sending the amendment now with a new date and treating that as delivery on Day 16, or persuading the buyer that the roof repair "doesn't change anything." **The third is the most tempting and the most serious**, because it substitutes the licensee's judgment for the buyer's on the exact question the disclosure existed to give him.
>
> **The process fix.** Every disclosure and every amendment is transmitted **directly to the party**, with delivery evidenced, and logged the day it is sent. Four of the five findings above disappear under that one rule.

### 6.6 Checkpoint

You should be able to apply the three-part test to a condition, state which failure mode a given file defect represents, and explain why late delivery is not cured by delivery before closing.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **Transfer disclosure statement** | The statutory statement in which a California seller of residential property discloses known material facts affecting value or desirability. |
| **What the seller knows** | The scope of the seller's obligation. The seller is not required to investigate or to become an expert — only to disclose what they actually know. |
| **Materiality** | Whether a fact would affect a reasonable buyer's decision to buy or the price they would pay. Measured against a reasonable buyer, not the seller's own view of importance. |
| **"Unknown"** | The honest, available, complete answer to an item the seller cannot answer. Distinct from a blank, which is ambiguous. |
| **Misattribution** | The defect created when a licensee completes the seller's disclosure on their behalf — the statement is attributed to a person who did not make it. |
| **Adopting the omission** | What a licensee does by delivering a disclosure they know omits a material condition. The licensee's own knowledge creates the licensee's own obligation. |
| **Visual inspection duty** | The licensee's reasonably competent and diligent visual inspection of reasonably and normally accessible areas, with disclosure of material facts it reveals. |
| **Independence of the duty** | The visual inspection is not satisfied by reviewing the seller's disclosure, recommending an inspector, the other agent's inspection, or the existence of an inspection report. |
| **Observation versus conclusion** | "Brown staining roughly three feet across on the northwest bedroom ceiling" is an observation the licensee may state. "There was a roof leak that has been repaired" is an inference the licensee is not qualified to make. |
| **Natural hazard disclosure** | Disclosure of whether the property lies within certain mapped hazard zones. Applies on **mapping**, not on event history, and certifies nothing about safety. |
| **Insurance availability** | The consequence of hazard zone status that most often decides whether a transaction closes — and the reason it belongs early in the contingency period. |
| **Age-triggered disclosure** | An obligation arising from the age of the housing rather than any observed condition. For pre-1978 housing, the federal lead-based paint requirements. |
| **Lead-based paint obligation** | Disclose what is **known**, provide available records and the required pamphlet, and give the buyer the opportunity to assess. Not: test, create records, certify, or remediate. |
| **Death and illness limits** | California's specific constraints on disclosing a prior occupant's death or illness. Do not volunteer, do not misrepresent, escalate. |
| **Stigma** | Rumor, reputation, and speculation. Never represented as fact. |
| **Attribution** | The professional treatment of unverified information: "the seller reports the roof was replaced in approximately 2019; this has not been verified." |
| **Laundering** | Repeating an unverified figure — from the seller, or from a prior listing — as though it had been verified. |
| **Delivery evidence** | What was delivered, to whom, when, by what authorized method, with proof. Rights attach to delivery, so a signed disclosure with no date proves the wrong thing. |
| **Amendment** | A re-delivered disclosure reflecting new information, with a new delivery date. The original stays in the file so the sequence shows what was known when. |
| **Correction obligation** | Correcting a wrong statement immediately, in writing, to everyone who received the original — before they act on it, if that is still possible. |
| **Three-part test** | Do I know it? Would a reasonable buyer consider it important? Am I qualified to evaluate it? |
| **The audit question** | Could a reviewer, reading only this file, determine what the buyer knew and when he knew it? |

## Practice problems

### Problem 1

At the listing appointment a seller says of a recurring drainage problem in the side yard: "It only
happens in heavy rain, it's not a problem for me, the buyer could see the grading himself, and
anyway it started before I bought the place. Nobody's asked about it."

**Address each objection.**

> **Solution.** Every one of them measures the wrong thing. The standard is whether a **reasonable
> buyer** would consider the fact important to the decision or the price.
>
> *Frequency is not the test* — a drainage problem appearing three times a year is material.
> *The seller's own tolerance is not the test* — the standard is a reasonable buyer, not this seller.
> *Obviousness is not the test* — assuming the buyer noticed is how disputes begin.
> *Predating ownership is not the test* — if the seller knows it, the seller discloses it.
> *And disclosure is not question-driven* — "nobody asked" is not a category of exemption.
>
> The counseling argument that works is practical rather than moral: disclosed, this is a fact the
> buyer prices in and moves past. Undisclosed and found at inspection, it is a renegotiation with the
> seller's credibility gone. Undisclosed and found after closing, it is a claim.

### Problem 2

A seller returns a draft disclosure with three items blank and one marked "no" about which he is
genuinely unsure. The licensee, wanting a clean form, fills in the blanks himself based on what the
seller told him verbally.

**Identify each defect.**

> **Solution.** Three, and the last is the most serious.
>
> **The blanks** are ambiguous — they invite a later dispute about whether the item was addressed at
> all. Every item is answered, and **"unknown" is an honest and complete answer.**
>
> **The uncertain item marked "no"** asserts knowledge the seller does not have. That is not a
> conservative choice; it is an affirmative statement that may be false.
>
> **The licensee completing it** is the worst of the three. The seller completes the seller's
> disclosure. Filling it in on his behalf misattributes the statement and may create a
> misrepresentation attributed to a person who did not make it.
>
> Also worth stating: the disclosure is a statement of what the seller knows **now**. New information
> arriving during the transaction updates it by amendment.

### Problem 3

Previewing a listing, the agent notices brown ceiling staining with newer paint around it. The seller
says a pipe leaked two years ago, was repaired, and there is nothing to disclose "because it's
fixed." The draft disclosure says nothing about it.

**What is required, and what is not sufficient?**

> **Solution.** **Three things, and two obligations that exist independently.**
>
> The **seller discloses the past leak and the repair.** A repaired condition is still a fact a buyer
> wants, because the adequacy of the repair is precisely the question — and here there are no records.
>
> The **agent separately records and discloses the visual observation.** Recorded as an observation,
> not a conclusion: "brown staining approximately three feet across on the northwest bedroom ceiling;
> paint in that area appears newer than the surrounding ceiling." Not "there was a roof leak that has
> been repaired," which is an inference the agent is not qualified to make and may be wrong in a way
> the buyer relies on.
>
> The **buyer is advised to have the repair evaluated** by an appropriate professional before his
> contingency expires, and the recommendation is documented.
>
> **Not sufficient:** disclosing the staining but not the leak, since the agent observed one and the
> seller knows the other and both obligations exist. Nor leaving it to the buyer's inspector, which
> discharges neither duty.

### Problem 4

A property has never flooded in the seller's twenty years of ownership but sits within a mapped flood
zone. The buyer says the mapping is "clearly outdated" and asks whether the disclosure is really
necessary. The licensee plans to order insurance quotes after the inspection contingency closes.

**Correct both.**

> **Solution.** **The disclosure applies on mapping, not on event history.** A property that has never
> flooded is disclosed as within a flood zone if it is mapped in one, and the disclosure does not
> certify the property is safe, predict an event, or substitute for the buyer's investigation. It
> tells the buyer he is in a mapped zone so he can investigate what that means.
>
> **The insurance timing is the more expensive error.** Hazard zone status drives insurance
> availability and cost — in some California areas now the determining factor in whether a transaction
> closes at all — along with construction requirements, financing conditions, and the disclosure
> obligations the buyer inherits as a future seller. Investigating it **early in the contingency
> period** rather than at the end is one of the clearest preventable failures in current practice.

### Problem 5

A 1964 home is being sold to a buyer with no children. The listing agent tells the seller that the
lead-based paint requirements do not apply, and separately assures a buyer that visible growth in the
laundry room "is just surface mold, it'll clean off."

**Assess both statements.**

> **Solution.** **Both are wrong, and the second is a competence failure.**
>
> Lead-based paint requirements are **triggered by the age of the housing**, not by whether children
> will occupy it — pre-1978 housing is covered. And the obligation is frequently misstated in the
> other direction too: disclose what is **known**, provide available records and reports, provide the
> required pamphlet, and give the buyer the opportunity to conduct an assessment. It does **not**
> require testing the property, creating records that do not exist, certifying the property is free of
> lead, or remediating before transfer.
>
> On the mold: the licensee has made an assessment they are not qualified to make, on which the buyer
> may rely. The correct sequence when the visual inspection reveals something the licensee cannot
> evaluate is **disclose the observation, recommend evaluation by the appropriate specialist, document
> the recommendation and the response** — not offer a general impression, not ask the listing agent
> whether the seller thinks it matters, and not wait to see whether the inspector flags it.

### Problem 6

A neighbor tells the listing agent the house flooded three years ago. The seller has disclosed
nothing about it. Separately, the same agent sold this property four years ago and recalls a
foundation condition disclosed at that time.

**How does each obligation run?**

> **Solution.** **A licensee's disclosure obligation attaches to what the licensee knows, from any
> source** — and these two run differently.
>
> The neighbor's report is **neither ignorable nor assertable as fact.** The path runs through the
> seller and the broker: raise it, ask the seller, and ensure the disclosure addresses what is known
> and what remains unverified. The file should show the issue was surfaced and how it was resolved.
> Stating it as fact would be representing an unverified report as verified.
>
> The prior-sale knowledge is different: **the agent knows it.** The obligation is the agent's,
> regardless of what the current seller discloses. That is the point separating a compliant licensee
> from a complicit one — delivering a disclosure the licensee knows omits a material condition adopts
> the omission.

### Problem 7

The seller delivers a required disclosure two days after the buyer removed all contingencies. The
listing agent proposes to say nothing, on the grounds that the buyer will have it well before closing,
and to offer a one-week closing extension if the buyer complains.

**Assess.**

> **Solution.** Neither proposal is a resolution, and the silence is itself the violation.
>
> **Rights that run from delivery are not extinguished** because the buyer acted earlier without the
> information. Late delivery may revive or extend the buyer's ability to respond — which is exactly
> what the agent is proposing to conceal.
>
> **Receipt before closing does not cure it**, and neither does a closing extension: neither addresses
> the buyer's right to respond, which is what the timing rule protects.
>
> **What is required:** identify the issue immediately and raise it, rather than proceeding as though
> the transaction were firm.
>
> **What transfers:** this is why the disclosure work belongs at the listing appointment. Early
> delivery gives the buyer time to review and investigate inside the contingency period; late delivery
> compresses the review, creates pressure, and generates the disputes that follow from pressure. A
> seller who says "let's wait until we have an offer" is asking for the arrangement most likely to
> cost him.

### Problem 8

A file review finds a disclosure signed by the buyer with no date, an agent visual inspection reading
"minor cosmetic settling cracks, nothing structural," a marketing sheet stating "roof replaced 2019"
copied from the prior listing, and a note that the agent told a buyer the prior owner "died of an
illness, but I probably shouldn't say more."

**Classify each failure and state the rule that covers all four.**

> **Solution.** Four **different** failure modes, which is the point of the exercise.
>
> **The undated signature** proves receipt and proves nothing about *when* — and timing is what
> disputes are about. Rights attach to delivery.
>
> **"Nothing structural"** is evaluating beyond competence: a statement the buyer may rely on that the
> licensee could not support. The observation was permitted; the conclusion was not.
>
> **"Roof replaced 2019"** launders an unverified figure from a prior listing into apparent fact. The
> professional treatment attributes and flags: "the seller reports approximately 2019; this has not
> been verified."
>
> **The death-and-illness comment** is disclosing what the law limits — a different exposure, and
> potentially a discrimination issue. Do not volunteer, do not misrepresent, and **escalate**: the
> precise boundary is a legal question with real consequences on both sides, not one to resolve at a
> showing.
>
> **The rule covering all four:** "disclose everything" is not it. **Disclose what is material and
> known, attribute what is unverified, decline what the law limits, and evaluate nothing.**

## Chapter summary

**The seller discloses what the seller knows**, measured by materiality to a reasonable buyer. The
recurring objections — it's fixed, it's rare, it's obvious, nobody asked, it doesn't bother me, it
predates me — each substitute a different standard for that one.

**The seller completes the seller's disclosure.** Every item answered, "unknown" where that is true,
uncertain items never marked "no," and amended when new information arrives.

**The licensee's visual inspection is an independent duty** — not discharged by the seller's
disclosure, by recommending an inspector, or by an inspection report existing. Record **observations**,
not conclusions, including observations whose significance is unclear.

**A licensee who knows of a material condition the seller omitted, and delivers the disclosure
anyway, has adopted the omission.**

**Natural hazard disclosure applies on mapping, not history**, and its most consequential downstream
effect is insurance availability — which belongs early in the contingency period, not at the end.

**Age triggers the lead-based paint obligations**, not occupancy by children. Disclose what is known,
provide records and the pamphlet, allow assessment. Do not test, certify, or remediate.

**Death and illness disclosure is legally constrained** for reasons worth understanding: without the
limits, disclosure practice would channel discrimination and attach permanent penalties unrelated to
condition. Do not volunteer, do not misrepresent, escalate.

**Attribute unverified information; never launder it.** Never represent a prediction, a legal
conclusion, or a specialist's assessment as fact.

**Delivery is half the obligation.** What, to whom, when, by what authorized method, with evidence.
Amendments carry their own delivery date and the originals stay in the file.

**Correct errors immediately, in writing, to everyone who received the original.** Waiting to see
whether an error matters is how an error becomes a misrepresentation.

**Three questions resolve any condition:** Do I know it? Would a reasonable buyer consider it
important? Am I qualified to evaluate it?

**The failures run in both directions**, so the rule is not "disclose everything" but *disclose what
is material and known, attribute what is unverified, decline what the law limits, and evaluate
nothing.* The audit question is whether a reviewer reading only the file could determine what the
buyer knew and when.
