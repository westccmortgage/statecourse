# Real Estate Practice — Lesson Assignment 02

## Building a lawful real estate practice

**Course version:** 1.0 development draft  
**Assignment time:** 180 instructional minutes  
**Regulatory status:** Not approved; no California credit is available.

A real estate practice is a controlled professional service, not merely a collection of leads. The practice must connect truthful marketing, lawful prospecting, fair access, broker identity, supervision, information security, and records. This assignment shows how those parts work together before a consumer signs a transaction document.

## Learning objectives

After completing this assignment, the student will be able to:

- convert a business goal into a supervised compliance workflow;
- distinguish lawful prospecting from misleading, intrusive, or discriminatory solicitation;
- identify California first-point-of-contact and responsible-broker advertising controls;
- explain the difference between a team name, a fictitious business name, and the responsible brokerage;
- recognize conversations and conduct that create antitrust risk;
- apply data-minimization, access, verification, and retention controls to consumer information; and
- create an advertising record that preserves sources, approvals, versions, distribution, and correction.

## 1. A professional practice begins with controls

### 1.1 From production goal to service standard

A business plan may include revenue, transaction, geographic, client, or specialty goals. Those goals are not themselves a compliance system. A lawful plan also describes how the brokerage will identify consumers, communicate services, verify claims, protect information, supervise people, document decisions, and measure whether service is fair and effective.

Consider a goal of securing twelve residential listings in a year. A weak plan says: “Contact as many owners as possible.” A stronger plan identifies:

- the lawful data source used to select prospective contacts;
- the service and property criteria defining the target market;
- the communication channels and applicable consent or contact restrictions;
- approved scripts and advertising materials;
- required license and responsible-broker identification;
- a process for honoring opt-outs and accommodation requests;
- objective follow-up standards;
- broker review and exception escalation;
- data security and deletion rules; and
- metrics comparing service across similarly situated consumers.

The controls protect the public and make the campaign reproducible. They also improve business quality because the brokerage can see which message, audience, and service promise produced a legitimate professional relationship.

### 1.2 Define the service before advertising it

An advertisement should describe a service the brokerage can actually deliver. Before making a claim such as “full-service listing representation,” the broker should define what that means: consultation, agency disclosure, property intake, pricing analysis, marketing authorization, showing coordination, offer presentation, disclosure management, contingency tracking, escrow coordination, closing, and records.

Promises become risky when they are broader than the office’s staffing, competence, license authority, or supervisory capacity. “We guarantee the highest price” presents a different risk from “we prepare a documented comparative market analysis and discuss pricing alternatives.” The first promises an outcome outside the licensee’s control; the second describes a professional process.

### 1.3 Assign responsibility

Every repeated marketing process should identify four roles:

| Role | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| Owner | Responsible broker or designated supervisor accountable for the process |
| Preparer | Person creating or assembling the content and supporting facts |
| Reviewer | Authorized licensee checking truthfulness, identity, fair-housing, and source support |
| Publisher | Person authorized to distribute the approved version through the controlled channel |

One person may perform several roles in a small office, but the review evidence should still exist. In a larger office, separating preparation from approval reduces the risk that speed or production pressure bypasses verification.

### 1.4 A campaign-control sheet

Before launch, a campaign-control sheet should state:

- campaign name and purpose;
- intended audience based on objective criteria;
- data source and permitted use;
- approved copy, images, links, and disclosures;
- licensee and responsible-broker identity;
- fair-housing and accessibility review;
- contact-rule review;
- approval date and approver;
- channels, dates, and version identifiers;
- response and opt-out procedure; and
- archive and correction location.

The sheet does not replace legal analysis. It makes the analysis visible and repeatable.

## 2. Prospecting and solicitation

### 2.1 Prospecting is professional contact

Prospecting seeks to create a professional relationship. It may occur through direct mail, email, telephone, text, social media, seminars, websites, open houses, referrals, or personal contact. The channel does not determine whether the communication is regulated. A digital message can be a solicitation, an advertisement, a first point of contact, or several at once.

The professional begins by asking:

1. Why was this consumer selected?
2. Is the selection criterion objective and lawful?
3. May the brokerage use the data and channel for this purpose?
4. Does the content identify the licensed parties as required?
5. Are every material claim and image supportable?
6. Is an opt-out or consent mechanism required?
7. Does the follow-up system provide comparable service?

### 2.2 Neutral audience selection

Audience selection can create risk even when the advertisement’s words appear neutral. A brokerage should not exclude or target people because of protected characteristics or use geography, language, name, browsing behavior, household composition, or other proxies to produce discriminatory housing opportunity.

Objective real-estate criteria can include a clearly defined property type, a prior lawful request for information, ownership of property within a service area, a transaction milestone, or a consumer’s stated professional need. The brokerage should document why the criterion relates to the offered service and review outcomes for unexplained differences.

### 2.3 Scripts and professional boundaries

A script can support consistency but must not force a false or misleading answer. A supervised script should contain:

- accurate identification of the caller and brokerage;
- a truthful purpose for the contact;
- no fabricated urgency, government affiliation, guaranteed result, or unsupported savings claim;
- a clear path for questions requiring a licensee;
- an opt-out process when applicable; and
- a method to record material consumer instructions.

An unlicensed assistant should not move from administrative scheduling into solicitation, qualification, pricing advice, property advice, or negotiation. The broker’s script and task matrix must define that boundary.

### 2.4 Referrals and compensation

Lead generation and referrals must be reviewed for licensing, compensation, disclosure, privacy, and federal or state restrictions applicable to the particular service. A label such as “marketing fee” does not control if the payment is actually for licensed activity. Likewise, a technology platform is not exempt from professional review merely because it automates introductions.

The licensee should identify who is being paid, what act produced the payment, whether the person is licensed or otherwise authorized, how the relationship is disclosed, and whether another law—such as a settlement-service or mortgage rule—applies.

### Applied prospecting scenario

A team buys a list labeled “likely Spanish-speaking homeowners” and plans to send a message offering a special listing service only to that group. The list vendor will not explain how language was inferred. The team also proposes that an unlicensed assistant call recipients and discuss likely sales price.

The correct analysis does not begin with translation quality. It asks whether the audience criterion is lawful and supportable, whether selective service creates fair-housing concerns, whether the data may be used, whether the assistant would perform licensed activity, and whether the content and identity comply with California advertising requirements. The campaign should not launch until the broker resolves those issues and records the decision.

## 3. California advertising and professional identity

### 3.1 Truth comes before design

Every material advertising statement should have a source. Property features, price, availability, permitted use, dimensions, school information, financing, incentives, experience, transaction volume, and claimed results can affect consumer decisions. The broker should determine which source supports the statement, when it was checked, and whether a limitation or attribution must accompany it.

“Buyer to verify” is not permission to publish a claim the licensee knows is false or lacks any reasonable basis to make. A disclaimer may explain limits, but it does not transform deception into truthful advertising.

### 3.2 First point of contact

Business and Professions Code section 10140.6 addresses licensee identification in specified advertising and first-point-of-contact solicitation materials. The current statute should be consulted for the exact application and exceptions. In operating terms, consumers should be able to identify the licensee and responsible broker rather than encountering a brand that appears to be an independent, unlicensed brokerage.

The review should consider:

- the name used by the licensee;
- the California license identification number;
- the responsible broker’s identity;
- an NMLS unique identifier when mortgage-loan-originator rules apply;
- whether the material is a first point of contact;
- whether a statutory sign exception applies; and
- whether the presentation is clear and conspicuous rather than technically present but hidden.

### 3.3 Team names

Business and Professions Code sections 10159.6 and 10159.7 govern team names. A qualifying team name uses the surname of at least one licensed team member with “associates,” “group,” or “team,” involves two or more licensees providing or holding themselves out as providing licensed services, and may not imply an independent brokerage.

Advertising containing a team name must display the team name and required licensed-member information conspicuously, and the responsible broker’s identity must be displayed as prominently and conspicuously as the team name. A team cannot use branding that leaves the public believing the team is a separately licensed brokerage.

The broker should not approve a design by checking words alone. Relative prominence, placement, size, contrast, mobile display, video duration, and accessibility can determine whether identification is genuinely clear.

### 3.4 Fictitious business names

A fictitious business name is a professional identity used for licensed activity and is subject to DRE approval requirements described in Business and Professions Code section 10159.5 and related rules. A county fictitious-business-name filing by itself does not establish DRE authority to conduct licensed brokerage activity under that name.

Before publication, the broker should verify that the exact name is authorized for the intended license record and that the advertisement satisfies responsible-broker and salesperson identification requirements applicable to its use.

### 3.5 Digitally altered property images

As of January 1, 2026, Business and Professions Code section 10140.8 addresses certain digitally altered real property images used in advertising. DRE’s 2026 AI advisory explains that materially altered images require clear disclosure and that the original, unaltered image must be available to consumers under the statute.

The rule is not limited to software branded as artificial intelligence. The professional should ask whether editing changes the apparent property rather than merely correcting ordinary image quality. Removing power lines, adding a view, replacing finishes, changing landscaping, eliminating structural elements, or virtually staging in a manner that hides actual condition may affect a consumer’s understanding.

An image-control record should preserve the original, edited version, description of changes, required disclosure, approval, publication locations, and correction history.

### Advertisement audit

Review a mock social-media post containing:

- a team name larger than the brokerage identity;
- a claim that the property includes “legal rental income” without a source;
- a digitally altered image removing a neighboring structure;
- “perfect for young families”; and
- no visible license number.

Identify each separate concern. Do not treat one disclaimer as a universal cure. The corrected post should use objective property facts, verified attribution, required identity, proper visual disclosure, and broker approval.

### California law note

Use the current [2026 California Real Estate Law](https://www.dre.ca.gov/Publications/RealEstateLaw.html), including Business and Professions Code sections 10140.6, 10140.8, and 10159.5–10159.7 and relevant Commissioner’s Regulations. DRE’s [2025 enforcement advisory](https://www.dre.ca.gov/Licensees/Advisories/Advisory_2025_08_26_Most_Common_Enforcement_Violations.html) identifies advertising, first-point-of-contact, team-name, and fictitious-name problems among recurring violations.

## 4. Competition and antitrust boundaries

### 4.1 Independent business decisions

Real estate professionals compete for clients, listings, buyers, talent, and services. Competitors must make independent decisions about commissions, fees, services, geographic focus, vendors, cooperation, and business terms. A trade association, multiple-listing environment, social group, or informal conversation does not authorize competitors to coordinate what should be decided independently.

Antitrust risk can arise from agreements or coordinated conduct involving:

- commission or fee fixing;
- division of territories, clients, property types, or price ranges;
- group boycotts or concerted refusals to deal;
- tying one service to another unlawfully; or
- exchange of competitively sensitive future information that supports coordination.

### 4.2 Commission language

A licensee should not say that a commission is “standard,” “fixed by law,” or “required by the MLS.” Brokerage compensation is negotiable within lawful arrangements. A professional may explain the firm’s independently established fee and service, but should not attribute that decision to an industry-wide agreement.

### 4.3 Risky conversations

Suppose competing brokers at a meeting complain about discount firms. One proposes that everyone refuse to show the discount firms’ listings until they raise compensation. A StateCourse student should recognize the risk immediately. The prudent response is to object, leave the discussion, avoid participating in any coordinated action, document the event, and obtain broker and legal guidance.

Silence followed by coordinated conduct may create risk. A licensee should not treat a joke, private message, or informal meal as outside antitrust review.

### 4.4 Cooperation without collusion

Cooperation is often necessary to complete a transaction. Agents may share property information, schedule access, present offers, coordinate disclosures, and communicate contract status. The boundary is between transaction-specific cooperation and agreements among competitors that restrain independent competition.

## 5. Privacy and information security

### 5.1 Collect only what the service needs

Real estate files may contain government identification, signatures, bank and loan records, access codes, wire instructions, contact information, family circumstances, and other sensitive data. The practice should not collect information merely because a form or software system permits it.

For each data element, ask:

- Why is it needed?
- Who is authorized to see it?
- How will it be verified?
- Where will it be stored?
- How long must or may it be retained?
- How will it be corrected or securely disposed of?

### 5.2 Access by role

Access should follow job responsibility. A marketing contractor does not ordinarily need loan documents. An assistant scheduling a showing does not need tax returns. A salesperson should not download an entire brokerage database to a personal device for convenience.

Controls can include unique accounts, multifactor authentication, least-privilege permissions, approved devices, encrypted transmission, secure sharing, audit logs, prompt offboarding, and periodic access review.

### 5.3 Wire and identity fraud

Transaction communications create opportunities for impersonation. A professional should treat changed wire instructions, urgent account requests, unfamiliar links, and altered contact details as high risk. Verification should use a trusted channel and a known contact method rather than replying only to the suspicious message.

The brokerage should teach consumers its verification procedure early, document the warning, and avoid transmitting sensitive account information through unapproved channels.

### 5.4 Incident response

When information may have been sent to the wrong person, an account is compromised, or a device is lost, the licensee should follow the brokerage incident plan immediately. The plan should identify containment, broker notification, technology response, preservation of evidence, legal review, required notices, consumer communication, and remediation.

Deleting an email does not establish that the recipient no longer has it. Delay can increase harm and reduce available options.

## 6. Records, measurement, and correction

### 6.1 Advertising archive

The brokerage should preserve enough information to reconstruct what the public saw and why it was approved:

- final copy and media;
- source records supporting material claims;
- required license and brokerage identification;
- original and altered images;
- approval actor and timestamp;
- publication channels and dates;
- audience criteria;
- lead or response disposition where appropriate;
- opt-out and complaint handling; and
- corrected versions with the reason for correction.

### 6.2 Transaction and listing records

Business and Professions Code section 10148 addresses broker retention and availability of specified records for examination. The student must review the current statute and applicable regulations rather than relying on a general statement that “files are kept for three years.” The event that starts a retention period, the records covered, longer obligations under other law, litigation holds, and broker policy must be correctly identified.

### 6.3 Measure fairness and accuracy

Useful compliance metrics include:

- time to first response;
- follow-up completion;
- opt-out processing time;
- advertising correction rate;
- source-verification exceptions;
- service differences across comparable inquiries;
- accommodation response; and
- unresolved supervisory exceptions.

Metrics should diagnose the process, not create incentives to conceal errors. A high correction rate may identify weak approval; a zero correction rate may indicate either excellent controls or failure to detect problems.

### 6.4 Applied campaign review

A California team proposes a campaign with these elements:

- purchased homeowner data of uncertain origin;
- language-based audience inference;
- an unlicensed caller discussing likely sales price;
- a team logo more prominent than the brokerage;
- a digitally altered property image without access to the original;
- the phrase “commission rates are standard in our market”; and
- consumer responses stored in a shared spreadsheet accessible to outside contractors.

Prepare a broker-review memo that identifies at least eight separate issues. For each issue, state the affected consumer or public interest, the current source or policy to consult, the person responsible for correction, and the evidence required before launch.

### Assignment summary

- A lawful practice connects business goals to supervision, truthful service promises, fair access, information security, and records.
- Prospecting requires lawful audience criteria, permitted data use, accurate identity, compliant contact, and consistent follow-up.
- California first-point-of-contact materials must satisfy current licensee and responsible-broker identification rules.
- Team and fictitious business names may not obscure the licensed brokerage.
- Material digital alteration of property images is subject to California’s 2026 disclosure and original-image requirements.
- Competitors must set prices and business terms independently.
- Sensitive consumer data should be minimized, protected by role, verified through trusted channels, and handled through an incident plan.
- Advertising and campaign files should preserve sources, approvals, versions, distribution, corrections, and outcomes.
