## 1. Business planning

### 1.1 The problem with a volume target

Most new licensees write a business plan that begins and ends with a number: transactions per quarter, gross commission income for the year. That number is not a plan. It is a wish with arithmetic attached, and it creates a specific hazard.

A transaction consumes a fixed quantity of work that cannot be compressed without cutting something. Intake and verification of authority. Disclosure preparation and delivery. Deadline tracking. Written confirmations. File assembly. When a licensee sets a target that exceeds their capacity to do that work, the target is still met — by skipping steps. The steps that get skipped are invisible until something goes wrong, which is precisely why they are the ones that get skipped.

A professional plan therefore works backward from capacity rather than forward from ambition:

1. Estimate the hours a transaction of your type actually requires, **including the compliance work**, not just the client-facing time.
2. Determine how many hours you can genuinely give to transactions after prospecting, administration, and continuing education.
3. Divide. That is your capacity.
4. If capacity is below your income requirement, the honest responses are to change your market, your price point, your efficiency, or your support — not to plan on doing less work per file.

### 1.2 Choosing a brokerage as a compliance decision

New licensees usually evaluate brokerages on commission split and brand. Those matter. They are not the whole question, because the brokerage you join determines the supervision you receive, and supervision is what protects you in your first two years when you do not yet know what you do not know.

Questions worth asking before signing:

- Who reviews my files, at what points, and what happens when something is missing?
- What is the written policy set, and may I read it before I join?
- What is the escalation path when I have a question I cannot answer?
- Who approves advertising, and how long does approval take?
- What training exists beyond the required licensing course?
- What happens to my files and my pending transactions if I leave?

A brokerage that cannot answer these is telling you something about the supervision you would receive.

### 1.3 Budgeting for the work nobody bills for

A realistic plan carries line items that new licensees routinely omit:

| Item | Why it belongs in the plan |
|---|---|
| Errors and omissions coverage | Terms and limits vary; the cost is real and the coverage is conditional on prompt notice |
| Continuing education | Required for renewal, and the deadline arrives whether or not the business is good |
| Records storage and security | Retention obligations run for years past closing |
| Transaction management tools | The deadline tracking that prevents most disputes |
| Time for corrections | Every practice generates errors; the plan should assume some |

### 1.4 What a defensible plan looks like

Rather than "twelve transactions this quarter," a defensible plan reads: *twelve transactions this quarter requires roughly N client hours and M compliance hours; that is X hours per week against my available Y; the gap is closed by adding transaction coordination support at Z cost, which the plan funds from the projected income.*

That version can be evaluated, adjusted, and defended. The number by itself cannot.

### 1.5 The revenue-timing problem

Capacity planning answers how much work you can do. It does not answer the question that ends most new practices, which is **when the money arrives.**

A transaction pays at closing. The work, the expenses, and the obligations begin long before that, and a purchase that opens in one month and closes two months later has consumed twelve weeks of a licensee's capacity before producing a dollar. **A plan built on annual income against annual expenses will balance on paper and fail in month five**, because the expenses are monthly and the income is lumpy.

The honest version of the plan carries three additional lines:

| Line | The question it answers |
|---|---|
| **Months of fixed costs held in reserve** | How long can the practice operate before the first closing pays? |
| **Expected lag from first client contact to first closing** | Measured for your market and your price point, not assumed |
| **What happens if the first two transactions fall out** | Which they may, for reasons having nothing to do with your work |

**The failure this prevents is not financial. It is ethical.** A licensee who is out of money in month five is a licensee under pressure to close a transaction that should not close, to skip a disclosure conversation that would slow things down, or to accept a client whose situation he is not equipped to handle. Every enforcement matter involving a new licensee has a version of this pressure somewhere behind it.

**Illustrative only — the figures are yours to determine:** a licensee who calculates fixed monthly costs of a given amount, an expected lag of ninety to one hundred twenty days from first contact to first closing, and a reserve covering three months, can see immediately that the reserve is short by the length of one transaction cycle. That is a solvable problem in month zero and an unsolvable one in month four.

**The plan is revisited quarterly**, against what actually happened rather than what was projected. A plan that is never compared to results is a document, not a plan — and the comparison is most valuable in the quarters where the projection was wrong.

### 1.6 A worked plan

> **Facts.** A newly licensed salesperson intends to work resale residential in a market where the median price supports a typical side commission of roughly nine thousand dollars at his brokerage's split. He wants sixty thousand dollars in his first year. He writes: "Ten transactions. Eight hundred contacts. Two open houses a week."
>
> **What the plan does not say.**
>
> **Capacity.** He has not estimated the hours a transaction takes. Working backward from Section 1.1: a resale purchase in his market consumes client-facing time — showings, negotiation, walkthrough — plus compliance time that does not vary with the price of the house. Disclosure preparation, delivery and receipt tracking, deadline management, written confirmations, and file assembly are the same work on a modest condominium as on an estate. **The compliance hours are the ones his plan has silently valued at zero.**
>
> **Sequence.** Ten transactions in twelve months is not ten transactions spread evenly. Buyers cluster, escrows overlap, and two files closing in the same week consume the compliance time of both at once. **A plan that averages is a plan that has not asked what the worst week looks like.**
>
> **Cash.** Eight hundred contacts produce a first client at some point, and that client closes some weeks after that. Section 1.5 asks the question this plan does not: what does he live on until then, and what does he do if the first two files fall out?
>
> **Support.** He has assumed he does everything himself. That is a decision, not a default, and it is the decision that determines whether the tenth transaction is done to the same standard as the first.
>
> **The rewritten plan.**
>
> *Ten sides at roughly nine thousand net is ninety thousand gross against a sixty-thousand income requirement, leaving thirty thousand for costs. Each transaction requires an estimated client-facing block plus a compliance block; ten transactions therefore consume a total that, spread across my available weekly hours, leaves a specific number of prospecting hours. Two open houses weekly plus follow-up consumes most of them. The gap closes by engaging transaction coordination at a per-file cost, funded from the thirty thousand, which also carries errors and omissions coverage, continuing education, records storage, and a correction allowance. I hold three months of fixed costs in reserve against an expected ninety-to-one-hundred-twenty-day lag to first closing. I will compare this to actuals at the end of each quarter.*
>
> **The difference between the two versions is not optimism.** It is that the second one can be wrong in a way that is visible in time to fix, and the first one can only be wrong in a way that is discovered in month nine.

## 2. Prospecting and solicitation

### 2.1 Prospecting is lawful; solicitation is regulated

Building a client base by reaching out to people is a normal and necessary part of the business. The regulation sits on *how* you reach out, and the rules come from several directions at once: telephone solicitation restrictions, requirements for identifying yourself, consent requirements for text and email, and the general prohibition on misleading representations.

A licensee who thinks of this as one topic — "am I allowed to contact this person?" — will get it wrong, because the answer differs by channel.

### 2.2 Telephone contact

Two obligations dominate.

**Do-not-call restrictions.** Registries exist and must be respected. A licensee cannot rely on a lead vendor's assurance that a list is clean; the obligation is the caller's. Established business relationships and inquiries create defined, limited exceptions — and their limits matter, because an exception that has lapsed is not an exception.

**Identification.** A caller must identify who they are and on whose behalf they are calling. A licensee who opens with a question rather than an identification has already created a problem.

#### Expired listings and unrepresented sellers

Contacting owners whose listings recently expired, or who are selling without representation, is a standard prospecting approach and is not prohibited as such. What the licensee must confirm first is whether the number may lawfully be called, and what identification the call requires. "The listing expired, so they are fair game" is not an analysis of either question.

### 2.3 Text and email

Text messaging carries consent requirements that are stricter than most licensees assume, and the consent is defined by **what the consumer was actually told when the information was collected** — not by the fact that a number was written down.

This is where open house sign-in sheets become a compliance question rather than a marketing one. If a visitor provides a phone number on a sheet that says nothing about future contact, that number has not been consented for a marketing text campaign. If the sheet says plainly what the visitor is agreeing to receive, it has.

Two further points:

- An **opt-out mechanism is necessary but not sufficient.** Including "reply STOP" does not create the consent required to send the first message.
- **Commercial email** carries its own requirements — accurate headers and subject lines, identification, a functioning opt-out, and honoring opt-outs promptly.

### 2.4 In-person and door-to-door

Local ordinances may regulate door-to-door solicitation, and some communities require permits. Common-interest developments frequently prohibit solicitation on the property entirely. A licensee working a neighborhood should know which rules apply before knocking, and should never represent that they are calling on behalf of a neighbor, an association, or any party who has not authorized it.

### 2.5 The representation that must not be made

Across every channel, one category of statement is uniformly prohibited: a representation that creates a false impression of urgency, endorsement, or authority. "The neighbors asked me to reach out." "Values in this area are about to drop." "I have a buyer for your house" — when no such buyer exists. Each is a misrepresentation, and prospecting pressure is not a defense.

### 2.6 The record that makes a contact defensible

Every rule in this section is proved or disproved by one thing: **what the file can show about where the contact came from and what the consumer agreed to.**

A licensee who cannot answer "why was this person contacted, and on what basis" is in the same position regardless of whether the contact was lawful. The complaint is answered by the record or it is not answered.

**What a defensible contact record contains:**

1. **The source of the contact information** — the specific open house, the specific inquiry, the specific referral, with its date. "Purchased list" is a source, and it is the one that carries the licensee's own obligation to verify.
2. **What the person was told when the information was collected** — the actual language of the sign-in sheet or the web form, retained as it appeared at that time.
3. **The registry check**, where the channel requires one, with its date.
4. **The exception relied on, if any**, and the date the relationship or inquiry that created it began — because **an exception has a duration, and a lapsed exception is not an exception.**
5. **Every opt-out received**, with the date and the channel, honored across all channels rather than only the one it arrived through.

**The sign-in sheet deserves a note of its own.** The version in the file must be the version the visitor actually signed. A brokerage that revises its sheet language and retains only the current version has destroyed the evidence for every contact collected under the prior wording — and the prior wording is precisely what a complaint about an older contact will turn on.

> **The practical test:** hand your contact records to someone who was not present and ask them to determine, for any single contact, whether it was permissible. If they cannot, neither can a regulator, and the burden of the ambiguity does not fall on the consumer.

### 2.7 Checkpoint

You should be able to state, for a phone call, a text, and an email, what makes the contact permissible and where the consent or exception comes from.

## 3. Advertising identification

### 3.1 Why identification is required

A consumer looking at an advertisement needs to know two things that the property description does not tell them: who is responsible for this, and are they licensed. Identification requirements exist so a consumer can answer both, and so the supervising brokerage can be held accountable for what goes out under its name.

That purpose explains the shape of the rules. California requires a licensee's identification — including license identification — to appear on solicitation materials, and requires that advertising identify the brokerage under which the licensee operates. A salesperson may not advertise in a way that presents them as an independent principal.

### 3.2 The most common defects

| Defect | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| Personal brand and mobile number, no brokerage named | The consumer cannot tell there is a supervising brokerage, or which one |
| License identification omitted from solicitation material | The consumer cannot verify licensure |
| Team name displayed more prominently than the brokerage, styled as a separate firm | Creates the impression of an independent company that is not licensed |
| Social media property posts treated as personal expression | Promoting listings is advertising regardless of the platform |
| Photographs no longer showing the property's condition | Truthfulness is measured against the property now, not when the photo was taken |
| Photographs of a different, nicer unit | Misrepresents the unit actually offered, however similar the units are |

### 3.3 Team names

Teams are ordinary and useful. The compliance question is whether the team's presentation misleads a consumer about who the responsible brokerage is. A team identity that appears to be a separate company obscures accountability just as effectively as omitting the brokerage entirely, and the internal organizational chart is not the test — the impression created for a consumer is.

Practical guidance: the brokerage's identity should be present and legible wherever the team's identity appears, and the team's name should not use words suggesting it is itself a brokerage or an independent entity.

### 3.4 Approval and archive

Two office controls make advertising compliance real rather than aspirational.

**Pre-publication approval.** A named approver, a defined turnaround, and no exceptions. If approval is optional in practice, the brokerage cannot demonstrate control of its own advertising.

**An archive.** What ran, when, on what platform, with what approval. An advertisement that cannot be produced cannot be reviewed — and a consumer complaint about an advertisement from eight months ago is exactly when the archive earns its cost.

### 3.5 Truthfulness beyond identification

Identification is necessary and not sufficient. An advertisement must also be accurate about the property, and accuracy is measured at the time it runs. Square footage should trace to a source and the source should be stated. Features described must exist. Photographs must represent the property as it is. "Priced below market" is an opinion presented as fact unless it can be supported.

### 3.6 Advertising where advertising actually happens

The identification requirements were written for a world of signs, print, and mailers, and they apply without modification to every place a licensee now actually advertises. The difficulty is not that the rules are unclear. It is that these channels do not feel like advertising while you are using them.

| Channel | Where licensees go wrong |
|---|---|
| **Social profile** | The profile itself is advertising. A bio naming the licensee and the service without the required identification is a defect on every post it appears above. |
| **Short video** | Identification burned into a caption that the platform crops, or spoken so quickly it is not conveyed. **Presence is not the standard; conveyance is.** |
| **Direct messages** | Feel like conversation, function as solicitation, and carry the same identification and consent obligations as any other channel. |
| **Review and referral sites** | Profiles written by the platform from scraped data still advertise the licensee's services. **The obligation is the licensee's, not the platform's.** |
| **Syndicated listings** | Content is reformatted by systems the licensee does not control, and identification can be stripped in the process. |
| **Paid targeting** | Restricting who sees a housing advertisement is a fair housing question before it is a marketing one. |

**Two habits close most of these gaps.**

**First, the archive.** Capture what was actually published, as published, with its date — a screenshot of the post as it rendered, not the draft that was submitted. Platforms edit, crop, expire, and delete, and a licensee whose only record is the text he wrote cannot show what the consumer saw.

**Second, the review point.** Advertising review that happens only for print and signage will never see the channels that produce the volume. **If approval is required, it is required for the medium where most of the advertising occurs**, and a policy that exempts social media in practice has exempted advertising in practice.

> A licensee who would not put an unidentified sign in a yard should not put an unidentified video on a platform. The consumer's confusion is identical and so is the rule.

### 3.7 A worked advertising review

> **Submitted for approval:** a thirty-second video for a social platform. On screen: exterior and interior footage of a listing, the licensee on camera for the last eight seconds. Overlaid text reads "JUST LISTED — 3BR — $749,000 — DM me!" The caption below the video reads: "Another one! My clients LOVE this neighborhood — best schools in the area and values here are only going up. Serving the whole county. Call or text me anytime — Jordan." The licensee's profile bio reads "Jordan | Real Estate | Let's find your home."
>
> **The findings, in order of seriousness.**
>
> **1. No license identification anywhere.** Not in the video, not in the overlay, not in the caption, not in the bio. The video, the caption, and the profile are each advertising, and **none of them identifies the licensee as a licensee or names the brokerage.** This is the defect that appears in every channel in Section 3.6 and it is present here three times.
>
> **2. "Best schools in the area."** A statement about the composition and quality of a neighborhood, attached to a housing advertisement. **This is a fair housing problem before it is an accuracy problem** — school-quality claims are among the most common ways an advertisement conveys a preference about who a neighborhood is for. It comes out, and if the client's interest is in schools, the licensee directs the client to the district's own published information.
>
> **3. "Values here are only going up."** A prediction stated as a fact, in an advertisement, about the thing the consumer is being invited to spend money on. **It is not defensible in any market**, and it is the sentence a complaint quotes.
>
> **4. "Serving the whole county."** Only if true. A claim about service area is a representation, and one that is aspirational today is a misrepresentation today.
>
> **5. "DM me" and "text me anytime."** An invitation to contact is not consent to be contacted. The messages the licensee sends **after** a consumer responds are governed by Section 2.3, and an inbound direct message does not create standing consent for a marketing campaign.
>
> **6. No archive.** Nothing in the submission captures how the post will render — where the platform crops the overlay, whether the caption truncates behind a "more" link, and whether the identification, once added, survives either. **The archive requirement in Section 3.6 is what makes the approval meaningful**, because approval of a draft is not evidence about the publication.
>
> **The corrected version** carries the required identification in the caption's first line where truncation cannot hide it and in the profile bio, drops the school claim and the value prediction entirely, states the service area accurately, and is archived as published with its date. **It is not a worse advertisement.** The two removed sentences were the two that could not be defended.

## 4. Antitrust and competition

### 4.1 Why this is the most serious topic in the lesson

Most compliance failures in real estate produce a fine, a correction, or discipline. Antitrust violations can produce criminal exposure and very large civil liability, and they attach to conduct that feels social rather than commercial — a conversation at a lunch, a comment at an association meeting, a thread in a group chat.

The core principle is short: **each brokerage sets its own prices and chooses its own business partners independently.** Anything that looks like coordination among competitors on those decisions is dangerous.

### 4.2 The four patterns to recognize

1. **Price fixing** — an agreement among competitors on what to charge, on a minimum, on a "customary" rate that everyone will observe, or even on future pricing intentions. Commission rates are negotiable between a brokerage and its client, and always have been. A licensee who tells a seller that a rate is "standard," "set by the MLS," or "what everyone charges" has both misstated the law and edged toward the violation.

2. **Group boycotts** — an agreement among competitors to refuse to deal with a particular brokerage, typically one competing on price or on a different model. "We shouldn't show their listings" is the sentence to recognize.

3. **Market allocation** — an agreement among competitors to divide territories, property types, or customer segments so as not to compete for them.

4. **Tying** — conditioning the availability of one service on the customer's purchase of another.

### 4.3 Agreements do not require documents

The single most dangerous misconception is that an agreement requires something signed, or explicit. It does not. Agreement can be inferred from conduct, from parallel behavior following a discussion, and from the exchange of information about future pricing intentions.

This is why simply *listening* is a risk. If competitors at a table discuss coordinating rates and you stay, your presence can be treated as participation.

#### The required response

> **Leave the conversation immediately and visibly. Say why. Report it to your broker.**
>
> Not "change the subject." Not "stay and say nothing." Leaving is what creates the record that you did not participate, and reporting it is what protects both you and the brokerage.

### 4.4 What is lawful

The rules do not prohibit competition or discussion of the industry. A brokerage may set whatever rates it chooses, may change them, may advertise them, and may decide independently which brokerages it prefers to work with and why. Licensees may discuss market conditions, inventory, and general practice. The line is coordination with competitors on price or on refusing to deal.

### 4.5 What to tell a seller about compensation

When a seller asks why the compensation is stated as a negotiated figure:

> "Compensation is negotiable between you and our brokerage. There is no standard rate and no rate set by any association or listing service. Here is what our brokerage charges and what it covers."

That answer is accurate, it is the law, and it forecloses the follow-up question that a wrong answer invites.

### 4.6 Leaving a conversation that has turned

The rules in this section describe conduct. The practical difficulty is different: these conversations begin in ordinary professional settings, among people who are friendly, and the moment they turn is not announced.

An agent at a caravan says, "Nobody around here is going below a certain rate anymore." A broker in a class says, "We should all stop showing the discount brokerages." A colleague says, "That new firm is going to have a hard time — I've told everyone I know not to work with them."

**Each of those is an invitation, and silence in the room reads as agreement.** Section 4.3 established that an agreement does not require a document — a course of conduct following a conversation is evidence of the conversation, and the participants who said nothing are participants.

**The exit has three parts, and all three matter:**

1. **Say it out loud, immediately.** "I set my own fees independently and I don't discuss them with other firms." It is short, it is not accusatory, and it is heard by everyone present — which is the point, because the record you are creating is the recollection of the other people in the room.
2. **Leave the conversation.** Physically, or from the thread. Continuing to participate on other topics after the statement weakens it.
3. **Write it down that day.** A dated note: where you were, who was present, what was said, what you said, and that you left. Send it to your broker.

**The third step is the one that gets skipped and the one that works.** Antitrust exposure is reconstructed years later from testimony about rooms full of people. A contemporaneous note from the person who objected is worth more than a confident memory, and it is the difference between being a witness and being a defendant.

> **What does not work:** changing the subject, laughing it off, replying privately afterward, or staying and saying nothing because the speaker is senior. **The response is the same regardless of who is speaking**, and a licensee who cannot make it to his own manager has learned something important about where he works.

## 5. Privacy and information security

### 5.1 What a transaction file actually contains

A residential transaction file holds identification documents, Social Security numbers, bank statements, tax returns, employment records, and the address where a family now lives. Collectively that is enough to open credit in someone's name. Real estate licensees hold this material routinely and often think of it as paperwork rather than as sensitive data.

Three controls carry most of the protection.

**Access.** Limit file access to the people who need it for the work. Office-wide access to every file "in case someone needs to cover" is convenient and indefensible.

**Transmission.** Protected channels for documents containing personal information. Ordinary email is not a protected channel, and "please delete this after you use it" is not a control.

**Disposal.** Destroy documents so the data cannot be reconstructed. A recycling bin is not disposal. This applies to electronic media as well as paper.

### 5.2 Wire fraud

This is the single most damaging fraud pattern in residential real estate, and it is worth learning as a specific sequence rather than as general caution.

**How it works.** An attacker gains access to an email account somewhere in the transaction — the licensee's, the escrow officer's, sometimes the consumer's. They watch quietly, learn the closing date and the amounts, then send wire instructions that look exactly like the real ones from an address one character different from the genuine one, usually timed to arrive when the consumer is expecting them.

**Why it succeeds.** The instructions arrive in the right thread, at the right time, with the right names and figures. Nothing about the content is wrong. Only the account details and one character of the sender address differ.

**What defeats it.** Verification through a channel the attacker does not control:

1. Call the settlement agent at a number you obtained independently — from the engagement documents or the company's published listing — **never** from the email itself.
2. Verify the account details by voice before any funds move.
3. Warn every consumer in writing, early, that wire instructions will never be changed by email and that they must call to verify.

**What does not defeat it.** Replying to the email to ask for confirmation — the attacker receives the reply. Encryption — the message is genuine-looking, not intercepted. A small test wire — it confirms nothing about who controls the account.

### 5.3 When something goes wrong

If a device is lost, an account is compromised, or a document is misdirected, the questions that determine severity are: what data was exposed, was it protected, and who could read it. Insurance addresses the hardware; procedure addresses fault; neither tells the brokerage what was actually at risk.

Report immediately to the broker. Preserve the evidence. Do not attempt to quietly assess it alone — breach obligations can carry notification requirements with timelines, and the clock does not wait for an internal decision.

### 5.4 The perimeter you do not control

A licensee's information security is only as good as the weakest party holding the same data, and in a transaction that data is held by many parties at once — the brokerage, the transaction coordinator, the lender, escrow, title, the inspector, the photographer, and whatever software each of them uses.

**The licensee cannot secure the perimeter. He can control four things.**

**What he sends.** The single most effective control in the list, and the least used. A vendor who needs to know the closing date does not need the file. A photographer does not need the seller's contact information. **Data not sent cannot be leaked**, and most over-sharing happens because sending the whole file is faster than assembling the part.

**How he sends it.** Documents containing identification numbers, financial account information, or dates of birth do not travel as ordinary email attachments. The brokerage's designated method exists for this, and "it was faster" is the explanation given after every incident of this kind.

**What is on his own devices.** A phone containing a year of transaction photographs, client messages, and document scans is a file cabinet that fits in a pocket and is left in restaurants. Device encryption, a real passcode, remote wipe capability, and separation of business from personal accounts are baseline rather than advanced.

**What happens when a relationship ends.** Access is removed when a coordinator, assistant, or vendor stops working on the file — not when someone remembers. An account that remains active after the relationship ends is the access no one is monitoring.

> **The vendor question to ask before engaging one:** what data will you hold, where, for how long, who else can see it, and what happens to it when we stop working together? A vendor who cannot answer has answered.

**And one obligation that survives everything:** the duty attaching to a client's confidential information does not end when the transaction closes. Section 5.1 listed what the file contains; **that content remains sensitive for as long as it exists**, which is why the retention workflow in Section 6 and the security discipline here are the same subject approached from two directions.

### 5.5 Checkpoint

You should be able to describe the wire fraud sequence and name the one verification step that defeats it, and explain why replying to the suspicious email does not.

## 6. Records and retention workflow

### 6.1 The records belong to the brokerage

A licensee generates documents, but the transaction records are the brokerage's, and the broker is answerable for them. That single fact resolves most questions in this section.

The licensee's obligation is therefore not to *keep* the record but to **route complete documentation into the brokerage's system**, promptly, in the form the office requires. A personal folder, a phone's photo library, and a personal email account are not the record. They are copies, and a file that exists only in them is a file the brokerage cannot produce.

### 6.2 What the workflow must produce

A working records process delivers four things:

1. **Completeness** — every required document, including for transactions that did not close. Failed and cancelled transactions are disproportionately the ones that later produce disputes.
2. **Dates and delivery evidence** — not just signatures.
3. **Retention** — for the required period, which runs from closing rather than from when the file stopped being interesting.
4. **Retrievability** — a file that cannot be found within a reasonable time is functionally not retained.

### 6.3 Retention length and the temptation to shorten it

Retention obligations run for years. Storage costs money, and the temptation to purge early is real and recurring.

Two points settle it. First, the obligation runs to the regulator, so neither the client's consent, nor payoff of a loan, nor the absence of any dispute authorizes early destruction. Second, if office policy sets a longer period than the legal minimum, the policy governs the licensee's conduct — and changing it is a deliberate policy decision that starts with confirming the current requirement, not an individual judgment call made at a filing cabinet.

### 6.4 Departure

When a licensee leaves a brokerage, three things must be settled and should have been settled in writing long before:

- **The files stay.** They are the brokerage's records. What the departing licensee may take in copy form is governed by office policy and by client direction.
- **The clients choose.** Representation agreements are between the client and the brokerage; where a client goes is the client's decision, made with accurate information about their options.
- **Pending transactions need a named owner.** A transaction mid-escrow with an unclear responsible licensee is where deadlines are missed.

### 6.5 The audit habit

The most useful records practice is a short review at two points: when a transaction closes, and periodically across open files.

At closing, confirm that every required document is present, dated, and evidenced; that the documents reconcile; and that the chronology makes sense against the contract's deadlines. Across open files, confirm that upcoming deadlines are tracked and that nothing is sitting unrouted in a licensee's personal storage.

Findings are recorded and corrected honestly. A gap noted in the file is survivable. A gap filled in afterward with a backdated document is falsification, and it converts a compliance problem into conduct that ends a career.

### 6.6 The close-out routine

Everything in this section works if one habit exists and fails if it does not: **the file is finished when the transaction closes, not eventually.**

A file left to be completed later is completed from memory, by someone who has since worked forty other transactions, if it is completed at all. The close-out routine exists because the only moment at which a file can be assembled accurately is the moment the facts are still fresh and the other participants still answer the phone.

**The routine, performed within days of closing:**

1. **Inventory against the checklist.** Every document the transaction type requires, present, including for the parts that did not go smoothly.
2. **Dates.** Everything dated. Delivery evidenced wherever a right or a deadline ran from receipt.
3. **Signatures.** Every required signature present, and no unexecuted draft filed where the executed version belongs.
4. **Consistency.** Names, addresses, amounts, and dates reconcile across documents. A property described two ways is a defect even where everyone knew what was meant.
5. **Versions.** Superseded versions retained, in sequence, so the file can show when a term changed and who agreed to it.
6. **Communications.** The messages that evidence a decision, an instruction, or a disclosure, moved from an inbox into the file — because **an email is not in the file merely because it exists somewhere.**
7. **The gaps, written down.** What is missing, why, and what was done to obtain it.

**Step seven is the one that separates a professional file from a hopeful one.** A file with a documented gap is a file that was audited. A file with an undocumented gap is a file that was not — and from the outside the two are indistinguishable until someone asks.

> **The obtainable-now test:** for every gap, ask whether the document can still be obtained from the party who holds it. The answer is usually yes in week one and usually no in year two, and that difference is the entire argument for the routine.

### 6.7 Checkpoint

You should be able to explain who owns transaction records and why, what a departing licensee may and may not take, and why cancelled transactions matter as much as closed ones.

### 6.8 A worked close-out

> **A transaction closed eleven days ago.** The licensee opens the file for the close-out routine and finds:
>
> - The purchase agreement, fully executed, and three counteroffers — one of which is present only as an unsigned draft.
> - A transfer disclosure with the seller's signature and no buyer acknowledgment.
> - A contingency removal, signed, dated in the buyer's handwriting.
> - An agency disclosure signed by the buyer, and none for the seller.
> - A text thread on the licensee's phone in which the parties agreed to extend the inspection period by four days.
> - An inspection report addressed to the buyer, forwarded by email.
> - A closing statement, and a commission disbursement authorization.
>
> **What the routine produces.**
>
> **The unsigned counteroffer is the most urgent item**, because the executed version exists somewhere — the other brokerage has it, and eleven days after closing the other agent still answers the phone. In eight months she may not, and the file will permanently show a negotiation with a missing link. **Obtainable now, not later.**
>
> **The missing buyer acknowledgment on the transfer disclosure** is the same kind of item and the second call. Receipt is what starts the buyer's rights running; a disclosure the file cannot show was received is a disclosure the file cannot rely on.
>
> **The seller agency disclosure** may exist in the listing file rather than the transaction file, which is a filing problem rather than a missing document — **but the licensee confirms that rather than assuming it.** If it does not exist, that is a finding, and it is recorded as one.
>
> **The text thread is evidence of a contract modification living on a personal device.** It is exported into the file today. An extension agreed by text and never reduced to an amendment is a separate issue to raise with the broker; the immediate act is preserving what exists before a phone is replaced.
>
> **The contingency removal is dated, which is the good news**, and the date is checked against the extension in the text thread — because if the removal is dated by reference to the original inspection deadline rather than the extended one, the file's chronology does not reconcile.
>
> **The disbursement authorization and closing statement are checked against each other** for amount and payee.
>
> **The written result** is a short close-out memorandum listing what was obtained, from whom, on what date; what remains missing and why; and which items were escalated to the broker. It is dated today and filed with the transaction.
>
> **That memorandum is the difference between a file with gaps and a file nobody checked.** Two years from now it will be the only document that says the licensee looked.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **Capacity** | The number of transactions a licensee can handle while still performing the compliance work each one requires. The honest basis for a business plan. |
| **Solicitation** | Contacting a person to obtain business. Lawful, and regulated differently by channel — telephone, text, email, and in person each carry their own requirements. |
| **Do-not-call obligations** | Restrictions on telephone solicitation that bind the caller. A lead vendor's assurance that a list is clean does not transfer the obligation. |
| **Established business relationship** | A defined, limited exception to certain solicitation restrictions. It has boundaries and it lapses. |
| **Consent** | For text and email marketing, defined by what the consumer was actually told when the information was collected — not by the fact that a number was written down. |
| **Opt-out mechanism** | A required means of stopping further messages. Necessary, and not a substitute for the consent needed to send the first one. |
| **Licensee identification** | The requirement that advertising and solicitation material identify the licensee and the brokerage, including license identification, so a consumer knows who is responsible and can verify licensure. |
| **Team name** | A brand used by a group within a brokerage. Compliant when it does not create the impression of a separate licensed firm. |
| **Price fixing** | An agreement among competitors on what to charge, on a minimum, or on future pricing intentions. Among the most serious antitrust violations. |
| **Group boycott** | An agreement among competitors to refuse to deal with a particular firm, typically one competing on price or model. |
| **Market allocation** | An agreement among competitors to divide territories, property types, or customer segments. |
| **Tying** | Conditioning the availability of one service on the customer's purchase of another. |
| **Inferred agreement** | An agreement established by conduct, parallel behavior, or exchange of pricing intentions rather than by a signed document. Why remaining in the conversation is itself a risk. |
| **Negotiable compensation** | The rule that brokerage compensation is set independently by each firm and negotiated with the client. There is no standard rate and none set by any association or listing service. |
| **Access control** | Limiting who can reach consumer personal information to those who need it for the work. |
| **Secure disposal** | Destroying documents and media so the data cannot be reconstructed. A recycling bin is not disposal. |
| **Wire fraud** | The pattern in which an attacker monitors a compromised email account, then sends altered wire instructions from an address one character different from the genuine one, timed to when funds are expected. |
| **Independent verification** | Confirming instructions through a channel the attacker does not control — a number obtained from engagement documents or a published listing, never from the email itself. |
| **Records ownership** | The principle that transaction records belong to the brokerage and the responsible broker answers for them, whoever created them. |
| **Retention period** | The period for which records must be kept, running from closing. It applies to cancelled and failed transactions as well as closed ones. |

## Practice problems

### Problem 1

A new licensee sets a target of sixteen transactions in her first year, based on what the top
producer in her office closed. She has no support staff and works part-time hours in her first six
months.

**What is wrong with the plan, and how should it be rebuilt?**

> **Solution.** The number is not tied to capacity. Each transaction consumes a fixed quantity of
> compliance work — intake and verification, disclosure preparation and delivery, deadline tracking,
> written confirmations, file assembly — that cannot be compressed without cutting something.
>
> Rebuild by working backward: hours a transaction actually requires including that work, hours she
> can genuinely give after prospecting and administration, divide. If capacity falls short of her
> income requirement, the honest responses are to change her market, price point, efficiency, or
> support — not to plan on doing less work per file.
>
> **What transfers:** a target that exceeds capacity is still met, by skipping the steps nobody sees
> until something goes wrong.

### Problem 2

A licensee collects phone numbers on an open-house sign-in sheet headed only "Please sign in." Two
weeks later he sends a marketing text to everyone who signed.

**Is the text permissible?**

> **Solution.** No, on the consent question. Consent for text marketing is defined by what the
> consumer was told when the information was collected. A sheet saying nothing about future contact
> has not obtained consent for a marketing campaign.
>
> Adding "reply STOP" does not cure it — an opt-out mechanism is necessary but does not create the
> consent required to send the first message.
>
> **The fix is upstream:** write the register so it states plainly what the visitor is agreeing to
> receive, and apply it to every visitor.

### Problem 3

At an industry lunch, a competing broker says he is tired of a discount brokerage undercutting
everyone, and proposes that those present hold their rates at the same level and stop showing that
brokerage's listings. Your licensee says nothing and leaves when the lunch ends.

**Is silence sufficient?**

> **Solution.** No. Two of the most serious antitrust violations were just proposed — price fixing
> and a group boycott — and agreement can be inferred from conduct rather than requiring anything
> signed. Remaining at the table can be treated as participation.
>
> The required response is to **leave the conversation immediately and visibly, say why, and report
> it to the broker.** Leaving is what creates the record that she did not participate; reporting is
> what protects her and the brokerage.
>
> **What transfers:** "I didn't agree to anything" is not the standard. Presence during the
> discussion is the exposure.

### Problem 4

A seller asks why the compensation in the listing agreement is stated as a negotiated figure rather
than the standard rate.

**What is the correct answer, and why does the wording matter?**

> **Solution.** "Compensation is negotiable between you and our brokerage. There is no standard rate,
> and no rate set by any association or listing service. Here is what our brokerage charges and what
> it covers."
>
> The wording matters because describing a rate as standard, customary, or set by a listing service
> is both factually wrong and an antitrust exposure. It is the sentence that later gets quoted, and
> a casual "everyone charges about this" is the version most licensees actually say.

### Problem 5

A team within a brokerage advertises under a name displayed far more prominently than the
brokerage's, styled with its own logo and suggesting an independent company. Every advertisement is
factually accurate about the properties.

**Is there a defect?**

> **Solution.** Yes. The test is the impression created for a consumer, not the internal
> organizational chart. A team identity presented as a separate firm obscures which licensed
> brokerage is accountable — the same defect as omitting the brokerage entirely.
>
> Accuracy about the properties is a different requirement and does not cure it.
>
> **The fix:** brokerage identity present and legible wherever the team identity appears, and a team
> name that does not use terms suggesting it is itself a brokerage.

### Problem 6

Two days before closing, a buyer receives wire instructions in the existing email thread from what
appears to be the escrow officer's address. The instructions match the expected amount and closing
date. The reply-to address differs from earlier messages by one character.

**What should happen, and what does not work?**

> **Solution.** Stop. Verify by calling escrow at a number obtained independently — from the
> engagement documents or the company's published listing, **never** from the email. Warn the client
> in writing not to send funds until verified.
>
> What does not work: replying to the email to ask for confirmation (the attacker receives the
> reply); forwarding it to the client with a note to be careful; a small test wire, which confirms
> nothing about who controls the account; or proceeding because the message arrived in a familiar
> thread.
>
> **What transfers:** verification must use a channel the attacker does not control. Everything else
> is theatre.

### Problem 7

A licensee is leaving her brokerage and wants to take copies of the files for every transaction she
personally handled, with the clients' personal information removed.

**May she?**

> **Solution.** Not unilaterally. Transaction records belong to the brokerage, and the responsible
> broker answers for them. What she may take is governed by the brokerage's policy and by client
> direction.
>
> Redaction addresses a different risk and does not change who owns the record. Neither does the fact
> that she created the documents.
>
> **What transfers:** the departure question should be settled in writing in the office policy long
> before anyone departs. A brokerage without a written answer will discover this during an argument.

### Problem 8

A brokerage's laptop containing client files is lost. It was password-protected but the drive was
not encrypted. The office manager suggests waiting to see whether anything comes of it.

**What determines the severity, and what should happen now?**

> **Solution.** Severity is determined by **what data the device held and whether it was protected** —
> not by whether a client has yet complained, and not by whether the hardware is insured.
>
> What happens now: report to the broker immediately, preserve the evidence, and determine the
> categories of personal information exposed. Breach obligations can carry notification timelines,
> and the clock does not wait for an internal decision about whether it matters.
>
> **What transfers:** password protection is not encryption, and waiting is a decision with a
> deadline attached.

## Chapter summary

**A business plan is a capacity calculation, not a target.** Each transaction consumes fixed
compliance work. A number that exceeds capacity gets met by skipping the steps nobody sees.

**Choosing a brokerage is a compliance decision.** File review, written policies, escalation path,
advertising approval, training, and what happens on departure determine the supervision a new
licensee actually receives.

**Prospecting is lawful; solicitation is regulated by channel.** Telephone contact carries do-not-call
and identification obligations. Text and email consent is defined by what the consumer was told at
collection. An opt-out is necessary and not sufficient.

**Advertising identification exists so a consumer can find who is responsible.** Personal branding,
team names, and social posts are all advertising, and identification belongs in the post rather than
only in the profile.

**Antitrust is the most serious topic in the lesson.** Each brokerage sets prices and chooses
partners independently. Agreement can be inferred from conduct, which is why the response to a
competitor raising it is to leave visibly and report — not to stay silent.

**Three controls carry information security:** access, protected transmission, and secure disposal.
Wire fraud is defeated by verification through a channel the attacker does not control, and by
nothing else.

**Records belong to the brokerage.** The licensee's obligation is to route complete documentation
into the brokerage's system — including for transactions that failed, which are disproportionately
the ones that later produce disputes.
