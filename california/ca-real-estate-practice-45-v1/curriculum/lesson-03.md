## 1. Seller and buyer agency

### 1.1 What agency is

An agency relationship exists when one person — the **agent** — is authorized to act on behalf of another — the **principal** — in dealings with third parties. Everything distinctive about a real estate licensee's legal position flows from that relationship: the duties owed, the confidences protected, the liability created, and the disclosures required.

In California residential practice the structure has an additional layer worth stating precisely. **The broker is the agent of the principal.** A salesperson acts for the broker, and therefore participates in the broker's agency. This is not a technicality. It is why assigning a second salesperson in the same office to the other side of a transaction does not eliminate a dual agency question: the agency belongs to the brokerage.

### 1.2 How agency is created

There are three routes, and only one of them involves paperwork.

1. **Express agreement** — a listing agreement, a buyer representation agreement, or another written or oral engagement.
2. **Conduct of the parties** — where the licensee acts as an agent and the consumer reasonably relies on that, an agency can arise even though nothing was signed.
3. **Ratification** — where a principal accepts the benefit of acts performed on their behalf, adopting them after the fact.

The second is the one that causes trouble. Consider a licensee who spends three weeks advising a homeowner on pricing, recommending repairs, and assessing what a buyer would accept. Nothing is signed and nothing is paid. If that licensee later takes a position adverse to the homeowner, the argument that "there was no agency because there was no agreement" is weak — the conduct was the conduct of an agent, and the homeowner reasonably relied on it.

Two corollaries follow, and both are counterintuitive to new licensees:

- **Payment does not create agency.** A licensee paid by the seller may represent the buyer. Who pays and whom you represent are separate questions.
- **The absence of payment does not prevent agency.** Duties can attach before any compensation is earned or agreed.

### 1.3 Seller agency

A seller's agent is engaged to market the property and represent the seller's interests in the transaction. The engagement is normally express, through a listing agreement, and the agency disclosure must precede that agreement — the seller needs to understand the relationship before committing to it.

The seller's agent owes the seller the full set of fiduciary duties. To the buyer and the buyer's agent, the seller's agent owes honesty, fair dealing, and the disclosure of material facts — including the results of the agent's own visual inspection of the property.

### 1.4 Buyer agency

A buyer's agent represents the buyer in locating property and negotiating terms. The engagement may be express, and increasingly is. It may also arise from conduct, which is exactly why the agency disclosure belongs early in the relationship rather than at the point an offer is written.

A buyer's agent owes the buyer the full fiduciary duties, and owes the seller honesty, fair dealing, and disclosure of material facts.

### 1.5 The non-client is not unprotected

A recurring error is to treat "I don't represent them" as meaning "I owe them nothing." The duties owed to a non-client are real and enforceable:

| Owed to the principal | Owed to everyone |
|---|---|
| Loyalty | Honesty |
| Obedience to lawful instructions | Fair dealing |
| Confidentiality | Disclosure of material facts |
| Accounting | Reasonable care in statements made |
| Disclosure of all material information | Visual inspection disclosure (residential) |
| Reasonable care and diligence | |

The distinction is not between duty and no duty. It is between *whose interests you advance* and *how honestly you deal with everyone*.

### 1.6 The accidental client

Section 1.2 established that agency can arise from conduct. That sentence deserves more weight than it usually gets, because **the most common agency problem in practice is not a wrong election. It is a relationship nobody meant to create.**

Consider what a licensee does with an unrepresented buyer at an open house who begins asking real questions:

| What the licensee does | How it reads to the visitor |
|---|---|
| Answers questions about the property's condition and history | Ordinary, and expected of a listing agent |
| Explains what the seller is likely to accept | **Advice**, given on the visitor's behalf against the seller's interest |
| Suggests an offer strategy | **Advice**, and the visitor now believes he has an advisor |
| Says "let's write it up and I'll take care of you" | A representation that the licensee is acting for him |
| Recommends a lender and an inspector | The service pattern of a buyer's agent |

**The first row is the listing agent doing her job. The rest are not**, and the visitor cannot be expected to know where the line falls, because from his side of the conversation the whole exchange looked the same.

**Three consequences follow.**

**First, the visitor may become a client by conduct**, which — in a transaction where the licensee already represents the seller — creates a dual agency that neither party consented to and that no one intended.

**Second, if he does not become a client, he may still reasonably believe he is one**, and a belief the licensee's conduct created is a belief the licensee is answerable for.

**Third, the seller's interests were damaged in either case**, because "here is what the seller will probably take" is confidential negotiating information disclosed to the party negotiating against him.

**The correction is a sentence, said early and without ceremony:** "I represent the seller in this transaction. I'm glad to tell you anything about the property, and if you want someone advising you, I can explain how that works." The sentence is not adversarial and it does not cost the licensee the transaction. **What costs the licensee the transaction is saying it after the offer is written.**

> **The test that resolves most of these:** if the visitor repeated your last statement to the seller, would the seller be comfortable? If not, you have begun acting for the wrong party.

## 2. California disclosure sequence

### 2.1 Three steps: disclose, elect, confirm

California's agency disclosure operates in three distinct steps, and they occur at different times. Licensees who remember it as "give them the form" get the timing wrong.

1. **Disclose.** Provide the statutory agency relationships disclosure, which explains what a seller's agent, a buyer's agent, and a dual agent are and what each owes.
2. **Elect.** The relationship for this transaction is selected — who represents whom.
3. **Confirm.** The elected relationship is confirmed in writing, in or with the purchase agreement, so the executed contract itself records who represented whom.

### 2.2 When each step happens

This is the part that must be memorized, because rights and defensibility depend on it.

| Role | When the disclosure must be provided |
|---|---|
| **Seller's agent** | **Before the listing agreement is signed** — so the seller understands the relationship before being bound |
| **Buyer's agent** | **As soon as practicable before the buyer signs an offer** — so the buyer understands whom the agent represents before committing |
| **Agent presenting an offer to the seller** | **Before or at the time the offer is presented** — so the seller knows whose interests the presenting agent serves while evaluating it |

Notice what these three have in common: in each case the disclosure precedes the decision it is meant to inform. That is the principle, and it will produce the right answer when a fact pattern does not match a memorized rule.

#### Why late delivery is a real problem

> A buyer's agent presents an offer without having provided the agency disclosure to the listing side. The seller accepts. The disclosure is delivered the following week and signed.
>
> The file now contains a signed disclosure — and it is still a defect. The seller evaluated and accepted an offer without knowing whom the presenting agent represented. The document exists; the protection it was meant to give does not. In a later dispute, the dates are what a reviewer looks at.

### 2.3 The confirmation step

Confirmation is not a repetition of the disclosure. It is the record, in the operative contract, of which relationship was actually elected. A file with a disclosure but no confirmation cannot show what relationship governed the transaction; a file with a confirmation contradicting the earlier disclosure shows a change nobody documented.

### 2.4 Sequencing against everything else

Placed in the wider transaction, the compliance order runs:

1. Agency disclosure
2. Representation or listing agreement
3. Offer and acceptance
4. Disclosure delivery and buyer investigation
5. Contingency removal
6. Closing
7. File audit and retention

Every common sequencing error puts a decision before the information meant to inform it: signing the listing before the agency disclosure, removing contingencies before the disclosures arrive, confirming agency only at closing.

### 2.5 The sequence, worked three ways

The three steps are the same in every transaction. **What changes is when each one arrives**, and the timing is what a file audit examines.

> **Transaction A — the listing side.**
>
> A licensee meets a homeowner considering selling. **The disclosure comes first**, before the listing agreement is presented, because the homeowner is about to commit to a relationship and must know what the relationship is. The election follows in the listing agreement itself. **The confirmation comes later**, when an offer produces a purchase agreement.
>
> **The dates run: disclosure, then agreement, then confirmation.** A file in which the listing agreement predates the disclosure has the seller committing before being informed, and Section 6.2 ranks that failure for a reason.

> **Transaction B — the buyer side.**
>
> A licensee begins working with a buyer. **The disclosure comes at the outset of the working relationship**, not at the first showing that produces an offer — because by the time an offer exists, weeks of advice have already been given.
>
> The election follows, in the representation agreement where one is used. **The confirmation arrives with the purchase agreement**, and it must agree with the election.
>
> **The failure here is subtler than in Transaction A.** The buyer file often contains a confirmation and no early disclosure at all, because nothing forced the conversation. The transaction proceeded, the confirmation was signed, and the file shows a relationship that was disclosed on the day it was already three weeks old.

> **Transaction C — presenting an offer to a listing brokerage.**
>
> A licensee representing a buyer delivers an offer to the listing agent. **The disclosure obligation runs to the seller too**, and it attaches to the presentation of the offer rather than to some later moment.
>
> This is the step most often missed, because the licensee is thinking about his own client. The seller is entitled to know, before considering the offer, who the person delivering it represents.

**The pattern across all three:** the disclosure attaches to **the beginning of the relationship or the moment of contact with the other party**, the election attaches to **the agreement**, and the confirmation attaches to **the purchase agreement.** A file whose dates do not run in that order is telling you something happened out of sequence, and Section 6.3 explains why that is worth knowing while the transaction can still respond.

> **One further point about delivery.** Each step is evidenced by delivery rather than by the existence of a signed page. A disclosure signed on the day of closing, whatever date is written on it, was not delivered at the beginning of the relationship — and the file's other documents will say so.

## 3. Fiduciary duties in practice

### 3.1 The duties, and what each one actually prohibits

Listing the duties is easy. Recognizing which one a given failure breaches is the skill.

**Loyalty.** Put the principal's interests ahead of your own. Prohibits: undisclosed personal interest in the transaction, undisclosed compensation from another party, steering the principal toward what benefits you.

**Obedience.** Follow the principal's lawful instructions. Prohibits: substituting your judgment for theirs on decisions that are theirs — price, terms, whether to accept. **Extends only to lawful instructions**, which is the limit that matters most.

**Disclosure.** Tell the principal everything material you know that affects their interests. Prohibits: withholding a competing offer, an inspection concern, a market change, or a conflict.

**Confidentiality.** Protect the principal's confidential information. Prohibits: revealing the lowest price the seller would take, the highest the buyer would pay, the principal's motivation or timeline. **Does not extend** to concealing material facts about the property — that is the boundary licensees most often get wrong.

**Accounting.** Account for all funds and property belonging to the principal. Prohibits: commingling, unexplained balances, delay in accounting.

**Reasonable care and diligence.** Perform with the skill of a competent licensee. Prohibits: careless work — and this duty can be breached without any dishonesty at all, which is why most claims against licensees are negligence claims rather than fraud claims.

### 3.2 Where confidentiality ends and disclosure begins

This is the single most consequential boundary in agency practice.

> **Scenario.** A listing agent learns the roof has a persistent leak. The seller instructs her to say nothing.
>
> **The tension.** Confidentiality runs to the seller. Disclosure of material facts runs to the buyer.
>
> **The resolution.** Confidentiality protects the seller's *negotiating position*, not concealment of a material physical condition. The duty of honesty and disclosure controls. The agent tells the seller that the condition must be disclosed, involves her broker, and does not follow the instruction.
>
> **What does not work:** staying silent while avoiding an affirmative false statement. Careful silence about a known material defect is concealment. Nor does the presence of a buyer's agent transfer the duty — a buyer's own agent does not relieve the listing side of disclosure.

### 3.3 Obedience and its limit

An instruction that is unlawful is not an instruction the agent may follow. A seller who directs that the property not be shown to a category of buyers has given an unlawful instruction. The agent's obligations are to refuse it, explain why, escalate to the broker, and address whether the listing can continue.

Three responses that do not work:

1. Following it while documenting that it came from the client. Documentation does not transfer responsibility for the act.
2. Following it informally — scheduling around those buyers without saying why. This achieves the same exclusion.
3. Withdrawing quietly without explanation. This leaves the client free to repeat the instruction with the next licensee.

### 3.4 Duties survive the relationship

Confidentiality generally continues after the agency ends. A former listing agent who knows the seller's lowest acceptable price may not use or disclose it afterward — including for a new client, and including after the listing expires and the property is relisted elsewhere. A duty that evaporated when the engagement ended would not be worth having.

### 3.5 Loyalty when your own interest appears

Loyalty is easy to state and easy to satisfy in the ordinary case, because in the ordinary case the licensee's interest and the client's point the same way: the transaction closes, the client is served, the licensee is paid.

**The duty does work only in the cases where they diverge**, and those cases are specific and recognizable.

**Buying or selling for your own account.** A licensee who wants to buy his own listing, or to sell property he owns to a client, has an interest directly opposed to the principal's on the one term that matters most. The disclosure obligation here is heightened rather than ordinary — the principal must know the licensee is a principal, not merely an agent, and the fact that a price is fair does not substitute for the disclosure.

**A relative in the transaction.** The analysis does not change because the interest belongs to a family member rather than to the licensee. A licensee whose sibling is the buyer has an interest, and the seller is entitled to know it.

**The offer that pays you more.** Two offers arrive. One is better for the seller. One produces a larger commission for the licensee. **The duty is not to disclose the conflict and then advocate for the second — it is to advise on the merits of both from the seller's position**, and to present both regardless.

**The transaction that ends your income.** A buyer client is deciding whether to continue renting for another year. A seller client is deciding whether to take the property off the market. **In both cases the advice that serves the client costs the licensee everything**, and it is the advice the duty requires.

**The referral you receive something for.** Covered in Section 5.2 as a conflict, and it is a loyalty question first: a recommendation the client believes is professional judgment, when it is partly economics, is a use of the client's trust for the licensee's benefit.

> **The operative question is not "am I being honest?"** Most loyalty failures involve licensees who are being honest about everything except the fact that their interest is in the room.
>
> **The question is: does my principal know everything that could influence what I am about to tell him?** If not, disclose it before the advice, not after the decision.

**And one structural point.** Loyalty is owed to the principal, and the principal is the brokerage's client rather than the individual licensee's. Where a licensee's personal interest conflicts with a client of his brokerage — including a client he has never met, worked by a colleague — **the conflict is real and the disclosure runs through the broker.**

### 3.6 Reasonable care, and why it produces most claims

Section 3.1 noted that reasonable care can be breached without any dishonesty. That observation deserves expansion, because **it describes the majority of what licensees are actually sued for.**

Fraud requires intent and is comparatively rare. Negligence requires only that the work fell below the standard of a competent licensee, and it is produced by ordinary pressure applied to an ordinary practice.

**What breaches it, in the order the claims arrive:**

| The failure | How it happens |
|---|---|
| **A deadline missed** | Tracked in memory or in one person's calendar rather than in a system |
| **A document not delivered** | Sent, assumed received, never confirmed |
| **A disclosure prepared from an old file** | Copied forward, carrying facts about a different property |
| **A condition observed and not mentioned** | Noticed in passing, deferred to "the inspector will catch it" |
| **A referral made carelessly** | A vendor recommended because he is familiar, not because he is qualified for this |
| **Advice given outside competence** | A legal, tax, structural, or environmental question answered rather than routed |
| **A number stated from memory** | Square footage, lot size, tax amount, or fee quoted without verification |

**Three of these deserve a specific note.**

**"The inspector will catch it" is not a standard.** A licensee who observes a condition has an obligation about what he observed, and the possibility that another professional will independently find it does not discharge it.

**Advice outside competence is the most tempting failure**, because the client asked, the licensee has an opinion, and saying "that is a question for your attorney" feels like a failure to help. It is not. **The competent answer to a legal question is the referral**, and the licensee who gives the legal answer has assumed responsibility for it.

**Stated numbers become representations.** Square footage from a memory of the listing, a tax figure from last year, a fee "usually around" an amount — each is relied on, and each is the licensee's statement regardless of where it originated. **Verify or attribute: give the source, or do not give the number.**

> **The protective habit is not caution. It is system.** Deadlines in a tracker, delivery confirmed and recorded, disclosures built from the current property, questions routed by category, and numbers sourced. Every item on that list is a procedure, and procedures survive a bad week in a way that carefulness does not.

## 4. Dual agency consent

### 4.1 What dual agency is

A dual agency exists when the same brokerage represents both the seller and the buyer in one transaction. Because the agency belongs to the brokerage, this occurs whether the same salesperson works both sides or two different salespersons in the same office do.

### 4.2 What is required

**Informed written consent from both principals.** Three words, each doing work:

- **Informed** — the parties must understand what dual agency means and what it costs them, particularly the limits on the agent's ability to advocate and to share information.
- **Written** — an oral understanding does not satisfy it.
- **From both** — one party's consent is not enough, and the paying party's consent is not privileged over the other's.

Broker approval is not a substitute for the principals' consent.

### 4.3 What consent does not authorize

This is the most commonly misunderstood point in the lesson, so state it precisely:

> **Consent to dual agency permits the brokerage to represent both parties. It does not convert either party's confidential information into shared information.**

A dual agent may not disclose to the buyer that the seller will accept less than the listed price, and may not disclose to the seller that the buyer would pay more — in each case without that party's express permission.

Two wrong answers to test yourself against:

- *"Both are my clients, so both are entitled to what I know."* No. Being a client entitles a party to the agent's loyalty and disclosure about their own interests, not to the other client's confidential negotiating position.
- *"I'll disclose both sides' limits, which keeps it balanced."* No. This does not cure the breach; it commits it twice.

A general answer that conveys the same information — "I think they'd take a bit less" — is a disclosure. The form of words does not matter.

### 4.4 What a dual agent can still do

Dual agency is not paralysis. The dual agent still owes both parties honesty, fair dealing, disclosure of material facts, accounting, and reasonable care. The agent can and must present offers, explain provisions, transmit communications, disclose property conditions, meet deadlines, and manage the transaction competently.

What the dual agent cannot do is advocate for one party against the other or move confidential information between them.

### 4.5 When to raise it

The moment a possible dual agency becomes foreseeable — typically when a buyer the brokerage is working with becomes interested in a property the brokerage has listed. Raising it at the point an offer is written is late, because both parties have already invested effort under an assumption that may be wrong.

### 4.6 Why the brokerage is the agent, and what follows

Section 4.1 stated that agency belongs to the brokerage. Almost every practical dual agency problem follows from that one fact, and it is worth working out.

**What follows immediately:**

- **Two salespersons in the same office on opposite sides of one transaction produce a dual agency**, even if they have never spoken about the file and work from different locations.
- **A licensee can create a dual agency without knowing it**, because he does not know every listing his brokerage holds or every buyer his colleagues work with.
- **The consent required is the brokerage's to obtain**, from both parties, and it cannot be given by one salesperson on behalf of a client he does not represent.
- **Confidential information held by one salesperson is held by the brokerage**, which is why the internal separation of the two sides matters and why a casual conversation between colleagues about "what their buyer will really pay" is a breach rather than office chatter.

**The practical control is a check, performed early:** before showing a brokerage-held listing to a buyer the brokerage works with, determine that it is a brokerage-held listing and raise the consent question then. Section 4.5 fixes the timing — the moment it becomes foreseeable — and the check is what makes that timing achievable in a brokerage large enough that no one person knows the whole inventory.

**Where a brokerage uses a designated or assigned arrangement**, in which different salespersons are designated to each party, the arrangement's mechanics and what it does and does not change are **matters of the brokerage's written policy and of law**, and they are questions for the responsible broker rather than for the individual licensee's judgment. **What does not change under any arrangement** is that the consent must be informed, in writing, from both parties, and obtained before the brokerage acts on both sides.

> **The failure to watch for:** a licensee who learns of the dual agency at the offer stage, obtains consent then, and treats the file as cured. The consent is dated after weeks of advice given to both parties by one firm. **Consent obtained after the conduct it was supposed to authorize is a record of the problem, not a resolution of it.**

## 5. Compensation and conflicts

### 5.1 Payment does not determine representation

A buyer's agent may be compensated from the seller's side of a transaction while representing the buyer. This is common, lawful, and frequently misunderstood by consumers — which makes explaining it part of the job.

Two consequences:

1. **The agency disclosure and confirmation, not the payment arrangement, establish who represented whom.** A file that shows compensation from the seller and confirmation of buyer agency is internally consistent, not contradictory.
2. **The compensation arrangement must still be disclosed**, because the client cannot evaluate advice without knowing the adviser's economics.

### 5.2 Conflicts that must be disclosed

Any interest that could reasonably influence the licensee's advice:

- A referral fee from a service provider — inspector, warranty company, lender, contractor.
- A financial interest, including a family member's, in a recommended provider.
- A bonus offered by a seller or builder contingent on a sale or a price threshold.
- The licensee's own interest in a property, or an interest of a relative.
- Representation of another party with a competing interest.

### 5.3 What disclosure requires

A conflict is managed by disclosure that reaches the client **while they can still act on it**. Three inadequate substitutes, each of which licensees genuinely propose:

| Inadequate approach | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| "The client is free to choose someone else" | The client cannot weigh a recommendation without knowing what the recommender receives |
| "I'll disclose it if they ask" | Waiting to be asked defeats the purpose; the client does not know to ask |
| "I won't let it influence me" | A private intention is not something the client can evaluate |
| Disclosing after the client has acted | Information that arrives after the decision has no value |

### 5.4 A concrete case

> A licensee will receive a bonus from a builder if a particular home sells above a set price. Her buyer client is considering that home among three.
>
> **Required:** disclose the bonus arrangement before the buyer relies on her advice about that property. Not after the offer is accepted, not only if he asks, and not on the theory that the bonus comes from the builder rather than from the buyer's pocket.
>
> **Why:** her advice about that property now carries a financial interest. The buyer is entitled to discount it accordingly — that is his decision to make, not hers.

### 5.5 Termination and compensation

An agency may end by completion of its purpose, expiration of its term, mutual agreement, revocation by the principal, or renunciation by the agent. The *power* to end the relationship is not the same as the *right* to end it without consequence: an early termination may still breach the underlying contract, and the compensation terms of that contract govern what is owed.

Because the listing is the brokerage's contract, a request to cancel is a broker-level decision, not one the individual licensee makes.

### 5.6 The vendor relationship, in practice

Section 5.2 lists referral fees among the conflicts requiring disclosure. In daily practice this is less a rule than a habit of mind, because a working licensee accumulates vendor relationships constantly and most of them are entirely proper.

**The relationships, sorted by what they require:**

| The relationship | What it requires |
|---|---|
| A vendor you use because he is good, with no arrangement | Nothing beyond ordinary care in recommending someone competent for this work |
| A vendor who refers business to you | **Disclosure** — the exchange runs both ways and the client cannot see it |
| A vendor who pays you a referral fee | **Disclosure before the recommendation**, and the arrangement must itself be lawful |
| A vendor in which you or a relative hold an interest | **Disclosure**, and the interest stated plainly rather than implied |
| A vendor who provides you marketing support, event sponsorship, or materials | **Disclosure** — value received is value received, and its form does not change the analysis |
| A vendor your brokerage owns or is affiliated with | **Disclosure on the terms the affiliated-business framework requires** |

**Two boundaries are worth stating explicitly.**

**First, whether a payment may be received at all is a separate question from whether it is disclosed.** Some payments are prohibited outright, and disclosing a prohibited payment does not make it lawful. A licensee who asks only "did I disclose it?" has asked the second question without asking the first.

**Second, a recommendation carries an implied representation of competence.** A client who is told "use this inspector" hears that the licensee believes the inspector is good at inspecting. Recommending a vendor the licensee has never evaluated, because the vendor is convenient or generous, is a reasonable-care problem independent of any conflict.

**The practice that handles all of it:** offer more than one name where you can, state what you receive if you receive anything, say what your basis for the recommendation is, and record that you did. **Three names and a sentence about your relationship with each takes thirty seconds and removes the entire category from a later dispute.**

## 6. Agency documentation case

### 6.1 What the file must show

Agency is the area where documentation most often fails, because the documents look complete while the dates tell a different story. A defensible agency record shows:

1. The **disclosure**, with the date it was provided.
2. The **listing or representation agreement**, dated **after** the disclosure.
3. The **election** of the relationship.
4. The **confirmation** in or with the purchase agreement, consistent with the disclosure.
5. Any **dual agency consent**, informed and written, from **both** parties, dated before the brokerage acted on both sides.
6. **Conflict disclosures**, dated before the client acted.
7. Evidence of **delivery**, not merely signature.

### 6.2 A worked audit

> A closed file contains:
>
> - An agency disclosure signed and dated **three days after** the listing agreement.
> - A confirmation in the purchase agreement naming the brokerage as buyer's agent, while the listing file shows it as the seller's agent, with no dual agency consent anywhere.
> - A referral fee disclosure dated the day of closing, for a home warranty the buyer purchased six weeks earlier.
>
> **Finding 1 — disclosure after the listing.** The seller committed before being informed. The document exists; the protection does not.
>
> **Finding 2 — the contradiction.** The file shows the brokerage on both sides with no consent from either party. This is the most serious of the three: it describes an undisclosed dual agency, which breaches duties actually owed rather than merely failing a paperwork step.
>
> **Finding 3 — late conflict disclosure.** Delivered after the buyer acted, which is the one thing a conflict disclosure must not be.
>
> **What happens now.** Each finding is recorded and escalated to the broker. What must *not* happen is any correction of dates or creation of a missing consent after the fact. A documented gap is survivable; a manufactured document is falsification.

### 6.3 The habit that prevents all three

Date everything at the moment it happens, evidence delivery rather than relying on signature alone, and check the agency chain — disclosure, agreement, election, confirmation — as a sequence rather than as a set of documents. If the dates do not run in order, something happened out of order, and that is worth knowing while the transaction can still respond.

### 6.4 A second worked audit — the buyer side

Section 6.2 audited a listing file. Buyer files fail differently, because nothing in a buyer relationship forces the paperwork the way a listing agreement does.

> **A closed buyer-side file contains:**
>
> - **(a)** An agency disclosure signed by the buyer, **dated the same day as the purchase agreement.** The file's earliest email to the buyer is dated five weeks earlier and reads "here are three properties I think fit what you described."
> - **(b)** **No buyer representation agreement** of any kind.
> - **(c)** A confirmation in the purchase agreement naming the licensee as the buyer's agent.
> - **(d)** An email from the licensee to the buyer stating "I spoke with the listing agent and I think they'd take 715 — let's come in at 705 and see."
> - **(e)** A home warranty referral, with no disclosure of the referral fee the file's commission statement later shows was received.
> - **(f)** No record of any disclosure delivered to the **seller** when the offer was presented.
>
> **Ranked by seriousness:**
>
> **1. (e) The undisclosed referral fee.** A payment received in connection with a recommendation the client relied on, disclosed nowhere. It is a conflict failure and a loyalty failure at once, and unlike the timing defects below it involves money moving without the client's knowledge.
>
> **2. (a) The five-week gap.** The disclosure exists and is dated the day the buyer's decision was already made. Five weeks of advice preceded it. The document is present; **the protection it was designed to give never operated**, and item (d) proves advice was being given throughout.
>
> **3. (f) No disclosure to the seller.** Section 2.5 established that the obligation attaches to the presentation of the offer. Its absence means the seller evaluated an offer without knowing who the person delivering it represented.
>
> **4. (d) The disclosure of the seller's position.** Read carefully: the licensee is reporting what the **listing agent** told him about what the seller would take. If that information was volunteered in breach of the listing agent's confidentiality duty, the failure is primarily the listing agent's — but a buyer's agent who solicits it is participating, and the file now documents the conversation. **This is escalated to the broker rather than resolved by the licensee.**
>
> **5. (b) No representation agreement.** The relationship was created by conduct, as Section 1.6 describes, and the file's only evidence of its terms is a confirmation signed at the end.
>
> **6. (c) is not a finding.** The confirmation is correct and consistent. It is listed to make the point that **a file can contain an accurate document and still fail**, because the question is never whether a document is right but whether the decision it governed had it in time.
>
> **What happens now.** Record every finding with its date. Escalate the referral fee and item (d) to the broker immediately — the first because money must be accounted for, the second because it describes conduct by another licensee. Determine whether the referral fee can still be disclosed and addressed with the buyer, which is a broker and counsel question rather than a licensee one. **Change the intake procedure**, because items (a), (b), and (f) are not this licensee's carelessness — they are a brokerage with no step that forces buyer-side paperwork at the beginning.
>
> **What must not happen:** re-dating the disclosure to the first email, producing a representation agreement now, or adding a seller disclosure to the file today. Section 6.2's closing sentence governs both audits. **A documented gap is survivable; a manufactured document is falsification.**

### 6.5 The agency chain, in one page

Everything in this lesson reduces to a chain of five links, each of which has a date, and each of which must precede the decision it was meant to inform.

1. **Disclosure** — delivered at the beginning of the relationship, and to the other party at the moment of contact. **Before the client commits to anything.**
2. **Election** — the relationship chosen, recorded in the agreement.
3. **Agreement** — the listing or representation contract, dated **after** the disclosure.
4. **Consent**, where the brokerage acts on both sides — informed, written, from both parties, dated **before** the brokerage acted on both sides.
5. **Confirmation** — in or with the purchase agreement, and **consistent with links 1 through 4.**

**Around the chain sit two continuing obligations** that are not dated events and are therefore the easiest to forget: **conflict disclosures**, each delivered before the client acts on the advice it affects, and **delivery evidence** for every link, because a signature proves execution and not receipt.

> **The audit question for each link:** what is its date, what decision did it precede, and can the file show it arrived?
>
> **Three answers indicate a defect.** A link with no date. A link dated after the decision it governed. A link the file can show was signed but cannot show was delivered.

**Run the chain forward once at intake and once at close-out.** At intake it prevents the problem; at close-out it finds it while the other participants still answer the phone.

### 6.6 Checkpoint

You should be able to take a file's agency documents, put them in date order, and say whether each decision was made with the information it required.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **Agency** | The relationship in which one person is authorized to act on behalf of another in dealings with third parties. |
| **Principal** | The party the agent represents. Also called the client. |
| **Agent** | The party authorized to act for the principal. In California residential practice, the **broker** is the agent; the salesperson acts for the broker. |
| **Third party** | A participant who is not the agent's principal. Owed honesty, fair dealing, and disclosure of material facts — but not the fiduciary duties. |
| **Express agreement** | Agency created by a listing agreement, representation agreement, or other engagement, written or oral. |
| **Agency by conduct** | Agency arising because the licensee acted as an agent and the consumer reasonably relied, even with nothing signed. |
| **Ratification** | Agency created when a principal accepts the benefit of acts performed on their behalf, adopting them after the fact. |
| **Fiduciary duties** | The duties owed to a principal: loyalty, obedience to lawful instructions, disclosure, confidentiality, accounting, and reasonable care and diligence. |
| **Loyalty** | Placing the principal's interests ahead of the agent's own, and surfacing rather than silently resolving conflicts. |
| **Obedience** | Following the principal's instructions — **limited to lawful instructions**. |
| **Confidentiality** | Protecting the principal's confidential information, such as their lowest acceptable price. Does **not** extend to concealing material facts about the property. |
| **Accounting** | Accounting for all funds and property belonging to the principal. |
| **Reasonable care and diligence** | Performing with the skill of a competent licensee. Breached by carelessness alone, without any dishonesty. |
| **Disclose, elect, confirm** | The three steps of California's agency disclosure, occurring at three different times. |
| **Agency confirmation** | The record, in or with the purchase agreement, of which relationship was actually elected. |
| **Dual agency** | One brokerage representing both seller and buyer in the same transaction. |
| **Informed written consent** | What dual agency requires from **both** principals: understanding of what dual agency means and its limits, in writing, from each. |
| **Confidential negotiating position** | A party's lowest acceptable or highest payable price and similar information. Never disclosed to the other side without that party's permission, even under dual agency. |
| **Conflict of interest** | Any interest that could reasonably influence the licensee's advice, requiring disclosure before the client relies on it. |
| **Termination of agency** | Ending the relationship by completion, expiration, mutual agreement, revocation, or renunciation — with the power to end distinct from the right to end without consequence. |

## Practice problems

### Problem 1

A homeowner and a licensee have talked for five weeks. He has advised her on pricing, recommended
repairs, and told her what he thinks buyers will accept. Nothing is signed and he has not been paid.
He now represents a buyer making an offer on her home and says no agency existed with her.

**Is he right?**

> **Solution.** No. Agency can arise from conduct, and five weeks of advice the homeowner reasonably
> relied on looks like the conduct of an agent. Payment is not what creates duties, and the absence
> of a signed agreement does not prevent the relationship.
>
> The consequence is not merely that he cannot claim a commission. He may have owed her fiduciary
> duties throughout, and representing the buyer without disclosure and consent breaches duties
> actually owed.
>
> **What transfers:** the reason the agency disclosure belongs early is precisely this — the
> relationship may already exist while the consumer is unaware of it.

### Problem 2

A seller's agent learns during a listing appointment that the home's foundation was underpinned
after movement six years ago. The seller says the work was done properly, produces no records, and
instructs the agent not to mention it because "it's fixed."

**What controls, and what does the agent do?**

> **Solution.** The duty of honesty and disclosure of material facts to the buyer controls. The
> seller's instruction to conceal cannot be followed.
>
> Confidentiality protects the seller's **negotiating position**, not concealment of a material
> physical condition — and the adequacy of a repair with no records is exactly what a buyer would
> want to evaluate.
>
> The agent tells the seller that the condition must be disclosed and why, involves the broker, and
> does not follow the instruction. Careful silence while avoiding an affirmative false statement is
> still concealment.
>
> **What transfers:** the boundary between confidentiality and disclosure is the most consequential
> line in agency practice, and it falls in the same place every time.

### Problem 3

A brokerage lists a home. A buyer the same brokerage has been working with wants to offer on it.
Both parties sign a dual agency consent. During negotiation the seller privately tells the broker
the lowest number he would take. The buyer asks the broker directly what the seller will accept.

**What may the broker say?**

> **Solution.** Nothing about the seller's floor, without the seller's permission.
>
> Consent to dual agency permits the brokerage to represent both parties. It does **not** convert
> either party's confidential negotiating position into shared information.
>
> Two wrong answers worth naming: that both are clients so both are entitled to what the broker
> knows — being a client entitles a party to loyalty about their own interests, not to the other
> client's confidences; and that disclosing both sides' limits keeps it balanced — that does not cure
> the breach, it commits it twice.
>
> A vague answer conveying the same information is still a disclosure. The form of words does not
> matter.

### Problem 4

A buyer's agent presents an offer. The agency disclosure is delivered to the listing side the
following week, after acceptance, and is signed and dated then.

**Is the file compliant?**

> **Solution.** No. The disclosure must be provided before or at the time the offer is presented, so
> the seller knows whose interests the presenting agent serves **while evaluating it**.
>
> The file now contains a signed disclosure and is still defective. The document exists; the
> protection it was meant to give does not, because the seller made the decision without it.
>
> **What transfers:** every timing rule in the agency sequence is the same principle — the disclosure
> precedes the decision it informs. Apply that and unfamiliar fact patterns resolve themselves.

### Problem 5

A licensee will receive a bonus from a builder if a particular home sells above a set price. Her
buyer client is weighing that home against two others. She has no intention of pushing him toward it.

**What is required?**

> **Solution.** Disclose the bonus arrangement before the buyer relies on her advice about that
> property.
>
> Three inadequate substitutes: that the bonus comes from the builder rather than the buyer's pocket
> (who pays does not change that her advice now carries a financial interest); that she will not let
> it influence her (a private intention is not something the client can evaluate); and disclosing
> after acceptance (information after the decision has no value).
>
> **What transfers:** a conflict is managed by putting it in front of the client while they can still
> act on it. That is the whole mechanism.

### Problem 6

A principal instructs her agent not to show the property to a category of buyers. The agent believes
refusing will cost her the listing.

**What must the agent do, and which three responses fail?**

> **Solution.** Refuse the instruction, explain that it would be unlawful discrimination, escalate to
> the broker, and address whether the listing can continue.
>
> The three failures: following it while documenting that it came from the client — documentation
> does not transfer responsibility for the act; following it informally by scheduling around those
> buyers — which achieves the same exclusion; and withdrawing quietly without explanation — which
> leaves the client free to repeat the instruction with the next licensee.
>
> **What transfers:** obedience extends only to lawful instructions. That limit is not negotiable
> against the value of the listing.

### Problem 7

A listing expires unsold. Six months later the same licensee represents a buyer interested in the
property, now listed with another brokerage. He knows the former seller's lowest acceptable price.

**May he use it?**

> **Solution.** No. Confidentiality generally survives the end of the agency. He may not use or
> disclose the former principal's confidential information afterwards — including for a new client,
> and including after the property is relisted elsewhere.
>
> **What transfers:** a duty that evaporated when the engagement ended would not be worth having.
> This is why the obligation is described as surviving rather than lapsing.

### Problem 8

An audit finds: an agency disclosure dated three days after the listing agreement; a purchase
agreement confirming the brokerage as buyer's agent while the listing file shows it as seller's
agent; and no dual agency consent anywhere.

**Rank the findings and state the correct response.**

> **Solution.** The **contradiction is the most serious**, and it is different in kind from the
> other. It describes the brokerage acting on both sides without either party's informed written
> consent — a breach of duties actually owed, not a documentation failure.
>
> The late disclosure is second: the seller committed before being informed.
>
> The correct response is to record both findings, escalate to the broker, and obtain anything that
> legitimately exists. What must **not** happen is creating the consent that was never obtained or
> re-dating the disclosure. A documented gap is survivable; a manufactured document is falsification.

## Chapter summary

**Agency is created three ways**, and only one involves paperwork. Conduct is the route that
surprises licensees, which is why disclosure belongs early rather than at the offer.

**The broker is the agent; the salesperson acts for the broker.** This is why assigning a second
salesperson to the other side does not eliminate a dual agency question.

**Payment and representation are separate questions.** A buyer's agent compensated from the seller's
side still represents the buyer — and the arrangement is still disclosed.

**Non-clients are not unprotected.** Honesty, fair dealing, and disclosure of material facts run to
everyone. The distinction is whose interests you advance, not whether you owe anything.

**The disclosure sequence is disclose, elect, confirm**, at three different times, each preceding the
decision it informs: before the listing is signed, before the buyer signs an offer, and before or
when the offer is presented.

**Confidentiality protects the negotiating position, never a material defect.** This single boundary
resolves the majority of agency questions in practice.

**Dual agency consent permits representation, not disclosure.** Neither party's confidential position
may cross without that party's permission, and disclosing both does not balance it.

**Conflicts are managed by disclosure that arrives in time to matter.** Freedom to choose another
provider, an intention not to be influenced, and disclosure after the decision are each inadequate.

**Duties survive the relationship.** Confidentiality does not end when the listing expires.
