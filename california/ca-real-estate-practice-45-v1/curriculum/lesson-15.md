## Integrated California residential transaction

## Learning objectives

- Sequence a complete California residential transaction and explain why each step precedes the next.
- Carry a single transaction from intake through closing, identifying the obligation engaged at each stage.
- Recognize, at each stage, the failure that most commonly occurs there and the control that prevents it.
- Audit a completed file and rank its defects by seriousness rather than listing them.
- Distinguish a documented gap from a falsified record, and state the correct response to each.
- Apply the course's recurring principles — whose decision, whose duty, what evidence — to an unfamiliar situation.

## 1. Intake and agency

### 1.1 The chronology, and why it is a chronology

A residential transaction is a sequence in which **each step supplies the information the next step requires**. That is the organizing principle of this lesson, and it is the test to apply when you are unsure whether something is in the right order:

> Does this decision have, in front of it, the information it was meant to be made with?

Every common sequencing error is a decision made before its information arrived.

| # | Step | Supplies |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | Agency disclosure | The consumer's understanding of the relationship |
| 2 | Listing or representation agreement | Authority to act, and its terms |
| 3 | Pricing analysis and advice | The basis for the client's price decision |
| 4 | Seller disclosure preparation | The material facts, before pressure exists |
| 5 | Marketing | Exposure, on compliant copy |
| 6 | Offer and negotiation | The terms |
| 7 | Acceptance | The contract, and the deadline clock |
| 8 | Disclosure delivery | What the buyer needs before committing |
| 9 | Investigation | What the buyer needs before removing contingencies |
| 10 | Contingency removal | The buyer's commitment, informed |
| 11 | Loan and appraisal | The financing |
| 12 | Final verification | Condition confirmed while leverage exists |
| 13 | Signing, funding, recording, disbursement | The transfer |
| 14 | Possession | Control |
| 15 | Audit and retention | The record |

### 1.2 The intake questions that determine everything after

**Authority.** Who can convey? Confirmed against title and the governing instrument — not assumed from occupancy or the tax roll. Trust means the trustee within the trust's authority; entity means the person the governing documents authorize; probate means what the appointment permits; co-owners means all of them.

**Subject matter.** The legal description, the boundaries, and what conveys — with disputable items named in the agreement so the fixture tests never need to be applied.

**Constraints.** Loans and liens, association obligations, leases and occupants, easements and encroachments, known conditions and repair history, permit status, and any pending matter affecting the ability to convey.

### 1.3 The agency sequence

Disclose, elect, confirm — at three different times:

| Role | Disclosure timing |
|---|---|
| Seller's agent | **Before the listing agreement is signed** |
| Buyer's agent | **As soon as practicable before the buyer signs an offer** |
| Agent presenting an offer to the seller | **Before or at the time the offer is presented** |

Each is the same principle: the disclosure precedes the decision it informs.

Where the brokerage will act on both sides, informed written consent from **both** parties is required, and it must precede the brokerage acting on both sides — not follow it.

### 1.4 The failure that occurs here

**The most common intake failure is unverified authority**, and it is expensive because it surfaces in escrow. A listing signed by one of two co-owners, by a beneficiary rather than the trustee, or by a person whose power of attorney does not authorize real property conveyance cannot deliver what it promises.

**The control:** verify against a source at intake, and stop and escalate where authority is unclear rather than postponing the question.

### 1.5 The buyer-side version of the same stage

Sections 1.2 and 1.3 walk the seller-side intake. **The buyer side runs the same chronology with different content**, and it fails more often because nothing forces the paperwork.

**What the buyer consultation must establish, in the order it matters:**

1. **Capacity and agency**, disclosed at the outset of the working relationship — Lesson 03, Section 2.5, Transaction B. **Not at the first showing that produces an offer**, by which point weeks of advice have been given.
2. **What the buyer is actually trying to do.** The property is the instrument; the objective is a household, a timeline, a monthly cost, or an investment return. **A search built on a property description and not on an objective produces the buyer who sees forty houses and likes none of them.**
3. **Financial readiness, in the three separate senses** — what a lender will lend, what the buyer can carry, and what cash exists at closing. Lesson 07 separates them; the intake is where the separation is made and recorded.
4. **The criteria, written down.** Lesson 07, Section 4.5 requires the search be documented against them, and the record only works if the criteria exist first.
5. **The constraints the buyer has not mentioned** — a lease expiration, a school year, a co-buyer in another city, a gift that arrives on a date, a job that has not started.
6. **What the licensee will and will not do**, including the categories that route to counsel, a tax advisor, and the lender.

**Two intake failures produce most buyer-side problems.**

**No agency disclosure until the offer.** Lesson 03's second worked audit is built on it, and the defect is invisible until a complaint arrives, because the file contains a signed disclosure.

**Readiness assumed from a preapproval letter.** Lesson 07, Section 3.2 explains how to read one. **A buyer whose readiness was assumed is a buyer whose transaction will fail during escrow rather than before it**, and Lesson 10's worked underwriting failure is what that looks like.

> **The buyer-side intake note is the same two-minute document as the seller-side one** in Section 1.4 of Lesson 02: what he said he wanted, what he was told about capacity and compensation, and what he was told the licensee would not do. **It is the only evidence that will exist about the conversation that framed the rest.**

## 2. Listing and marketing

### 2.1 The agreement

The listing is the brokerage's contract with the seller. The licensee should be able to explain every operative provision, and three deserve particular care:

- **Compensation** is negotiable between the seller and the brokerage. Not standard, not set by any association or listing service.
- **The definite termination date** protects the seller from an indefinite engagement.
- **The protection clause** applies on its terms, ordinarily to buyers identified to the seller within a defined period after the term.

### 2.2 Pricing

The analysis and the advice are the licensee's obligation; the price is the seller's decision. Both halves.

The file should show the comparables used with their sources as of the date, the adjustment grid with the direction and basis of each adjustment, the reconciliation and its weighting, the comparables rejected and why, the market conditions evidence, the recommendation as a range with a most-probable point, and the written advice with the seller's decision.

Where the seller lists above the supported range, the file must show that the decision was informed — and must **not** adopt the seller's reasoning as the brokerage's opinion of value.

### 2.3 Disclosures start now, not at offer

The disclosure work belongs at the listing appointment. A seller who wants to defer it until an offer arrives is asking for the arrangement most likely to cost them: compressed buyer review, pressure at the worst moment, and the disputes that follow from pressure.

Separate the categories openly: price, showing schedule, and cancellation terms are negotiable; disclosure obligations are not.

### 2.4 Marketing

Truthful against the property's **current** condition. Every factual claim traceable to a source, with the source stated where it is the seller. Licensee and brokerage identification present and legible, including on each social post rather than only in the profile. Copy that describes the property rather than the people who should live in it — and audience targeting examined as part of the advertisement.

Approval before publication, and an archive of what ran, where, when, and with whose approval.

### 2.5 The failure that occurs here

**Late disclosure preparation**, and **advertising that runs without approval or without identification**. Both are invisible until they are not.

**The control:** disclosure work scheduled at listing, and an approval process fast enough that licensees do not route around it.

### 2.6 When the listing is not straightforward

The listing stage in Sections 2.1 through 2.4 assumes an owner who can convey, a property that is what it appears to be, and a market with comparables. **Where any of those fails, the stage takes longer and skipping the extra work is the defect that surfaces later.**

| The complication | What it adds before marketing begins |
|---|---|
| **Multiple owners, or an owner acting for another** | The authority question, answered from documents rather than description — Lesson 04, Section 1.6 |
| **A trust, estate, or dissolution** | Authority, plus disclosure limits from a seller with little knowledge — Lesson 09, Section 1.6 |
| **Unpermitted or unrecorded improvements** | Verification of what may be advertised, before a square footage or a room count is published — Lesson 06, Section 1.7 |
| **A tenant in possession** | Access rules, notice requirements, and disclosures the tenancy generates — Lesson 12 |
| **A property in a common-interest development** | Association documents ordered at the start, because the buyer's review period is what remains after delivery |
| **Insufficient equity** | A different conversation entirely, with routing rather than pricing — Lesson 05, Section 4.6 |
| **A property with a hazard or insurance problem** | Insurance availability established early, because a buyer discovers it late otherwise — Lesson 09, Section 3.7 |
| **A thin comparable set** | A range with its reasoning rather than a number with false precision — Lesson 05, Section 1.6 |

**The common structure across every row:** the complication is knowable at intake and expensive after an offer. **A licensee who takes the listing and resolves the complication later has moved the work to the point in the chronology where it collides with a buyer's deadlines**, and Section 1.1 explains why that is the definition of a timing defect.

> **The professional posture:** an intake that identifies three items requiring resolution before listing has done its job. **The seller who wants to go on the market Friday is served, not obstructed, by being told which two of the three can run in parallel and which one cannot.**

## 3. Offer and negotiation

### 3.1 Formation

An acceptance must match the offer. Any change to a material term is a counteroffer, which **rejects** the original and substitutes a new one — the original is gone, not suspended. This is true of a handwritten change on a signed counteroffer as much as of a formal document.

An offer may generally be revoked before acceptance even where it states a deadline, unless supported by consideration as an option.

### 3.2 Multiple offers

The seller chooses; there is no obligation to take the highest and no priority by order of receipt. Whether competing terms are disclosed is the seller's decision.

The line to hold: **declining to disclose competing terms is permissible; inventing them is a misrepresentation.** A client's instruction cannot authorize the second.

Where multiple counteroffers go out, the seller needs the protection of a final election, or the seller may be exposed on more than one acceptance.

### 3.3 Terms are the client's; advice is the licensee's

For a buyer waiving contingencies, a seller weighing a credit-heavy offer, or either party considering an escalation clause: explain in writing what the term does and what it exposes them to, then follow the informed decision.

Both failure modes are wrong — refusing to write what the client instructs overstates the licensee's role; writing it without comment abandons the advisory duty.

### 3.4 The failure that occurs here

**Misrepresenting competing offers**, and **failing to document that a waiver was informed.**

**The control:** never describe an offer that does not exist, and put the consequences of a waiver in writing before the client decides.

### 3.5 A worked negotiation

> **The situation.** A listing at 849,000, eleven days on market, one showing-heavy weekend. Two offers arrive Sunday evening.
>
> - **Offer A:** 842,000, conventional financing with twenty percent down, standard periods, no repair requests contemplated, close in thirty days, preapproval from a lender the listing agent has not heard of.
> - **Offer B:** 855,000, ten percent down, investigation period shortened to ten days, appraisal contingency waived, close in twenty-one days, a letter about how much the buyers love the house, preapproval from a national lender.
>
> **What the licensee does first.** Presents both. Lesson 08, Section 2.4 governs, and neither offer is characterized before the seller has seen them.
>
> **Then the analysis the seller needs, which is not a ranking.**
>
> **On price:** B is 13,000 higher nominally. **The net comparison is what matters** — Lesson 10, Section 5.3 — and the offers must be compared on proceeds after costs and credits rather than on the headline.
>
> **On the appraisal waiver:** B's waiver is worth something only if the buyer can perform it. **Ten percent down and a waived appraisal contingency is a combination that deserves scrutiny** — if the property appraises 20,000 low, the buyer must produce that cash on top of his down payment, and the file gives no indication he has it. Lesson 08, Section 4.7 explains the mechanism, and Lesson 10, Section 1.6 explains what verifying capacity actually looks like.
>
> **On the lenders:** the unfamiliar lender on A is not a defect. **Lesson 10, Section 1.3 governs how to read a preapproval**, and the reading is about what the letter says was verified, not about brand recognition. Treating a national name as inherently stronger is a shortcut, and it is the sort of shortcut that quietly disadvantages some buyers.
>
> **On the timelines:** B closes in twenty-one days. **Whether B's lender can close in twenty-one days is a question with an answer**, obtainable by asking, and an aggressive date the lender cannot meet converts the buyer's strength into his default.
>
> **On the letter:** it is set aside. **Personal letters can convey protected characteristics**, and Lesson 14's analysis applies — a seller's decision influenced by what a letter reveals about who the buyers are is exactly the decision fair housing law reaches. **The listing agent's handling of these is a brokerage policy question, decided in advance rather than offer by offer.**
>
> **What the licensee says.** The analysis, the risks in each, the questions he recommends asking before the seller decides, and **no recommendation about which buyer deserves the house.** Lesson 03, Section 3.1: the terms and the decision are the seller's; the analysis is the licensee's.
>
> **What the licensee does not do.** Tell either buyer's agent what the other offered. Lesson 08, Section 3.3 draws the line between confidentiality and misrepresentation, and **"there's another offer at 855" is confidential negotiating information about the seller's position, disclosed to the party negotiating against him.**
>
> **What the file records:** both offers, the analysis presented, the questions asked and their answers, the seller's decision and its date, and the disposition of the offer not accepted — Lesson 07, Section 6.9.

## 4. Disclosure and investigation

### 4.1 Two independent duties

The **seller** discloses known material facts. The **licensee** conducts a reasonably competent visual inspection of accessible areas and discloses what it reveals. Neither discharges the other, and a professional inspection discharges neither.

Record observations as observations. "Brown staining approximately three feet across on the northwest bedroom ceiling; paint appears newer in that area" is what a licensee is qualified to state. "There was a roof leak that has been repaired" is an inference the licensee is not qualified to make and that the buyer may rely on.

### 4.2 Materiality

Measured by effect on a reasonable buyer's decision — not by the seller's view of importance, not by how often the condition appears, and not by whether the buyer could have seen it. A repaired condition is disclosed, because the adequacy of the repair is the question.

Every item answered; "unknown" where uncertain; never "no" where the seller does not know; never completed by the licensee on the seller's behalf.

### 4.3 Delivery is where rights attach

Content is half. **Delivery and its date is the other half**, because rights run from delivery.

Where a disclosure arrives after contingencies were removed, the correct response is to raise it immediately rather than proceed as though the transaction were firm. Delivery before closing does not cure late delivery, and moving the closing date does not address the buyer's right to respond.

### 4.4 Investigation

Recommend investigation matched to the property, not recited generically. Confirm the buyer engaged with the report; do not interpret it. Where a finding exceeds the licensee's competence, disclose the observation, recommend the specialist, and document the recommendation and the buyer's response.

Start the slow items first — insurance in mapped hazard areas, association documents, permit research — because a finding that arrives with two days left in the contingency period is a finding the buyer cannot act on.

### 4.5 Contingency removal

Under the standard California residential form, contingencies **remain in effect until removed in writing**. A passed date removes nothing. The seller's route is the contractual notice, which starts the period the contract specifies.

Read the contract's own mechanism rather than assuming deadlines are self-executing.

### 4.6 The failure that occurs here

**Undated or missing delivery evidence**, and **assuming a passed date removed a contingency.**

**The control:** evidence every delivery with its date, and build the deadline calendar from the contract's own terms.

## 5. Escrow, finance, and closing

### 5.1 Escrow

Neutral, acting only on **mutual** written instructions. It does not advise, does not decide disputes, and does not release funds because one party asserts entitlement. Where the parties disagree, escrow stops.

Escrow's instructions implement the purchase agreement; where they conflict, the parties' agreement governs and the discrepancy is reconciled by the parties in writing.

### 5.2 Title

The preliminary report states the terms on which the insurer would insure, including exceptions. **An exception means the policy does not insure against that item** — which is why exceptions are read rather than skimmed, and why the underlying documents matter.

Vesting is a legal and tax question. Refer it to counsel **before escrow prepares the deed**.

### 5.3 Financing

Lenders lend against the lower of appraised value and purchase price. A low appraisal is a financing event first: the buyer may bring funds, renegotiate, seek another resolution, or cancel if a contingency permits and its procedure is followed.

Where the appraisal contingency has been removed, lay out the practical options, state that the removed contingency limits his contractual rights, and **recommend counsel before he decides to stop performing.**

A credit is not a price reduction for appraisal purposes. Program limits constrain how large a credit can be.

Warn the buyer in writing at the start of escrow about the events that void a conditional approval — new credit, job changes, large unexplained deposits, moving funds — and about **wire fraud**, before any funds move.

### 5.4 Closing

Signing, funding, recording, disbursement — four events, in that order. Recording establishes the interests of record and generally makes the buyer the owner of record; disbursement follows confirmation of recording. Recording **order** establishes lien priority.

Possession transfers when the contract says, which may not be at recording. Any occupancy arrangement is documented with term, payment, condition, insurance, and a remedy for holdover.

### 5.5 The failure that occurs here

**Changes made without a signed amendment.** A date change agreed by email between agents does not change the contract, and if the transaction fails the parties are litigating on the original terms with correspondence between non-parties as the evidence.

**The control:** every change to a term is a written amendment signed by both parties and delivered to escrow.

### 5.6 The closing week

The last seven days of a transaction concentrate more failure modes than the preceding forty, because everything deferred arrives at once and everyone involved is working against the same date.

**What should already be done by day minus seven**, and the list is short because it is the list that cannot be compressed:

1. **All contingencies resolved**, with the removals dated and delivered.
2. **All amendments signed and at escrow** — Lesson 11, Section 1.6. **Anything agreed and not transmitted does not exist to escrow.**
3. **Loan clear to close**, with any remaining conditions identified by name rather than described as "just a couple of things."
4. **Payoff demands obtained**, with good-through dates that cover the actual closing.
5. **Association documents delivered and any transfer requirements started.**
6. **Insurance bound**, which is the item most often assumed and least often confirmed.
7. **The estimated closing statement reviewed** against the agreement and the amendments — Lesson 10, Section 5.7.

**What happens in the final days:**

**The walkthrough** — Lesson 11, Section 4.7 — performed with enough margin before signing that a finding can be addressed. **A walkthrough the morning of closing is a walkthrough that cannot produce a response.**

**Wire instructions**, and the single non-negotiable discipline in this course: **verify by voice, at a number obtained independently, before any funds move.** Not the number in the email. Not by replying to the thread. The warning is given at the start of the transaction and repeated in the closing week, because that is when the attack arrives.

**The signing**, where the client will be handed documents he has not seen. **The licensee's contribution is that nothing at the table is a surprise** — which is a statement about the preceding six weeks rather than about the signing.

**Possession**, arranged, documented, and — if anyone will be in the property after closing — **reduced to a written agreement rather than a courtesy.** Lesson 11, Section 5.2.

**The failure modes to watch for, each addressed elsewhere in the course:**

| The failure | Where it was prevented |
|---|---|
| A credit agreed and never transmitted | Lesson 11, Section 1.6 |
| A condition surfacing on day 33 | Lesson 10, Section 4.7 |
| A payoff demand expiring before closing | Lesson 10, Section 5.6 |
| A walkthrough finding with no time to respond | Lesson 11, Section 4.7 |
| Wire instructions from a compromised thread | Lesson 11, Section 4.6 |
| Possession by handshake | Lesson 11, Section 5.2 |

> **The pattern in all six:** none is a hard problem. **Each is an easy problem that arrived on the day it could no longer be solved**, and the closing week is where the chronology's earlier shortcuts present their bill.

## 6. Complete file compliance audit

### 6.1 The standard

**Reconstructability:** could a person who was not there determine what happened, when, and on what basis?

### 6.2 The audit sequence

1. **Completeness** — every required document present, including for the parts that did not go smoothly.
2. **Dates** — everything dated; delivery evidenced where timing matters.
3. **Consistency** — names, dates, amounts, and property descriptions reconcile.
4. **Chronology** — the order makes sense against the contract and against the disclose-before-decide principle.
5. **Nothing improper present** — no unexecuted draft mistaken for final, no superseded figure presented as current.
6. **Retention** — filed where it will be found, for the required period.

Keep superseded versions. The sequence of versions is frequently what shows when a term changed and who agreed — which is the question a dispute usually asks.

### 6.3 A worked audit, ranked

> A closed file contains:
>
> - **(a)** An agency disclosure signed and dated three days **after** the listing agreement.
> - **(b)** A confirmation naming the brokerage as buyer's agent, while the listing file shows it as seller's agent, with **no dual agency consent** anywhere.
> - **(c)** An email agreeing to move the closing date, with **no signed amendment**.
> - **(d)** A contingency removal, signed but **undated**.
> - **(e)** A transfer disclosure with **two items left blank**.
> - **(f)** A referral fee disclosure dated the day of closing, for a warranty the buyer bought six weeks earlier.
>
> **Ranked by seriousness:**
>
> **1. (b) The undisclosed dual agency.** Different in kind from the others. It describes the brokerage acting on both sides without either party's consent — a breach of duties actually owed, not a documentation failure.
>
> **2. (d) The undated removal.** Rights ran from it and the file cannot show when. This is the defect a dispute is most likely to turn on.
>
> **3. (a) Disclosure after the agreement.** The seller committed before being informed. The document exists; the protection it was meant to give does not.
>
> **4. (f) Late conflict disclosure.** Delivered after the buyer acted, which is the one thing a conflict disclosure must not be.
>
> **5. (c) Unsigned date change.** The operative contract still says the original date.
>
> **6. (e) Blanks in the disclosure.** Ambiguous about whether the items were addressed.
>
> **The response:** record each finding, escalate to the broker, obtain what legitimately exists, and address the processes that produced them.

### 6.4 The line

> **What must not happen:** dating the removal now, completing the blanks now, producing an amendment now with a matching date, or creating the consent that was never obtained.
>
> A **documented gap** is a compliance problem a brokerage can survive.
>
> A **manufactured document** is falsification. It converts a defensible situation into a question about integrity, and it ends careers.

Corrections are made visibly, with the original preserved and the change attributable. A record that can be silently altered is not a record.

### 6.5 The course in four questions

When you meet a situation this course did not cover, four questions will usually produce the right answer:

1. **Whose decision is this?** Price, terms, and whether to proceed belong to the client. The analysis and the advice belong to you. Legal effect belongs to counsel.
2. **Whose duty is engaged?** Fiduciary duties run to the principal; honesty, fair dealing, and disclosure of material facts run to everyone.
3. **Does this decision have the information it requires?** If not, something is out of order.
4. **What will the file show?** If the answer is "nothing," create the record now, while it is contemporaneous and true.

### 6.6 The four questions, applied

Section 6.5 supplies four questions for situations this course did not cover. Their value is only demonstrable on situations it did not cover, so here are three.

> **Situation one.** A past client calls. Her elderly father is selling his house and moving in with her. She wants you to list it. On the visit, the father is present but says little; the daughter answers for him, produces a power of attorney, and mentions that "he gets confused sometimes." She wants a fast sale at a price you think is well below the range.
>
> **Whose decision is this?** The seller's. **Which raises the question of who the seller is** — the father, with the daughter acting under an authority whose scope is a document you have not read and whose validity is a legal question.
>
> **Whose duty is engaged?** If you take the listing, fiduciary duties run to the principal. **Determining who the principal is, and whether the person instructing you has authority to instruct you, is the first task and not a formality.**
>
> **Does this decision have the information it requires?** The price is below your range and the seller has not spoken. **You do not know that the father knows what is being done.**
>
> **What will the file show?** Right now, nothing. **The correct actions:** read the authority, speak with the father directly and privately, deliver the pricing analysis to him, document what you observed, and take the whole thing to your broker — because elder financial abuse is a live concern with specific California frameworks, and this fact pattern is one of its recognized shapes. **You do not accuse anyone. You escalate facts.**

> **Situation two.** Your buyer client, in contract, tells you he has decided to rent the property out rather than live in it, and asks you not to mention it because "the loan is an owner-occupied loan and it's none of their business."
>
> **Whose decision is this?** His occupancy is his. **What he tells his lender is not a matter of preference** — occupancy is material to the credit decision.
>
> **Whose duty is engaged?** Confidentiality runs to him. **It does not extend to participating in a misrepresentation to a third party**, which is the boundary Lesson 03, Section 3.2 draws in a different context and which holds here.
>
> **Does this decision have the information it requires?** He may not know that occupancy is material or that a misstatement is loan fraud. **Telling him is the first step and it frequently ends the problem.**
>
> **What will the file show?** Whatever you write down today. **The correct actions:** tell him plainly, tell him to inform his lender, decline to be part of any communication that states otherwise, and escalate to your broker. **You do not resolve it by not asking again.**

> **Situation three.** A seller you represent tells you at the listing appointment that the neighbor's fence has been three feet onto his property for twenty years and "we've had an understanding." He would rather not raise it now.
>
> **Whose decision is this?** Whether to resolve the boundary is his. **Whether it is disclosed is not.**
>
> **Whose duty is engaged?** Disclosure of material facts runs to everyone. **A boundary discrepancy affects what the buyer is acquiring**, and it is squarely material.
>
> **Does this decision have the information it requires?** No — **and neither does the seller**, because the legal effect of twenty years of an "understanding" is a question with real answers that neither of you knows.
>
> **What will the file show?** **The correct actions:** disclose it, recommend the seller obtain legal advice about it before listing, recommend a survey, document the conversation the day it happens, and do not characterize the boundary yourself.

**The pattern across all three.** None was covered by a rule you memorized. **All three resolved to the same four steps: identify the decision-maker, identify the duty, determine whether the decision is informed, and create the record.** That sequence is what this course was building, and it will answer situations that have not happened yet.

### 6.7 Checkpoint

You should be able to take an unfamiliar transaction problem, apply the four questions, and produce both the action and the record it requires.

| **Exception** | An item the title policy does not insure against — which is why exceptions are read and the underlying documents obtained. |
| **Lower of value or price** | What lenders lend against. The sentence from which every consequence of a low appraisal follows. |
| **Signed amendment** | The only way a contract term changes. An agent email, a verbal agreement, or an instruction to escrow alone changes nothing. |
| **The closing sequence** | Signing, funding, recording, disbursement — in that order, with recording order establishing lien priority. |
| **Reconstructability** | The audit standard: could a person who was not there determine what happened, when, and on what basis? |
| **Superseded versions** | Kept, because the sequence of versions is what shows when a term changed and who agreed — the question a dispute usually asks. |
| **Documented gap versus manufactured document** | A gap is a compliance problem a brokerage survives. A backdated or created document is falsification, and it ends careers. |
| **The four questions** | Whose decision is this? Whose duty is engaged? Does this decision have the information it requires? What will the file show? |

## Practice problems

### Problem 1

A licensee takes a listing from a woman living in the home. Title is held by her late mother's trust;
she is one of three beneficiaries. The listing agreement is signed at the appointment; the agency
disclosure is signed three days later; the property goes live the same week with photographs from the
prior listing and a square footage figure copied from it.

**Apply the ordering test to each event.**

> **Solution.** Four failures, and three of them are sequencing failures of the same kind.
>
> **Authority was never verified** — the most common and most expensive intake failure. The
> **trustee** signs, within the authority the trust document grants; beneficiaries do not, however many
> and however clearly they benefit. This surfaces in escrow, where unwinding costs the most.
>
> **The agency disclosure came after the listing agreement.** The seller committed before being
> informed. The document now exists and the protection it was meant to give does not.
>
> **The photographs and the square footage** each precede the information they required: truthfulness
> is measured against the property's **current** condition, and copying a figure from a prior listing
> launders an unverified number rather than verifying it.
>
> **The ordering test disposes of all three:** does this decision have, in front of it, the information
> it was meant to be made with?

### Problem 2

A seller lists $150,000 above the licensee's supported range after receiving the analysis and written
advice. Two weeks later the licensee revises her comparative market analysis upward so the file
"matches the list price," and tells a buyer's agent that the price reflects the brokerage's opinion of
value.

**What was right, and what was wrong?**

> **Solution.** **The listing at the seller's number was right.** Price is the seller's decision; the
> analysis and the advice are the licensee's obligation. She discharged both — the analysis, the
> written advice, and the seller's informed decision are what the file needs.
>
> **The revision is the failure**, and it is worse than having no analysis. An analysis that follows
> the price rather than informing it documents nothing. And representing the list price as the
> brokerage's opinion of value **adopts the seller's reasoning as the brokerage's** — the one thing the
> file must not do.
>
> **What the file should show:** the comparables with sources as of the date, the adjustment grid with
> direction and basis, the reconciliation and its weighting, the comparables rejected and why, the
> market conditions evidence, the recommendation as a range with a most-probable point, the written
> advice, and the seller's decision.

### Problem 3

A buyer offers $880,000. The seller strikes the price to $895,000, signs, and returns it. The buyer
signs but writes a different closing date before returning it. Meanwhile the seller's agent tells two
other buyers there are "several offers well above asking," when there is one, and counters three
offers at $940,000 with no further provision.

**State where the parties stand and identify every defect.**

> **Solution.** **No contract.** The seller's changes were a counteroffer, which **rejected** the
> $880,000 offer and substituted a new one — the original is gone, not suspended. The buyer's
> handwritten date change is a **further counteroffer**, however small; signing does not convert a
> modification into an acceptance. The buyer's counter is outstanding.
>
> **"Several offers well above asking" is a misrepresentation.** Declining to disclose competing terms
> is permissible and is the seller's decision; inventing them is not, and a client's instruction
> cannot authorize it.
>
> **Countering three offers without a final election** exposes the seller on more than one acceptance
> — the customary protection makes the seller's obligation contingent on subsequent written acceptance
> of the buyer's acceptance.
>
> Worth adding for the competing buyers' agents: the seller chooses, there is no obligation to accept
> the highest, and there is no priority by order of receipt.

### Problem 4

The listing agent observes ceiling staining with fresh paint. The seller says a pipe leaked, was
repaired, and "there's nothing to disclose." The seller's disclosure comes back with two blanks. The
transfer disclosure is delivered to the buyer nine days after he removed his contingencies, and the
listing agent proposes extending the closing by a week if he objects.

**Address each.**

> **Solution.** **Both duties are engaged and neither discharges the other.** The seller discloses the
> leak and the repair — a repaired condition is still material, because the adequacy of the repair is
> exactly what a buyer evaluates, and here there are no records. The agent separately records and
> discloses **the observation**: "brown staining approximately three feet across on the northwest
> bedroom ceiling; paint appears newer in that area." Not the inference that there was a leak that has
> been repaired.
>
> **The blanks are completed** — every item answered, "unknown" where that is true, never "no" where
> the seller does not know, and never completed by the licensee on the seller's behalf.
>
> **The late delivery is raised immediately**, not managed. Rights run from delivery and are not
> extinguished because the buyer acted earlier without the information; late delivery may revive or
> extend his ability to respond. **Receipt before closing does not cure it, and moving the closing date
> does not address the right to respond.**

### Problem 5

The buyer's investigation contingency period ends on the 17th. Nothing is delivered. On the 18th the
seller's agent tells the seller she is free to accept a better offer. The buyer's agent, separately,
has not yet ordered insurance quotes for a property in a mapped fire area, with four days left in the
period.

**Correct the first and assess the second.**

> **Solution.** **The contingency remains in effect.** Under the standard California form contingencies
> end only by **written removal**; a passed date removes nothing, the buyer is not committed, and the
> seller is not free to sell elsewhere. The seller's route is the contractual notice, which starts the
> period the contract specifies — after which she may cancel per its procedure.
>
> **The insurance is the other half of the same lesson.** Most failed transactions fail because a track
> **started late**, and in mapped fire and flood areas availability and cost have become a common cause
> of failure. A buyer who learns in week one that coverage will cost far more than assumed can act; one
> who learns it with four days left generally cannot.
>
> **The control for both:** build the deadline calendar from the contract's own terms, share it in
> writing, and start the slow items first — insurance, association documents, permit research,
> specialist inspections.

### Problem 6

The appraisal comes in $20,000 below the contract price on a transaction where the buyer waived the
appraisal contingency. The buyer asks whether he can cancel and recover his deposit. His agent emails
the appraiser three sales and adds that "the deal dies without another $20,000." The parties then
agree by email to move closing back a week, and the buyer separately asks escrow to hold $3,000 from
the seller's proceeds.

**Assess each of the four.**

> **Solution.** **The deposit question is legal.** Lay out the practical options — bring funds,
> renegotiate, seek another resolution — state plainly that the removed contingency limits his
> contractual rights, and **recommend counsel before he decides to stop performing.** Telling him he
> may cancel and recover the deposit, or that he simply must perform, are both legal conclusions and
> both may be wrong.
>
> **The email to the appraiser mixes the permitted with the prohibited.** Relevant sales through the
> proper channel are **information** and are fine. "The deal dies without another $20,000" is a
> **target**, and a value that moves after that conversation is worthless for the purpose the appraisal
> serves — including when the request serves the licensee's own client.
>
> **The email closing extension changes nothing.** A change to a term is a written amendment signed by
> both parties and delivered to escrow. If the transaction fails, the parties litigate on the original
> date with correspondence between non-parties as the evidence.
>
> **Escrow cannot act on the holdback.** It acts only on mutual written instructions; one party cannot
> redirect funds, and an agent's confirmation is not the other party's consent. **Escrow stops** — and
> the agents' job is to get a mutual instruction in place before the closing date.

### Problem 7

Rank the defects in this closed file and state the correct response: (a) an agency disclosure dated
three days after the listing agreement; (b) a confirmation naming the brokerage as buyer's agent while
the listing file shows seller's agent, with no dual agency consent anywhere; (c) an email agreeing to
move closing, no signed amendment; (d) a contingency removal signed but undated; (e) a transfer
disclosure with two blanks; (f) a referral fee disclosure dated the day of closing for a warranty the
buyer bought six weeks earlier.

**Rank, and give the response.**

> **Solution.** **1. (b), the undisclosed dual agency** — different in kind from every other item. It
> describes the brokerage acting on both sides without either party's informed written consent: a
> breach of duties actually owed, not a documentation failure.
>
> **2. (d), the undated removal.** Rights ran from it and the file cannot show when. This is the defect
> a dispute is most likely to turn on.
>
> **3. (a), disclosure after the agreement.** The seller committed before being informed.
>
> **4. (f), the late conflict disclosure.** Delivered after the buyer acted — the one thing a conflict
> disclosure must not be, since information after the decision has no value.
>
> **5. (c), the unsigned date change.** The operative contract still says the original date.
>
> **6. (e), the blanks.** Ambiguous about whether the items were addressed.
>
> **The response:** record each finding, escalate to the broker, obtain what legitimately exists, and
> address the **processes** that produced them rather than only the instances.
>
> **What must not happen:** dating the removal now, completing the blanks now, producing an amendment
> with a matching date, or creating the consent that was never obtained. A documented gap is survivable;
> a manufactured document is falsification.

### Problem 8

A licensee faces a situation this course did not cover: a seller's adult son, who holds a power of
attorney, wants to accept an offer while the seller is hospitalized, and asks the licensee whether the
power of attorney is sufficient and whether the seller's condition must be disclosed to the buyer.

**Apply the four questions.**

> **Solution.** **Whose decision is this?** Not the licensee's, on either question. Whether the power
> of attorney authorizes conveyance of real property is a **legal** question — it depends on the
> instrument's terms and its current validity — and it goes to counsel, with escrow and title involved.
> Whether to accept the offer is the principal's decision, exercised by whoever actually holds
> authority.
>
> **Whose duty is engaged?** Honesty and fair dealing run to the buyer; fiduciary duties run to the
> seller. The disclosure question is genuinely constrained on both sides — a seller's illness is
> subject to specific California limits, and this is squarely a **do not volunteer, do not
> misrepresent, escalate** situation rather than one to resolve at a signing table.
>
> **Does this decision have the information it requires?** No. Acceptance cannot precede verified
> authority, and the licensee does not yet have it.
>
> **What will the file show?** Right now, nothing. So the record is created contemporaneously: what was
> asked, what was said, what was escalated and when, and what was referred to counsel.
>
> **What transfers:** the four questions are what the course leaves you with. They resolve situations
> nobody wrote a lesson about.

## Chapter summary

**A transaction is a chronology in which each step supplies the information the next requires.** Every
common sequencing error is a decision made before its information arrived, and the ordering test finds
them.

**Verify authority at intake against a source** — trustee, entity representative, personal
representative, all co-owners — and stop and escalate where it is unclear.

**Disclose, elect, confirm**, each preceding the decision it informs, with dual agency consent
preceding the brokerage acting on both sides.

**Compensation is negotiable; the termination date protects the seller; the protection clause is
explained rather than initialed past.**

**The analysis and advice are the licensee's obligation; the price is the client's decision.** Never
revise the analysis to match the price.

**Start the disclosure work at the listing appointment**, and separate the negotiable preferences from
the obligations openly.

**Advertising is truthful against current condition, sourced, identified, property-focused, approved,
and archived** — with audience targeting examined as part of the advertisement.

**A counteroffer rejects and replaces.** An offer may generally be revoked before acceptance.
Declining to disclose competing terms is permissible; inventing them is not. Counter multiple offers
only with a final election preserved.

**Terms are the client's; advice is the licensee's** — put waivers and their consequences in writing
before the decision, and then follow it.

**Two independent disclosure duties**, observations recorded as observations, every item answered,
delivery evidenced with its date.

**Contingencies end by written removal**, not by a passed date, and the seller's route is the
contractual notice.

**Escrow acts only on mutual written instruction and stops when the parties disagree.** An exception
means the policy does not insure against that item. Vesting goes to counsel before the deed is drawn.

**Lenders lend against the lower of value or price**, a credit is not a price reduction for appraisal
purposes, and the start-of-escrow warnings — credit, employment, moving funds, and wire fraud — are
the cheapest protection available.

**Every change is a signed amendment.** Signing, funding, recording, disbursement, in that order.

**Audit for reconstructability, keep superseded versions, obtain what exists, record what does not,
and fix the process.** A documented gap is survivable; a manufactured document is not.

**And when the situation is unfamiliar, four questions:** whose decision, whose duty, does this
decision have its information, and what will the file show.
