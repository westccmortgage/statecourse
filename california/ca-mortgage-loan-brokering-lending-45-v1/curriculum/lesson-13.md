## 1. Payment processing and servicing records

### 1.1 What servicing is

**Servicing is the administration of a loan after it funds** — collecting payments, applying them, maintaining escrow accounts, remitting to the owner of the loan, handling payoffs, communicating with the borrower, and managing delinquency.

**The servicer is not necessarily the owner of the loan**, and neither is necessarily the party that originated it. **All three can be different, and any of them can change.**

> **A borrower confused about who owns his loan and who services it is asking a reasonable question with a genuinely complicated answer.**

### 1.2 Why a mortgage licensee needs this

Three reasons:

**Borrowers ask.** A former borrower calls the person he knows — the originator — about a payment problem, an escrow increase, or a notice he received.

**Brokers sometimes service.** A broker who arranged a private money loan and collects payments for investors **is servicing**, and the obligations are real — including trust fund obligations.

**Origination decisions become servicing consequences.** An escrow estimate built on the seller's tax figure becomes a shortage; a product feature nobody explained becomes a complaint.

### 1.3 Payment application

Payments are applied in the order the note and the servicer's practice provide — typically to interest, principal, escrow, and then other amounts.

**Two points that produce disputes:**

**Partial payments.** A servicer's handling of a payment less than the full amount due is governed by the note and applicable requirements, and a borrower who assumes a partial payment cures a delinquency is frequently wrong.

**Extra principal.** A borrower sending more than the amount due may find it applied to the next installment rather than to principal, unless he directs otherwise. **A borrower intending to reduce principal must say so**, and telling him that in advance is worth more than explaining it afterward.

### 1.4 Servicing records

**A servicer maintains a record of every receipt and every application**, and the record is what answers a dispute about whether a payment was made, when, and how it was applied.

**Where a broker services**, the record obligations of Lesson 08 apply in full: complete records of receipts and disbursements, **identifying whose funds they are**, and retention for the required period.

> **Payments collected for an owner or investor are trust funds** — separated, recorded by beneficiary, and disbursed per the arrangement's terms.

### 1.5 Servicing transfers

RESPA addresses transfers of servicing, including **notice to the borrower** and **a period during which payments sent to the prior servicer are treated as timely.**

**The framework anticipates the confusion**, which is why the protection exists. A borrower who sends a payment to the old servicer during that window has not defaulted.

**What a borrower needs to know at a transfer:** who is servicing now, where to send payments, whether anything else changes — **the note's terms do not change on a servicing transfer** — and what happens to his escrow account.

### 1.6 What a licensee tells a former borrower who calls

**May:** explain the difference between the owner and the servicer; explain that servicing transfers are ordinary and that the loan's terms do not change; explain what an escrow account is and why a payment can change; and **direct him to his servicer, with the specific procedure to use.**

**May not:** contact the servicer as though authorized without authority; interpret a notice he received; tell him whether he is in default; predict what the servicer will do; or advise him to stop paying.

### 1.7 Checkpoint

You should be able to distinguish owner, servicer, and originator; explain partial payment and extra principal handling; and state what a servicing transfer does and does not change.

## 2. Escrow accounts

### 2.1 What an escrow account is

An **escrow account** — also called an impound account — is maintained by the servicer to pay **property taxes and insurance**, funded by an amount collected with each payment.

**Its purpose is protective on both sides:** the lender's collateral stays insured and free of tax liens, and the borrower spreads two large annual obligations across twelve payments.

### 2.2 Why the payment on a fixed-rate loan changes

The single most common servicing question, and one an originator can prevent.

> **A fixed-rate loan has a fixed principal and interest payment. The total payment is not fixed**, because the escrow portion moves with the taxes and insurance it funds.

**When taxes or insurance rise, the escrow portion rises** — and where the account has fallen short, the borrower repays the shortage on top of the higher ongoing amount.

### 2.3 The California reassessment problem

**This is where California originators create escrow shortages at scale.**

A buyer's property taxes are based on **a new base year value established on the change in ownership** — not on the seller's bill. An estimate built from the seller's figure on a long-held property can be low **by a multiple.**

> **The result is predictable: the escrow account is underfunded from the first month, the analysis finds a shortage, and the borrower's "fixed" payment jumps in the first or second year.**

**A borrower told this at application accepts it. A borrower who discovers it in month fourteen files a complaint** — and he is not wrong to be angry.

### 2.4 Escrow analysis

Servicers perform a periodic **analysis** comparing what the account collected against what it paid and will pay, producing a statement showing the new payment and the disposition of any shortage or surplus.

| Finding | What follows |
|---|---|
| **Shortage** | The account has less than required. Repaid over a period, or in a lump sum at the borrower's option, plus a higher ongoing amount |
| **Deficiency** | The account is negative |
| **Surplus** | The account holds more than required. Refunded or applied, per the rules |

**RESPA limits the cushion** a servicer may maintain, and governs the analysis and the statements provided.

### 2.5 Escrow waivers

Some programs permit a borrower to waive escrow and pay taxes and insurance directly, sometimes for a fee or at a specified loan-to-value.

**What a borrower must understand before choosing it:** he becomes responsible for paying two large obligations on time; **failure to pay taxes or maintain insurance is a default under the security instrument** even where payments are current; and the lender may **force-place** insurance at a far higher cost, protecting only the lender.

### 2.6 What a licensee explains at origination

Four things, in writing, at application:

1. **The payment includes taxes and insurance**, and PITI is the number that matters — not principal and interest.
2. **In California the tax figure will be based on a new assessment**, not the seller's bill, so the estimate is an estimate.
3. **The payment can change** when taxes or insurance change, even on a fixed-rate loan.
4. **A supplemental tax bill will arrive** after closing for the difference between the old and new assessed value for the remainder of the year — **and it is usually not covered by the escrow account.**

> **The supplemental bill is the surprise nobody warns about**, and it arrives months after closing for an amount the borrower did not budget.

### 2.7 Checkpoint

You should be able to explain why a fixed-rate payment changes, describe the California reassessment problem, describe an escrow analysis, and list what to explain at origination.

## 3. Payoff demand and reconveyance

### 3.1 What a payoff demand is

**A statement from the lienholder of what satisfies the obligation**, good through a stated date.

### 3.2 What it contains, and why it exceeds the statement balance

| Component |
|---|
| **Unpaid principal balance** |
| **Accrued interest to the payoff date** |
| **Any prepayment charge**, where the note provides one and it is permitted |
| **Late charges and other amounts owed under the note** |
| **Fees for preparing the demand and for reconveyance**, as permitted |
| **Any advances the lender made** — force-placed insurance, taxes paid |

> **Interest is paid in arrears, and it accrues to the day of payoff.** That is why a payoff is routinely more than the last statement balance — and why a payoff good through the 15th is wrong if the loan funds on the 20th.

**Explaining this at the listing or the application prevents an unhappy conversation at closing**, and it is one of the small things separating a licensee who prepared the client from one who did not.

### 3.3 The per diem and the good-through date

A demand states a **per diem** so the amount can be computed for a later date.

> **$412,000 at 6.25%, 365-day basis:** 412,000 × 0.0625 ÷ 365 = **$70.55 per day.**
> Five days beyond the good-through date adds **$352.75.**

**Escrow obtains and updates the demand**, and a demand that expires before disbursement must be refreshed — an ordinary cause of a delayed closing.

### 3.4 Reconveyance

When the obligation is satisfied, the beneficiary directs the trustee to reconvey, and a **reconveyance is recorded** releasing the deed of trust.

> **Payment of the debt does not clear the record. The recorded document does.**

**Timelines apply** to the delivery and recording of the release after payoff, and a borrower whose loan was paid years ago and whose title still shows the lien has a real problem that requires a document — not a recollection.

**A licensee encountering an unreleased deed of trust on a preliminary report should treat it as a requirement with a deadline**, because obtaining a reconveyance from a lender that has merged, been acquired, or ceased to exist can take weeks.

### 3.5 Partial reconveyance

In a **blanket** loan securing multiple parcels, a **release clause** permits individual parcels to be released as they are sold, on the terms the instrument states — typically a release price. **A partial reconveyance effects that release.**

### 3.6 Payoff versus assumption versus subordination

Three different things a borrower may confuse:

| Action | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Payoff** | The obligation is satisfied and the lien released by reconveyance |
| **Assumption** | The loan continues with a new borrower, generally requiring consent — **and the seller remains liable without a novation** |
| **Subordination** | The lien remains and moves to a lower priority position |

### 3.7 Checkpoint

You should be able to explain why a payoff exceeds the statement balance, compute a per diem extension, and state what clears a satisfied lien from the record.

## 4. Delinquency and loss mitigation

### 4.1 What default is

A failure to perform an obligation under the note or the security instrument. **Non-payment is the common form**, and the instrument creates others: failure to pay taxes, failure to maintain insurance, waste, and unauthorized transfer triggering the alienation clause.

**Acceleration is what makes default consequential.** Without an acceleration clause a lender could pursue only missed payments; with one, **the entire balance becomes due.**

### 4.2 The loss mitigation options, in outline

| Option | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Reinstatement** | Curing the arrearage and permitted costs, restoring the loan |
| **Repayment plan** | Curing the arrearage over time alongside regular payments |
| **Forbearance** | A temporary reduction or suspension, with the arrearage addressed afterward |
| **Modification** | A permanent change to the loan's terms |
| **Short sale** | A sale for less than the balance, with lienholder approval |
| **Deed in lieu of foreclosure** | A voluntary conveyance to the lender |

### 4.3 What each carries that borrowers do not expect

**Forbearance is not forgiveness.** The suspended amounts are still owed, and **how they are repaid at the end** — lump sum, spread over payments, deferred to maturity, or capitalized — is the question that matters, and it must be in writing.

**A modification changes the loan permanently**, and may extend the term, capitalize arrears, or change the rate. **Its credit and tax consequences are questions for other professionals.**

**A short sale requires every lienholder's approval**, and **a junior lienholder can defeat one.** Its deficiency treatment depends on what the lienholders agree to.

**A deed in lieu does not automatically extinguish junior liens**, which is a common reason a lender declines one.

### 4.4 The California servicer requirements

California imposes **borrower-protective requirements on servicers** in the residential foreclosure context, including obligations around **borrower contact before a notice of default**, a **single point of contact**, and constraints on pursuing foreclosure while a complete loss mitigation application is pending.

**The specifics are legal.** A licensee's role is to **know these protections exist** and to route the borrower to counsel and to a **HUD-approved housing counselor** — because a borrower who does not know a protection exists cannot assert it.

### 4.5 What a licensee may and may not tell a distressed borrower

**May:** explain what the options are called and what each generally does; explain that a notice has deadlines and that the borrower should read it; explain that borrower protections exist in California; **refer to counsel, to a HUD-approved housing counselor, and to a tax professional, in writing**; and help him gather documents his servicer requests.

**May not:**

- **Predict whether any option will be approved.**
- **Tell him whether he owes a deficiency** — a legal question with significant consequences.
- **Characterize the credit consequences** of a short sale, modification, or foreclosure.
- **Advise on the tax treatment.**
- **Tell him to stop paying**, which is advice with irreversible consequences.
- **Tell him a short sale "won't hurt as much."**

### 4.6 Foreclosure rescue fraud

Distressed situations attract schemes, and California regulates foreclosure consulting and equity purchase specifically.

**The patterns to recognize:**

| Pattern | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Advance fees for loan modification** | Collecting up front to obtain a modification, which California restricts |
| **Equity stripping** | A transfer that moves the property away from a distressed owner with equity |
| **Sale-leaseback with a repurchase option** | Presented as a rescue; frequently a transfer the owner cannot undo |
| **"Stop paying your lender and pay us instead"** | Diverts payments and accelerates the foreclosure |
| **A transfer of title as a condition of help** | The owner ends up without the property |
| **Instructions to avoid contact with the servicer** | Removes the borrower's access to the protections above |

> **A licensee who sees one of these escalates to the broker and does not participate, however the arrangement is framed and however genuinely the borrower needs help.**

**The advance fee point connects to Lesson 08:** a fee collected before services are rendered is an advance fee, and California restricts advance fees for loan modification services specifically.

### 4.7 Checkpoint

You should be able to name the loss mitigation options and what each carries, state what California servicer protections exist in outline, list what a licensee may not tell a distressed borrower, and recognize rescue fraud patterns.

## 5. Nonjudicial foreclosure orientation

### 5.1 The two paths

| | Nonjudicial | Judicial |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | The trustee's **power of sale** | A court action |
| Speed | Faster | Slower |
| Cost | Lower | Higher |
| Deficiency judgment | **Generally not available** | May be available, subject to limits |
| Post-sale redemption | **Generally none** | A statutory redemption period may apply |

> **The trade-off is the point.** A beneficiary proceeding nonjudicially gets speed and gives up the deficiency. **In California residential practice, nonjudicial predominates.**

### 5.2 The sequence, in outline

The process is statutory and its details are legal. In outline:

1. **Default occurs**, and the beneficiary elects to proceed.
2. **Borrower contact requirements** are satisfied where they apply.
3. **A notice of default is recorded**, beginning the statutory period.
4. **A reinstatement period runs.**
5. **A notice of sale is recorded, published, posted, and mailed**, after the applicable period.
6. **The trustee's sale occurs**, and title passes by **trustee's deed.**

### 5.3 Reinstatement and redemption

**The distinction borrowers misunderstand most, and getting it wrong can cost someone their home.**

> **Reinstatement:** for a period, the borrower may cure by paying **the arrearage and permitted costs — not the accelerated balance** — and the loan is restored.
>
> **Redemption before sale:** after the reinstatement window closes, the route is generally paying **the full accelerated amount.**

**Those are materially different sums**, and a licensee who tells a borrower he can "catch up any time before the sale" has conflated them.

### 5.4 The sale

The property is sold at a public auction conducted by the trustee. The beneficiary may **credit bid** up to the amount owed; other bidders pay in the form required.

**Title passes by trustee's deed**, and a buyer takes what the sale conveys — generally **without the protections of an ordinary transaction**, and subject to whatever senior interests survive.

### 5.5 What happens to junior liens

> **A senior foreclosure generally extinguishes junior liens as claims against the property**, while the underlying debts may survive as personal obligations — subject to California's deficiency restrictions.

**That is why priority matters and why junior lienholders monitor senior loans.** A second wiped out by a first's foreclosure has lost its security entirely, whatever the merits of the debt.

**A broker who arranged a second for an investor should have explained that exposure before the money was placed.**

### 5.6 Deficiency

A **deficiency** is the shortfall where sale proceeds do not satisfy the debt. **California restricts deficiency judgments substantially:**

- **Generally unavailable after a nonjudicial sale**, whatever the loan type.
- **Restricted for purchase money loans** in defined circumstances — protection connected to the loan having been used to acquire the property.
- **Subject to limits even in judicial foreclosure**, including valuation-based constraints.

> **Whether a deficiency is available in a specific case is a legal question with significant consequences** — and it is exactly what a distressed borrower asks. **The answer is a referral, not a reassurance.**

### 5.7 Occupants after a sale

A purchaser at a trustee's sale takes subject to whatever occupancy rights exist, and **removing an occupant is a legal process** with its own requirements — including protections for tenants in defined circumstances.

**Self-help is prohibited.** Changing locks, removing belongings, or shutting off utilities is unlawful regardless of who now holds title.

### 5.8 Bankruptcy

A borrower's bankruptcy filing generally triggers an **automatic stay** halting collection and foreclosure activity.

> **Continuing collection or foreclosure activity after a filing can carry serious consequences.** A licensee's role is **recognition and immediate escalation** — the analysis is entirely legal.

### 5.9 Checkpoint

You should be able to compare the two paths, outline the nonjudicial sequence, distinguish reinstatement from redemption, and state what happens to junior liens and to a deficiency.

## 6. Borrower communication and complaint handling

### 6.1 Why communication is a compliance subject

Because **most servicing disputes are not about substance. They are about what the borrower was told, by whom, and when** — and because the frameworks impose defined procedures that informal handling defeats.

### 6.2 The defined procedures

RESPA and its implementing rules provide procedures under which a borrower may submit:

| Procedure | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Notice of error** | Asserting the servicer made an error |
| **Request for information** | Seeking information about the loan |

**Both carry acknowledgment and response obligations with deadlines**, and both are defeated when a borrower's concern is handled as a phone conversation nobody recorded.

> **A licensee's role is to recognize these as defined procedures with deadlines, tell the borrower they exist, and route him correctly — not to resolve a servicing dispute informally on his behalf.**

### 6.3 Communicating with a distressed borrower

**What helps:**

- **Answer promptly.** Silence reads as evasion and is what escalates a matter.
- **Say plainly what you can and cannot do**, and why.
- **Give him the specific next step** — the procedure, the party, the deadline.
- **Put referrals in writing**, so he has them after the call.
- **Record what was said and when.**

**What does not help:**

- **Reassurance you cannot support.** "It'll be fine" is the sentence that produces the complaint.
- **Predictions** about approval, timing, credit, or outcome.
- **Speculation** about what the servicer or the lender is doing.
- **Criticism of the servicer**, which he will repeat.

### 6.4 The referrals, in writing

For a distressed borrower, three referrals belong in writing and early:

1. **Counsel** — for the legal consequences: deficiency, remedies, rights, and the effect of any document he is asked to sign.
2. **A HUD-approved housing counselor** — for the process, and because the service is available to him.
3. **A tax professional** — for the tax consequences of a short sale, modification, or forgiveness.

**Early and in writing** matters because a borrower under pressure does not retain a spoken referral, and because **most of these questions have deadlines attached.**

### 6.5 Handling a complaint

When a borrower alleges mishandling:

1. **Acknowledge promptly.**
2. **Notify the broker immediately.**
3. **Preserve everything** — the file, the disclosures as delivered, communications, drafts, texts, and the advertising as it ran.
4. **Respond factually**, without admitting or denying liability before the file has been reviewed.
5. **Offer nothing on your own initiative** — no fee reduction, no credit.
6. **Report to the carrier** where the matter could become a claim.

**Why the wrong responses are wrong:** an immediate detailed defense commits a position before anyone reviewed the file — and in a mortgage file, **what was disclosed and when can be checked within minutes**; an apology accepting fault may be treated as an admission and can compromise coverage; a fee reduction commits the firm and rarely ends the matter; and handling it quietly compounds the original problem with a supervision failure.

> **Deleting a record that reflects poorly is far worse than the record.** It is generally discoverable anyway, and it changes the subject from professional judgment to integrity.

### 6.6 When a regulator makes contact

**Notify the broker immediately. Preserve records. Do not respond independently or informally.**

**Cooperation is expected. Improvised cooperation without review is where licensees make statements they cannot support.**

### 6.7 What the file must show

- Every communication material to a term or a decision, with dates.
- What the borrower was told and by whom.
- Referrals made, to whom, and the borrower's response.
- Documents delivered, with **evidence of what, to whom, when, and by what method.**
- Anything the licensee declined to do, and the escalation.
- Corrections issued, with the originals retained.

### 6.8 What origination owes servicing

The lesson's closing point, and the reason this material sits in an origination course:

> **Most servicing complaints are origination failures arriving late.**

| Servicing complaint | Origination failure |
|---|---|
| "My fixed payment went up" | PITI never explained; California reassessment never mentioned |
| "I got a supplemental tax bill nobody told me about" | Not explained at application |
| "My payoff was thousands more than my balance" | Accrued interest never explained |
| "Nobody told me the balance was due at maturity" | Balloon presented without its number |
| "My payment tripled after the fifth year" | Fully indexed rate and worst-case path never shown |
| "I was told I could just refinance" | A prediction stated as a plan |
| "I didn't know I had to pay my own taxes" | Escrow waiver consequences never explained |

**Every one of them is preventable in a conversation that costs nothing at application.**

### 6.9 Checkpoint

You should be able to name the defined procedures, describe how to communicate with a distressed borrower, state the complaint sequence, and connect a servicing complaint to its origination failure.

## Practice problems

### Problem 1

A former borrower calls his originator eighteen months after closing. He received a letter from a
company he has never heard of, does not know where to send his payment, sent an extra $500 last month
that "disappeared," and says his fixed-rate payment went up by $310.

**Address each.**

> **Solution.** **Four ordinary servicing facts, and one of them is an origination failure.**
>
> **The letter is a servicing transfer.** The **owner, the servicer, and the originator can be three
> different parties, and any of them can change.** RESPA requires **notice** and provides a **period in
> which payments sent to the prior servicer are treated as timely** — the framework anticipates exactly
> this confusion. **The note's terms do not change on a transfer.**
>
> **The $500 was probably applied to the next installment.** Extra funds may be applied that way **unless
> the borrower directs otherwise** — he must say he wants it applied to principal. Telling a borrower
> that at closing is worth more than explaining it afterward.
>
> **The payment increase is not a contradiction.** A fixed-rate loan has a fixed **principal and
> interest** payment; **the total payment is not fixed**, because the escrow portion moves with the taxes
> and insurance it funds. And where the account fell short, he repays the shortage **on top of** the
> higher ongoing amount.
>
> **What the licensee does:** explain the mechanics, and **direct him to his servicer with the specific
> procedure.** What the licensee does not do: interpret the letter, contact the servicer without
> authority, or tell him whether anything is wrong with his account.

### Problem 3

A seller's payoff demand shows $418,730 where his last statement showed $409,900. The demand is good
through the 15th; escrow now expects to disburse on the 20th. The demand states a per diem based on
$412,000 at 6.25%. Separately, a borrower who paid off a loan in 2016 finds it still on title.

**Explain the difference, compute the extension, and address the 2016 loan.**

> **Solution.** **A payoff states what satisfies the obligation, not the balance.** It contains the
> unpaid principal, **accrued interest to the payoff date**, any permitted prepayment charge, late
> charges and other amounts under the note, permitted preparation and reconveyance fees, and **any
> advances the lender made** — force-placed insurance or taxes paid.
>
> **Interest is paid in arrears and accrues to the day of payoff**, which is the core of it.
>
> **Per diem:** 412,000 × 0.0625 ÷ 365 = **$70.55 per day.** Five days beyond the good-through date adds
> **$352.75** — and **a demand that expires before disbursement must be refreshed**, an ordinary cause of
> a delayed closing.
>
> **On the 2016 loan: payment of the debt does not clear the record. The recorded document does.** He
> needs a **reconveyance**, and obtaining one from a lender that has merged, been acquired, or ceased to
> exist can take **weeks** — which is why an unreleased deed of trust on a preliminary report is a
> **requirement with a deadline**, not a formality.

### Problem 4

A borrower two payments behind asks his originator whether he can still keep the house, whether he
should stop paying so he "qualifies for help," whether a short sale will hurt less than a foreclosure,
and whether he will owe the difference. The originator answers all four.

**Correct each answer.**

> **Solution.** **Three of the four questions may not be answered at all, and the second is dangerous.**
>
> **On keeping the house:** explain the mechanism. **Reinstatement** lets him cure by paying **the
> arrearage and permitted costs — not the accelerated balance** — within the statutory window. After it
> closes, the route is generally **redemption before sale**, paying **the full accelerated amount.**
> **Those are materially different sums**, and conflating them can cost someone their home.
>
> **"Should I stop paying?" — never advise this.** It is advice with irreversible consequences, and it
> is also a foreclosure rescue pattern in its own right.
>
> **"Will a short sale hurt less?" — not a licensee's to characterize.** Credit consequences are outside
> the license, a short sale requires **every lienholder's approval** and a junior lienholder can defeat
> one, and its deficiency treatment depends on what the lienholders agree to.
>
> **"Will I owe the difference?" — a legal question with significant consequences.** California restricts
> deficiency judgments substantially — generally unavailable after a **nonjudicial** sale, and restricted
> for **purchase money** loans in defined circumstances — **but whether one is available in his case is
> for counsel.**
>
> **What the licensee does:** explain that **California servicer protections exist** — borrower contact
> before a notice of default, a single point of contact, constraints on foreclosing while a complete
> application is pending — and **refer in writing to counsel, a HUD-approved housing counselor, and a
> tax professional.** A borrower who does not know a protection exists cannot assert it.

### Problem 5

A company offers to help the borrower above: $3,500 up front to negotiate a modification, instructions
to stop contacting his servicer and to send payments to the company instead, and a proposed
sale-leaseback with an option to repurchase in two years. An originator is asked to refer clients to
them for a fee.

**Assess.**

> **Solution.** **Every element is a recognized foreclosure rescue pattern, and the referral offer adds
> two more violations.**
>
> **The $3,500 up front is an advance fee**, and California **restricts advance fees for loan
> modification services specifically.** A fee collected before services are rendered is an advance fee
> whatever it is called.
>
> **"Stop contacting your servicer" removes his access to the protections** — borrower contact
> requirements, the single point of contact, and the constraint on foreclosing while a complete
> application is pending. It is not incidental advice; it disables his defenses.
>
> **"Pay us instead" diverts payments and accelerates the foreclosure.**
>
> **The sale-leaseback with a repurchase option is equity stripping** — presented as a rescue, and
> frequently a transfer the owner cannot undo. California regulates foreclosure consulting and equity
> purchase specifically for this reason.
>
> **The referral fee is separately prohibited.** A licensee may not accept anything of value for
> directing settlement business, and paying for referrals is prohibited **whether or not it is
> disclosed.**
>
> **What the originator does: decline, escalate to the broker, and do not participate — however the
> arrangement is framed and however genuinely the borrower needs help.**

### Problem 6

A borrower asks what happens at a trustee's sale, whether the second he also owes disappears, whether
the buyer can change the locks on his tenant the same day, and whether his bankruptcy filing next week
"stops everything."

**Answer each.**

> **Solution.** **Four different bodies of law, and two require immediate escalation.**
>
> **The sale** is a public auction conducted by the trustee. The beneficiary may **credit bid** up to the
> amount owed; other bidders pay in the form required. **Title passes by trustee's deed**, and a buyer
> takes what the sale conveys — generally **without an ordinary transaction's protections** and subject
> to whatever senior interests survive.
>
> **The second is extinguished as a claim against the property** by a senior foreclosure — **but the
> underlying debt may survive as a personal obligation**, subject to California's deficiency
> restrictions. The junior lienholder has lost its security entirely, which is why juniors monitor senior
> loans and why a broker arranging a second should explain that exposure before the money is placed.
>
> **No, the buyer cannot change the locks.** Removing an occupant is a **legal process** with its own
> requirements, including **protections for tenants** in defined circumstances — and **self-help is
> prohibited** regardless of who now holds title.
>
> **The bankruptcy is the item requiring immediate escalation.** A filing generally triggers an
> **automatic stay** halting collection and foreclosure activity, and **continuing activity after a
> filing can carry serious consequences.** The analysis is entirely legal: **recognize and escalate
> immediately.**

### Problem 7

A borrower emails his originator that his servicer misapplied three payments and will not respond. The
originator calls the servicer on his behalf without authorization, tells the borrower the servicer is
"completely incompetent," promises to "get it fixed," and takes no notes.

**Identify every failure and state the correct handling.**

> **Solution.** **Four failures, and the first defeated the borrower's actual remedy.**
>
> **RESPA provides defined procedures** — a **notice of error** and a **request for information** — each
> carrying **acknowledgment and response obligations with deadlines.** Handling the concern as an
> unrecorded phone call **defeats the procedure that would have obligated the servicer to respond.** The
> borrower's remedy was a written submission, and nobody told him it existed.
>
> **Contacting the servicer without authority** is acting as though authorized when he is not.
>
> **"Completely incompetent" is criticism the borrower will repeat**, and it commits the firm to a
> characterization it cannot support.
>
> **"I'll get it fixed" is reassurance he cannot support** — the sentence that produces the complaint.
>
> **No notes means no record**, and most servicing disputes are about **what the borrower was told, by
> whom, and when.**
>
> **Correct handling:** answer promptly, because silence escalates; say plainly what he can and cannot do
> and why; give the **specific next step — the procedure, the party, the deadline**; put it in writing so
> the borrower has it after the call; and **record what was said and when.**

### Problem 8

A firm reviews its servicing complaints for a year: "my fixed payment went up," "my payoff was thousands
more than my balance," "nobody told me the balance was due at maturity," "my payment tripled after the
fifth year," "I was told I could just refinance," and "I didn't know I had to pay my own taxes." The
servicing manager says these are servicing problems.

**Assess, and state where each originated.**

> **Solution.** **They are not servicing problems. Every one is an origination failure arriving late.**
>
> **"My fixed payment went up"** — PITI was never explained, and in California the **reassessment** was
> never mentioned, so the escrow was underfunded from month one.
>
> **"My payoff was thousands more"** — accrued interest and the other amounts under the note were never
> explained. **Interest is paid in arrears and accrues to the day of payoff.**
>
> **"Nobody told me the balance was due at maturity"** — the balloon was presented without its number.
> The obligation was to state the amount due, when, and that the three paths all **depend on conditions
> at maturity.**
>
> **"My payment tripled after the fifth year"** — the **fully indexed rate** and the worst-case path were
> never shown. The fully indexed rate is the fact most often omitted, and it tells a borrower whether his
> start rate is a discount that expires even if markets never move.
>
> **"I was told I could just refinance"** — a **prediction stated as a plan**, and the single most
> damaging sentence in mortgage sales, because the borrower stops evaluating the risk.
>
> **"I didn't know I had to pay my own taxes"** — the escrow waiver's consequences were never explained,
> including that **failure to pay taxes or maintain insurance is a default even where payments are
> current** and that force-placed coverage is far more expensive and protects only the lender.
>
> **Every one is preventable in a conversation that costs nothing at application** — which is why this
> material sits in an origination course.

## Chapter summary

**Owner, servicer, and originator are three roles that can be three parties**, and a servicing transfer
requires notice, protects payments briefly, and **changes none of the note's terms.**

**Extra principal must be directed**, and a partial payment does not necessarily cure a delinquency.

**A fixed-rate loan has a fixed principal and interest payment, not a fixed total** — and in California
the reassessment problem makes a first-year escrow shortage close to predictable when the estimate used
the seller's bill.

**Explain PITI, the reassessment, the possibility of change, and the supplemental bill at application, in
writing.**

**A payoff exceeds the statement balance** because interest accrues to the payoff date, and a demand that
expires before disbursement is refreshed. **Payment does not clear the record; a reconveyance does.**

**Acceleration is what makes default consequential**, and the loss mitigation options each carry
something borrowers do not expect — forbearance is not forgiveness, a short sale needs every
lienholder, and a deed in lieu does not clear juniors.

**California servicer protections exist**; know that, and route the specifics to counsel and a
HUD-approved housing counselor **in writing and early.**

**Never predict approval, characterize credit consequences, advise on tax treatment, tell a borrower he
does or does not owe a deficiency, or tell him to stop paying.**

**Recognize the rescue fraud patterns and escalate rather than participate**, however the arrangement is
framed.

**Nonjudicial foreclosure trades the deficiency for speed.** **Reinstatement cures the arrearage;
redemption requires the accelerated balance** — and conflating them can cost someone their home.

**A senior foreclosure wipes out junior liens as claims against the property**, occupant removal is a
legal process, self-help is prohibited, and **a bankruptcy filing generally triggers an automatic stay
requiring immediate escalation.**

**Use the defined procedures** — a notice of error and a request for information have deadlines that
informal handling defeats.

**Answer promptly, say what you can and cannot do, give the specific next step, refer in writing, and
record it.**

**And the closing point: most servicing complaints are origination failures arriving late.**
