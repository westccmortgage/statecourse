## 1. California broker mortgage activities

### 1.1 What the license authorizes

A California real estate broker license authorizes, among other activities, **soliciting borrowers or lenders for, negotiating, arranging, and performing services in connection with loans secured by real property.**

**The activity is licensed; the title is not the test.** And a salesperson performs it **only for and under the responsible broker**, may accept compensation for it **only from that broker**, and **may not contract in her own name** for licensed activity.

### 1.2 Two distinct roles

A broker in mortgage practice can occupy either of two positions, and the compliance consequences differ.

| Role | What the broker does |
|---|---|
| **Agent for the borrower or lender** | Arranges a loan between them, acting for one or both |
| **Principal** | Makes or purchases a loan for the broker's own account, or sells one |

**Which role the broker occupies must be clear to the parties**, and a broker acting as a principal while a borrower believes he is being represented has a serious problem.

### 1.3 The mortgage loan disclosure statement

Where a broker negotiates a loan secured by real property in defined circumstances, California requires delivery to the borrower of a **mortgage loan disclosure statement** within a defined period.

**Its function:** to state the loan's costs, the broker's compensation, and other required particulars, **in a form the borrower receives from the broker** — separate from, and in addition to, the federal disclosures of Lesson 07.

**Two points a licensee must hold:**

**It is a California requirement with its own timing**, and satisfying a federal disclosure obligation does not satisfy it.

**The compensation disclosed must be accurate and complete**, including compensation from every source. Lesson 09 develops the compensation framework.

### 1.4 The broker's reporting obligations

California imposes reporting obligations on brokers engaged in mortgage activity, which vary with the nature and volume of the activity — including annual and, where thresholds are met, more frequent reporting of specified transactions.

**Whether a particular broker's activity triggers a particular report is a compliance determination**, and it depends on facts about the firm's business rather than on any individual file.

**An originator's contribution is accurate transaction records**, because the reports are compiled from them.

### 1.5 Threshold activity and multi-lender transactions

Where a broker's activity exceeds defined thresholds, or where a loan is arranged with **multiple lenders** on a single note, additional requirements attach — including requirements about how the transaction is structured, documented, and serviced.

**Multi-lender arrangements are technical and heavily regulated**, and Lesson 12 develops private money. The operative point here: **a broker assembling investors into a single loan is in a regulated structure**, not merely finding money for a borrower.

### 1.6 The broker-controlled escrow question

A broker may perform escrow services in a transaction in which the broker is otherwise involved, under an exemption with limits.

**Whether a particular arrangement fits an exemption is a legal and licensing question**, and it is not one an individual licensee resolves. **A broker performing escrow outside an applicable exemption is conducting escrow activity without authority.**

### 1.7 What the broker may not do

- **Act as a principal without disclosure** where the parties believe otherwise.
- **Accept compensation for licensed activity from anyone other than the responsible broker**, in the case of a salesperson.
- **Pay an unlicensed person** for producing loans — both an unlicensed activity problem and, where a settlement service referral is involved, a federal prohibition **disclosure does not cure**.
- **Collect an advance fee** outside the framework of Section 2.
- **Perform activity requiring a license the firm does not hold.**

### 1.8 Checkpoint

You should be able to distinguish the broker's two roles, state what the mortgage loan disclosure statement does and that it is separate from federal disclosures, and name what a broker may not do.

## 2. Advance fees and compensation

### 2.1 What an advance fee is

> **An advance fee is a fee collected before the services for which it is charged have been rendered.**

The concept is simple and the compliance treatment is not, because **California regulates advance fees specifically**, including requirements about the agreement's form, the handling of the funds, and accounting to the person who paid.

### 2.2 Why the framework exists

The consumer protection logic is direct: **a fee collected in advance creates an incentive to collect and not perform**, and a consumer who has already paid has lost his leverage.

The historical abuses the framework addresses — collecting fees for loans never arranged, for services never performed, from consumers least able to absorb the loss — are exactly the pattern the rules were written against.

### 2.3 The operative requirements, in outline

Where a broker collects an advance fee:

| Requirement | In outline |
|---|---|
| **The agreement** | Must meet form requirements, and materials used may require submission to the Department in advance |
| **The funds** | Are **trust funds** and must be handled as such — Section 3 |
| **Accounting** | A verified accounting must be provided to the person who paid, at defined intervals and on demand |
| **Use** | Funds may be applied only as the framework permits, and only as earned |

> **Advance fee funds are trust funds from the moment received.** A broker who deposits them into the operating account has commingled, whatever the agreement says.

### 2.4 What is and is not an advance fee

The determination turns on **when the fee is collected relative to the services**, not on what it is called.

| Charge | Analysis |
|---|---|
| A fee collected at application for arranging a loan not yet arranged | **Advance fee** analysis applies |
| A third-party cost collected to pay an appraiser or credit vendor | Generally a pass-through, and it must **actually be paid** to the vendor |
| A fee collected at and paid from closing | Not collected in advance |
| A "processing fee" collected up front | **Advance fee** analysis applies |
| A non-refundable "commitment fee" collected up front | **Advance fee** analysis applies, and the label does not change it |

**Two disciplines:**

**Calling a fee non-refundable does not remove it from the framework**, and a broker who believes a label resolves the question has not read the framework.

**Third-party costs collected must be used for the stated purpose.** Collecting an appraisal fee and not ordering the appraisal, or keeping the difference between what was collected and what the vendor charged, is a separate and serious problem.

### 2.5 Compensation generally

A broker's compensation in a mortgage transaction can come from the borrower, from the lender, or from both where permitted — and **the arrangement is constrained by both California and federal requirements.**

**Three constraints a licensee must know exist:**

**Federal loan originator compensation rules** limit how compensation may be structured — notably restricting compensation based on a transaction's terms, and restricting receipt of compensation from both the consumer and another party on the same transaction. Lesson 09 develops it.

**Points and fees limits** in the qualified mortgage and high-cost frameworks **can count broker compensation**, so a compensation arrangement can move a loan into a category with requirements the firm may not be prepared to meet.

**Disclosure requirements** attach under both frameworks, and the amounts disclosed must reconcile to what is actually received.

### 2.6 The salesperson's compensation

Restated because it is a recurring finding: **a salesperson may accept compensation for licensed activity only from the responsible broker.**

A salesperson paid directly by a lender, by a borrower, or by a third party has a violation regardless of whether the amount was correct and regardless of anyone's intention.

### 2.7 What must never happen with fees

- **Collecting a fee for a service not performed.**
- **Collecting a third-party cost and not paying it to the third party.**
- **Marking up a third-party cost** and presenting the marked-up figure as the third party's charge.
- **Collecting an advance fee outside the framework.**
- **Structuring a payment to avoid a limit** — which is a compliance question for counsel, and a pattern an examiner recognizes.
- **Disclosing one compensation figure and receiving another.**

### 2.8 Checkpoint

You should be able to define an advance fee, explain why the framework exists, identify what a label does not change, and state where a salesperson's compensation may come from.

## 3. Trust fund handling

### 3.1 What a trust fund is

> **Money received in the course of licensed activity that belongs to someone else is a trust fund.**

In mortgage practice that includes: **advance fees**, funds collected for third-party services, **loan payments** collected where the broker services or collects, **investor funds** in a broker-arranged loan, impound or reserve funds held, and any borrower funds held for any purpose.

### 3.2 The three obligations

1. **Separation.** Trust funds go into a **trust account or neutral depository**, kept separate from the broker's own funds.
2. **Records.** A complete accounting of every receipt and disbursement, **identifying whose funds they are.**
3. **Timeliness.** Funds are placed as required, without being held personally in the interim.

**A check held in a desk drawer over a weekend is a trust fund handling problem**, whatever the intention — and the requirement runs to the broker.

### 3.3 Commingling and conversion

**Commingling** is mixing trust funds with the broker's own funds — including depositing them into the wrong account.

**Conversion** is using trust funds for a purpose other than the one for which they are held. **It is the more serious offense and is treated as such.**

> **Commingling is complete when the funds are deposited in the wrong account, regardless of how carefully the total is tracked and regardless of whether anyone loses money.**

### 3.4 Why an internal ledger does not cure commingling

The most common defense a licensee offers is that funds in the operating account are tracked separately in the accounting system. **It does not work, for a structural reason worth teaching:**

> **Funds in a broker's operating account are exposed to the broker's creditors, to its operating losses, and to ordinary business risk. A ledger entry does not protect the money. Separation is what protects it, and the record is what proves the separation was maintained.**

### 3.5 Shortages and overages

| Finding | What it means | Correct response |
|---|---|---|
| **Shortage** | Someone else's money is missing | Cure **immediately** from the broker's own funds; document the cause; reconcile |
| **Overage** | The records do not accurately reflect whose funds are held | Investigate and document the source; **the broker may not simply keep it** |

**Neither is a rounding matter.** There is **no tolerance threshold**, and neither may be deferred to the next reconciliation.

> A processor discovers she deposited a borrower's appraisal fee into the operating account by mistake. **The correct response is to correct it immediately, document the error and the correction, and notify the broker** so the trust records reconcile and the cause is addressed.
>
> **Correcting it quietly without a record is its own problem: trust accounting is judged by the record as much as by the balance.**

### 3.6 Reconciliation

The trust account is reconciled on a regular cycle, comparing **three** balances:

1. **The bank balance.**
2. **The book balance.**
3. **The total of the individual beneficiary ledgers.**

**All three must agree.**

> **A reconciliation that balances bank against book but never against the beneficiary ledgers will not detect the error that matters most** — money in the account belonging to a different beneficiary than the records say. **The total is right the whole time**, which is exactly why it goes unnoticed.

### 3.7 Signatory authority

Where an unlicensed person is authorized to draw on a trust account, additional requirements attach — including bonding requirements in defined circumstances.

**A licensee should know this exists** and should not assume that giving an office manager check-signing authority is an administrative decision.

### 3.8 Investor funds

Where a broker arranges a loan funded by private investors, **the investors' funds are trust funds** from receipt until properly disbursed.

**Three requirements a licensee must recognize:** the funds are separated and accounted for by beneficiary; disbursement follows the arrangement's terms and the investors' instructions; and **funds held pending a transaction that does not close must be returned** rather than applied elsewhere.

**Using one investor's funds to complete another transaction is conversion**, however temporary and however well intentioned.

### 3.9 Checkpoint

You should be able to identify trust funds in mortgage practice, explain why a ledger does not cure commingling, name the three balances a reconciliation must agree, and state the correct response to a shortage and an overage.

## 4. Broker records and retention

### 4.1 The obligation

**A broker must retain records of the transactions the license authorizes**, for the required period, available for inspection.

**The records belong to the brokerage**, and the responsible broker answers for them **whoever created them.**

### 4.2 What a mortgage file must contain

| Category | Contents |
|---|---|
| **The application** | As taken, with the date, and every subsequent version |
| **Borrower documentation** | Income, assets, credit authorization, identity, explanations |
| **Disclosures** | Federal and California, with **evidence of what was delivered, to whom, when, and by what method** |
| **The mortgage loan disclosure statement** | With its delivery evidence |
| **Third-party reports** | Credit, appraisal, title, insurance, flood determination |
| **Underwriting** | Findings, conditions, condition responses, the decision |
| **Compensation** | The arrangement, the amounts disclosed, and the amounts received |
| **Communications** | Every communication material to a term or a decision |
| **Closing documents** | Note, security instrument, closing statement, funding evidence |
| **Trust fund records** | Receipts, disbursements, beneficiary ledgers, reconciliations |
| **Advertising** | What ran, where, when, and with whose approval |
| **Corrections** | With the originals retained |
| **Disposition** | The outcome, its date, and any notice with delivery evidence |

### 4.3 Retention

Records are retained for the required period, **which runs from the transaction rather than from convenience**, and different frameworks impose their own periods — California's, and the federal periods under Regulations Z, X, B, C, and V.

**Two disciplines:**

> **Where periods differ, the longest governs the record's fate in practice.** A firm that discards on the shortest applicable period will eventually discard something another framework still required.

> **Retention extends where a complaint has been made or a proceeding is pending.** "We closed that file" is not a reason to discard it, and discarding after notice of a complaint is a different and much more serious problem.

### 4.4 Denied, withdrawn, and failed files

**These are retained too**, and they are disproportionately the files that later produce disputes and examination findings.

> **A denied file needs a complete record as much as a funded one** — arguably more, because a denial is the disposition an applicant is most likely to question, and because the adverse action content and the disposition coding are both examinable from it.

**An application quietly abandoned and never dispositioned is a file with a gap in it**, and it is exactly the kind an examiner samples.

### 4.5 Electronic records

Electronic records carry the same integrity requirements as paper.

**What that means operationally:** the record must be **retrievable** for the required period in a usable form; the **version history** must be preserved so a correction does not erase what it corrected; **access must be controlled**; and **the system must not permit silent alteration.**

> **A record that can be silently altered is not a record.**

### 4.6 Corrections

Where an error is discovered, it is **corrected immediately, in writing, to everyone who received the original**, and before anyone acts on it if that is still possible.

**Corrections are made visibly**, with the original preserved and the change attributable — **who changed what, when, and why.**

**The instinct to wait and see whether an error matters is the instinct that converts a mistake into a misrepresentation.**

### 4.7 What must never happen to a record

- **Creating a document after the fact and dating it to match.**
- **Altering an executed document.**
- **Deleting a record that reflects badly.**
- **Removing surrounding documents so an inconsistency does not show.**
- **Signing on a borrower's behalf.**

> **A documented gap is a compliance problem a firm can survive. A manufactured or destroyed record is a question about integrity, and it ends careers.**

### 4.8 Departing licensees

**A licensee leaving a firm may not unilaterally take files**, redacted or not. Records belong to the brokerage, and what may be taken is governed by firm policy and by client direction.

**That question should be settled in writing in the office policy long before anyone departs**, because a firm without a written answer will discover the issue during an argument.

### 4.9 Checkpoint

You should be able to list what a mortgage file must contain, state how retention periods interact, explain why denied files are retained, and state what must never happen to a record.

## 5. Advertising and license identification

### 5.1 Why mortgage advertising carries more identification

A compliant mortgage advertisement carries **more identification than a compliant real estate sales advertisement**, because two frameworks apply at once:

**California licensing** requires the licensee's identification, including license identification, and identification of the brokerage.

**The SAFE Act framework** requires the **individual's unique identifier** and the **company's identifier** as required.

**A social post promoting a loan program that names only the originator is deficient under both.**

### 5.2 The federal advertising constraints

**Regulation Z trigger terms.** Where an advertisement states certain specific terms — a payment amount, a term, a rate expressed in a particular way — **additional disclosures are required.** An advertisement can trigger obligations its author did not intend.

**The general standard: an advertisement may not be misleading**, including **by prominence.** A teaser rate displayed large with the qualifying terms in small type is misleading even where every number is accurate.

**Specific prohibitions** reach misleading claims about rates, terms, government affiliation, and comparisons — and an advertisement implying an affiliation with a government agency where none exists is a distinct and serious problem.

### 5.3 What mortgage advertising must not do

| Practice | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| Quote a rate without the terms that produce it | Misleading; may trigger additional disclosure |
| Display a teaser payment prominently with the real payment buried | Misleading by prominence |
| Imply government affiliation or endorsement | A distinct prohibition |
| State or imply approval before underwriting | Misrepresents the file's status |
| Advertise terms not actually available | Bait, and unsupportable |
| Omit identifiers | Deficient under both frameworks |
| Make production or ranking claims without support | Must be supportable, with the basis stated |
| Compare against a competitor on incomplete terms | Misleading even where each number is accurate |

### 5.4 Every medium is advertising

**Social media, text campaigns, email sequences, videos, webinars, open house flyers, business cards, signage, and a personal profile that promotes loan products are all advertising.**

Three consequences:

**Content is fragmentary.** A post, a story, a short video — **each is an advertisement in itself and each needs identification.** Identifying the profile is not identifying the advertisement.

**Content is durable and shareable.** A post remains discoverable after a rate has changed, and reshared content carries claims into a context where they are no longer true.

**Automated sequences run unattended.** A campaign that continues after a consumer opts out is a violation that operates without anyone deciding — which is why sequences need periodic review rather than one-time setup.

### 5.5 Solicitation and consent

Marketing that arrives in a consumer's inbox or phone carries **consent obligations** defined by what the consumer was told when the information was collected — and **an opt-out mechanism is necessary but does not create the consent needed to send the first message.**

**Telephone solicitation carries its own obligations**, including do-not-call requirements that bind the caller. **A lead vendor's assurance that a list is clean does not transfer the obligation.**

### 5.6 Approval and archive

**Pre-publication approval** is how a broker exercises accountability for advertising rather than discovering problems afterward.

A working process has: a **named approver** and a backup; a **defined turnaround** short enough that licensees do not route around it — because most non-compliance with approval processes is caused by the process being slow; a **checklist** covering identifiers, rate and term claims, prominence, and required disclosures; and a **record of the approval.**

**The archive must hold what actually ran** — the creative itself, where and when it ran, who approved it, **and for digital campaigns the audience targeting** — because an advertisement that cannot be produced cannot be reviewed.

> **Ephemeral formats need deliberate capture.** A printed flyer survives in a file; a story post that expired in 24 hours does not, unless someone captured it.

### 5.7 Corrections and takedowns

When advertising is found inaccurate:

1. **Correct or remove it promptly**, across every platform where it appeared — which requires knowing where it appeared, which requires the archive.
2. **Record what was wrong, when it was corrected, and by whom.**
3. **Address the process gap**, not only the instance.

**What is not on the list: quietly deleting the post and moving on.** Silent removal leaves no record that the problem existed, no evidence it was addressed, and no correction of the process that produced it.

### 5.8 Checkpoint

You should be able to state what identification a mortgage advertisement requires and why, name the practices that make an advertisement misleading, and describe an approval and archive process.

## 6. DRE audit and enforcement risk

### 6.1 How an examination proceeds

An examination generally involves notice, a request for records, an on-site or remote review, an exit discussion, and a written report of findings — followed by the firm's response and, where warranted, further action.

**What is examined:** trust fund records and reconciliations, transaction files, disclosures and their delivery evidence, compensation records, advertising and its approval, licensing and endorsement status, supervision, and the firm's policies against its actual practice.

### 6.2 What findings look like

Findings are usually not dramatic. **They are the ordinary failures repeated across files:**

| Finding | What produced it |
|---|---|
| Trust account not reconciled to beneficiary ledgers | Bank-to-book reconciliation only |
| Commingled funds | Deposits to the wrong account |
| Shortage not cured | Deferred to the next cycle |
| Missing delivery evidence for disclosures | Nobody owned the tracking |
| Mortgage loan disclosure statement missing or late | Treated as satisfied by the federal disclosure |
| Advertising without identifiers | No pre-publication approval |
| No advertising archive | Ephemeral content never captured |
| Unlicensed or lapsed-endorsement activity | No renewal tracking |
| Compensation received not matching compensation disclosed | No reconciliation between the two |
| Applications never dispositioned | "Stopped responding" treated as an outcome |
| Supervision policy not followed in practice | A manual rather than a system |

> **Notice what these have in common: none requires anyone to have intended anything.** They are process failures, and **a process failure repeats on every file until someone notices** — which is why a single examination can produce a finding across dozens of transactions.

### 6.3 The one that is different

**Falsification.** Creating a document after the fact and dating it to match, altering an executed document, deleting records that reflect badly, or removing surrounding documents so an inconsistency does not show.

> **Everything else in this lesson is survivable — findings, corrective action, even discipline — provided the conduct in response was honest.**
>
> **A documented gap is a compliance problem. A manufactured document is a question about integrity, and it converts a defensible situation into one that ends a career.**

### 6.4 When a regulator makes contact

**Notify the broker immediately. Preserve records. Do not respond independently or informally.**

**Cooperation is expected.** **Improvised** cooperation without review is where licensees make statements they cannot support — and in a mortgage file, a statement about what was disclosed and when **can be checked against the file within minutes.**

### 6.5 Responding to findings

**Respond factually and completely**, within the time allowed.

**Correct the instance and the process.** A response that fixes twelve files and leaves the process unchanged produces the same finding next time, and a regulator reading the second report sees a firm that did not learn.

**Document the corrective action** — what was changed, when, by whom, and how compliance will be monitored.

**Do not minimize.** A response characterizing a trust fund shortage as a clerical matter has told the examiner something about the firm's understanding.

### 6.6 What actually prevents findings

The controls that pay for themselves, in order of how much they prevent:

1. **Monthly three-way trust reconciliation**, bank to book to beneficiary ledgers.
2. **Disclosure delivery tracking** — what, to whom, when, by what method, with evidence.
3. **Pre-publication advertising approval and an archive**, including targeting.
4. **Licensing and endorsement renewal tracking**, with a calendar that does not depend on anyone remembering.
5. **Compensation reconciliation** — disclosed against received, on every file.
6. **Disposition of every application**, coded as what actually happened.
7. **File review close enough in time** to catch a problem before the loan closes.
8. **A usable escalation path**, so a problem raised is a problem addressed.

### 6.7 What to report to the broker, promptly

Any error affecting a borrower's decision or a loan's terms; any borrower dissatisfaction that could become a complaint; any allegation of misrepresentation or discrimination; any contact from counsel or a regulator; any suspected fraud; **any wire fraud incident or near-miss**; any trust fund discrepancy of any size; any request to alter, backdate, or omit anything; and any instruction declined.

**The bias should be toward reporting.** A report that turns out unnecessary costs a conversation. **An unreported matter that becomes a finding costs the credibility of everything else the firm says.**

### 6.8 The governing test

> **Reconstructability: could a person who was not there determine what happened, when, and on what basis?**

**A file that passes that test survives an examination even where it contains a problem. A file that does not fails even where it contains none**, because nobody can show what happened.

### 6.9 Checkpoint

You should be able to describe what an examination reviews, name the findings that recur and what produces them, state the correct response to regulator contact, and list the controls that prevent findings.

## Practice problems

### Problem 1

A brokerage collects a $795 "non-refundable processing fee" at application, deposits it into the
operating account, and applies it to overhead. The owner says it is not an advance fee because it is
labeled non-refundable and because the firm "always does the processing eventually."

**Assess every element.**

> **Solution.** **It is an advance fee, it was commingled, and the label changed nothing.**
>
> **An advance fee is a fee collected before the services for which it is charged have been rendered.**
> The determination turns on **when the fee is collected relative to the services**, not on what it is
> called — so a "processing fee" or a "non-refundable commitment fee" collected up front is squarely
> within the framework. **A broker who believes a label resolves the question has not read the
> framework.**
>
> **The framework's requirements are specific:** the agreement must meet form requirements and the
> materials may require advance submission to the Department; **the funds are trust funds**; a **verified
> accounting** must be provided to the person who paid at defined intervals and on demand; and the funds
> may be applied **only as earned**.
>
> **Depositing to the operating account is commingling, complete on deposit** — and **applying the money
> to overhead before the services were rendered is conversion**, which is the more serious offense.
>
> **Why the framework exists is worth saying to the owner:** a fee collected in advance creates an
> incentive to collect and not perform, and a consumer who has already paid has lost his leverage.

### Problem 2

A firm collects $650 from each borrower for the appraisal. The appraiser charges $525. The firm keeps
the difference as "coordination." On three files the appraisal was never ordered and the fee was not
refunded. A salesperson also accepted a $500 payment directly from a lender for a completed loan.

**Identify each violation.**

> **Solution.** **Three distinct violations, and the third is a recurring examination finding.**
>
> **The markup, presented as the appraiser's charge, is a separate problem.** A pass-through cost is
> collected to pay a vendor and **must actually be paid to the vendor** — marking it up while presenting
> the marked-up figure as the third party's charge misrepresents the charge to the borrower. If the firm
> charges for coordination, it charges for coordination and discloses it as its own fee.
>
> **Collecting the fee and not ordering the appraisal is collecting a fee for a service not performed**,
> and not refunding it compounds it. Those funds were trust funds and remain so.
>
> **The salesperson's $500 is a violation regardless of amount or intention.** **A salesperson may accept
> compensation for licensed activity only from the responsible broker.** Paid directly by a lender, a
> borrower, or a third party, it is a violation on its face — and it is one of the findings that appears
> in DRE files most often because it feels like an ordinary bonus to everyone involved.

### Problem 3

A firm's monthly trust reconciliation has matched bank to book to the penny for fourteen months. An
examination finds a borrower's $1,400 deposit credited to a different borrower's ledger, a $340 overage
the broker had been treating as a fee, and a $900 shortage the bookkeeper planned to cure "at the next
cycle."

**Explain what the reconciliation missed and state the correct response to each finding.**

> **Solution.** **The reconciliation missed the third balance.** It must compare **the bank balance, the
> book balance, and the total of the individual beneficiary ledgers** — all three agreeing.
>
> **Bank-to-book alone cannot detect money held for the wrong beneficiary**, because **the total is right
> the whole time.** That is exactly why it went unnoticed for fourteen months, and why this is the single
> most valuable control in a mortgage trust practice.
>
> **The overage may not be kept.** An overage means the records do not accurately reflect whose funds are
> held; it is **investigated and documented**, and treating it as a fee is conversion of someone's money.
>
> **The shortage is cured immediately from the broker's own funds**, with the cause documented and the
> account reconciled. **There is no tolerance threshold and it may not be deferred to the next cycle** —
> someone else's money is missing now.
>
> **On the misapplied deposit:** correct it immediately, **document the error and the correction**, and
> notify the broker. Correcting it quietly without a record is its own problem — **trust accounting is
> judged by the record as much as by the balance.**

### Problem 4

A broker arranging a private money loan receives $340,000 from four investors. The transaction stalls.
The broker uses $80,000 of it to complete a different transaction that is closing that week, intending
to replace it from the next funding. He deposits all investor funds into an account that also holds the
firm's operating balance, tracked by a spreadsheet.

**Assess.**

> **Solution.** **Both acts are violations, and the first is the more serious by a wide margin.**
>
> **Using one investor's funds to complete another transaction is conversion** — using trust funds for a
> purpose other than the one for which they are held. **However temporary and however well intentioned**,
> and regardless of whether the money is replaced. Intent to replace is not a defense; it is a
> description of the plan.
>
> **The deposit arrangement is commingling, complete on deposit.** And the spreadsheet defense fails for
> the structural reason: **funds in an operating account are exposed to the broker's creditors, operating
> losses, and ordinary business risk. A ledger entry does not protect the money — separation does**, and
> the record proves the separation was maintained.
>
> **Investor funds are trust funds from receipt until properly disbursed**, separated and accounted for
> by beneficiary, disbursed per the arrangement's terms and the investors' instructions — and **funds
> held pending a transaction that does not close must be returned**, not applied elsewhere.
>
> Note also that a broker assembling investors into a single loan is in a **regulated multi-lender
> structure**, not merely finding money for a borrower.

### Problem 5

A firm discards loan files three years after closing, on the shortest period it could find. It also
discarded a denied file six weeks after a borrower's attorney sent a letter alleging discrimination,
because "that one never funded." Its electronic system overwrites documents on correction without
retaining prior versions.

**Assess each practice.**

> **Solution.** **Three failures, and the second is categorically worse.**
>
> **Retention periods interact.** California imposes its own, and the federal frameworks — Regulations Z,
> X, B, C, and V — impose theirs. **Where periods differ, the longest governs the record's fate in
> practice**, and a firm discarding on the shortest applicable period **will eventually discard something
> another framework still required.**
>
> **Discarding after notice of a complaint is a different and much more serious problem.** Retention
> **extends** where a complaint has been made or a proceeding is pending, and "that one never funded" is
> no answer — **a denied file needs a complete record as much as a funded one**, arguably more, because a
> denial is the disposition most likely to be questioned and because the adverse action content and the
> disposition coding are both examinable from it. This now looks like destruction after notice.
>
> **Overwriting on correction defeats electronic record integrity.** Records must be **retrievable**,
> **version-preserving** so a correction does not erase what it corrected, **access-controlled**, and not
> silently alterable. **A record that can be silently altered is not a record** — and corrections are made
> **visibly**, with the original preserved and the change attributable.

### Problem 6

An originator posts a story to her personal Instagram: "5.5% — call me!" The post carries her name and
photo. Her brokerage is named in her profile bio; her NMLS identifier appears nowhere. The story expires
after 24 hours and is not captured. She also texts the rate to 900 numbers from a purchased list whose
vendor assured her it was "scrubbed."

**Identify every defect.**

> **Solution.** **Six defects across two frameworks and a consent problem.**
>
> **Identification fails under both frameworks.** California requires the licensee's identification
> including license identification and identification of the brokerage; the SAFE Act framework requires
> the **individual's unique identifier and the company's identifier** as required. **Identifying the
> profile is not identifying the advertisement** — each post and each story is an advertisement in
> itself.
>
> **The rate quote likely triggers additional disclosure.** Where an advertisement states certain
> specific terms, **additional disclosures are required**, and "5.5%" with none of the terms that produce
> it is also **misleading** on the general standard.
>
> **A personal account posting loan products is advertising**, whatever she considers it.
>
> **There is no archive.** An advertisement that cannot be produced cannot be reviewed, and **ephemeral
> formats need deliberate capture** — a story that expired in 24 hours does not survive unless someone
> captured it. The archive must hold what ran, where, when, with whose approval, **and the targeting.**
>
> **The text campaign has a consent problem.** Consent is defined by **what the consumer was told when
> the information was collected**, an opt-out is necessary but does not create it, and telephone
> solicitation carries its own do-not-call obligations that **bind the caller** — **a lead vendor's
> assurance does not transfer the obligation.**

### Problem 7

An examination report lists: trust account never reconciled to beneficiary ledgers; nineteen files
missing delivery evidence for the mortgage loan disclosure statement; advertising without identifiers
across two quarters; four applications never dispositioned; and compensation received exceeding
compensation disclosed on six files. The broker responds that no borrower complained, corrects the
nineteen files, and characterizes the trust finding as "a bookkeeping preference."

**Assess the findings and the response.**

> **Solution.** **Every finding is a process failure, and none required anyone to intend anything** —
> which is the point. **A process failure repeats on every file until someone notices**, which is why one
> examination produces findings across dozens of transactions.
>
> **The mortgage loan disclosure statement is a California requirement with its own timing**, and firms
> most often miss it by treating the federal disclosure as satisfying it. It does not.
>
> **Compensation received exceeding compensation disclosed** is the finding with the widest
> consequences — it reaches the California disclosure, the federal framework, and potentially the
> points-and-fees calculation.
>
> **"No borrower complained" is not a defense.** Complaints are one route; **examination is another, and
> it does not require anyone to be dissatisfied.**
>
> **Correcting nineteen files and leaving the process unchanged produces the same finding next time** —
> and a regulator reading the second report sees a firm that did not learn. **Correct the instance and
> the process**, and document what was changed, when, by whom, and how it will be monitored.
>
> **Characterizing the trust finding as a bookkeeping preference has told the examiner something about
> the firm's understanding.** Three-way reconciliation is not a preference, and minimizing a trust
> finding is among the worst available responses.

### Problem 8

The day before an examination begins, a manager tells a processor to print and backdate two missing
disclosure acknowledgments, delete an email thread in which an originator promised a rate the firm did
not honor, and "tidy up" a file by removing a superseded application version that conflicts with the
final one. He says nothing was really wrong on any of the files.

**Respond, and state what the firm should do instead.**

> **Solution.** **Refuse all three, do not participate, and escalate above the manager immediately.**
>
> **This is falsification**, and it is the one category in this lesson that is not survivable. Creating a
> document after the fact and dating it to match, deleting a record that reflects badly, and removing
> surrounding documents so an inconsistency does not show are each independently disqualifying.
>
> > **Everything else here is survivable — findings, corrective action, even discipline — provided the
> > conduct in response was honest. A documented gap is a compliance problem. A manufactured document is
> > a question about integrity, and it converts a defensible situation into one that ends a career.**
>
> **"Nothing was really wrong" makes it worse, not better.** If the files would have withstood review, the
> firm has now created the only problem in them. And the superseded version is exactly what a proper
> record keeps — **version history must be preserved so a correction does not erase what it corrected.**
>
> **What the firm should do instead:** produce the records as they are; **respond factually and
> completely** within the time allowed; correct the instance **and the process**; and document the
> corrective action.
>
> **On regulator contact generally: notify the broker, preserve records, and do not respond independently
> or informally.** Cooperation is expected; improvised cooperation is where people make statements they
> cannot support — and in a mortgage file, **what was disclosed and when can be checked within minutes.**

## Chapter summary

**A broker may act as agent or as principal in mortgage activity**, and which role applies must be clear
to the parties.

**The mortgage loan disclosure statement is a California requirement with its own timing**, separate from
and additional to the federal disclosures — and the compensation stated must be accurate and complete.

**An advance fee is one collected before the services are rendered**, and the label does not decide it.
The funds are trust funds, an accounting is owed, and the framework exists because prepayment removes a
consumer's leverage.

**Pass-through costs must actually be paid to the vendor**, and a markup presented as the vendor's charge
is a separate problem.

**A salesperson may accept compensation for licensed activity only from the responsible broker.**

**Trust funds require separation, records identifying the beneficiary, and timeliness.** Commingling is
complete on deposit, conversion is worse, and **a ledger does not cure commingling because a ledger does
not shield money from the broker's creditors — separation does.**

**Shortages are cured immediately; overages are never kept.** There is no tolerance threshold.

**Reconcile three balances**, because bank-to-book alone misses money held for the wrong beneficiary.

**Investor funds are trust funds, and borrowing from one transaction to complete another is conversion**
however temporary.

**Records belong to the brokerage, the longest applicable retention period governs, retention extends on
notice of a complaint, and denied and abandoned files are retained** — because "stopped responding" is
not a disposition.

**Electronic records must be retrievable, version-preserving, access-controlled, and not silently
alterable.**

**Mortgage advertising carries dual identification**, must not mislead including by prominence, and
reaches every medium — with each post an advertisement, sequences running unattended, consent defined at
collection, and an archive that captures ephemeral formats and targeting.

**Examination findings are ordinary process failures repeated across files**, and "nobody complained" is
not a defense. **Correct the instance and the process.**

**Falsification is the one thing that is not survivable**, and the governing test remains
**reconstructability**.
