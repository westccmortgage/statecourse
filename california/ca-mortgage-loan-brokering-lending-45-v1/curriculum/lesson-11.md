## 1. Appraisal independence

### 1.1 Why independence exists

An appraisal exists to measure the lender's collateral risk. **A value that moves because someone needed it to move measures nothing**, and the transaction, the lender, and any investor who buys the loan all rely on a number that is no longer an opinion.

**Independence requirements exist to keep the people with an interest in the outcome away from the person forming the opinion.**

### 1.2 The core rule

**A licensee may provide genuinely relevant information through the proper channel.** A licensee **may not communicate a target.**

| Permitted — information | Prohibited — a target |
|---|---|
| Sales the licensee believes relevant, with data | "We need $920,000" |
| Information about condition, permitted improvements, and features | "The deal dies without another $25,000" |
| A copy of the contract | "Can you take another look?" with a number attached |
| Factual corrections — a miscounted bedroom, an omitted addition | Any statement of the value required |

> **The distinction is between supplying information and supplying a target** — and a request to reconsider that arrives with a number attached is the pattern to refuse, **including when it comes from your own borrower, your own agent, or your own manager.**

### 1.3 Who may order and communicate

Independence requirements govern **who may select, order, and communicate with the appraiser**, and firms implement them through defined channels precisely so that **individual originators are not the point of contact.**

**Common structures** include an appraisal management company or an internal function separated from production. **The separation is the control**, and routing around it — by calling the appraiser, by suggesting an appraiser, or by having someone else make the call — defeats it.

### 1.4 Coercion and its softer forms

The prohibition reaches more than an explicit demand.

**Also prohibited in substance:** conditioning payment on a value; withholding payment for a value the requester dislikes; implying future assignments depend on the outcome; providing an anticipated or target value with the order; and selecting an appraiser because of a history of favorable values.

**The last one is the subtlest and the most common.** A firm that steers work toward appraisers who "understand the market" has built coercion into its ordering pattern without anyone saying anything prohibited.

### 1.5 The borrower's copy

The borrower is entitled to receive copies of appraisals and other written valuations developed in connection with the application — **promptly upon completion or a defined period before consummation, whichever is earlier** — with notice of the right early in the process.

**Delivery is evidenced.** A file that cannot show when the valuation reached the borrower has a gap exactly where an examiner looks.

### 1.6 Appraisal bias

Valuation is a judgment, and **judgment is where bias operates.** Concerns about appraisal bias — including in the treatment of neighborhoods and of the people occupying properties — are a recognized issue receiving increased federal and California attention.

**The structural control is objective, market-derived support.** A derived adjustment can be examined; an impression cannot. Comparable selection, adjustment derivation, and neighborhood description are each places where a stated basis constrains discretion.

**Where a licensee believes an appraisal reflects bias:** escalate to the broker and follow the lender's **reconsideration** process. **Do not confront the appraiser**, and **do not advise the borrower that he has a claim** — that is a legal conclusion. Lesson 14 develops the framework.

### 1.7 What a licensee never does

- **Order or select the appraiser** where the firm's structure assigns that elsewhere.
- **Contact the appraiser directly** where the channel is defined.
- **Send a target**, in any form.
- **Suggest which comparables to use** as a way of steering the conclusion.
- **Tell a borrower what the value "should" come in at.**

### 1.8 Checkpoint

You should be able to state the information-versus-target distinction, name the softer forms of coercion, and state what a licensee does when an appraisal appears biased.

## 2. Valuation review

### 2.1 What the lender does with the report

Lenders review appraisals for **completeness, support, and reasonableness** — through desk or field review, automated tools, or a second appraisal where required.

**A review that questions an appraisal is not an accusation.** It is the lender testing whether the collateral conclusion is supported — the same discipline the appraisal itself applies to comparable selection.

### 2.2 Reading a report critically

An originator is not an appraiser and does not re-value the property. **What an originator can do is read for factual accuracy and internal consistency**, which is a different and legitimate exercise.

| What to check | Why |
|---|---|
| **Property facts** | Bedroom and bath count, square footage, lot size, garage, year built |
| **Permitted additions** | An omitted permitted addition is a factual gap |
| **Condition description** | Against what the file and the borrower say |
| **Occupancy noted** | Against the file's representation |
| **Comparable dates and distances** | Whether they are recent and within a defensible market area |
| **Adjustment direction** | Adjustments are made **to the comparable, never the subject** |
| **Net versus gross adjustment** | A small net can conceal a large gross, and reliability tracks gross |
| **Whether the conclusion follows** | Whether the reconciliation explains the weighting |

### 2.3 The direction of adjustment

Worth stating because it is the mechanic most often misread:

> **Adjustments are made to the comparable, never to the subject.** If the comparable is **superior**, its price is adjusted **downward**; if **inferior**, **upward.**
>
> **Comparable better, subtract.**

An originator who sees adjustments running the other way is looking at either an unusual presentation or an error, and it is worth asking through the proper channel.

### 2.4 What a low value means

> **Lenders lend against the lower of the appraised value and the purchase price.**

On a **refinance** there is no purchase price, so **value is the appraised value** — which is why a refinance is more exposed to the appraisal outcome and why **cash-out availability is not knowable until the appraisal arrives.**

**The borrower's options are practical rather than automatic:** bring additional funds, renegotiate the price, negotiate another resolution, or cancel if a contingency permits and its procedure is followed.

**What does not happen automatically:** the price does not adjust to the appraised value by operation of any program, **the loan is not denied**, and **no one is entitled to a second appraisal as of right.**

**Where an appraisal contingency has been removed**, the practical options remain but the contractual rights are limited — and **whether the borrower may cancel and what happens to a deposit is a legal question** for counsel.

### 2.5 The reconsideration of value

**Done correctly:**

- **Through the lender's defined channel**, never by contacting the appraiser.
- **With relevant sales the appraiser may not have considered**, identified with data rather than asserted.
- **With factual corrections** — a miscounted room, an omitted permitted addition, a misdescribed condition.
- **Without a target number.**

**What is not a reconsideration:** an assertion that the value is wrong because the contract price is higher, or because the transaction fails without a different number.

### 2.6 Property condition findings

Appraisals frequently identify condition items, and program requirements can make some of them **conditions to funding** — health and safety items, a failing roof, missing systems, or incomplete construction.

**Two practical consequences:**

**A property with deferred maintenance may not satisfy a program's requirements**, which is a **closing-probability** fact rather than a preference — and never translated into a preference against buyers using a program.

**Repairs and reinspection take time**, which puts them among the slow items rather than the closing items.

### 2.7 Other valuation products

**Automated valuation models**, **broker price opinions**, and **desk reviews** each have defined uses and limits, and **whether a particular product may be relied on for a particular purpose is a program and compliance question.**

**A licensee should not describe one as equivalent to an appraisal**, and should not obtain one to test a value before ordering the appraisal in a way that would defeat the independence structure.

### 2.8 Checkpoint

You should be able to read a report for factual accuracy, state the adjustment direction rule, explain what a low value produces, and describe a correct reconsideration request.

## 3. Title exceptions and liens

### 3.1 What the preliminary report is

A **preliminary report** reports the condition of title as shown in the records and states **the terms on which the title insurer would be willing to issue a policy**, including its exceptions.

**Three things it is not:** a guarantee that title is clear; the policy itself; or a survey — it addresses **record title**, not the physical property.

### 3.2 The three categories

| Category | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Exception** | An item the policy **will not insure against** |
| **Exclusion** | A category the policy form does not cover at all — governmental regulation, eminent domain, matters created by the insured |
| **Requirement** | Something the insurer needs **before issuing** |

> **Requirements are the items with deadlines attached**, and they are what delays closings.

**Three responses wrong in every case:** treating an item as routine because most properties have one; asking title to "remove" an exception; and predicting an easement's or restriction's effect on the borrower's plans.

### 3.3 What the lender needs answered

**Who owns it, what encumbers it, and where will our lien sit.**

### 3.4 The recurring items and their routing

| Item | Handling |
|---|---|
| **An unreleased deed of trust the borrower says was paid** | A recorded **reconveyance** must be obtained. **Payment does not clear the record; the document does** — and obtaining one from a lender that merged or ceased to exist can take weeks |
| **An existing second on a refinance** | A **subordination agreement** is generally required, because paying off and re-recording the first would otherwise move the second into first position. **That is the second lienholder's decision with counsel — never "standard"** |
| **A judgment against a similarly named person** | Routed to title and resolved through a **statement of information.** Never assumed away |
| **Recent construction** | **Mechanic's lien priority may relate back to when work commenced**, so a lien recorded later can outrank the new deed of trust — and rights arise from **work**, not from a contract with the owner |
| **A lis pendens** | A recorded notice of pending litigation affecting title. A serious cloud; route to counsel |
| **Property tax delinquency** | The tax lien **generally takes priority regardless of when it attached** |
| **Authority requirements** | Trustee evidence, corporate resolutions, powers of attorney — each a **requirement** with a deadline |
| **Easements and CC&Rs** | Obtain the underlying documents; they can prevent what the borrower intends |

### 3.5 The mechanic's lien question at intake

> **Has any work been done on this property recently, by whom, and is everyone paid?**

A borrower who renovated last spring with an unresolved contractor dispute has a title problem that has not appeared yet — and a lender has a **priority** problem. Lenders on construction and recently improved property require indemnities, title endorsements, and evidence of payment for exactly this reason.

**Whether a particular lien is valid or timely is a legal question.** The licensee's contribution is timing: **a defect surfaced at intake is a condition; the same defect surfaced after funding is a loss.**

### 3.6 The policies

**The lender's policy** insures the lender's lien and its priority. **The owner's policy** insures the borrower's interest.

> **They insure different interests**, which is why both commonly exist — and a borrower who declines an owner's policy because "there's a title policy on the loan" has left his own interest uninsured.

**Endorsements** extend or modify coverage for particular risks. **A licensee should know they exist and are ordered, and should not represent what any particular endorsement covers.**

### 3.7 What title insurance does not cover

**Zoning and governmental regulation. Physical condition. Boundary location under a standard policy** — the survey exception. **Matters the insured created or agreed to. Matters known to the insured and not disclosed. Anything the policy excepted.**

> A borrower asks whether a fence is on the line and is told the title report "came back clean." **That answer is wrong.** The report addresses record title, and boundary location is precisely what the standard policy excepts. **A survey answers it.**

### 3.8 Order it first

**The preliminary report and its underlying documents belong at the front of the queue**, with the association documents and the hazard-area insurance quotes.

An exception discovered late — an easement across a building site, an unreleased lien requiring a document from a lender that no longer exists — takes time to resolve. **A borrower who receives the report on day three has options; one who receives it on day twelve has a formality.**

### 3.9 Checkpoint

You should be able to distinguish exceptions, exclusions, and requirements; route each recurring item; state the mechanic's lien intake question; and state what title insurance does not cover.

## 4. Escrow instructions and funds

### 4.1 What escrow is

**Escrow is a neutral third party holding funds and documents under conditional delivery, authorized to act only according to the parties' mutual written instructions.**

**Two elements make an arrangement an escrow:** a **binding contract** between the parties, and **conditional delivery** to a neutral holder.

### 4.2 Why it exists

**Simultaneity.** A buyer will not release funds before receiving title; a seller will not release title before receiving funds. **Escrow breaks the impasse by having both go first to a neutral party**, who releases to each only when the conditions are satisfied.

That single function explains every rule that follows.

### 4.3 Neutrality and what it forbids

| Escrow may | Escrow may not |
|---|---|
| Follow **mutual** written instructions | Act on one party's instruction alone |
| Hold funds and documents | **Advise** either party |
| Prepare and circulate documents per the instructions | **Decide a dispute** between the parties |
| Record and disburse when conditions are met | Release funds because one party asserts entitlement |
| Report status to both parties | Take a position on who is right |

> **Where the parties disagree, escrow stops.** And **an agent's or an originator's confirmation is not a party's consent.**

### 4.4 Instructions and the underlying agreement

**Escrow's instructions implement the parties' agreement.** Where they conflict, **the agreement governs**, and the discrepancy is reconciled **by the parties in writing** — not by escrow deciding and not by a licensee telling escrow which figure to use.

**Instructions are amended the same way the agreement is: in writing, signed.** An email from a licensee amends nothing.

### 4.5 The reconciliation nobody else performs

**Nobody compares the escrow instructions and the closing figures against the loan terms and the disclosures line by line** — escrow prepares from what it received, the lender reads its own documents, and the borrower reads a summary.

**The originator's contribution at this stage is that reconciliation**: loan amount, rate, term, payoffs, credits, compensation, prorations, and cash to close, against what was disclosed and agreed.

> **A discrepancy found the day before closing is a problem. The same discrepancy found after disbursement is a dispute.**

### 4.6 Funds

**Funds to close** must arrive in a form and by a time the closing requires, and **cleared** rather than merely deposited.

**Payoff demands** are obtained by escrow from each lienholder and state **what satisfies the obligation — including accrued interest to the payoff date and other amounts owed under the note.** They are routinely more than the last statement balance, and routinely a surprise to a borrower nobody warned.

**Prorations** allocate continuing charges at the ownership boundary, and **escrow computes the operative figures** — a licensee explains the concept and checks that an estimate is in the right range.

### 4.7 Trust funds

Where funds pass through the **brokerage** rather than directly to escrow, they are **trust funds**: separated from the firm's own funds, recorded by beneficiary, and handled timely.

**A check held in a desk drawer over a weekend is a trust fund handling problem**, whatever the intention.

### 4.8 Selection and referral

**Selection of escrow is negotiable between the parties** and is often addressed in the purchase agreement.

> **A licensee must not accept anything of value for directing settlement business.** Referrals are ordinary; **payment for them is prohibited, and disclosure does not cure it.**

**Where the firm has an ownership interest**, that is an **affiliated business arrangement** — permitted subject to disclosure with an estimate at or before referral, **no required use**, and **no thing of value beyond a return on ownership.**

### 4.9 Wire fraud

**The highest-risk moment in the transaction is when funds move.**

1. **Warn in writing at application** — wire instructions will never change by email; verify by voice at an independently obtained number.
2. **Treat any change as presumptively fraudulent** until verified through a channel the attacker does not control.
3. **Never verify using a number from the email carrying the instructions.**

**Report any incident or near-miss to the broker immediately**, whether or not funds moved.

### 4.10 Checkpoint

You should be able to state what makes an arrangement an escrow, state what neutrality forbids, explain what governs a conflict between instructions and the agreement, and state the wire fraud protocol.

## 5. Settlement statements

### 5.1 What the statement does

The settlement statement — and for a consumer mortgage, the **Closing Disclosure** — shows each party's **debits and credits** and the resulting cash position.

**It is the arithmetic of the transaction**, and it is the last place a discrepancy can be caught cheaply.

### 5.2 The structure

| Side | Typical entries |
|---|---|
| **Borrower/buyer debits** | Purchase price, closing costs the borrower pays, prorated items reimbursed to the seller |
| **Borrower/buyer credits** | Deposit, **loan proceeds**, seller credits, prorated items owed by the seller |
| **Seller debits** | Loan payoffs, compensation, seller-paid costs, credits to buyer, prorated items owed, liens satisfied |
| **Seller credits** | Sale price, prorated items prepaid |

> **The reliable check: the price is a credit to the seller and a debit to the buyer; the deposit and the loan proceeds are credits to the buyer.** Everything else follows from asking who benefits and who pays.

**On a refinance there is no seller side** — the statement shows the new loan proceeds against the payoff, costs, prepaids, and any cash to or from the borrower.

### 5.3 Prorations

Continuing charges are allocated at the ownership boundary.

> **Seller prepaid beyond the boundary → seller credited, buyer debited.**
> **Charge accrued, or money held for the buyer's period → seller debited, buyer credited.**

> **Taxes $7,300 annually, paid through June 30; closing May 31; 365-day basis; day of closing to the buyer.**
> Buyer's period May 31–June 30 = 31 days. 7,300 ÷ 365 = $20.00/day × 31 = **$620.00 credited to the seller.**

**Three conventions change the answer and must be read rather than assumed:** the **day-count basis**, **who receives the day of closing**, and the fact that **California's property tax year does not align with the calendar year.**

### 5.4 The lines an originator reconciles

| Line | Against |
|---|---|
| **Loan amount, rate, term** | The lock and the approval |
| **Points and lender charges** | The Loan Estimate, within tolerance |
| **Third-party charges** | The estimate and their tolerance category |
| **Prepaid interest** | The actual disbursement date |
| **Escrow deposits** | The program requirement and the actual tax and insurance figures |
| **Payoffs** | The payoff demands, good through the disbursement date |
| **Credits** | What was agreed and disclosed |
| **Compensation** | **What was disclosed — the reconciliation firms skip** |
| **Prorations** | The convention stated and the boundary used |
| **Cash to close** | The whole statement |

### 5.5 Tolerances at the statement

The final charges are where the **tolerance** framework of Lesson 09 becomes concrete.

**Zero tolerance** charges generally may not increase. **Ten percent** category totals may not exceed the estimate by more than ten percent. **Unlimited** charges may vary, subject to a real **good faith** standard.

**Where a tolerance is exceeded without a valid changed circumstance, a cure is owed** — refunding the excess within a defined period with a corrected disclosure. **A cure is a remedy, not a business decision.**

### 5.6 Explaining the statement to a borrower

**What a licensee does:** walk the borrower through what each category is, distinguish **lender charges from third-party charges and prepaids**, explain that prepaids are amounts he would owe anyway, and identify anything that changed from the estimate and why.

**What a licensee does not do:** describe a fee as "standard," explain a lender's charge as though qualified to, or minimize a change the borrower is entitled to understand.

> **A borrower surprised at the table is a borrower who was not told**, and the surprise is almost always avoidable — most of it is prepaids and escrow deposits nobody explained.

### 5.7 Checkpoint

You should be able to place entries on the correct side, compute a proration in both directions, list the lines an originator reconciles, and explain a statement to a borrower.

## 6. Recording, funding, and document custody

### 6.1 The four events, in order

| # | Event | What happens |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | **Signing** | Documents are executed. **The transaction is not closed** |
| 2 | **Funding** | The lender releases proceeds, subject to its conditions |
| 3 | **Recording** | The deed and security instrument are recorded, giving **constructive notice** |
| 4 | **Disbursement** | Escrow disburses once recording is confirmed |

**Recording precedes disbursement deliberately** — it prevents a gap in which money has moved but the interests are not of record.

### 6.2 Recording order and priority

**Where multiple instruments record, the order establishes lien priority.**

An out-of-sequence recording can place a new lender behind an interest it was to precede — which is why **closing is sequenced rather than simultaneous**, and why this is not a clerical preference.

**The exceptions from Lesson 03 still apply:** property tax liens generally take priority regardless of timing, and **mechanic's lien priority may relate back to when work commenced.**

### 6.3 Rescindable transactions

On a transaction carrying a **right of rescission** — in outline, certain transactions secured by the consumer's **principal dwelling**, notably a refinance with a different lender, and **not** a purchase-money loan on the dwelling being acquired — **funds are not disbursed until the rescission period expires.**

**Each consumer entitled to rescind must receive the required notices**, and the period runs from **the later** of consummation, delivery of the notice, or delivery of the material disclosures — **so a defective or undelivered notice can extend it.**

**A refinance closing is therefore not a purchase closing**, and a borrower expecting funds at signing should have been told in advance.

### 6.4 Funding conditions

**Prior-to-funding conditions are not formalities.** An unsatisfied one means the loan does not fund, whatever the schedule says.

**The recurring final-stage items:** the **verbal verification of employment**; a final credit re-check; evidence of **insurance in force with the correct mortgagee clause**; the payoff demand good through the disbursement date; and confirmation that funds to close have arrived and cleared.

### 6.5 Constructive notice

**Recording gives constructive notice to the world** — a recorded document is knowable by anyone who looks, so the law treats everyone as knowing it.

**Inquiry notice** is the one that surprises lenders, because nothing was recorded: an occupant who is not the record owner, rent paid to a third party, visible recent construction. **A party may be charged with what an investigation would have revealed** — and it threatens **bona fide encumbrancer** status, which requires taking for value, in good faith, and **without notice.**

### 6.6 Document custody

After recording, the **original recorded instruments** are returned as directed, and the **note** is delivered to the lender or its custodian.

**Two points a licensee should understand:**

**The note is the instrument that carries the debt**, and its custody matters — which is why lenders and investors use custodians and why an original's whereabouts is not a clerical question.

**The recorded instruments come back weeks later**, and a borrower asking why he does not have his deed yet is asking a reasonable question with an ordinary answer.

### 6.7 Confirming closing to the borrower

**When funding and recording are confirmed, tell the borrower in writing, with the date and what it means.**

**What a borrower needs to know at that point:** that the loan funded and recorded, and on what dates; **when the first payment is due** — remembering that **interest is paid in arrears**, so it is generally not the following month; **where to send it**, and that **servicing may transfer**; what his escrow account covers and that the payment can change when taxes or insurance do; and where his documents will come from and roughly when.

### 6.8 The file at closing

- The final Closing Disclosure and its delivery evidence.
- The signed note and security instrument.
- The settlement statement and the **reconciliation** performed against the disclosures.
- Payoff demands and evidence of payoff.
- Evidence of insurance with the correct mortgagee clause.
- The title policy or commitment to issue.
- Funding and recording confirmations, with dates.
- **Compensation disclosed and received, reconciled.**
- Any cure issued, with its corrected disclosure.

### 6.9 The test

> **Reconstructability: could a person who was not there determine what was disclosed, what was agreed, what was charged, when funds moved, and on what basis?**

**A file that passes survives an examination even where it contains a problem. A file that does not fails even where it contains none.**

### 6.10 Checkpoint

You should be able to sequence the four events and explain the ordering, describe how a rescindable closing differs, distinguish constructive from inquiry notice, and state what the file must show at closing.

## Practice problems

### Problem 1

An appraisal comes in $30,000 low. The originator emails the appraiser directly with three sales he
believes were overlooked, notes that a permitted addition was omitted from the report, and adds: "We
need $920,000 to make this work." His borrower and his manager both asked him to send it. His firm uses
an appraisal management company.

**Separate what is permitted from what is not, and state the correct process.**

> **Solution.** **Two of the three contents are permitted; the third contaminates the message; and the
> channel was wrong regardless.**
>
> **Permitted — information:** relevant sales identified **with data**, and a **factual correction** such
> as an omitted permitted addition. Those are exactly what a reconsideration is built from.
>
> **Prohibited — a target.** "We need $920,000" is the communication independence rules exist to prevent,
> and **a request to reconsider that arrives with a number attached is the pattern to refuse — including
> when it comes from your own borrower and your own manager.**
>
> **The channel is the control.** Independence requirements govern who may select, order, and communicate
> with the appraiser, and firms use an appraisal management company or a separated internal function
> **precisely so individual originators are not the point of contact.** Emailing the appraiser directly
> **defeats the separation**, even with a message containing only permitted content.
>
> **Correct process:** a **reconsideration of value through the lender's defined channel**, with the sales
> and the factual correction, **and no number.** And what is *not* a reconsideration: an assertion that
> the value is wrong because the contract price is higher or because the transaction fails without a
> different number.

### Problem 2

A branch manager notices that two appraisers "always come in short" and directs the ordering desk to
route work toward three others who "understand this market." He also proposes paying the appraisal
invoice only after the report is reviewed, and including an "estimated value" field on the order form.

**Assess each practice.**

> **Solution.** **All three are coercion in substance, and the first is the subtlest and most common.**
>
> **Selecting appraisers because of a history of favorable values builds coercion into the ordering
> pattern** without anyone saying anything prohibited. The appraisers are never told what to conclude;
> they are simply selected for having concluded favorably before — and the effect on the population of
> reports is the same as an instruction.
>
> **Conditioning payment on the review is withholding payment based on a value**, which is prohibited in
> substance whatever the stated rationale.
>
> **An "estimated value" field on the order is providing a target with the order** — the target arrives
> before the work begins rather than after.
>
> **Why it matters beyond compliance:** an appraisal exists to measure the lender's collateral risk, and
> **a value that moves because someone needed it to move measures nothing.** The lender, the transaction,
> and any investor who buys the loan all rely on a number that has stopped being an opinion.
>
> **Escalate to the broker.** This is a firm-level practice, not an individual file question.

### Problem 3

A refinance borrower wants $150,000 cash out and has been told by his originator that it "shouldn't be a
problem." The appraisal returns below expectations. The borrower asks whether the price adjusts,
whether he can demand a second appraisal, and — since he waived his contingency on a separate purchase —
whether he can cancel that one and recover his deposit.

**Address each.**

> **Solution.** **The first error was the assurance.** **On a refinance there is no purchase price, so
> value is the appraised value** — which makes a refinance more exposed to the appraisal outcome than a
> purchase, and means **cash-out availability is not knowable until the appraisal arrives.** "Shouldn't be
> a problem" was a prediction about someone else's independent opinion.
>
> **Lenders lend against the lower of appraised value and purchase price**, so on the purchase the loan
> is computed on the appraisal.
>
> **The price does not adjust automatically** by operation of any program, **the loan is not denied**, and
> **no one is entitled to a second appraisal as of right.** His practical options are to bring funds,
> renegotiate, negotiate another resolution, or cancel if a contingency permits and its procedure is
> followed.
>
> **The waived contingency question is legal and must not be answered.** The practical options remain, but
> the removed contingency limits his contractual rights, and **whether he may cancel and what happens to
> the deposit turns on the contract's terms and the circumstances.** Recommend counsel before he stops
> performing.
>
> Also confirm he **received his copy of the appraisal** and that delivery is evidenced.

### Problem 4

A refinance preliminary report arrives on day eleven of a fourteen-day lock. It shows an unreleased 2016
deed of trust the borrower says was paid, an existing second, a judgment against a similarly named
person, and a lis pendens. The borrower also mentions a $180,000 renovation finished four months ago
with an unpaid contractor.

**Route each and identify the first failure.**

> **Solution.** **The timing failure came first and made everything harder.** The preliminary report and
> its underlying documents belong at **the front of the queue**, because **requirements carry the
> deadlines** and several here take weeks. A borrower who receives it on day three has options; on day
> eleven he has a formality.
>
> **The 2016 deed of trust requires a recorded reconveyance.** **Payment does not clear the record; the
> document does** — the borrower's recollection is not that document — and obtaining one from a lender
> that merged or ceased to exist can take weeks.
>
> **The existing second requires a subordination agreement**, because paying off and re-recording the
> first would otherwise move the second into first position. **That is the second lienholder's decision
> with counsel — never "standard."**
>
> **The judgment goes to title**, resolved through a **statement of information.** Never assumed away.
>
> **The lis pendens is a serious cloud** — a recorded notice that pending litigation may affect anyone
> acquiring an interest. Route to counsel immediately.
>
> **The renovation is the exposure a mortgage licensee must recognize.** **Mechanic's lien priority may
> relate back to when work commenced**, so a lien recorded next month can outrank the new deed of trust —
> and **rights arise from work, not from a contract with the owner**, so subcontractors the borrower never
> met can have them. **A defect surfaced at intake is a condition; the same defect surfaced after funding
> is a loss.**

### Problem 5

A borrower asks whether the neighbor's fence encroaches, is told the title report "came back clean," and
declines an owner's policy because "there's already a title policy on the loan." He also asks what the
"requirements" section means, since the exceptions looked more important.

**Correct all three.**

> **Solution.** **"Clean" answers a question he did not ask.** A preliminary report addresses **record
> title** — what the records show about ownership and encumbrances. **It is not a survey**, and the
> standard policy **excepts** matters a survey would disclose. **Nothing in the report speaks to where the
> fence is.** A survey does.
>
> Also outside coverage: zoning and governmental regulation, physical condition, matters the insured
> created or agreed to, matters known and not disclosed, and anything excepted.
>
> **Declining the owner's policy is not economizing on a duplicate.** The **lender's policy insures the
> lender's lien and its priority**; the **owner's policy insures his interest.** They insure **different
> interests**, which is why both exist — and declining leaves his own interest uninsured.
>
> **The requirements are the more urgent section, not the less.** An **exception** is a risk the policy
> will not insure against; an **exclusion** is a category outside the policy entirely; a **requirement**
> is something the insurer needs **before issuing** — a reconveyance, a resolution, authority evidence.
> **Requirements carry the deadlines, and they are what delays closings.**

### Problem 6

The day before recording, the buyer emails escrow instructing it to hold back $4,000 from the seller's
proceeds for a repair the seller promised verbally. The seller knows nothing about it. The originator
emails escrow confirming the request is legitimate, and separately tells escrow to use the loan amount
from the approval rather than the lower figure in the escrow instructions.

**Assess.**

> **Solution.** **Escrow stops on the first, and the second is not the originator's to direct.**
>
> **A change to disbursement requires mutual written instructions.** One party cannot redirect funds; and
> escrow **may not simply disburse in full** either, since that also acts on one side's position once a
> genuine dispute is raised. **Escrow holds, does not advise, and does not decide** — and where the
> parties disagree, **it stops.**
>
> **The originator's confirmation supplies nothing.** An originator is not a party, and **a licensee's
> confirmation is not a party's consent.** The parties resolve it in writing and the resolution reaches
> escrow as a **mutual instruction** — and the job is to get that done before the closing date, not to
> argue with escrow about its role.
>
> **On the loan amount: escrow's instructions implement the parties' agreement, and where they conflict
> the agreement governs**, reconciled **by the parties in writing** — not by escrow deciding and **not by
> a licensee telling escrow which figure to use.** Instructions are amended the way the agreement is: in
> writing, signed. **An email from a licensee amends nothing.**
>
> What the originator **should** do is the reconciliation nobody else performs — compare the instructions
> and figures against the loan terms and disclosures, and raise the discrepancy immediately.

### Problem 7

Reconcile a purchase settlement statement. Price $920,000; deposit $30,000; loan $736,000; annual taxes
$7,300 paid by the seller through June 30 with a May 31 closing on a 365-day basis, day of closing to
the buyer. The originator notices the compensation line shows $5,650 where the Loan Estimate disclosed
$4,200, and an appraisal fee $200 above the estimate with no changed circumstance documented.

**Place the entries, compute the proration, and address both discrepancies.**

> **Solution.** **Placement:** the **price is a credit to the seller and a debit to the buyer**; the
> **deposit and the loan proceeds are credits to the buyer**. Everything else follows from asking who
> benefits and who pays.
>
> **Proration.** The seller prepaid through June 30 but owns only through May 30. Buyer's period is
> **May 31–June 30 = 31 days.** 7,300 ÷ 365 = **$20.00/day** × 31 = **$620.00 credited to the seller,
> debited to the buyer.** Three conventions must be read rather than assumed: the **day-count basis**,
> **who gets the day of closing**, and the fact that **California's tax year does not align with the
> calendar year.**
>
> **The compensation discrepancy is the finding with the widest consequences** — reaching the California
> mortgage loan disclosure statement, the federal disclosures, and potentially the points-and-fees
> calculation. **Reconciling disclosed against received is the control firms skip**, because one function
> prepares the disclosure and another receives the payment.
>
> **The appraisal fee is generally zero tolerance** — a service the consumer could not shop for — so
> absent a **valid changed circumstance** it may not increase, and a **cure** is owed: refunding the
> excess within a defined period with a corrected disclosure. **A cure is a remedy, not a business
> decision.**
>
> **Both were caught the day before closing, which is the point.** A discrepancy found then is a problem;
> found after disbursement it is a dispute.

### Problem 8

A refinance borrower is told he is "clear to close," signs on Monday, and calls Wednesday angry that he
has no money and no recorded deed. He also asks when his first payment is due, where to send it, why his
payoff exceeded his statement by $6,400, and whether his payment can change since it is a fixed-rate
loan.

**Answer each.**

> **Solution.** **"Clear to close" means the lender's conditions to document and fund are satisfied. It is
> not funding and not closing.**
>
> **The four events, in order:** **signing** — documents executed, **the transaction is not closed**;
> **funding**; **recording**, which gives **constructive notice**; and **disbursement**, after recording is
> confirmed — deliberately, so money does not move before the interests are of record. **Recording order
> also establishes lien priority**, which is why closing is sequenced rather than simultaneous.
>
> **His refinance is rescindable, and that is the real reason for the delay.** On certain transactions
> secured by the **principal dwelling** — notably a refinance with a different lender — **funds are not
> disbursed until the rescission period expires.** Each entitled consumer must receive the notice, and the
> period runs from **the later** of consummation, delivery of the notice, or delivery of the material
> disclosures, **so a defective notice extends it.** He should have been told this before signing: **a
> refinance closing is not a purchase closing.**
>
> **His recorded documents come back weeks later**, which is ordinary.
>
> **The payoff included accrued interest to the payoff date and other amounts owed under the note** —
> routinely more than the last statement.
>
> **Interest is paid in arrears**, so his first payment is generally not the following month. **Servicing
> may transfer**, so where to send it can change.
>
> **And yes, his payment can change** even on a fixed-rate loan: the **escrow account** covers taxes and
> insurance, and a shortage after an increase produces a payment increase on a loan he believed was fixed.

## Chapter summary

**Independence exists because a value that moves because someone needed it to move measures nothing.**
Supply **information, never a target**, and use the defined channel — routing around it defeats the
control.

**Soft coercion counts**, including selecting appraisers with a history of favorable values.

**Read a report for factual accuracy and consistency**, remembering that adjustments are made **to the
comparable** and that reliability tracks **gross** adjustment.

**Lenders lend against the lower of value or price, and on a refinance value is the appraisal.** Nothing
about a low value is automatic, and the deposit question on a waived contingency is legal.

**Reconsider through the channel, with data and factual corrections, and without a number.**

**A preliminary report addresses record title.** Distinguish **exceptions, exclusions, and requirements**
— and requirements carry the deadlines. Order it first.

**Payment does not clear the record; a reconveyance does.** A refinance with a second usually needs a
subordination, and recent construction raises relation-back exposure — so ask the intake question.

**Title does not insure boundary location under a standard policy**, and the owner's and lender's policies
insure different interests.

**Escrow solves simultaneity by being neutral.** It acts only on **mutual written instructions**, advises
no one, decides nothing, and stops where the parties disagree — and **a licensee's confirmation is not a
party's consent.**

**Instructions implement the agreement, and the agreement governs a conflict.**

**Nobody but the originator reconciles the closing figures against what was disclosed and agreed** —
including compensation, which is the reconciliation firms skip.

**Prorations run in two directions**, and three conventions change the answer.

**Where a tolerance is exceeded without a valid changed circumstance, a cure is owed.**

**Signing, funding, recording, disbursement** — with recording before disbursement deliberately, recording
order setting priority, and **funds waiting on a rescindable transaction.**

**Constructive notice comes from the record; inquiry notice comes from the ground** and threatens bona
fide encumbrancer status.

**Confirm closing in writing** with the first payment date, where to send it, that servicing may transfer,
and that an escrowed payment can change — and keep a file someone who was not there could reconstruct.
