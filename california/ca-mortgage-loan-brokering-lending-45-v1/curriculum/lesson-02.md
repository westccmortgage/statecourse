## 1. Principal, interest, and term

### 1.1 The three variables

Every loan is described by three quantities, and every calculation in this lesson manipulates them.

| Variable | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Principal** | The amount borrowed, and thereafter the amount still owed |
| **Interest** | The charge for the use of money, expressed as a rate per period |
| **Term** | The period over which the obligation runs |

**Principal changes.** The word describes the original amount and the current balance, and a licensee who does not track which one a calculation calls for will compute interest on the wrong number.

### 1.2 Simple interest

Real estate loans generally use **simple interest** — computed on the outstanding principal, not on accumulated interest.

> **I = P × R × T**

Rearranged, it answers four questions:

| Unknown | Formula |
|---|---|
| Interest | I = P × R × T |
| Principal | P = I ÷ (R × T) |
| Rate | R = I ÷ (P × T) |
| Time | T = I ÷ (P × R) |

### 1.3 Worked in all four directions

> **Interest.** $400,000 at 6% for one year: 400,000 × 0.06 × 1 = **$24,000.**
> One month: 24,000 ÷ 12 = **$2,000.**

> **Principal.** A borrower paid $18,750 of interest in one year at 7.5%.
> P = 18,750 ÷ (0.075 × 1) = **$250,000.**

> **Rate.** A $320,000 loan produced $20,800 of interest in a year.
> R = 20,800 ÷ 320,000 = **6.5%.**

> **Time.** $9,000 of interest accrued on a $240,000 loan at 6%.
> Annual interest = 240,000 × 0.06 = $14,400. T = 9,000 ÷ 14,400 = 0.625 years = **7.5 months.**

### 1.4 Per diem interest

Interest computed per day, and it appears at every closing.

> **Per diem = annual interest ÷ days in the year basis**

> A $400,000 loan at 6% closing on the 22nd of a 30-day month, on a 360-day basis:
> Annual interest = $24,000. Per diem = 24,000 ÷ 360 = **$66.67.**
> Days from the 22nd through month end = 9. **9 × 66.67 = $600.03.**

**Two conventions change the answer**, and both must be read rather than assumed: whether the basis is **360 or 365 days**, and **which days are counted.** A licensee who assumes and is wrong has misstated a closing figure.

### 1.5 Interest in arrears

Residential mortgage interest is generally paid **in arrears** — a payment covers interest that has already accrued, rather than interest for the period ahead.

Two practical consequences that surprise borrowers:

**The first payment is not due the month after closing.** A loan closing in March typically has its first payment in May, because interest for the remainder of March was collected at closing and April's interest accrues before the May payment.

**A payoff includes interest through the payoff date**, which is why the payoff exceeds the last statement balance.

### 1.6 Checkpoint

You should be able to solve I = P × R × T in all four directions, compute per diem interest, and explain why the first payment is not due the following month.

## 2. Simple interest and amortization

### 2.1 What amortization does

**Amortization** is the retirement of a debt through periodic payments covering both interest and principal.

| Structure | Behavior |
|---|---|
| **Fully amortized** | Payments retire the balance to zero by maturity |
| **Partially amortized** | Payments reduce the balance but leave a **balloon** at maturity |
| **Interest-only** | Payments cover interest only; **principal is not retired** during the period |
| **Straight (term) note** | Interest payments with the full principal due at maturity |
| **Negative amortization** | The payment does not cover accrued interest, and **the balance grows** |

### 2.2 How each payment splits

Each payment covers the interest accrued since the last payment; **the remainder reduces principal.**

**$400,000 at 6%, monthly payment $2,398.20:**

| Month | Interest | Principal | Ending balance |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | $2,000.00 | $398.20 | $399,601.80 |
| 2 | $1,998.01 | $400.19 | $399,201.61 |
| 3 | $1,996.01 | $402.19 | $398,799.42 |

> **Month 1 interest:** 400,000 × 0.06 ÷ 12 = $2,000.00. Principal = 2,398.20 − 2,000.00 = $398.20.
>
> **Month 2 interest:** 399,601.80 × 0.06 ÷ 12 = $1,998.01. Principal = $400.19.

### 2.3 The pattern, and what it explains

**Early payments are almost entirely interest. The principal portion grows each month while the interest portion shrinks**, slowly at first and then faster.

That single pattern explains several things borrowers ask about:

- **Why five years of payments retired so little principal.** On this loan, sixty payments totaling roughly $143,900 reduce the balance by roughly $27,800.
- **Why the payoff exceeds what the borrower expected.**
- **Why a small additional principal payment early has a disproportionate effect**, since it removes principal that would otherwise accrue interest for the whole remaining term.

### 2.4 Negative amortization

Where a scheduled payment is less than the accrued interest, the shortfall is added to the balance and **the debt grows despite payments being made.**

Two features make it dangerous and worth teaching plainly:

**The borrower is paying and losing ground**, which does not match anyone's intuition about what a payment does.

**Recast provisions can produce a payment increase that is large and sudden**, when the loan is required to begin fully amortizing the enlarged balance over the remaining term.

**A licensee presenting any product with a negative amortization feature must present that mechanism in plain terms**, and Lesson 09 addresses the disclosure obligations that attach.

### 2.5 The effect of term

Term changes the payment and changes the total interest far more than borrowers expect.

**$400,000 at 6%:**

| Term | Approximate payment | Approximate total interest |
|---|---:|---:|
| 30 years | $2,398 | $463,400 |
| 20 years | $2,866 | $287,800 |
| 15 years | $3,375 | $207,600 |

**The payment rises by about 41% from 30 to 15 years; the total interest falls by more than half.** That comparison is the one a borrower is usually trying to understand, and a licensee can compute and present it — while leaving **which to choose** to the borrower with their advisors.

### 2.6 Prepayment

Additional principal reduces the balance and therefore all future interest.

> On the loan above, an extra $200 per month from the start retires it roughly six years early and saves well over $100,000 of interest.

**Two cautions.** Whether a **prepayment penalty** applies is a note provision, addressed in Section 3, and its permissibility is regulated. And an extra payment must be **applied to principal** — a borrower who simply sends more may find it applied to the next installment instead.

### 2.7 Checkpoint

You should be able to build three rows of an amortization schedule, explain the shifting split, and describe negative amortization in terms a borrower would understand.

## 3. Promissory note terms

### 3.1 Two documents, two jobs

Restated because everything in this course depends on it:

| Instrument | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Promissory note** | The **promise to repay.** It creates the debt and states its terms |
| **Security instrument** | Pledges the **property** as security for that debt |

> **The note is the debt. The security instrument is the remedy.**

A note without a security instrument is an unsecured obligation. A security instrument without a note secures nothing, because there is no obligation to secure.

### 3.2 The parties and the essential terms

**Maker** (borrower) promises to pay; **payee** (holder) is entitled to receive.

The note states the principal, the rate and how it is determined, the payment amount and schedule, the maturity date, the place and manner of payment, and the consequences of default.

### 3.3 The provisions that matter operationally

| Provision | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Acceleration** | On **default**, makes the entire balance immediately due |
| **Alienation (due-on-sale)** | Permits the lender to call the loan due on **transfer** of the property |
| **Prepayment** | Whether and on what terms the borrower may pay early, and any penalty |
| **Late charge** | A charge for a payment received after a stated period |
| **Default interest** | A higher rate applying after default, where provided |
| **Attorney fees** | Recovery of enforcement costs, where provided |
| **Subordination** | Agreement to take a lower lien position |
| **Release** | In a blanket loan, releasing individual parcels as they are sold |
| **Assumption** | Whether and on what conditions the loan may be assumed |

### 3.4 Acceleration and alienation are not the same

The two students merge, and the distinction is clean:

> **Acceleration responds to default. Alienation responds to transfer.**

Without an acceleration clause, a lender could pursue only the missed payments — which is why foreclosure depends on it. Without an alienation clause, a property could be transferred with the loan intact — which is why loans are generally **not assumable without lender consent.**

"A lender can always call the loan" is wrong. **The lender's rights come from the clauses actually in the instrument and the events that trigger them.**

### 3.5 Adjustable-rate provisions

For an adjustable-rate note, four terms determine the rate at every adjustment:

| Term | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Index** | The published measure the rate follows |
| **Margin** | The amount added to the index. **Set at origination and fixed** |
| **Adjustment frequency** | How often the rate resets |
| **Caps** | **Periodic** caps limit change at a single adjustment; a **lifetime** cap limits total movement |

> **The rate at adjustment is the index plus the margin, subject to the caps.** Not the lender's discretion, and **not the borrower's credit at that time.**

Some notes also carry an **initial** or first-adjustment cap and a **floor** below which the rate will not fall.

**A licensee who cannot explain a borrower's own index, margin, and caps should not be presenting that product.**

### 3.6 Negotiability and transfer

A note meeting certain requirements is a **negotiable instrument**, transferable so a subsequent holder may take it free of certain defenses.

Practically, this is why loans are sold and why a borrower's payments may be directed to a servicer they never chose. **The obligation follows the note.**

Whether a particular note is negotiable and what defenses survive are **legal questions.**

### 3.7 What a licensee says about note terms

**May:** identify each provision and explain what it does mechanically; explain that a rate adjusts by index plus margin within caps; explain that a prepayment provision exists and what it says.

**May not:** advise whether a provision is enforceable, predict how a court would read it, tell a borrower a provision "never gets enforced," or characterize a term as standard.

### 3.8 Checkpoint

You should be able to name the operative note provisions, distinguish acceleration from alienation, and state what determines an adjustable rate at adjustment.

## 4. Points, fees, and annual percentage rate

### 4.1 Points

> **One point equals one percent of the loan amount.** Not of the purchase price.

**Discount points** are paid to reduce the interest rate. **Origination points** are a charge for making the loan. Both are quoted the same way and do different things, and a borrower asking "how many points?" frequently does not know which is meant.

> **2 points on a $400,000 loan:** 400,000 × 0.02 = **$8,000.**
> **1.5 points on a $612,500 loan:** 612,500 × 0.015 = **$9,187.50.**

### 4.2 The break-even

The computation a borrower actually needs:

> **Cost of the points ÷ monthly saving = months to recover**

> Paying 2 points ($8,000) reduces the payment by $140 per month.
> 8,000 ÷ 140 = **57 months**, about 4 years 9 months.

A borrower who expects to hold the loan longer comes out ahead; one who expects to sell or refinance sooner does not.

**A licensee may compute and present the break-even.** Whether this borrower should pay points depends on his plans, his tax situation, and his alternatives — which is a lender and advisor question.

### 4.3 The categories of cost

A borrower sees a long list and cannot tell what varies. The useful grouping:

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Interest** | The rate itself |
| **Lender charges** | Origination, underwriting, processing, discount points |
| **Third-party services** | Appraisal, credit report, title, escrow, recording, pest, survey |
| **Prepaid items** | Per diem interest, initial hazard premium, initial impound deposits |
| **Government charges** | Recording fees, transfer taxes |

**Prepaid items are not a cost of the loan in the ordinary sense** — they are amounts the borrower would owe anyway, collected at closing. Borrowers who see a large "cash to close" and conclude the loan is expensive are frequently looking mostly at prepaids.

### 4.4 Annual percentage rate

The **APR** expresses the cost of credit as a yearly rate, including certain finance charges beyond interest, and it exists to make offers comparable.

**What it does:** captures costs the note rate alone omits, so two loans with the same note rate and different fees do not appear identical.

**What it does not do:**

- **It is not the note rate**, and the payment is computed from the note rate.
- **It does not include every cost** — its contents are defined, and some third-party charges are outside it.
- **It assumes the loan runs its full term**, which makes it a poor comparison for a borrower who will refinance or sell in five years.
- **It behaves oddly on adjustable-rate loans**, because it must assume future rates.

> **A licensee explains what the APR is for and points the borrower to it as a comparison tool.** A licensee does not compute it, interpret a difference between two APRs as a conclusion, or tell a borrower which loan is cheaper for him.

### 4.5 Nominal, effective, and note rate

**The note rate** is stated in the note and drives the payment. **The effective rate** reflects the actual cost including points and certain fees. **The nominal rate** is the stated annual rate before considering compounding or costs.

The relationship a borrower needs: **a lower note rate purchased with points is not automatically cheaper.** The break-even answers it for his holding period.

### 4.6 The comparison discipline

When presenting alternatives, present them on a common basis:

1. **The same loan amount**, or state the difference.
2. **The same term and structure**, or state the difference.
3. **The note rate, the payment, the points, and the lender charges**, each separately.
4. **The break-even** where points differ.
5. **The APR**, identified as a comparison tool with its assumptions.
6. **What is a lender charge and what is a third-party or prepaid item.**

**Comparisons that omit the fee difference, or that compare a lower rate bought with points against a higher rate without disclosing the cost, are misleading even where every individual number is accurate.**

### 4.7 Checkpoint

You should be able to compute points and a break-even, state what the APR does and does not capture, and present two offers on a common basis.

## 5. Loan-to-value and combined loan-to-value

### 5.1 Loan-to-value

> **LTV = loan amount ÷ value**, where value is **the lower of appraised value and purchase price.**

> **A $920,000 purchase with a $736,000 loan:** 736,000 ÷ 920,000 = **80%.**

### 5.2 The rule that explains every low appraisal

> **Lenders lend against the lower of appraised value and purchase price.**

> Same purchase, appraisal at $900,000. The lender lends against $900,000.
> At 80%: 900,000 × 0.80 = **$720,000** — **$16,000 less** than planned.

The borrower's options are then: bring the additional funds, renegotiate the price, negotiate another resolution, or cancel if a contingency permits and its procedure is followed.

**What does not happen automatically:** the price does not adjust to the appraised value by operation of any program, the loan is not denied, and **no one is entitled to a second appraisal as of right.**

### 5.3 Refinance transactions

On a refinance there is no purchase price, so **value is the appraised value** — which is why a refinance is more exposed to an appraisal outcome than a purchase, and why a borrower planning a cash-out refinance should understand that the amount available is not knowable until the appraisal is in.

### 5.4 Combined loan-to-value

Where more than one loan is secured by the property:

> **CLTV = total secured debt ÷ value**

> $920,000 property, $736,000 first and $46,000 second:
> 782,000 ÷ 920,000 = **85%.**

For a home equity line, the **full line amount** rather than the drawn balance is generally what counts, which surprises borrowers who have drawn little.

### 5.5 Equity and cash-out

> **Equity = value − liens.**

> A $920,000 property with a $600,000 first has $320,000 of equity.
> A cash-out refinance to 80% CLTV: 920,000 × 0.80 = $736,000 available; less the $600,000 payoff = **$136,000** before costs.

**Two points a borrower needs.** Cash-out proceeds are **borrowed money**, not income, and the tax treatment is a question for a tax professional. And **equity converted to debt is still gone from the equity side** — the property has not changed.

### 5.6 Why LTV matters to the lender

LTV measures the lender's exposure if the loan is not repaid, and it drives:

- **Whether the loan can be made at all** under the program.
- **Pricing**, since higher LTV generally carries higher risk.
- **Mortgage insurance**, commonly required on conventional loans below a down payment threshold — and which **protects the lender**, though the borrower pays for it.
- **The cushion** available if values decline.

### 5.7 The three numbers, for a borrower

**What the borrower can borrow** — the lender's number.
**What cash is available** — down payment, closing costs, reserves, and the immediate costs of occupancy. Borrowers routinely plan to the down payment and forget the rest.
**What the borrower is willing to pay monthly** — often lower than the first, and the number that should drive the search.

**Where they conflict, the conflict is the conversation** — held early and deliberately, rather than discovered at the closing table.

### 5.8 Checkpoint

You should be able to compute LTV and CLTV, apply the lower-of-value-or-price rule, and compute cash-out availability.

## 6. Payment and yield calculations

### 6.1 The payment factor method

A payment on a fully amortized loan can be computed from a **factor** — the monthly payment per $1,000 of loan at a given rate and term.

> **Payment = (loan ÷ 1,000) × factor**

> A factor of 5.9955 per $1,000 at 6% for 30 years:
> 400,000 ÷ 1,000 = 400 × 5.9955 = **$2,398.20.**

Factors come from a table or a financial calculator. **The examinable skill is applying one correctly**, and the recurring error is forgetting to divide the loan by 1,000 first.

### 6.2 Working backward from a payment

> **A borrower can afford $2,700 monthly principal and interest. At a factor of 5.9955, what loan does that support?**
>
> 2,700 ÷ 5.9955 = 450.4 → × 1,000 = **$450,400.**

### 6.3 PITI

The housing payment is more than principal and interest:

> **PITI = Principal + Interest + Taxes + Insurance** — plus association dues where applicable, and mortgage insurance where required.

> $2,398.20 P&I; taxes $958 monthly; hazard insurance $140; mortgage insurance $122; association dues $310.
> **Total housing payment = $3,928.20.**

**A borrower quoted a principal and interest figure and then shown a PITI figure will feel misled**, which is why the full payment belongs in the first conversation. In California, the **property tax line is the one most often understated**, because a buyer's taxes are based on a new assessed value rather than the seller's bill.

### 6.4 Qualifying ratios

| Ratio | Formula |
|---|---|
| **Front-end (housing)** | Housing expense ÷ gross monthly income |
| **Back-end (total debt)** | Total monthly obligations ÷ gross monthly income |

> **Gross monthly income $9,000.** PITI $2,520; car $480; student loan $300; card minimums $120.
> **Front-end:** 2,520 ÷ 9,000 = **28%.**
> **Back-end:** 3,420 ÷ 9,000 = **38%.**

**Thresholds vary by program, by compensating factors, and over time.** A licensee computes and explains the ratios and **never states a threshold as a rule** — that is an underwriting question, and stating it produces a confident error that costs a borrower a property.

### 6.5 Yield

**Yield** is the return to the holder of a note, and it differs from the note rate whenever the note was acquired for something other than face value.

> **A $200,000 note at 7% interest-only pays $14,000 a year.**
>
> Purchased at par: yield = 14,000 ÷ 200,000 = **7.0%.**
> Purchased at a 10% discount ($180,000): 14,000 ÷ 180,000 = **7.78%.**
> Purchased at a premium ($210,000): 14,000 ÷ 210,000 = **6.67%.**

> **Buying at a discount raises the yield; buying at a premium lowers it.**

This matters in Lesson 12's private money material, where notes are bought and sold, and it is the same inverse relationship as the capitalization rate: **price and yield move in opposite directions for a given income.**

### 6.6 Discount and points from the lender's side

Discount points paid at origination raise the lender's yield for the same reason: the lender advances less than face while receiving the stated payments.

That is the mechanism behind a rate buydown, and it is worth understanding because it explains why a lower rate has a price rather than being a favor.

### 6.7 The errors that produce wrong answers

1. **Applying a rate to the wrong base** — points to the price rather than the loan; a percentage to the result rather than the source.
2. **Unit and convention errors** — annual rate where monthly was needed, 360 versus 365 day basis, forgetting to divide by 1,000 in the factor method.
3. **Confusing note rate with APR**, or payment with PITI.
4. **Skipping the reasonableness check.**

> **The reasonableness check catches nearly all of them.** A $2,398 payment on a $400,000 loan at 6% is plausible; $23,980 is not. **Before reporting a number, ask whether it is the right order of magnitude.**

### 6.8 Every figure carries its basis

A number given to a borrower should carry the assumptions it rests on: the loan amount, the rate, the term, whether it is P&I or PITI, and whether taxes and insurance are estimated.

> **A quote without its assumptions is not information. It is a number the borrower will hold you to.**

### 6.9 Reading a payment quote critically

Borrowers are quoted payments constantly, and the quoted figure is frequently not the figure they will pay. **A broker who can take a quote apart protects the borrower from a surprise and protects himself from having repeated a number he did not verify.**

**What a quoted "payment" may or may not include:**

| Component | Frequently omitted from a quick quote |
|---|---|
| **Principal and interest** | Always included — this is usually the whole quote |
| **Property taxes** | Often omitted, and Section 6.3's PITI is the reason it matters |
| **Hazard insurance** | Often omitted, and in some California markets it is now the largest surprise |
| **Mortgage insurance** | Omitted whenever the quote assumes a down payment the borrower is not making |
| **Association dues** | Not part of the loan payment and entirely part of the borrower's obligation |
| **Flood or other required coverage** | Omitted until a determination is made |
| **Special assessments** | Rarely surfaced before closing |

**Three checks a broker performs on any quote before repeating it:**

1. **Does the payment reconcile to the rate, term, and loan amount?** Section 6.1's factor method answers this in seconds, and **a payment that does not reconcile means one of the four inputs is not what was stated.**
2. **What down payment does it assume?** A quote built on twenty percent is not a quote for a borrower bringing ten, and the difference is not only the loan amount.
3. **What is the escrow assumption?** A quote excluding an escrowed item is a quote for a payment the borrower will not make.

**And the disclosure that resolves all of it:** Section 6.8's requirement that **every figure carries its basis.** A payment stated with its rate, term, amount, down payment, and what it includes is a professional statement. **The same number without those five is a figure the borrower will hold the broker to.**

> **The failure this prevents.** A borrower quoted a payment early, who budgeted against it, and who sees a materially higher figure on his disclosures. **Nothing was misrepresented on purpose** — the quote assumed a different down payment and excluded taxes and insurance — and the borrower experiences it as having been misled, which for every practical purpose is the outcome that matters.

### 6.10 Checkpoint

You should be able to compute a payment from a factor and work backward, build a PITI figure, compute both ratios without stating thresholds, and compute yield at a discount and a premium.

## Practice problems

### Problem 1

Solve each: (a) one month's interest on a $400,000 balance at 6%; (b) the principal, where a borrower
paid $18,750 of interest in one year at 7.5%; (c) the rate, where a $320,000 loan produced $20,800 in a
year; (d) per diem on a $400,000 loan at 6%, 360-day basis, and the interest collected at a closing on
the 22nd of a 30-day month.

**Answer, and name the convention that could change (d).**

> **Solution.** (a) 400,000 × 0.06 ÷ 12 = **$2,000.00.**
>
> (b) P = I ÷ (R × T) = 18,750 ÷ 0.075 = **$250,000.**
>
> (c) R = I ÷ (P × T) = 20,800 ÷ 320,000 = **6.5%.**
>
> (d) Annual interest = $24,000. Per diem = 24,000 ÷ **360** = **$66.67.** Days from the 22nd through
> month end = 9. **9 × 66.67 = $600.03.**
>
> **Two conventions change (d) and both must be read rather than assumed:** whether the basis is **360
> or 365 days** — at 365 the per diem is $65.75 — and **which days are counted.** A licensee who assumes
> and is wrong has misstated a closing figure to a borrower.

### Problem 2

A borrower closing in March asks why his first payment is not due in April, and why his payoff quote
five years later exceeds his last statement balance. He also says that after five years of payments he
expected to have paid down "at least a fifth" of a $400,000 loan at 6%.

**Explain all three.**

> **Solution.** **Interest is paid in arrears** — a payment covers interest that has **already accrued**,
> not the period ahead. Interest for the remainder of March was collected at closing, April's interest
> accrues during April, and the May payment covers it. Hence the first payment in May.
>
> **The payoff includes interest through the payoff date**, which is why it exceeds the last statement
> balance — along with any other amounts owed under the note.
>
> **The principal expectation is the amortization pattern.** Each payment covers accrued interest first
> and the remainder reduces principal, so **early payments are almost entirely interest.** Month 1 here
> is $2,000.00 interest and $398.20 principal. Sixty payments totaling roughly $143,900 reduce the
> balance by roughly **$27,800** — about seven percent, not a fifth.
>
> **The same pattern explains the reverse:** a small additional principal payment early has a
> disproportionate effect, because it removes principal that would otherwise accrue interest for the
> entire remaining term.

### Problem 3

A borrower's note has an acceleration clause and an alienation clause. He misses three payments; a
different borrower deeds her property to her son. An originator tells both that "the lender can always
call the loan." A third borrower asks what his adjustable rate will be next year, and the originator
says it depends on the lender and on whether his credit has improved.

**Correct every statement.**

> **Solution.** **Acceleration and alienation respond to different events.** **Acceleration responds to
> default** — it makes the entire balance immediately due, and without it a lender could pursue only the
> missed payments, which is why foreclosure depends on it. **Alienation responds to transfer** — it
> permits the lender to call the loan due on transfer, which is why loans are generally not assumable
> without consent. The missed payments trigger the first; the deed to the son triggers the second.
>
> **"A lender can always call the loan" is wrong.** The lender's rights come from **the clauses actually
> in the instrument** and the events that trigger them.
>
> **The adjustable rate is index plus margin, subject to the caps.** Not the lender's discretion, and
> **not the borrower's credit at that time** — the margin was fixed at origination. The borrower should
> also be shown his **periodic cap**, his **lifetime cap**, and any initial cap or floor.
>
> **A licensee who cannot explain a borrower's own index, margin, and caps should not be presenting that
> product.**

### Problem 4

Two offers on a $400,000 loan. Offer A: 6.25%, no points, $1,200 in lender fees. Offer B: 5.875%, 2
points, $1,200 in lender fees, payment $140 lower. The originator tells the borrower B is "the cheaper
loan because the APR is lower."

**Compute what matters and assess the statement.**

> **Solution.** **The points cost 400,000 × 0.02 = $8,000** — one point is one percent **of the loan
> amount**, not the price.
>
> **Break-even: 8,000 ÷ 140 = 57 months**, about 4 years 9 months. A borrower holding longer than that
> comes out ahead on B; one selling or refinancing sooner does not.
>
> **"The cheaper loan" is not the originator's conclusion to draw.** The **APR** exists to make offers
> comparable by including certain finance charges beyond interest — but **it assumes the loan runs its
> full term**, which makes it a poor guide for a borrower who will move in five years. It is also **not
> the note rate**, does not include every cost, and behaves oddly on adjustable loans.
>
> **What the licensee does:** present both on a common basis — same amount, term, and structure; note
> rate, payment, points, and lender charges each stated separately; the break-even; and the APR
> identified as a comparison tool with its assumptions. Then let the borrower and his advisors choose.
>
> A comparison that omitted the $8,000 while showing the lower payment would be misleading **even though
> every individual number in it was accurate.**

### Problem 5

A $920,000 purchase with 20% down. The appraisal comes in at $900,000. Separately, a refinance borrower
with a $920,000 home and a $600,000 first asks how much cash he can take out at 80% CLTV, and whether
the proceeds are taxable income.

**Compute both and correct any assumption.**

> **Solution.** **Purchase.** Lenders lend against **the lower of appraised value and purchase price** —
> here $900,000. At 80%: 900,000 × 0.80 = **$720,000**, against the $736,000 planned. **A $16,000
> shortfall.**
>
> His options: bring the funds, renegotiate the price, negotiate another resolution, or cancel if a
> contingency permits and its procedure is followed. **The price does not adjust automatically, the loan
> is not denied, and no one is entitled to a second appraisal as of right.**
>
> **Refinance.** There is no purchase price, so **value is the appraised value** — which is why a
> refinance is more exposed to the appraisal outcome, and why the amount available **is not knowable
> until the appraisal is in.** Assuming $920,000: 920,000 × 0.80 = $736,000 available, less the $600,000
> payoff = **$136,000 before costs.**
>
> **The proceeds are borrowed money, not income.** The tax treatment is a question for **a tax
> professional**, and the licensee should say so rather than answering. And the borrower should
> understand that **equity converted to debt is gone from the equity side** — the property has not
> changed.

### Problem 6

A borrower is quoted "$2,398 a month" on a $400,000 loan at 6% for 30 years. Later he sees a figure of
$3,928 and says he was misled. His California property taxes were quoted from the seller's current bill.

**Build the correct figure and identify the two failures.**

> **Solution.** **P&I from the factor:** 400,000 ÷ 1,000 = 400 × 5.9955 = **$2,398.20.** (The recurring
> error is forgetting to divide by 1,000 first.)
>
> **PITI:** P&I $2,398.20 + taxes $958 + hazard insurance $140 + mortgage insurance $122 + association
> dues $310 = **$3,928.20.**
>
> **The first failure is quoting P&I as the payment.** A borrower quoted principal and interest and then
> shown PITI **will feel misled**, which is why the full housing payment belongs in the first
> conversation.
>
> **The second is the California property tax line**, which is the one most often understated: **a
> buyer's taxes are based on a new assessed value established on the change in ownership, not the
> seller's bill.** Quoting the seller's number can be wrong by a multiple, and the estimate belongs with
> the county assessor and a tax professional.
>
> **Every figure carries its basis** — loan amount, rate, term, whether it is P&I or PITI, and what is
> estimated. **A quote without its assumptions is a number the borrower will hold you to.**

### Problem 7

Gross monthly income $9,000. PITI $2,520; car $480; student loan $300; card minimums $120. The
originator computes the ratios and tells the borrower he "qualifies easily, since 38% is well under the
43% limit." Separately, the borrower asks whether a 15-year term is worth it.

**Compute, correct, and present the term comparison.**

> **Solution.** **Front-end:** 2,520 ÷ 9,000 = **28%.** **Back-end:** (2,520 + 480 + 300 + 120) ÷ 9,000
> = 3,420 ÷ 9,000 = **38%.**
>
> **Stating a threshold as a rule is the error.** Thresholds vary by program, by compensating factors,
> and over time. A licensee **computes and explains the ratios** and leaves the qualifying determination
> to underwriting — and telling a borrower he "qualifies easily" is an underwriting conclusion the
> licensee is not authorized to make.
>
> **The term comparison on $400,000 at 6%**, which the licensee may compute and present:
>
> 30 years: payment ≈ **$2,398**, total interest ≈ **$463,400.**
> 20 years: ≈ **$2,866**, ≈ **$287,800.**
> 15 years: ≈ **$3,375**, ≈ **$207,600.**
>
> **The payment rises about 41% from 30 to 15 years; the total interest falls by more than half.** That
> is the comparison the borrower is trying to understand — and **which to choose** is his decision with
> his advisors.

### Problem 8

An investor is offered a $200,000 note bearing 7% interest-only, at three prices: par, a 10% discount,
and a $210,000 premium. He says a 7% note yields 7% regardless of what he pays for it.

**Compute each yield and explain the relationship.**

> **Solution.** The note pays 200,000 × 0.07 = **$14,000 a year** at every price — that is fixed by the
> note.
>
> **At par ($200,000):** 14,000 ÷ 200,000 = **7.00%.**
> **At a 10% discount ($180,000):** 14,000 ÷ 180,000 = **7.78%.**
> **At a premium ($210,000):** 14,000 ÷ 210,000 = **6.67%.**
>
> **He is wrong: the note rate is fixed, but the yield depends on what he paid.** **Buying at a discount
> raises the yield; buying at a premium lowers it** — price and yield move in opposite directions for a
> given income stream, the same inverse relationship as a capitalization rate and value.
>
> **The same mechanism explains discount points from the lender's side:** the lender advances less than
> face while receiving the stated payments, which raises its yield. That is why a rate buydown **has a
> price rather than being a favor**, and it is worth understanding before presenting one.

## Chapter summary

**Simple interest is I = P × R × T**, solvable in four directions, and per diem interest depends on a
day-count basis that must be read rather than assumed.

**Mortgage interest is paid in arrears**, which explains the first payment date and the payoff figure.

**Each amortized payment covers accrued interest first**, so early payments are nearly all interest —
which explains slow principal reduction, payoff surprise, and why early prepayment has an outsized
effect.

**Negative amortization means paying while losing ground**, with a recast producing a large sudden
increase. Present the mechanism plainly or do not present the product.

**The note is the debt; the security instrument is the remedy.** Acceleration responds to default;
alienation responds to transfer.

**An adjustable rate is index plus margin within caps** — never lender discretion and never the
borrower's later credit. Know your borrower's index, margin, and caps.

**A point is one percent of the loan.** Compute the break-even; leave the decision to the borrower.

**The APR is a comparison tool with defined contents and full-term assumptions** — not the note rate,
not every cost, and not a conclusion about which loan is cheaper for this borrower.

**Present offers on a common basis**, because a comparison omitting a fee difference misleads even when
every number in it is accurate.

**Lenders lend against the lower of value or price**, and on a refinance value is the appraisal — which
is why cash-out availability is unknown until it arrives.

**Cash-out proceeds are borrowed money**, and the tax treatment goes to a tax professional.

**Compute payments from a factor after dividing by 1,000, quote PITI rather than P&I**, and remember
that California property taxes reset on transfer.

**Compute qualifying ratios; never state a threshold as a rule.**

**Discount raises yield, premium lowers it** — which is also why a bought-down rate has a price.

**Every figure carries its basis, and every figure gets a reasonableness check** before it reaches a
borrower.
