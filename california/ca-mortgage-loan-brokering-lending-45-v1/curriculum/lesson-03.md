## 1. Deed of trust structure

### 1.1 What a security instrument does

A security instrument pledges real property as security for an obligation. It does not create the obligation — **the note does that** — and it has no independent existence: a security instrument without a note secures nothing.

> **The note is the debt. The security instrument is the remedy.**

### 1.2 The California instrument

California residential and commercial lending overwhelmingly uses the **deed of trust**, and it involves **three** parties.

| Party | Role |
|---|---|
| **Trustor** | The borrower, who conveys title in trust as security |
| **Beneficiary** | The lender, for whose benefit the security is held |
| **Trustee** | A neutral third party holding the **power of sale** |

**The memory device:** the trus**tor** gives the security; the trus**tee** holds the power; the bene**ficiary** benefits.

### 1.3 What the instrument contains

| Element | Function |
|---|---|
| **Parties** | Trustor, trustee, beneficiary |
| **Legal description** | The property secured — **not a street address** |
| **Granting language** | The conveyance in trust |
| **Reference to the obligation** | Identifying the note secured |
| **Power of sale** | The trustee's authority to sell on default |
| **Borrower covenants** | Maintain the property, pay taxes, maintain insurance, not commit waste |
| **Acceleration and default provisions** | What constitutes default and its consequence |
| **Alienation clause** | Rights on transfer |
| **Assignment of rents** | Where included, giving the beneficiary rights to rents on default |
| **Future advances / dragnet clause** | Where included, securing additional or later obligations |

### 1.4 The clauses that surprise borrowers

**The assignment of rents.** On income property, the beneficiary may have the right to collect rents on default. A borrower who assumes rents are his regardless has not read the instrument.

**The dragnet (future advances) clause.** Where present, it can secure obligations beyond the original loan. Its scope and enforceability are legal questions, and **a licensee who tells a borrower it "only covers this loan" has interpreted an instrument.**

**Covenants to maintain insurance and pay taxes.** Failure is a **default** independent of payment — a borrower current on payments whose hazard policy lapsed is in default under the instrument, and the lender may force-place coverage at the borrower's cost.

### 1.5 What the borrower retains

**The borrower possesses, uses, and may transfer the property**, subject to the security interest and to the alienation clause. The deed of trust does not turn the lender into an owner.

That is what Section 2's lien-theory discussion means in practice: **the borrower is the owner; the lender holds a claim.**

### 1.6 Checkpoint

You should be able to name the three parties and their roles, list the operative elements of the instrument, and identify a default that does not involve missing a payment.

## 2. Mortgage comparison

### 2.1 The two-party instrument

A **mortgage** involves two parties: **mortgagor** (borrower) and **mortgagee** (lender). There is no trustee and, ordinarily, no power of sale.

| | Deed of trust | Mortgage |
|---|---|---|
| Parties | **Three** — trustor, trustee, beneficiary | **Two** — mortgagor, mortgagee |
| Power of sale | Held by the trustee | Generally absent |
| Foreclosure | Predominantly **nonjudicial** | **Judicial** |
| Speed | Faster | Slower |
| Deficiency judgment | Generally **not available** after a nonjudicial sale | May be available, subject to limits |
| Post-sale redemption | Generally **none** | A statutory redemption period may apply |
| Release on satisfaction | **Reconveyance** by the trustee | Satisfaction or release by the mortgagee |

### 2.2 Why the third party matters

**The trustee holds a power of sale** — authority to sell the property on default **without going to court.**

That single feature produces everything in Section 6: the speed of California foreclosure, the reinstatement window, and the deficiency consequences. **A licensee who understands why the trustee exists understands the rest of California's default framework.**

### 2.3 Title theory and lien theory

Jurisdictions describe the security relationship differently. Some treat the instrument as passing legal title to the lender until repayment; others treat it as creating a lien while the borrower retains title.

**California's deed of trust is generally treated as a lien for most practical purposes**, notwithstanding its form as a conveyance in trust.

For a licensee the practical consequence is the one already stated: **the borrower is the owner, and the lender holds a claim.** The distinction becomes visible in questions about possession, transfer, and what the lender may do before a foreclosure completes — all of which are legal questions.

### 2.4 Terminology in practice

Borrowers, and much national material, use "mortgage" for any loan secured by real property. In California the instrument is usually a deed of trust.

**A licensee should use the correct term in documents and should not correct a borrower's casual usage into a lecture** — but should know which instrument the transaction actually uses, because the default consequences differ.

### 2.5 Other security devices

**A land contract** — also called a contract for deed or installment land contract — is a financing arrangement in which the seller retains title until the price is paid. It is treated differently from a deed of trust, carries its own risks for both parties, and its consequences are legal questions.

**A security agreement and financing statement** cover personal property, which matters where a transaction includes both real and personal property — a common circumstance in commercial and mixed transactions.

### 2.6 Checkpoint

You should be able to compare the two instruments on all seven dimensions and explain what the power of sale produces.

## 3. Trustor, beneficiary, and trustee

### 3.1 The trustor

The borrower. Conveys title in trust as security, retains possession and use, and remains the owner for practical purposes.

**Obligations under the instrument** typically include paying the obligation, paying taxes and assessments, maintaining hazard insurance, maintaining the property, not committing waste, and complying with the alienation clause.

### 3.2 The beneficiary

The lender. Holds the secured claim, receives payments, and may enforce on default.

**What the beneficiary may do:** demand payment, declare a default, instruct the trustee to commence a nonjudicial sale, elect judicial foreclosure instead, consent or decline to consent under the alienation clause, and — where the instrument provides — collect rents on default and force-place insurance.

**What the beneficiary may not do:** take possession without a legal basis, sell without following the applicable procedure, or ignore the borrower-protective requirements that apply in the residential context.

**The beneficiary can change.** Notes are sold and beneficial interests assigned, which is why a borrower's payments may be directed to a servicer they never chose. **The obligation follows the note.**

### 3.3 The trustee

The party students understand least, and the one whose role most needs stating precisely.

**The trustee holds bare legal title for the limited purposes of the instrument**, and has essentially two functions:

1. **Reconvey** when the obligation is satisfied, on the beneficiary's direction.
2. **Sell** on default, under the power of sale, following the statutory procedure.

**The trustee is not a party's agent in the ordinary sense.** It acts on the beneficiary's instruction to commence a sale, and it owes duties in conducting one. It does not decide whether the borrower defaulted, negotiate, or advise anyone.

**The trustee can be substituted.** A beneficiary may substitute a trustee by recorded instrument, and in practice the trustee acting in a foreclosure is frequently a substituted trustee rather than the one originally named.

### 3.4 Servicers

Not a party to the instrument, but the party the borrower actually deals with. The **servicer** collects payments, maintains impound accounts, handles payoffs and loss mitigation, and communicates with the borrower.

**Servicing transfers**, sometimes repeatedly, and California and federal law impose requirements on servicers in the residential context — including contact and single-point-of-contact obligations in the foreclosure setting, addressed in Lesson 13.

**A borrower confused about who owns his loan and who services it is asking a reasonable question**, and the answer is that they can be different entities and both can change.

### 3.5 What a licensee says about the parties

**May:** identify the parties and explain each role mechanically; explain that the trustee holds a power of sale; explain that servicing may transfer.

**May not:** advise whether a default has occurred, whether a foreclosure is procedurally proper, whether a substitution was valid, or what a borrower's rights are against a beneficiary or servicer. **Every one of those is a legal conclusion**, and they arrive in exactly the moments when a borrower most wants a confident answer.

### 3.6 Checkpoint

You should be able to state each party's role and limits, explain what a trustee does and does not do, and distinguish the beneficiary from the servicer.

## 4. Recording and priority

### 4.1 What recording accomplishes

California maintains a public record of documents affecting real property, and the system answers one question: **who has notice of what?**

**Recording does not make a document valid, and failing to record does not make it invalid between the parties. Recording protects the recorded party against third parties.**

### 4.2 The three kinds of notice

| Type | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Actual notice** | The person in fact knows |
| **Constructive notice** | The law charges the person with knowing, because it was recorded |
| **Inquiry notice** | Facts that would prompt a reasonable person to investigate |

**Constructive notice is the engine.** A recorded document is knowable by anyone who looks, so the law treats everyone as knowing it.

**Inquiry notice is the one that surprises lenders**, because nothing was recorded: someone visibly in possession who is not the record owner, an occupant paying rent to a third party, evidence of recent construction. Each is a fact prompting investigation, and a party may be charged with what the investigation would have revealed.

### 4.3 The general priority rule

> **First in time, first in right** — as measured by recording, for those entitled to the recording system's protection.

The protection generally runs to a **bona fide purchaser or encumbrancer**: one taking **for value**, **in good faith**, and **without notice** — actual, constructive, or inquiry.

**A lender is an encumbrancer for value**, which is why a lender's title work and its diligence about what is visible on the property both matter.

### 4.4 The exceptions that defeat first-in-time

These are what a mortgage licensee actually needs, because each can put a lender behind something recorded later.

| Exception | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Property tax liens** | Generally take priority **regardless of when they attached** |
| **Mechanic's liens** | Priority **may relate back** to when work commenced — so a lien recorded after a deed of trust may outrank it **where that deed of trust attached after the work commenced**, and not where it was already attached and of record |
| **Subordination agreement** | A lienholder voluntarily takes a lower position |
| **Purchase-money priority** | Certain purchase-money security interests receive favored treatment |
| **Special assessment liens** | May carry their own priority treatment |

### 4.5 The mechanic's lien problem

The exception a mortgage broker must be able to recognize, because it is the one that surfaces on a file that looked clean.

**Priority may relate back to when work commenced** — a date earlier than the lien's recording — which means a deed of trust recorded in between can be outranked. And **lien rights arise from work rather than from a contract with the owner**, so subcontractors and suppliers the owner never dealt with can have them.

> **The intake question: has any work been done on this property recently, by whom, and is everyone paid?**
>
> A borrower who renovated last spring with an unresolved contractor dispute has a title problem that has not appeared yet — and a construction lender has a priority problem.

This is why lenders on construction and recently improved property require indemnities, title endorsements, and evidence of payment. **Whether a particular lien is valid or timely is a legal question**, and the licensee's role is to surface the facts early and route them to escrow, title, and counsel.

### 4.6 Position language

**First, second, and junior** describe recording position, not loan type. A "second" is simply a deed of trust recorded after another.

**Priority determines who is paid first from a limited fund and whose interest survives a foreclosure.** A junior lienholder wiped out by a senior foreclosure has lost its security whatever the merits of the debt.

### 4.7 The preliminary report

A **preliminary report** reports record title and states the terms on which the insurer would issue a policy, including its **exceptions.**

> **An exception is an item the policy will not insure against.**

Three responses wrong in every case: treating an item as routine because most properties have one; asking title to "remove" an exception; and predicting an easement's or restriction's effect on the borrower's plans.

**Distinguish exceptions from requirements.** A **requirement** is something the insurer needs before issuing — a reconveyance, a resolution, evidence of trustee authority — and **requirements are the items with deadlines attached.**

### 4.8 Checkpoint

You should be able to state the priority rule and its exceptions, explain the mechanic's lien relation-back, and distinguish an exception from a requirement.

## 5. Subordination and reconveyance

### 5.1 What subordination does

A **subordination agreement** is a lienholder's agreement to take a **lower priority position** than it would otherwise hold.

The recurring situations:

**Construction lending.** A seller who carried financing is asked to subordinate so a construction lender can record in first position.

**Refinancing a first with a second in place.** Paying off and re-recording a first would ordinarily move the existing second into first position. A **subordination agreement from the second** keeps the new first ahead of it, and without one the refinance may not close.

**Adding a new senior loan.**

### 5.2 Why it matters so much

**Priority determines who is paid first from a limited fund and whose interest survives a foreclosure.** A lienholder who subordinates has agreed to absorb a loss ahead of the party it moved behind.

> **Subordination is a significant decision, not a formality**, and "it's standard" is the single most expensive sentence a licensee can say about one.
>
> **Whether a party should subordinate is a decision for that party with counsel.** A licensee explains the mechanism and routes the decision.

### 5.3 Subordination clauses in the original instrument

Some instruments contain a **subordination clause** obligating the holder to subordinate to future financing meeting stated conditions. Their scope, the conditions, and their enforceability are legal questions.

**A seller carrying financing who signs an instrument with an open-ended subordination clause has agreed to something they may not have understood**, which is exactly why seller-carry documentation belongs with counsel rather than being assembled from a form. Lesson 12 develops this.

### 5.4 Reconveyance

When the obligation is satisfied, the beneficiary directs the trustee to reconvey, and a **reconveyance** is recorded releasing the deed of trust.

> **Payment of the debt does not clear the record. The recorded document does.**

A borrower who says a loan was paid off in 2016 has stated a fact about the debt and nothing about the title. **An unreleased deed of trust in a preliminary report requires a document**, and obtaining one from a lender that has merged, been acquired, or ceased to exist can take weeks.

**That is why the preliminary report belongs at the front of the queue.** An unreleased lien discovered on day twelve of a fourteen-day period is a formality where a resolution should have been.

### 5.5 Partial reconveyance and release clauses

In a **blanket** loan securing multiple parcels, a **release clause** permits individual parcels to be released as they are sold, on the terms the instrument states — typically a release price.

A **partial reconveyance** effects that release. Whether a particular parcel qualifies, and on what payment, is governed by the instrument.

### 5.6 Assumption, subject to, and wraparound

Three structures a mortgage licensee must be able to distinguish.

| Structure | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Assumption** | The buyer takes over the loan. Generally requires **lender consent** under the alienation clause, and **the seller remains liable unless released by novation** |
| **Subject to** | The buyer takes title **without assuming**; the loan remains the seller's obligation and remains secured by the property. Triggers the alienation clause |
| **Wraparound (all-inclusive)** | A new loan encompassing an existing one, with the new lender collecting and paying the underlying loan |

**The assumption point is the one sellers get wrong.** A seller who "let the buyer assume" and believes he is released has assumed a **novation** he did not obtain — and remains liable on a debt secured by property he no longer owns.

**Wraparound structures carry particular risk**, including exposure if the wrap holder fails to pay the underlying loan, and the alienation clause on the underlying loan. **They belong with counsel**, and Lesson 12 develops them.

### 5.7 Checkpoint

You should be able to explain why a refinance may require a subordination agreement, state what clears a paid deed of trust, and distinguish assumption from taking subject to.

## 6. Default remedies and security risk

### 6.1 What default is

A failure to perform an obligation under the note or the security instrument. **Non-payment is the common form**, and the instrument creates others: failure to pay taxes, failure to maintain insurance, waste, and unauthorized transfer triggering the alienation clause.

**Acceleration is what makes default consequential.** Without it a lender could pursue only missed payments; with it, the entire balance becomes due.

### 6.2 The two foreclosure paths

| | Nonjudicial | Judicial |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | The trustee's **power of sale** | A court action |
| Speed | Faster | Slower |
| Cost | Lower | Higher |
| Deficiency judgment | **Generally not available** | May be available, subject to limits |
| Post-sale redemption | **Generally none** | A statutory redemption period may apply |

> **The trade-off is the point.** A beneficiary proceeding nonjudicially gets speed and gives up the deficiency; one proceeding judicially may preserve a deficiency claim and accepts a slower, costlier process.
>
> **In California residential practice, nonjudicial foreclosure predominates.**

### 6.3 The nonjudicial sequence, in outline

The process is statutory and its details are legal. In outline:

1. **Default occurs**, and the beneficiary elects to proceed.
2. **A notice of default is recorded**, beginning the statutory period.
3. **A reinstatement period runs**, during which the borrower may generally cure by paying **the arrearage and permitted costs — not the accelerated balance.**
4. **A notice of sale is recorded, published, posted, and mailed**, after the applicable period.
5. **The trustee's sale occurs**, and title passes by **trustee's deed.**

**The reinstatement window is what borrowers misunderstand most.** For a period, curing the arrearage restores the loan. After that window closes, the route is generally **redemption before sale** — paying the full accelerated amount — which is a materially different sum.

California also imposes **borrower-protective requirements on servicers** in the residential foreclosure context, including contact and single-point-of-contact obligations. **The specifics are legal**, and a licensee's role is to know they exist and route the borrower to counsel and to a HUD-approved housing counselor.

### 6.4 Deficiency

A **deficiency** is the shortfall where sale proceeds do not satisfy the debt. **California restricts deficiency judgments substantially:**

- **Generally unavailable after a nonjudicial sale**, whatever the loan type.
- **Restricted for purchase money loans** in defined circumstances — protection connected to the loan having been used to acquire the property.
- **Subject to limits even in judicial foreclosure**, including valuation-based constraints.

**Whether a deficiency is available in a specific case is a legal question with significant consequences**, and it is exactly what a distressed borrower asks. The answer is a referral.

### 6.5 What happens to junior liens

A senior foreclosure generally **extinguishes junior liens as claims against the property** — while the underlying debts may survive as personal obligations, subject to the deficiency limits above.

**That is why priority matters and why junior lienholders monitor senior loans.** A second wiped out by a first's foreclosure has lost its security entirely.

It is also why a lender in second position prices for that risk, and why a broker arranging a second should be able to explain the exposure to an investor.

### 6.6 Security risk from the lender's and broker's side

The risks a secured lender carries, and that a broker arranging a loan should be able to identify:

| Risk | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Priority risk** | Being behind something unexpected — a tax lien, a relating-back mechanic's lien, an unrecorded interest with inquiry notice |
| **Title risk** | A defect in the chain, a forged instrument, an authority failure |
| **Value risk** | Collateral worth less than expected, or declining |
| **Occupancy and use risk** | A property used differently than represented, affecting value and program eligibility |
| **Insurance risk** | Lapsed or inadequate coverage, or unavailability in a mapped hazard area |
| **Condition risk** | Deferred maintenance, hazards, unpermitted work |
| **Legal risk** | Foreclosure limits, borrower protections, bankruptcy |
| **Fraud risk** | Identity, occupancy, income, appraisal, and straw-buyer patterns |
| **Documentation risk** | An instrument that does not secure what the parties intended, or a description error |

### 6.7 Bankruptcy, in outline

A borrower's bankruptcy filing generally triggers an **automatic stay** halting collection and foreclosure activity, and a secured creditor's treatment depends on the chapter and the facts.

**A licensee's role is recognition and immediate escalation.** Continuing collection or foreclosure activity after a filing can carry serious consequences, and the analysis is entirely legal.

### 6.8 What the broker actually contributes

Nothing in this section authorizes a licensee to advise on remedies. What a broker contributes is **surfacing the facts early enough that the right people can act:**

- Ask the recent-work question at intake, before a construction or refinance loan is arranged.
- Order the preliminary report at the front of the queue and read the exceptions and requirements.
- Reconcile the legal description across the instruments.
- Confirm insurance is in force and adequate.
- Escalate anything about identity, occupancy, authority, or a party who will not appear.
- Route every question about rights, remedies, and validity to counsel.

> **A defect surfaced at intake is a condition. The same defect surfaced after funding is a loss.**

### 6.9 What the security instrument means to the borrower in front of you

This lesson has treated the deed of trust structurally. **Borrowers experience it as a set of consequences**, and a broker who can state those plainly is doing the part of the job the documents do not do.

**What the borrower is actually agreeing to:**

1. **He is conveying an interest as security**, and Section 1 explains the mechanism. **He keeps possession and use, and he remains the owner for practical purposes** — which is the reassurance most borrowers need and rarely receive.
2. **The obligation is the note; the deed of trust is what secures it.** Section 3 separates them, and the separation matters: **the note is what he owes, and the instrument is what happens if he does not pay it.**
3. **Priority is determined by the recording system**, Section 4, which is why the order of recording is not a clerical matter and why a subordination — Section 5 — changes his position materially.
4. **A default has a defined sequence**, Section 6.3, with defined opportunities that depend on where in the sequence he is. **Reinstatement and redemption are different things with different amounts**, and the difference is the single most consequential thing a borrower in trouble does not know.
5. **Reconveyance is not automatic in the sense he assumes.** Section 5's mechanism should occur; **whether it did is a question with a record**, and a borrower who paid off a loan years ago and never confirmed may discover the problem at his next transaction.

**Three questions to answer before he signs, whether or not he asks:**

- **"What happens if I miss a payment?"** The sequence, stated accurately, without prediction about what a servicer will do.
- **"What if I want to pay it off early?"** Whether a prepayment provision applies, and what the instrument requires on payoff.
- **"What if I want a second loan later?"** Priority, and what a lender in second position is agreeing to.

> **The professional boundary holds throughout.** Section 6.8's point governs: **the broker explains how the instruments work; the legal effect on this borrower's rights, in his circumstances, is counsel's.** A broker who explains reinstatement as a concept is doing his job. **A broker who tells a delinquent borrower what he can do about his particular default is not.**

**And the reason this belongs at the end of a security-instrument lesson.** Every concept in Sections 1 through 5 exists in a document the borrower will sign without reading. **The broker is the only participant with both the knowledge to explain it and a reason to be in the room** — and Lesson 13's finding, that most servicing complaints are origination failures arriving late, begins with the explanation that was never given.

### 6.10 Checkpoint

You should be able to compare the foreclosure paths, explain the reinstatement window, state what happens to junior liens, and name the security risks a broker should surface.

## Practice problems

### Problem 1

A borrower is current on every payment. His hazard policy lapsed four months ago and he has not paid
the second installment of his property taxes. He tells his broker he "can't be in default — I've never
missed a payment."

**Assess.**

> **Solution.** **He may well be in default, and non-payment is only one form of it.**
>
> A deed of trust contains **borrower covenants** independent of the payment obligation: maintain the
> property, **pay taxes and assessments**, **maintain hazard insurance**, and not commit waste. Failure
> on any of them is a default under the instrument even where payments are current.
>
> **Two practical consequences follow.** The lender may **force-place coverage at the borrower's cost**,
> which is typically far more expensive than a policy he could obtain himself. And the **property tax
> lien** attaching is one that **generally takes priority regardless of when it attached** — so it sits
> ahead of the lender's deed of trust, which is why lenders care about it more than borrowers expect.
>
> **What the licensee does not do** is tell him whether a default has occurred or what the lender may
> do about it. Both are legal conclusions. Surface the facts, tell him to contact his servicer and
> restore coverage immediately, and route the rest.

### Problem 2

A borrower asks why California foreclosures are "so fast" compared with what his cousin experienced in
another state, and whether he will owe the difference if his house sells for less than he owes. His
loan was used to purchase the home.

**Explain the mechanism and address the deficiency question.**

> **Solution.** **The answer traces to the third party.** California uses a **deed of trust** with a
> **trustee holding a power of sale** — authority to sell on default **without going to court**. A
> two-party **mortgage** has no trustee and no power of sale, and is foreclosed **judicially**, which is
> slower and costlier.
>
> **The trade-off is the point.** A beneficiary proceeding **nonjudicially** gets speed and **gives up
> the deficiency**; one proceeding **judicially** may preserve a deficiency claim and accepts the slower
> process. Nonjudicial predominates in California residential practice, and the borrower may also have
> **no post-sale redemption period**, where a judicial foreclosure might carry one.
>
> **On the deficiency: the licensee explains the framework and refers the question.** California
> restricts deficiency judgments substantially — **generally unavailable after a nonjudicial sale**, and
> **restricted for purchase money loans** in defined circumstances, which his loan appears to be. But
> **whether a deficiency is available in his case is a legal question with significant consequences**,
> and the answer is a referral to counsel, not a reassurance.

### Problem 3

A borrower says he sent his payment to "the bank that owns my loan," received a letter from a company
he has never heard of, and does not know who to call. He also asks whether the trustee named on his
deed of trust can help him work something out, and notes a different trustee's name appeared on a
recent notice.

**Explain each.**

> **Solution.** **Owner and servicer can be different entities, and both can change.** Notes are sold
> and beneficial interests assigned — **the obligation follows the note** — and the **servicer**, which
> is not a party to the instrument at all, is the entity that collects payments, maintains impounds,
> handles payoffs and loss mitigation, and communicates with him. Servicing transfers, sometimes
> repeatedly.
>
> **The trustee cannot help him work anything out.** The trustee holds bare legal title for the limited
> purposes of the instrument and has essentially two functions: **reconvey** when the obligation is
> satisfied, and **sell** on default under the power of sale. It does **not** decide whether he
> defaulted, negotiate, or advise anyone.
>
> **The different name is ordinary.** A beneficiary may **substitute a trustee** by recorded instrument,
> and the trustee acting in a foreclosure is frequently a substituted trustee rather than the one
> originally named.
>
> **What the licensee must not do** is tell him whether a substitution was valid or a foreclosure
> procedurally proper — legal conclusions arriving exactly when he most wants a confident answer.

### Problem 4

A lender is arranging a refinance. The preliminary report is clean. The borrower mentions he completed
a $180,000 renovation four months ago and is in an unresolved dispute with the general contractor over
the final payment. Two subcontractors he never met have been calling.

**Identify the exposure and state what should happen.**

> **Solution.** **A clean report is a snapshot, not a forecast**, and mechanic's lien exposure is
> precisely what a current report can fail to show.
>
> **Two features make this the exception a mortgage licensee must recognize.** Priority **may relate
> back to when work commenced** — a date months before any lien is recorded — so **a lien recorded next
> month can outrank a deed of trust recorded before the lien, where that deed of trust attached after
> the work commenced.** The refinance being arranged here is exactly such a deed of trust: the work
> began four months ago and the new loan has not recorded yet. **What relation-back does not do is
> displace an encumbrance already attached and of record when the work began.** And **lien rights arise from the work rather
> than from a contract with the owner**, which is why subcontractors and suppliers the borrower never
> dealt with can have them.
>
> **This is why lenders on construction and recently improved property require indemnities, title
> endorsements, and evidence of payment** — and why the intake question is *has any work been done
> recently, by whom, and is everyone paid?*
>
> **What should happen:** surface it immediately to escrow, title, and counsel; obtain evidence of
> payment and any releases; and let title determine what it will insure. **Whether a particular lien is
> valid or timely is a legal question**, and the licensee's contribution is the timing — **a defect
> surfaced at intake is a condition; the same defect surfaced after funding is a loss.**

### Problem 5

A refinance is scheduled to close in eight days. The preliminary report, received this morning, shows
an existing second deed of trust and an unreleased first from 2016 that the borrower says was paid off
"years ago." The originator tells the borrower both are "just paperwork."

**Assess and state what each requires.**

> **Solution.** **Neither is paperwork, and the timing failure came first.**
>
> **The existing second creates a priority problem.** Paying off and re-recording the first would
> ordinarily move the existing second **into first position**. Keeping the new first ahead of it
> requires a **subordination agreement from the second** — and **without one the refinance may not
> close.** That is a decision for the second lienholder, not a formality: priority determines who is
> paid first from a limited fund and whose interest survives a foreclosure, and a lienholder who
> subordinates has agreed to absorb a loss ahead of the party it moved behind. **"It's standard" is the
> single most expensive sentence a licensee can say about a subordination.**
>
> **The 2016 first requires a recorded reconveyance.** **Payment of the debt does not clear the record;
> the document does** — and the borrower's recollection is not that document. Obtaining one from a
> lender that has merged, been acquired, or ceased to exist can take **weeks.**
>
> **The timing failure:** the preliminary report belongs at the **front** of the queue. Discovered with
> eight days left, an unreleased lien leaves a scramble where a resolution should have been.

### Problem 6

A seller carried a $200,000 second when he sold his rental three years ago. The buyer has now sold the
property to a third party who took title "subject to" both loans. The original seller believed he was
released when the first buyer "assumed" the underlying first. A wraparound structure has been proposed
for the next transfer.

**Untangle the positions.**

> **Solution.** **Three distinct structures, and the seller has misunderstood the one that mattered
> most to him.**
>
> **Assumption:** the buyer takes over the loan, generally requiring **lender consent** under the
> alienation clause — and **the seller remains liable unless released by novation.** A seller who "let
> the buyer assume" and believes he is released **has assumed a novation he did not obtain**, and
> remains liable on a debt secured by property he no longer owns. That is this seller's position.
>
> **Subject to:** the current owner took title **without assuming**. The loans remain the prior
> parties' obligations and remain secured by the property — so the debt and the ownership have been
> separated, and the alienation clause was triggered on the transfer.
>
> **Wraparound (all-inclusive):** a new loan encompassing an existing one, with the new lender
> collecting and paying the underlying loan. It carries particular risk, including **exposure if the
> wrap holder fails to pay the underlying loan** and the alienation clause on that loan.
>
> **All of it belongs with counsel.** A licensee explains the mechanics and does not assemble a
> seller-carry or wraparound structure from a form — and should note that an open-ended **subordination
> clause** in seller-carry documentation commits the seller to something he may not have understood.

### Problem 7

A borrower two payments behind asks his broker: can he still keep the house, what does the notice of
default mean, and can he just pay what he missed. The broker tells him the reinstatement period will
let him "catch up any time before the sale."

**Correct the advice and outline the sequence.**

> **Solution.** **The advice conflates two different windows, and the difference is a large sum of
> money.**
>
> **The nonjudicial sequence in outline:** default occurs and the beneficiary elects to proceed; **a
> notice of default is recorded**, beginning the statutory period; **a reinstatement period runs**,
> during which the borrower may generally cure by paying **the arrearage and permitted costs — not the
> accelerated balance**; a **notice of sale** is recorded, published, posted, and mailed; and the
> **trustee's sale** occurs, with title passing by **trustee's deed.**
>
> **"Any time before the sale" is wrong.** After the reinstatement window closes, the route is
> generally **redemption before sale** — paying the **full accelerated amount** — which is a materially
> different sum from the arrearage. That distinction is the one borrowers misunderstand most, and
> stating it wrongly can cost someone their home.
>
> **What the broker actually does:** explain that the windows differ, and **refer immediately and in
> writing** — to counsel, and to a **HUD-approved housing counselor.** California also imposes
> borrower-protective requirements on servicers in the residential foreclosure context, including
> contact and single-point-of-contact obligations, and **the specifics are legal.** Predict nothing.
>
> **And if a second lienholder asks whether the first's foreclosure affects him:** a senior foreclosure
> generally **extinguishes junior liens as claims against the property**, while the underlying debts may
> survive as personal obligations, subject to California's deficiency restrictions. He has lost his
> security entirely — which is why junior lienholders monitor senior loans, and why a broker arranging a
> second should explain that exposure to an investor before the money is placed.

### Problem 8

A broker is arranging a loan on a property where: the borrower will not appear in person and acts
through an intermediary; the occupant paying rent is not the record owner; the legal description on
the draft deed of trust differs from the preliminary report; hazard coverage was declined because the
property is in a mapped fire area; and the borrower's counsel mentions a bankruptcy filing "next
month."

**Identify each risk and state the broker's contribution.**

> **Solution.** **Six risks, and the last requires immediate action.**
>
> **Fraud and identity risk** — a principal who never appears and acts only through an intermediary is
> a recognized pattern. **Escalate to the broker; do not investigate alone or confront anyone.**
>
> **Inquiry notice** — an occupant who is not the record owner is a fact that would prompt a reasonable
> person to investigate, and a party may be charged with **what the investigation would have revealed**,
> recorded or not. It also threatens **bona fide encumbrancer** status, which requires taking for value,
> in good faith, and **without notice.**
>
> **Documentation risk** — a description mismatch means the instrument may not secure what the parties
> intend. Reconcile the description across the instruments before anything records.
>
> **Insurance risk** — coverage unavailability in a mapped hazard area is now a common cause of
> transactions failing, and it must be resolved early rather than at funding.
>
> **Legal risk and the automatic stay** — a bankruptcy filing generally triggers an **automatic stay**
> halting collection and foreclosure activity, and continuing activity after a filing can carry serious
> consequences. **Recognize and escalate immediately**; the analysis is entirely legal.
>
> **What the broker contributes is timing, not advice:** ask the recent-work question at intake, order
> the preliminary report first and read its exceptions and requirements, reconcile the description,
> confirm insurance is in force, escalate anything about identity, occupancy, or authority, and route
> every question about rights and validity to counsel. **A defect surfaced at intake is a condition; the
> same defect surfaced after funding is a loss.**

## Chapter summary

**The note is the debt; the security instrument is the remedy**, and security without a note secures
nothing.

**California uses a three-party deed of trust**, and the **trustee's power of sale** produces
nonjudicial foreclosure, the reinstatement window, and the deficiency consequences.

**Borrower covenants create defaults that do not involve missing a payment** — taxes, insurance, waste.

**A mortgage is a two-party instrument foreclosed judicially**, trading speed for a possible deficiency
and a redemption period.

**California's deed of trust is treated as a lien:** the borrower is the owner and the lender holds a
claim.

**The trustee reconveys and sells, and does nothing else** — it does not decide, negotiate, or advise.
It can be substituted. **The servicer is not a party to the instrument**, and owner and servicer can
both change.

**Recording answers who has notice.** Constructive notice comes from the record; **inquiry notice**
comes from facts on the ground, and it threatens bona fide encumbrancer status.

**First in time, first in right — subject to tax lien priority, mechanic's lien relation-back,
subordination, and purchase-money priority.** The mechanic's lien is the one a mortgage licensee must
recognize, and the intake question is whether work was recently done and everyone is paid.

**An exception is a risk not insured; a requirement is what must be done before issuing** — and
requirements carry the deadlines.

**Subordination is a significant decision for the subordinating party with counsel**, never a formality
— and a refinance with an existing second usually needs one.

**Payment does not clear the record; a recorded reconveyance does**, and obtaining one can take weeks.

**Assumption leaves the seller liable without a novation; subject to separates the debt from the
ownership; wraparounds carry their own exposure.** All belong with counsel.

**Nonjudicial foreclosure trades the deficiency for speed.** Reinstatement cures the arrearage;
redemption requires the accelerated balance — and confusing the two can cost someone their home.

**A senior foreclosure generally wipes out junior liens as claims against the property.**

**A bankruptcy filing generally triggers an automatic stay.** Recognize and escalate immediately.

**The broker's contribution is surfacing facts early**, because a defect surfaced at intake is a
condition and the same defect surfaced after funding is a loss.
