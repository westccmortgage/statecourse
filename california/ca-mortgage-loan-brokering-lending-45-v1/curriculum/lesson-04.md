## 1. Primary and secondary markets

### 1.1 The two markets

**The primary market** is where loans are originated — the transaction between the lender and the borrower.

**The secondary market** is where existing loans are bought and sold between investors.

### 1.2 What the secondary market is for

**Liquidity.** A lender who can sell a loan recovers its capital and can lend again, so the supply of mortgage credit does not depend on any single lender's balance sheet or deposit base.

Without it, mortgage credit would be limited by how much capital each lender could hold for thirty years. With it, capital cycles.

### 1.3 The participants

| Participant | Function |
|---|---|
| **Government-sponsored enterprises** | Purchase qualifying loans from lenders and package them for investors |
| **Government-related issuers** | Guarantee securities backed by government-insured or guaranteed loans |
| **Private investors and funds** | Purchase loans and mortgage-backed securities |
| **Servicers** | Administer loans after sale, frequently as a separately traded right |

**Mortgage-backed securities** are the mechanism: pools of loans are securitized and sold to investors, who receive the cash flows. That is how capital markets money reaches a borrower in Bakersfield.

### 1.4 The consequence that matters most to an originator

> **Underwriting standards flow backward from the secondary market.**

Lenders originate to standards that make loans **saleable**. That is why documentation requirements can feel disconnected from a particular borrower's obvious ability to pay: the question is not only whether this borrower will repay, but whether the loan will meet the purchaser's requirements.

**Three things follow, and they explain most originator frustration:**

- **A documentation requirement that seems pointless may exist because an investor requires it**, not because the underwriter doubts the borrower.
- **Standards change**, sometimes quickly, because investor appetite changes.
- **"But he obviously qualifies" is not an argument** that reaches the requirement.

### 1.5 Conforming and non-conforming

**A conforming loan** meets the purchasing standards of the enterprises that buy loans — including size limits that vary by area, and credit, documentation, and property requirements.

**A non-conforming loan** does not, for any reason. The recurring subcategories:

| Type | Why it does not conform |
|---|---|
| **Jumbo** | Exceeds the applicable size limit |
| **Non-QM** | Documentation or product features outside the qualified mortgage parameters |
| **Portfolio** | Held by the originating lender rather than sold, so it follows that lender's own standards |
| **Private / hard money** | Funded by private investors on their own criteria |

**Non-conforming does not mean improper.** It means the loan is not saleable into the standard channels, which generally makes it more expensive and sometimes makes it the only workable option.

### 1.6 Servicing

**Servicing is a separate right that can be sold apart from the loan.** A borrower's loan may be owned by one entity and serviced by another, and either can change.

**A borrower confused about who owns his loan and who services it is asking a reasonable question**, and Lesson 13 develops the servicing obligations.

### 1.7 Checkpoint

You should be able to explain what the secondary market provides, state why underwriting standards flow backward from it, and distinguish conforming from non-conforming.

## 2. Depository and nondepository sources

### 2.1 The sources of mortgage credit

| Source | Character |
|---|---|
| **Depository institutions** | Banks, savings institutions, and credit unions lending from deposits and other funds |
| **Mortgage bankers** | Originate and **fund** loans with their own or warehouse funds, frequently selling them |
| **Mortgage brokers** | **Arrange** loans between borrowers and lenders; do not lend their own funds |
| **Private and hard money lenders** | Individuals or funds, typically shorter term and higher rate, often collateral-focused |
| **Seller financing** | The seller carries all or part of the price |
| **Institutional investors** | Insurance companies, pension funds, and others, principally in commercial lending |

### 2.2 Banker and broker: the distinction that matters

> **A mortgage banker funds the loan. A mortgage broker arranges it.**

The consequences a licensee should be able to state:

| | Mortgage banker | Mortgage broker |
|---|---|---|
| Funds the loan | Yes, at closing | No |
| Underwrites | Frequently, to investor guidelines | No — the lender does |
| Lender of record | Itself | The funding lender |
| Access to products | Its own menu | Multiple lenders' menus |
| Compensation | From the loan's economics | Disclosed compensation, addressed in Lesson 09 |

**A broker's value proposition is access and fit.** A broker's compliance exposure is correspondingly about **what was represented, what was disclosed, and how compensation worked** — which is why Lessons 08 and 09 concentrate there.

**A licensee must not describe the firm as a lender if it is a broker.** A borrower who believes he is dealing with the lender misunderstands who decides, who is paid, and who holds his file.

### 2.3 Warehouse lines and correspondent lending

**A warehouse line** is short-term credit a mortgage banker uses to fund loans before selling them. It explains how a firm that is not a depository can fund at closing.

**Correspondent lending** is a middle position: the firm originates and funds in its own name to another lender's guidelines, then sells the loan to that lender.

**Table funding** is an arrangement in which a loan closes in one party's name but is funded by another at closing. **Whether a particular arrangement is table funding, and what disclosure it requires, is a compliance question**, and its treatment is one of the recurring subjects of examination.

### 2.4 Why the source affects the borrower

Not merely academic. The source determines:

- **What products are available** and on what terms.
- **How quickly decisions are made** and by whom.
- **What happens if the file has an exception** — a portfolio lender may accommodate what a conforming channel cannot.
- **Where servicing goes.**
- **How compensation works**, and therefore what must be disclosed.

### 2.5 Private and hard money

Lending funded by private investors, typically shorter term, higher rate, and focused more on collateral than on the borrower's income.

**Legitimate uses:** bridge financing, transactions that cannot wait for conventional timelines, property conditions no conforming channel will accept, and business-purpose transactions.

**The risks**, developed in Lesson 12: cost, short terms and balloons, and the possibility of a borrower placed in a product that does not fit because it was the product available.

> **A licensee arranging private money must be able to explain why the borrower is in that product rather than a conventional one, and the file should show it.**

### 2.6 Checkpoint

You should be able to distinguish a banker from a broker and state the consequences, explain what a warehouse line does, and state what a licensee must not represent about the firm.

## 3. Conventional, FHA, and VA orientation

### 3.1 Conventional financing

**A conventional loan is one not insured or guaranteed by a government agency.**

Terms vary by the borrower's credit profile, the down payment, the property type, and occupancy. Where the down payment is below a program threshold, **mortgage insurance** is commonly required.

> **Mortgage insurance protects the lender.** The borrower pays for it and receives access to financing at a lower down payment in exchange.

Students and borrowers consistently get that backwards, and correcting it is part of presenting the product honestly.

### 3.2 Government-backed programs: the common structure

**An agency insures or guarantees the lender against certain losses**, reducing the lender's risk and supporting terms — notably lower down payments — that qualifying borrowers might not otherwise obtain.

**Three points licensees regularly misstate:**

1. **The government is not the lender.** Private lenders make the loans.
2. **The loans bear interest** on ordinary terms.
3. **They are not restricted to new construction**, and not to first-time buyers as a general matter.

### 3.3 FHA, in outline

**FHA insures** loans made by approved lenders. Characteristic features:

- **Lower down payment** than most conventional programs.
- **Mortgage insurance** — both an upfront amount and an annual amount collected monthly — which is a different structure from conventional mortgage insurance and can behave differently over the life of the loan.
- **Property condition and appraisal requirements** the property must satisfy.
- **Loan limits** that vary by area.

### 3.4 VA, in outline

**VA guarantees** loans made by approved lenders for eligible veterans, service members, and certain surviving spouses.

- **Eligibility is based on service**, established by a certificate of eligibility.
- **No down payment** is required in many cases.
- **No monthly mortgage insurance**, though a **funding fee** typically applies, with exemptions.
- **Property condition and appraisal requirements** apply, with the valuation issued as a notice of value.

**The entitlement can be restored and reused**, which matters to a veteran who has used it before, and is a question for the VA and the lender rather than the licensee's to compute.

### 3.5 The point that affects a seller's decision

**Government programs commonly carry property condition requirements**, and a property with deferred maintenance may not satisfy them.

> **That is a closing-probability question, not a preference.**

A listing agent advising a seller to weigh offers should be able to explain that in neutral terms — and must not translate it into a preference against buyers using those programs, which raises the fair lending and fair housing issues Lesson 14 develops.

### 3.6 Other programs

**USDA** programs support financing in eligible rural areas for qualifying borrowers.

**State and local programs**, including California housing finance and down payment assistance programs, carry their own eligibility, occupancy, and repayment terms — and **assistance that is a silent second or a shared-appreciation obligation is a real obligation** the borrower must understand.

**A licensee presenting assistance must present what it costs and what it requires**, not only what it provides.

### 3.7 What a licensee may say about programs

**May:** explain the general characteristics of each program type; explain what mortgage insurance is and whom it protects; explain that eligibility and property requirements exist and where they are determined; recommend the borrower speak with more than one lender.

**May not:** determine eligibility; state a qualifying threshold as a rule; predict what a lender or agency will approve; recommend a specific program as suitable without the analysis Section 6 describes; or advise on the tax treatment of anything.

### 3.8 Checkpoint

You should be able to state the common structure of government-backed programs, correct the three misstatements, and explain whom mortgage insurance protects.

## 4. Fixed, adjustable, and balloon structures

### 4.1 The structures

| Structure | Behavior | The borrower's exposure |
|---|---|---|
| **Fixed rate** | Rate and payment fixed for the term | None to rate movement; pays for that certainty |
| **Adjustable rate** | Rate changes per an index plus a margin, within caps | Payment changes over time |
| **Hybrid** | Fixed for an initial period, then adjustable | None during the fixed period; full exposure after |
| **Interest-only period** | Payments cover interest only for a period | **Principal is not retired**, and the payment jumps at the end |
| **Balloon** | Payments do not retire principal by maturity | **A large sum due at maturity** |
| **Negative amortization** | Payment does not cover accrued interest | **The balance grows**, with a recast increase |

### 4.2 The adjustable-rate terms

Four terms determine the rate at every adjustment, and **a licensee who cannot state a borrower's own four should not be presenting the product.**

| Term | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Index** | The published measure the rate follows |
| **Margin** | The amount added to the index — **set at origination and fixed** |
| **Adjustment frequency** | How often the rate resets |
| **Caps** | **Periodic** caps limit change at one adjustment; a **lifetime** cap limits total movement |

> **The rate at adjustment is the index plus the margin, subject to the caps.** Not the lender's discretion, and **not the borrower's credit at that time.**

Some notes add an **initial cap** applying to the first adjustment, and a **floor** below which the rate will not fall.

### 4.3 The calculation a borrower needs

Not "what will rates do" — nobody knows — but **what happens if they move.**

> **A 5/6 hybrid: 6.0% start, margin 2.75%, index currently 4.50%, caps 2/1/5.**
>
> **Fully indexed rate today:** 4.50 + 2.75 = **7.25%.** The start rate is below it.
>
> **First adjustment, worst case:** limited by the 2% initial cap → **8.0%.**
> **Subsequent adjustments:** limited by the 1% periodic cap.
> **Ceiling:** 6.0 + 5 = **11.0%** lifetime.
>
> On a $500,000 balance, the payment at 6.0% is roughly $2,998; at 8.0% roughly $3,619; at the 11.0% ceiling roughly $4,672.

**That table is the honest presentation.** A borrower who has seen the worst case and accepted it has made a decision. One who was told "rates should come down" has been given a prediction.

### 4.4 What a licensee may never do with rates

**Predict them.** Mortgage rates respond to broader capital market conditions and are not set by any single institution.

> **"Rates will be lower in the spring, you can refinance then" is a prediction stated as certainty, on which a borrower may accept a payment he cannot otherwise afford.**

It is also the sentence behind a large share of adjustable-rate complaints.

### 4.5 Interest-only and balloon exposure

**An interest-only period** produces a lower initial payment and retires no principal — so at the end, the borrower faces a payment recalculated to amortize the **full original balance** over the **remaining, shorter** term. The increase is larger than borrowers expect.

**A balloon** requires a large payment at maturity. The borrower's realistic paths are to refinance, sell, or pay it — and **all three depend on conditions at maturity**, including his credit, the property's value, and the availability of credit.

> **Refinance risk is the exposure to name.** A balloon maturing in an unfavorable market is the structure's whole risk, and a borrower who assumes he will "just refinance" has assumed a market.

### 4.6 Rate locks

**A lock** fixes the rate for a period, and it is a recurring source of borrower complaints.

What a borrower must understand **in writing, before he relies on it**: what rate and points are locked, **for how long**, what happens if the loan does not close in time, whether an extension is available and at what cost, and what circumstances can void it.

> **A lock is not a guarantee that the loan will close, and a lock that expires because the file moved slowly is a foreseeable event rather than an accident.**

### 4.7 Buydowns

**A permanent buydown** uses discount points to lower the note rate for the life of the loan — the Lesson 02 break-even calculation applies.

**A temporary buydown** reduces the payment for an initial period using funds placed in an account, after which the payment rises to the note rate amount.

**The disclosure obligation is to present the payment after the buydown ends, with the same prominence as the initial payment.** A borrower who budgeted to the first-year payment and did not register the third-year payment has been sold rather than advised.

### 4.8 Checkpoint

You should be able to compute a fully indexed rate and a worst-case path, name the four adjustable terms, and explain interest-only, balloon, and buydown exposure to a borrower.

## 5. Owner-occupied and business-purpose distinctions

### 5.1 Why this distinction carries so much

**Consumer residential mortgage requirements — including the MLO endorsement requirement and much of the federal disclosure regime — attach to consumer-purpose loans secured by a dwelling.** Loans made **primarily for a business purpose** are treated differently.

That makes the classification consequential, and it makes it a target for misuse.

### 5.2 Occupancy

**Occupancy** — owner-occupied, second home, or investment — affects pricing, program eligibility, and which consumer protections apply.

> **It is therefore a material fact the lender relies on, and misstating it is loan fraud.**

A borrower who asks a licensee to record owner-occupancy on a property he intends to rent is asking for participation in that fraud. **Decline plainly, explain why, and record the loan as it actually is.**

**Whether he could afford the payment either way is beside the point.** The misstatement is not about whether he will pay; it is about obtaining terms on a false representation of the risk being priced.

### 5.3 Occupancy is a fact, not a category on a form

The recurring patterns a licensee should recognize:

| Pattern | What it suggests |
|---|---|
| A "primary residence" the borrower will not move into | Occupancy misrepresentation |
| A second home in the same city as the primary | Investment property in another name |
| A lease on the subject property dated **before** the application | The file contains its own contradiction |
| A borrower who cannot describe the property's location relative to his work | Straw or absentee arrangement |
| Multiple simultaneous "primary residence" applications | Coordinated misrepresentation |

**The third one is different in kind from a missing document.** It is not a paperwork gap — **the file itself contains the evidence contradicting a material representation to the lender.** The response is escalation, not a judgment call about whether it matters.

### 5.4 Business purpose

A loan is business-purpose when it is made **primarily for a business, commercial, agricultural, or investment purpose** rather than for personal, family, or household purposes.

**The determination rests on the actual purpose and the surrounding facts**, and the analysis considers what the proceeds are used for, the borrower's relationship to the property and the activity, and the transaction's overall character.

### 5.5 What a certification does and does not establish

> **A borrower's signature on a business-purpose certification records his statement. It does not establish the fact.**

A file assembled around a certification that the surrounding facts contradict is a file with a problem in it — visible to any examiner who reads the occupancy line, the intended use, or the lease dated before the application.

### 5.6 The errors run in both directions

**Treating a consumer loan as business-purpose** — because the borrower signed a certification, because the property is a rental, or because a manager instructed it. This is the more common error and the more serious one, because it strips protections the borrower was entitled to.

**Treating every investment property loan as exempt from everything** — which sweeps in requirements that still apply. **A business-purpose classification does not suspend fair lending obligations, licensing requirements that attach independently, fraud prohibitions, or the firm's own standards.**

### 5.7 Where the line is genuinely hard

Some transactions are genuinely close: a borrower refinancing a rental he once occupied; a cash-out on a primary residence to fund a business; a loan on a property with both a residence and a commercial use; a fourplex the borrower will occupy.

> **The response to a genuinely close case is not to pick the classification that is convenient. It is to route it to compliance and, where necessary, counsel — before the file is built around an answer.**

A file constructed on the wrong classification is expensive to unwind, because the disclosures, the licensing analysis, and sometimes the product all rest on it.

### 5.8 Checkpoint

You should be able to state why occupancy is material, recognize the misrepresentation patterns, state what a certification establishes, and identify both directions of the business-purpose error.

## 6. Product suitability and documentation

### 6.1 What a licensee may and may not conclude

**May:** describe products and their mechanics; compute and present comparisons; identify the questions a borrower should take to a lender, a tax professional, and counsel; explain what a feature exposes the borrower to; and record what was presented and what the borrower decided.

**May not:** determine that a product is suitable in the sense an underwriter or an advisor would; predict rates or values; state a qualifying threshold as a rule; advise on tax treatment; or present a comparison that omits a material difference.

### 6.2 The presentation standard

Products are presented **on a common basis**, and a comparison that omits a material difference is misleading **even when every number in it is accurate.**

The elements of an honest comparison:

1. **The same loan amount**, or the difference stated.
2. **The same term and structure**, or the difference stated.
3. **The note rate, the payment, the points, and the lender charges**, each separately.
4. **The break-even** where points differ.
5. **The APR**, identified as a comparison tool with its assumptions.
6. **What is a lender charge and what is a third-party or prepaid item.**
7. **For any adjustable or non-amortizing feature: the worst-case path**, not only the initial payment.
8. **PITI**, not principal and interest alone.

### 6.3 The features that require a plain-language explanation

Any feature that changes the payment or the balance in a way the initial figure does not show:

| Feature | What must be said |
|---|---|
| **Adjustable rate** | Index, margin, caps, and the worst-case payment |
| **Interest-only period** | Principal is not retired, and the payment recalculates over a shorter remaining term |
| **Balloon** | The amount due at maturity and that refinancing depends on conditions then |
| **Negative amortization** | The balance can grow while payments are made, and a recast can raise the payment sharply |
| **Temporary buydown** | The payment after it ends, with equal prominence |
| **Prepayment penalty** | That it exists, what triggers it, and what it costs |
| **Mortgage insurance** | That it protects the lender, what it costs, and whether and how it ends |
| **Impounds** | That the payment includes taxes and insurance and can change when they do |

### 6.4 The questions that produce a fitting recommendation

Before presenting anything, the originator should know:

- **How long does the borrower expect to hold the property and the loan?** This decides points, buydowns, and hybrid terms.
- **What payment is he comfortable with**, as distinct from what he can qualify for?
- **What is his tolerance for payment change?**
- **What cash does he have**, beyond the down payment?
- **What is the property, and how will it be occupied?**
- **What does he expect to happen** — income change, a sale, a move?

**The answers are recorded**, because they are what makes a later presentation defensible.

### 6.5 The recurring failure

Not that a licensee computed something wrong. **That a licensee presented the product the borrower could be approved for as though it were the product that fit him** — and the borrower could not tell the difference.

**The controls are simple:** ask the holding-period question, present the worst case alongside the best, present PITI, present alternatives on a common basis, and record what was presented and what was decided.

### 6.6 What the file must show

- The borrower's stated objectives and constraints, with dates.
- The products presented, with the figures as presented.
- The assumptions underlying each figure.
- Every feature requiring a plain-language explanation, and that it was given.
- The comparison as presented, on a common basis.
- The borrower's decision, and any instruction departing from the recommendation.
- Referrals made — to a tax professional, to counsel — and the response.

### 6.7 The test

> **Reconstructability: could a person who was not there determine what the borrower was shown, on what assumptions, and what he decided?**

If a comparison in the file cannot be reproduced from the figures it contains, it is not a record of advice. It is a marketing document that happens to be in a loan file.

### 6.8 A worked product presentation

> **The borrower.** Purchasing a primary residence at 720,000 with fifteen percent down. Employed on salary for nine years, stable. Two children, ages 4 and 7. He says he expects to stay "probably forever, but who knows." Reserves after closing: about four months of expenses. He mentions his brother-in-law told him adjustable loans are "how you actually save money."
>
> **Three structures are available**, and Section 6.2's standard requires all three on a common basis.
>
> **What is held constant:** the same loan amount, the same assumed closing date, the same horizon for total cost, and the same treatment of costs paid versus financed.
>
> **What is shown for each:** rate, payment, **worst-case payment where the payment can change**, mortgage insurance where the down payment triggers it, total cost over the stated horizon, and every feature that changes the obligation.
>
> **The plain-language explanations Section 6.3 requires, each with its number:**
>
> **On the fixed structure:** the payment does not change; the mortgage insurance applies and here is what ends it and when.
>
> **On the adjustable structure:** the rate is fixed for the initial period and then can change on a schedule, by a limited amount each time, to a stated maximum — **and at that maximum the payment is this figure**, which is the number the presentation exists to convey.
>
> **On the balloon structure:** the payment is based on a longer schedule and the entire remaining balance is due on a date. **The borrower must repay, refinance, or sell by then, and none of those is guaranteed to be available.**
>
> **The suitability facts, from Section 6.4, and what they indicate.**
>
> **"Probably forever, but who knows."** The most common answer, and the least useful as stated. **The follow-up is what makes it useful:** what would cause you to move? A job change, a school change, a family need. **A borrower with two young children and a nine-year employment history is not a short-horizon borrower**, which weighs against paying for a rate that is fixed for a period shorter than his likely holding period.
>
> **Four months of reserves.** Thin against a payment that can rise. **This is a fact about payment-shock tolerance, and it is not a function of income size.**
>
> **The brother-in-law.** Not a reason for anything, and **not a thing to argue with either.** The response is the presentation: here is what the adjustable does under its worst case, here is what the fixed costs to avoid that, and the decision is yours.
>
> **What the broker does not do.** Recommend a product, tell the borrower the adjustable is "safe because you'll refinance," present the initial payment as the payment, or characterize the fixed structure as expensive without showing what it buys. **Section 6.1's boundary is that the conclusion is the borrower's**, and Section 6.5's recurring failure is the presentation that made it for him.
>
> **What the file shows afterward**, per Section 6.6: the three structures on a common basis with worst cases, the explanations given, the suitability questions asked and answered, and the borrower's selection with its date. **Section 6.7's test applied: could a reviewer reconstruct the presentation without the broker present?**

### 6.9 Checkpoint

You should be able to present two products on a common basis, list the features requiring plain-language explanation, and state what the file must show.

## Practice problems

### Problem 1

An originator complains to his manager that an underwriter is demanding a documentation item that
"makes no sense — the borrower has 40% equity and eight months of reserves, he obviously qualifies."

**Explain why the requirement may exist anyway.**

> **Solution.** **Underwriting standards flow backward from the secondary market.** Lenders originate to
> standards that make loans **saleable**, because a lender who can sell a loan recovers its capital and
> can lend again — which is what the secondary market supplies and why mortgage credit is not limited by
> any single lender's balance sheet.
>
> So the question is not only whether this borrower will repay. **It is whether the loan will meet the
> purchaser's requirements**, and a documentation item can exist because an investor requires it rather
> than because the underwriter doubts the borrower.
>
> Two related facts worth knowing: **standards change**, sometimes quickly, because investor appetite
> changes; and **"but he obviously qualifies" is not an argument that reaches the requirement.**
>
> If the loan genuinely cannot meet conforming standards, the alternative is a **non-conforming**
> channel — portfolio, non-QM, or private. **Non-conforming does not mean improper**; it means not
> saleable into standard channels, generally more expensive, and sometimes the only workable option.

### Problem 2

A borrower tells an originator at a brokerage, "So you're the bank — you decide, right?" The originator
says yes for simplicity. The firm arranges loans and does not fund them.

**Assess, and state the consequences of the misstatement.**

> **Solution.** **The statement is false and it misleads the borrower about three things at once.**
>
> **A mortgage banker funds the loan; a mortgage broker arranges it.** A broker does not lend its own
> funds, does not underwrite, and is not the lender of record — **the funding lender is.**
>
> **What the borrower now misunderstands:** **who decides** — the lender underwrites, not the broker;
> **who is paid and how** — a broker's compensation is disclosed and is a distinct compliance subject;
> and **who holds his file and his information.**
>
> **A licensee must not describe the firm as a lender if it is a broker.** The broker's actual value
> proposition is access and fit — multiple lenders' menus rather than one — and its compliance exposure
> is correspondingly about **what was represented, what was disclosed, and how compensation worked.**
>
> If the borrower asks how a non-depository funds anything, the answer is a **warehouse line** — short-
> term credit a mortgage banker uses to fund before selling — which is a different arrangement from what
> this firm does.

### Problem 3

Four statements made in one conversation: "FHA is a government loan, so the government is lending you
the money"; "VA loans are only for first-time buyers"; "these programs are basically for new
construction"; and "the mortgage insurance protects you if you can't pay."

**Correct each.**

> **Solution.** **All four are wrong, and the last is the one that changes what the borrower thinks he
> is buying.**
>
> **The government is not the lender.** Government-backed programs share a structure: **an agency
> insures or guarantees the lender against certain losses**, reducing risk and supporting terms —
> notably lower down payments. Private lenders make the loans, and **the loans bear interest on ordinary
> terms.**
>
> **Not first-time buyers.** VA eligibility is based on **service**, established by a certificate of
> eligibility, and the **entitlement can be restored and reused** — a question for the VA and the lender,
> not the licensee's to compute.
>
> **Not new construction.** Neither program is restricted to it.
>
> **Mortgage insurance protects the lender.** The borrower pays for it and receives access to financing
> at a lower down payment in exchange. Borrowers consistently get this backwards, and correcting it is
> part of presenting the product honestly. Note also that FHA's structure — an **upfront** amount plus an
> **annual** amount collected monthly — differs from conventional mortgage insurance and can behave
> differently over the life of the loan, while VA typically carries a **funding fee** with exemptions
> rather than monthly insurance.

### Problem 4

A seller has two offers at the same price, one from a buyer using an FHA loan. The listing agent tells
the seller to take the other because "FHA buyers are a hassle." The property has a failing roof and
peeling exterior paint.

**Separate what is legitimate from what is not.**

> **Solution.** **There is a legitimate fact here and an illegitimate translation of it.**
>
> **Legitimate:** government programs commonly carry **property condition requirements**, and a property
> with deferred maintenance may not satisfy them. A failing roof is exactly the kind of condition that
> can stop such a loan. **That is a closing-probability question**, and a seller is entitled to weigh
> probability of closing alongside price.
>
> **Not legitimate:** "FHA buyers are a hassle" converts a property-condition fact into a **preference
> against buyers using a program.** That is a categorical judgment about people rather than an
> assessment of this property against this program's requirements, and it raises the fair lending and
> fair housing issues developed in Lesson 14.
>
> **The correct presentation is neutral and specific:** this property has conditions that may not
> satisfy the program's requirements, here is what that means for the probability and timing of closing,
> and here is what repair or a different structure would change. **The seller decides.**

### Problem 5

A 5/6 hybrid: 6.0% start rate, margin 2.75%, index at 4.50%, caps 2/1/5, balance $500,000. The
originator tells the borrower "the start rate is great and rates should come down before it ever
adjusts."

**Compute the honest presentation and assess the statement.**

> **Solution.** **Fully indexed rate today:** 4.50 + 2.75 = **7.25%.** The **start rate is below it**,
> which means that even with no market movement at all the rate moves **up** at the first adjustment —
> the single most important fact in this conversation, and the one the originator did not mention.
>
> **The worst-case path:** first adjustment limited by the **2% initial cap** → **8.0%.** Subsequent
> adjustments limited by the **1% periodic cap.** Lifetime ceiling 6.0 + 5 = **11.0%.**
>
> **Payments on $500,000:** roughly **$2,998** at 6.0%; **$3,619** at 8.0%; **$4,672** at the 11.0%
> ceiling.
>
> **That table is the honest presentation.** A borrower who has seen the worst case and accepted it has
> made a decision. One who was told rates should come down has been given a **prediction stated as
> certainty**, on which he may accept a payment he cannot otherwise afford — and it is the sentence
> behind a large share of adjustable-rate complaints.
>
> **Rates are never predicted.** They respond to broad capital market conditions and are set by no single
> institution. And **the rate at adjustment is index plus margin within the caps** — not the lender's
> discretion and not the borrower's credit at that time.

### Problem 6

A borrower is offered a temporary buydown reducing the payment for the first two years, on a loan with
an interest-only period and a balloon at year seven. He says he will "just refinance before any of that
matters." His rate lock expires in eleven days and the file is missing two conditions.

**Identify every exposure and what must be presented.**

> **Solution.** **Four exposures, and the borrower's plan depends on all of them going well.**
>
> **The temporary buydown:** the payment after it ends must be presented **with equal prominence** to
> the initial payment. A borrower who budgeted to the first-year payment and never registered the
> third-year payment has been sold rather than advised.
>
> **The interest-only period:** **principal is not retired**, so at the end the payment recalculates to
> amortize the **full original balance** over the **remaining, shorter** term. The increase is larger
> than borrowers expect.
>
> **The balloon:** a large sum due at maturity, with only three real paths — refinance, sell, or pay —
> and **all three depend on conditions at maturity**, including his credit, the property's value, and
> whether credit is available. **"I'll just refinance" assumes a market**, and that is refinance risk.
>
> **The lock:** it fixes a rate for a period and **is not a guarantee the loan will close.** He needs in
> writing what rate and points are locked, **for how long**, what happens if the loan does not close in
> time, whether an extension is available and at what cost, and what voids it. **A lock that expires
> because the file moved slowly is a foreseeable event, not an accident** — and two outstanding
> conditions with eleven days left is exactly that situation.

### Problem 7

A branch manager instructs an originator to process a loan as business-purpose on a fourplex the
borrower will occupy, "since it's an income property," and to have the borrower sign the business-
purpose certification. The file also contains a lease on the subject property dated a month before the
application.

**Assess both, and state which is different in kind.**

> **Solution.** **The instruction inverts the analysis and the certification does not fix it.**
>
> Consumer residential mortgage requirements — including the MLO endorsement requirement and much of the
> federal disclosure regime — attach to **consumer-purpose loans secured by a dwelling.** A borrower
> occupying one unit points hard toward consumer purpose, and **"it's an income property" is not the
> test.** The determination rests on **the actual purpose and the surrounding facts**, considering what
> the proceeds fund, the borrower's relationship to the property and the activity, and the transaction's
> overall character.
>
> **A signature records the borrower's statement; it does not establish the fact** — and a file built
> around a certification the surrounding facts contradict is visible to any examiner who reads the
> occupancy line.
>
> **The lease is different in kind.** It is not a missing document or a late delivery: **the file itself
> contains the evidence contradicting a material representation to the lender.** The response is
> **escalation**, not a judgment call about whether it matters or whether the borrower could qualify
> either way.
>
> **The originator declines, does not transmit the file, and escalates above the manager.** Following the
> instruction while noting it was directed **documents that he knew** — and misstating occupancy is loan
> fraud regardless of affordability, because the misstatement is about obtaining terms on a false
> representation of the risk being priced.

### Problem 8

An originator presents one product: a 30-year fixed at 6.25%, principal and interest of $2,398, "with
no points." He does not ask how long the borrower plans to stay, does not mention taxes and insurance,
and does not present the 15-year or the buydown alternatives the lender offers. The borrower is a
physician relocating on a two-year contract.

**Identify the failures and state what the file must show.**

> **Solution.** **The recurring failure in this lesson: presenting the product the borrower could be
> approved for as though it were the product that fit him** — and the borrower could not tell the
> difference.
>
> **The holding-period question was never asked**, and it is the one that decides points, buydowns, and
> hybrid terms. A borrower on a two-year contract is precisely the case where a **break-even** analysis
> matters and where a hybrid or a no-point structure may fit better than a long fixed term.
>
> **The payment quoted was P&I, not PITI.** A borrower quoted principal and interest and later shown the
> full housing payment **will feel misled** — and in California the property tax line is most often
> understated, because a buyer's taxes reset on the change in ownership rather than tracking the
> seller's bill.
>
> **Alternatives were not presented on a common basis.** A comparison must carry the same amount, term,
> and structure or state the differences; the note rate, payment, points, and lender charges separately;
> the break-even where points differ; the APR identified as a comparison tool with its assumptions; what
> is a lender charge versus a third-party or prepaid item; and the worst-case path for any adjustable or
> non-amortizing feature.
>
> **What the file must show:** the borrower's stated objectives and constraints with dates; the products
> presented with the figures as presented; the assumptions underlying each; that every feature requiring
> plain-language explanation received one; the comparison on a common basis; the borrower's decision;
> and any referrals made and the response.
>
> **The test is reconstructability.** If a comparison cannot be reproduced from the figures it contains,
> it is not a record of advice — it is a marketing document that happens to be in a loan file.

## Chapter summary

**The secondary market supplies liquidity, and underwriting standards flow backward from it** — which is
why a requirement can exist for investor reasons rather than credit reasons.

**Conforming, jumbo, non-QM, portfolio, and private describe saleability**, not propriety.

**A banker funds; a broker arranges.** Never describe a broker as the lender, because it misleads the
borrower about who decides, who is paid, and who holds the file.

**Government-backed means insured or guaranteed, not government-lent.** The loans bear ordinary
interest, are not limited to first-time buyers or new construction, and **mortgage insurance protects
the lender.**

**Property condition requirements are a closing-probability fact** — never translated into a preference
against buyers using a program.

**An adjustable rate is index plus margin within caps.** Compute the **fully indexed rate**, present the
**worst-case path**, and never predict rates.

**Interest-only, balloon, and temporary buydown structures each hide a later payment.** Present it with
equal prominence, and name refinance risk rather than assuming a market.

**A rate lock is not a guarantee the loan will close**, and its terms belong in writing before reliance.

**Occupancy is a material fact and misstating it is fraud regardless of affordability.** Learn the
patterns, and treat a file containing its own contradiction as an escalation rather than a judgment
call.

**A business-purpose certification records a statement; the actual purpose governs** — and the error runs
in both directions, with the more serious one stripping protections the borrower was entitled to.

**Route genuinely close classification questions before the file is built around an answer.**

**Present on a common basis, explain every feature that changes the payment or the balance, quote PITI,
and ask the holding-period question first.**

**The file must let someone who was not there reconstruct what was shown, on what assumptions, and what
was decided.**
