## 1. Ability-to-repay orientation

### 1.1 What underwriting is

**Underwriting is the decision whether to extend credit on stated terms, made against the lender's guidelines and applicable law.**

It is not a formality that follows a preapproval, and it is not something an originator performs. **A licensee who tells a borrower what underwriting will do has made the decision without the authority to make it.**

### 1.2 The ability-to-repay requirement

Federal law requires a creditor making a covered residential mortgage loan to make a **reasonable and good faith determination, based on verified and documented information, that the consumer has a reasonable ability to repay** the loan according to its terms.

**Two features define it:**

**It is a determination, not a form.** The creditor must actually consider the required factors and be able to show it.

**It rests on verified and documented information.** Stated information the creditor did not verify does not support the determination — which is why the verification discipline of Lesson 05 is a legal requirement rather than a processing preference.

### 1.3 The factors considered

In outline, the determination considers the consumer's:

| Factor |
|---|
| Current or reasonably expected income or assets |
| Current employment status |
| The monthly payment on the covered transaction |
| The monthly payment on any simultaneous loan |
| The monthly payment for mortgage-related obligations |
| Current debt obligations, alimony, and child support |
| The monthly debt-to-income ratio or residual income |
| Credit history |

**Two of these are the ones files most often miss.** A **simultaneous loan** — a second recorded at the same closing — must be considered, and **mortgage-related obligations** means taxes, insurance, assessments, and dues, which is why the housing figure is PITI rather than principal and interest.

### 1.4 The payment used

For adjustable and non-amortizing structures, the payment used in the determination is not necessarily the initial payment. The framework requires consideration of a payment computed on defined assumptions — which is why **a product with a discounted start rate does not qualify a borrower on that start rate.**

**A licensee who tells a borrower the initial payment is what he must qualify on has described a different loan.**

### 1.5 Qualified mortgages, in outline

A **qualified mortgage** is a category of loan meeting defined requirements — including limits on certain features and on points and fees, and requirements about how the payment is computed — that provides the creditor a degree of protection regarding the ability-to-repay determination.

**Features generally inconsistent with the category** include negative amortization, interest-only periods, balloon payments in the ordinary case, and terms exceeding a defined length.

**Two points for a licensee:**

**Non-QM is not improper.** It means the loan does not meet the category's requirements, which generally makes it more expensive and sometimes makes it the only workable option for a legitimate borrower.

**Whether a particular loan is a qualified mortgage is a compliance determination**, and the points and fees limit is calculated by rule rather than by intuition — which matters directly to a broker, because **broker compensation can count toward it.** Lesson 09 develops that.

### 1.6 Exclusions

Certain transactions fall outside the ability-to-repay framework — including, in outline, **business-purpose loans** and certain other categories.

**That is precisely why the Lesson 04 classification discipline matters.** A loan misclassified as business-purpose is a loan for which the required determination may never have been made, and **the borrower was stripped of a protection he was entitled to.** The certification does not fix it; the actual purpose governs.

### 1.7 Checkpoint

You should be able to state what the ability-to-repay determination requires, name the factors most often missed, and explain why a discounted start rate does not qualify a borrower.

## 2. Automated and manual underwriting

### 2.1 Automated underwriting

Automated systems evaluate a file against programmed criteria and return a **finding** or recommendation, together with the documentation the finding requires.

**What an automated finding does:** applies consistent criteria quickly, identifies the documentation level required, and tells the originator what the file needs.

**What it does not do:**

> **A finding is not an approval.** It is a recommendation based on **the data entered**, and it is conditioned on that data being verified.

**Three consequences licensees must hold:**

1. **Garbage in, garbage out.** A favorable finding produced from an income figure that documentation does not support is worth nothing, and it will reverse when the documents arrive.
2. **The finding is re-run.** Data changes as the file is verified, and a re-run can return a different recommendation.
3. **The underwriter still underwrites.** The finding directs the documentation; a human confirms the file matches it.

**"The system approved it" is not a defense for a file whose data was wrong**, and an originator who enters an optimistic figure to get a finding has produced an answer to a question nobody asked.

### 2.2 Manual underwriting

An underwriter evaluates the file directly against guidelines. It applies where a file is outside automated parameters, where the program requires it, and where compensating factors need human assessment.

**Manual underwriting is not a lesser process.** It frequently applies to files that are strong in unusual ways — substantial reserves with thin credit, or documented income of a kind a system handles poorly.

### 2.3 Compensating factors

Strengths that may support a file weak on one dimension: substantial reserves, a low loan-to-value, a long tenure in employment or residence, a modest payment increase over current housing cost, demonstrated ability to carry a similar payment, and limited other debt.

> **Whether a compensating factor supports a particular file is an underwriting determination.**

An originator who tells a borrower his reserves "will make up for" a credit issue has made it — and the borrower stops considering alternatives on the strength of it.

### 2.4 Exceptions

Lenders sometimes grant **exceptions** to guidelines. Three things a licensee should understand:

**They are the lender's to grant**, on the lender's criteria, and they are not obtained by pressure.

**They are documented**, with the rationale, because an examiner will ask why this file was treated differently.

**Exception patterns are a fair lending issue.** Where exceptions are granted more readily to some applicants than others without a documented neutral basis, the pattern is exactly what fair lending examinations look for. Lesson 14 develops it.

### 2.5 What the originator contributes to underwriting

**Accurate data at the front.** Every hour spent verifying before submission is returned in conditions not raised.

**Complete documentation matched to the finding.**

**Honest presentation of weaknesses**, with explanations documented in the borrower's own words.

**Prompt, complete condition responses**, addressed in Section 5.

**What the originator does not contribute:** advocacy that misdescribes the file, pressure for an exception, or a characterization of the borrower's likelihood of approval to the borrower.

### 2.6 Checkpoint

You should be able to state what an automated finding is and is not, explain why "the system approved it" fails, and state who decides whether a compensating factor applies.

## 3. Appraisal and collateral review

### 3.1 What the appraisal is for

**The lender obtains an appraisal to measure its collateral risk** — what could be recovered if the loan is not repaid.

> **Lenders lend against the lower of the appraised value and the purchase price.**

That single sentence explains every consequence of a low appraisal, and it is worth memorizing in exactly that form. **On a refinance there is no purchase price, so value is the appraised value** — which is why a refinance is more exposed to the appraisal outcome and why cash-out availability is not knowable until it arrives.

### 3.2 Appraiser independence

**A licensee may provide genuinely relevant information through the proper channel** — sales the licensee believes relevant, information about condition and improvements, a copy of the contract.

**A licensee may not communicate a target.**

> "The deal dies without another $25,000" is precisely the communication independence rules exist to prevent, and **a value that moves after that conversation is unreliable for the purpose the appraisal serves.**

**The distinction is between supplying information and supplying a target** — and requests to "take another look" that arrive with a number attached are the pattern to refuse, **including when the request comes from your own borrower or your own manager.**

Appraiser independence requirements also govern **who may order and communicate with the appraiser**, and firms implement them through defined channels precisely so that individual originators are not the point of contact.

### 3.3 Valuation review

Lenders review appraisals for completeness, support, and reasonableness — through desk or field review, automated tools, or a second appraisal where required.

**A review that questions an appraisal is not an accusation.** It is the lender testing whether the collateral conclusion is supported, which is the same discipline the appraisal itself applies to comparable selection.

### 3.4 The reconsideration process

Where an originator or a borrower believes an appraisal is materially wrong, lenders provide a **reconsideration of value** process.

**How it is done correctly:**

- **Through the lender's defined channel**, not by contacting the appraiser directly.
- **With relevant sales the appraiser may not have considered**, identified with data.
- **With factual corrections** — a bedroom miscounted, a permitted addition omitted, a condition misdescribed.
- **Without a target number.**

**What is not a reconsideration:** an assertion that the value is wrong because the contract price is higher, or because the transaction will fail without a different number.

### 3.5 The low appraisal

The buyer's options are practical rather than automatic: bring additional funds, renegotiate the price, negotiate another resolution, or cancel if a contingency permits and its procedure is followed.

**What does not happen automatically:** the price does not adjust to the appraised value by operation of any loan program, the loan is not denied, and **no one is entitled to a second appraisal as of right.**

**Where an appraisal contingency has been removed**, the practical options remain but the contractual rights are limited — and **whether the borrower may cancel and what happens to a deposit is a legal question** for counsel, not for the licensee.

### 3.6 The borrower's copy

The borrower is entitled to receive a copy of the appraisal and other written valuations developed in connection with the application, under the framework Lesson 07 develops.

**Delivery is evidenced**, and a file that cannot show when the valuation reached the borrower has a gap in exactly the place an examiner looks.

### 3.7 Other collateral considerations

| Consideration | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Property type** | Single-family, condominium, planned development, two-to-four units, manufactured, mixed use — each with its own eligibility |
| **Project eligibility** | Condominium and planned development characteristics can make a project ineligible even for a strong borrower |
| **Condition** | Deferred maintenance, health and safety items, and unpermitted work can stop a loan under program requirements |
| **Unpermitted space** | Cannot be counted as living area without disclosure of its status, and it affects value, insurability, and eligibility |
| **Legal nonconformity** | A use lawful when established that no longer conforms carries its own treatment |
| **Environmental and hazard exposure** | Contamination, mapped hazard zones, and insurability |
| **Access and utilities** | Particularly on rural property — legal access, water source, septic |

### 3.8 Checkpoint

You should be able to state the lower-of-value-or-price rule, distinguish supplying information from supplying a target, and describe a correct reconsideration request.

## 4. Title insurance and hazard insurance

### 4.1 Two different insurances

| | Title insurance | Hazard insurance |
|---|---|---|
| Covers | Defects in **title** | Physical **loss or damage** |
| Looks | **Backward** — defects already existing in the record or chain | **Forward** — future events |
| Premium | Generally paid **once**, at closing | Recurring |
| Lender's interest | Lender's policy insuring the lien and its priority | Mortgagee clause naming the lender |

**Borrowers conflate them constantly**, and a licensee who cannot separate them will not be able to explain why both are required.

### 4.2 The lender's title policy

**The lender's policy insures the lender's lien and its priority.** The **owner's policy** insures the borrower's interest.

> **They insure different interests**, which is why both commonly exist — and why a buyer who declines an owner's policy because "there's a title policy on the loan" has not economized on a duplicate. He has left his own interest uninsured.

### 4.3 Exceptions, exclusions, requirements

The three categories a licensee must distinguish on a preliminary report:

| Category | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Exception** | An item the policy **will not insure against** |
| **Exclusion** | A category the policy form does not cover at all — governmental regulation, eminent domain, matters created by the insured |
| **Requirement** | Something the insurer needs **before issuing** — a reconveyance, a resolution, evidence of authority |

> **Requirements are the items with deadlines attached**, and they are the ones that delay closings.

**Three responses wrong in every case:** treating an item as routine because most properties have one; asking title to "remove" an exception; and predicting an easement's or restriction's effect on the borrower's plans.

### 4.4 What the title work must resolve

For a lender, the operative questions are **who owns it, what encumbers it, and where will our lien sit.**

The recurring items:

**An unreleased deed of trust the borrower says was paid.** A recorded **reconveyance** must be obtained — **payment does not clear the record; the document does** — and obtaining one from a lender that has merged or ceased to exist can take weeks.

**A judgment against a similarly named person.** Routed to title, resolved by a statement of information, and never assumed away.

**Mechanic's lien exposure from recent work.** Priority **may relate back to when work commenced**, so a lien recorded later can outrank the new deed of trust — which is why the intake question is whether work was recently done and everyone is paid.

**An existing second on a refinance.** A **subordination agreement** may be required, and it is that lienholder's decision with counsel — never "standard."

**Authority requirements.** Trustee evidence, corporate resolutions, powers of attorney — each a **requirement** with a deadline.

**A lis pendens.** A recorded notice of pending litigation affecting title, and a serious cloud.

### 4.5 Endorsements

Lenders commonly require **endorsements** extending or modifying coverage for particular risks — including matters relating to surveys, environmental protection liens, and specific encumbrances.

**A licensee should know endorsements exist and are ordered**, and should not represent what any particular endorsement covers.

### 4.6 Hazard insurance

Lenders require hazard coverage naming the lender under a **mortgagee clause**, in an amount and with terms the program requires.

**What a licensee must confirm:** coverage is in force at closing, the amount satisfies the requirement, the mortgagee clause is correct, and the premium is paid or escrowed.

**Force-placed coverage.** Where a borrower's policy lapses, the lender may place coverage and charge the borrower — typically at a far higher cost and protecting only the lender's interest. **This is a real consequence of a lapse**, and borrowers should hear it.

### 4.7 Availability, and why it fails transactions

In mapped fire and flood areas, **availability and cost of hazard insurance have become a common cause of failed transactions in California**, and the failure is usually discovered late because borrowers treat insurance as a formality.

> **Insurance is a first-week item in a mapped hazard area, not an administrative closing task.** A borrower who learns in week one that coverage will cost far more than assumed can act. A borrower who learns it two days before closing generally cannot.

**Flood insurance** carries its own requirement where the property is in a designated special flood hazard area and the loan is federally related, and the determination is made by a defined process.

### 4.8 Other required coverages

Depending on the property and program: **condominium master policy** review and any required walls-in coverage; **earthquake coverage** where required or elected; **rent loss** coverage on income property; and **liability** coverage where the program requires it.

### 4.9 Checkpoint

You should be able to distinguish the two insurances, distinguish exceptions from requirements, name what title work must resolve, and explain why hazard insurance is a first-week item.

## 5. Conditions and verification

### 5.1 What a condition is

**A condition is a requirement that must be satisfied before the loan proceeds to the next stage.** Approvals are commonly conditional, and the conditions are the work.

| Type | When it must be satisfied |
|---|---|
| **Prior to document** | Before loan documents are drawn |
| **Prior to funding** | Before the lender releases funds |
| **At funding** | Concurrently |
| **Post-closing** | After funding, typically documentation cleanup |

### 5.2 Conditions are not formalities

> **An unsatisfied prior-to-funding condition means the loan does not fund**, whatever the schedule says and whoever has already scheduled movers.

An originator who describes conditions to a borrower as "just paperwork" has set an expectation the file cannot meet — and has removed the borrower's motivation to respond quickly.

### 5.3 The categories

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Income and employment** | Verification of employment, paystubs, returns, a written explanation of a gap |
| **Assets** | Statements, sourcing of a deposit, gift documentation |
| **Credit** | Explanation of a derogatory item, evidence of a payoff, a dispute resolution |
| **Property** | Appraisal conditions, repairs, a certification of completion, project documentation |
| **Title** | Reconveyance, subordination, authority evidence, resolution of a lien |
| **Insurance** | Policy, mortgagee clause, flood determination, evidence of premium |
| **Compliance** | Signed disclosures, a corrected document, evidence of timing |
| **Verbal verification of employment** | Commonly required **shortly before funding** |

### 5.4 The re-verification problem

**An approval rests on the file as underwritten**, and lenders commonly **re-verify credit and employment before funding.**

That is why the events of Lesson 05 matter so much: new credit, a job change, large unexplained deposits, moving money, paying off debt with closing funds, and missed payments each can undo an approval **after** it was granted.

> **The written warning at application — do not open credit, change jobs, or move large sums without telling us first — prevents more failed closings than any other communication an originator sends.**

### 5.5 Managing conditions

**Read the condition as written.** Half of condition responses fail because the response addressed something adjacent.

**Respond completely.** A partial response restarts the cycle and consumes days that a lock does not have.

**Track them with owners and dates.** Each condition has one person responsible and a date.

**Escalate early.** A condition that cannot be satisfied is a problem on the day it is identified, not on the day it is due — and there is usually an alternative if there is time to find one.

**Never satisfy a condition with something other than what it asks for**, and never represent that a condition is satisfied when it is not.

### 5.6 What must never happen to a condition

- **Altering a document** to satisfy a condition.
- **Backdating** anything.
- **Submitting a document the originator knows to be inaccurate.**
- **Representing that a verbal verification occurred** when it did not.
- **Signing on a borrower's behalf.**
- **Telling a borrower a condition is satisfied** to relieve pressure.

**Each is a misrepresentation**, and each occurs on files that would probably have closed anyway — which is what makes them so costly.

### 5.7 The lock and the timeline

Conditions consume the days a **rate lock** provides. A borrower needs in writing what is locked, **for how long**, what happens if the loan does not close in time, whether an extension is available and at what cost, and what voids it.

> **A lock that expires because the file moved slowly is a foreseeable event, not an accident.**

### 5.8 Checkpoint

You should be able to name the condition types, explain why a prior-to-funding condition is not a formality, describe how conditions are managed, and state what must never be done to satisfy one.

## 6. Approval, denial, and adverse action

### 6.1 The dispositions

| Disposition | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Approval** | The credit decision to extend, subject to conditions |
| **Counteroffer** | An offer on different terms than requested |
| **Denial** | A refusal to extend credit on the terms requested |
| **Withdrawal** | The applicant withdraws the request |
| **Incomplete** | The applicant does not supply information after a request |

**Each carries its own treatment**, and recording which occurred, when, and on what communication is what makes the treatment defensible.

### 6.2 Adverse action

**Adverse action** includes a denial or revocation of credit, a change in terms unfavorable to the applicant, or a refusal to grant credit in substantially the amount or on substantially the terms requested.

**The requirement, in outline:** an applicant against whom adverse action is taken must receive **notice**, within a defined period, containing **either a statement of the specific reasons or a disclosure of the right to obtain them** — together with the other content the rule requires, including information about the credit reporting agency where a consumer report was used.

### 6.3 The specific reasons

> **The reasons stated must be the actual, specific reasons — not a general reference to a policy or a check-box that does not describe what happened.**

The requirement exists so an applicant learns something usable. A denial reason of "does not meet our standards" tells the applicant nothing and does not satisfy the purpose.

**Where a consumer report contributed, additional content is required**, including the agency's identity and a statement that the agency did not make the decision and cannot supply the reasons.

### 6.4 The counteroffer

Where a creditor offers different terms and the applicant does not accept, the treatment depends on the framework and on how the offer and the response were handled.

**A licensee should understand that a counteroffer is a defined event with consequences**, not an informal conversation — and should route the disposition question rather than deciding it.

### 6.5 Withdrawal and incompleteness

**A withdrawal is the applicant's act**, and it is recorded with the date and the communication.

**An incomplete application** arises where requested information is not supplied, and the framework prescribes how it may be treated — including notice requirements in defined circumstances.

> **An application quietly abandoned and never dispositioned is a file with a gap in it**, and it is exactly the kind an examiner samples. "The borrower stopped responding" is not a disposition; a documented notice and a recorded outcome is.

### 6.6 What a licensee does with a denial

**Deliver it promptly and accurately.** A denial delayed while an originator looks for an alternative is a compliance problem that helps no one.

**Do not soften the reason.** Telling a borrower he was denied "because of the appraisal" when he was denied for income is a misstatement he will act on — and the notice will say otherwise.

**Do not editorialize about the decision.** "They're being unreasonable" is a characterization the borrower will repeat.

**Do explain what a licensee legitimately can:** what the notice says, that he may request the specific reasons where the notice discloses that right, that he may obtain his consumer report from the agency named, and that other lenders and programs exist.

**Do not promise another lender will approve him.**

### 6.7 Fair lending in the underwriting context

Underwriting is where fair lending exposure concentrates, because it is where discretion operates.

**Three practices constrain it**, developed fully in Lesson 14:

**Consistent application of criteria**, so a file's treatment does not depend on who handled it.

**Documented exceptions with a neutral basis**, because an exception pattern granted more readily to some applicants is precisely what a fair lending examination looks for.

**Outcome review** — examining approval, denial, pricing, and exception patterns rather than intentions. **Individual decisions rarely reveal anything; patterns do.**

### 6.8 The file at disposition

Whatever the outcome, the file should show: the application and its versions; what was verified and how; the underwriting decision and its basis; every condition and its resolution; the disposition, its date, and the notice delivered with evidence; and any explanation given to the borrower.

> **A denied file needs a complete record as much as a funded one** — arguably more, because a denial is the disposition an applicant is most likely to question.

### 6.9 Checkpoint

You should be able to name the dispositions, state what adverse action requires, explain why a generic reason fails, and describe how a denial is communicated.

## Practice problems

### Problem 1

A borrower is offered a 5/6 hybrid with a 6.0% start rate, a 2.75% margin, and an index at 4.50%. The
originator tells him he qualifies "on the 6% payment." The file also includes a simultaneous second
recorded at closing, and the housing figure used omits the $340 condominium dues.

**Identify each error.**

> **Solution.** **Three errors, and each understates the obligation the determination must consider.**
>
> **The start rate does not qualify him.** The ability-to-repay framework requires the payment to be
> computed on **defined assumptions** for adjustable and non-amortizing structures — and here the
> **fully indexed rate** is already 4.50 + 2.75 = **7.25%**, above the start rate, so even with no market
> movement the payment rises. Telling him he qualifies on the 6% payment **describes a different loan.**
>
> **The simultaneous loan must be considered.** A second recorded at the same closing is one of the
> factors, and it is among the two files most often miss.
>
> **Mortgage-related obligations include dues.** That is why the housing figure is **PITI plus dues and
> mortgage insurance**, not principal and interest — the other commonly missed factor.
>
> Underlying all three: the determination must be **reasonable and good faith, based on verified and
> documented information**, and the creditor must be able to show it actually considered the required
> factors. **It is a determination, not a form.**

### Problem 2

An originator enters an optimistic self-employment income figure to obtain a favorable automated
finding, planning to "true it up when the returns come in." When the underwriter questions the file he
says the system approved it.

**Assess.**

> **Solution.** **A finding is not an approval.** It is a recommendation based on **the data entered**,
> conditioned on that data being verified — and it identifies the documentation the file needs rather
> than deciding the credit question.
>
> **Garbage in, garbage out.** A favorable finding produced from an income figure documentation does not
> support is worth nothing, and **it will reverse when the documents arrive.** The finding is also
> **re-run** as data changes during verification, so the optimistic entry buys nothing except a borrower
> expectation that will be broken.
>
> **"The system approved it" is not a defense for a file whose data was wrong.** The underwriter still
> underwrites; the finding directs the documentation and a human confirms the file matches it.
>
> **What the originator should have contributed instead:** accurate data at the front — every hour spent
> verifying before submission is returned in conditions not raised — complete documentation matched to
> the finding, and honest presentation of the weakness with the borrower's explanation documented in his
> own words.

### Problem 3

An appraisal comes in $30,000 below the contract price. The originator emails the appraiser three
sales he believes were overlooked, notes that the roof was replaced last year and was not mentioned,
and adds: "We need $920,000 to make this work — can you take another look?" His borrower and his branch
manager both asked him to.

**Separate what is permitted from what is not, and describe the correct process.**

> **Solution.** **The email mixes something permitted with something prohibited, and the prohibited part
> contaminates the rest.**
>
> **Permitted:** supplying **genuinely relevant information** through the proper channel — sales the
> licensee believes relevant, information about condition and improvements, a copy of the contract. The
> three sales and the roof replacement are exactly that, and the roof is a **factual correction** if the
> report omitted a permitted improvement.
>
> **Prohibited: "We need $920,000."** That is a **target**, and a value that moves after that
> conversation is unreliable for the purpose the appraisal serves. Requests to "take another look" that
> arrive with a number attached are the pattern to refuse — **including when they come from your own
> borrower and your own manager.**
>
> **The correct process is a reconsideration of value through the lender's defined channel**, not a
> direct email to the appraiser. Appraiser independence requirements govern who may order and
> communicate with the appraiser, and firms implement defined channels precisely so individual
> originators are not the point of contact.
>
> **And what is not a reconsideration:** an assertion that the value is wrong because the contract price
> is higher, or because the transaction fails without a different number.

### Problem 4

The borrower asks what happens now that the appraisal is low, whether the price "automatically drops,"
whether he can demand a second appraisal, and — since he waived the appraisal contingency — whether he
can cancel and get his deposit back.

**Answer each.**

> **Solution.** **Lenders lend against the lower of appraised value and purchase price**, so the loan
> amount is computed on the appraised value. On a refinance there would be no price and value would be
> the appraisal alone.
>
> **His options are practical rather than automatic:** bring additional funds, renegotiate the price,
> negotiate another resolution such as a credit or a term change, or cancel if a contingency permits and
> its procedure is followed.
>
> **What does not happen automatically:** the price does not adjust to appraised value by operation of
> any loan program; **the loan is not denied**; and **no one is entitled to a second appraisal as of
> right.**
>
> **On the waived contingency: that is a legal question and the originator must not answer it.** The
> practical options remain, but the removed contingency limits his contractual rights, and **whether he
> may cancel and what happens to the deposit turns on the contract's terms and the circumstances.**
> Recommend counsel before he decides to stop performing.
>
> Separately, confirm the borrower **received his copy of the appraisal** and that delivery is evidenced
> — a file that cannot show when the valuation reached him has a gap exactly where an examiner looks.

### Problem 5

A refinance file shows an unreleased 2016 deed of trust the borrower says was paid, a judgment against
a similarly named person, an existing second, and a $180,000 renovation completed four months ago with
an unpaid contractor dispute. The preliminary report arrived on day eleven of a fourteen-day lock.

**Handle each, and identify what went wrong before any of it.**

> **Solution.** **The timing failure came first**, and it made everything else harder. The preliminary
> report belongs at the **front** of the queue, because **requirements carry the deadlines** and some
> take weeks.
>
> **The 2016 deed of trust requires a recorded reconveyance.** **Payment does not clear the record; the
> document does** — the borrower's recollection is not that document — and obtaining one from a lender
> that has merged or ceased to exist can take weeks.
>
> **The judgment goes to title**, resolved through a statement of information. Never assumed away.
>
> **The existing second likely requires a subordination agreement**, because paying off and re-recording
> the first would otherwise move the second into first position. **That is the second lienholder's
> decision with counsel — never "standard."**
>
> **The renovation is the exposure a mortgage licensee must recognize.** Mechanic's lien priority **may
> relate back to when work commenced**, so a lien recorded next month can outrank the new deed of trust
> — and lien rights arise from **work**, not from a contract with the owner, so subcontractors the
> borrower never met can have them. Surface it to escrow, title, and counsel now.
>
> **And a lock that expires because the file moved slowly is a foreseeable event, not an accident.**

### Problem 6

A property is in a mapped fire hazard severity zone. The originator plans to have the borrower obtain
insurance quotes "once we're through underwriting." Separately, the borrower's existing policy on
another property lapsed and he asks why his servicer charged him $4,100 for coverage.

**Assess both.**

> **Solution.** **The insurance plan is the single most preventable failure in California mortgage
> practice right now.** In mapped fire and flood areas, **availability and cost of hazard insurance have
> become a common cause of failed transactions** — and the failure is discovered late precisely because
> borrowers and originators treat insurance as a formality.
>
> **Insurance is a first-week item in a mapped hazard area, not an administrative closing task.** A
> borrower who learns in week one that coverage will cost far more than assumed can act — restructure,
> renegotiate, or walk. One who learns it two days before closing generally cannot.
>
> The file must also confirm coverage is **in force at closing**, that the amount satisfies the program,
> that the **mortgagee clause** is correct, and that the premium is paid or escrowed. And where the
> property is in a designated special flood hazard area on a federally related loan, **flood insurance**
> carries its own requirement determined by a defined process.
>
> **The $4,100 is force-placed coverage.** Where a policy lapses, the lender may place coverage and
> charge the borrower — **typically at far higher cost, and protecting only the lender's interest.**
> That is a real consequence of a lapse, and it is worth telling every borrower at closing rather than
> letting them discover it.

### Problem 7

Four days before funding, three conditions remain: a verbal verification of employment, sourcing of a
$14,000 deposit, and a signed corrected disclosure. The borrower is unreachable, movers are booked, and
the branch manager suggests marking the verbal verification complete since "we spoke to them last
month" and having the originator sign the disclosure correction on the borrower's behalf.

**Respond.**

> **Solution.** **Refuse both suggestions, and treat the conditions as what they are.**
>
> **An unsatisfied prior-to-funding condition means the loan does not fund**, whatever the schedule says
> and whoever has booked movers. Describing conditions to a borrower as "just paperwork" is what
> produced this situation — it removed his motivation to respond quickly.
>
> **The verbal verification is commonly required shortly before funding** precisely because an approval
> rests on the file **as underwritten** and lenders **re-verify employment before funding.** A
> verification from last month is not the condition. **Representing that a verbal verification occurred
> when it did not is a misrepresentation.**
>
> **Signing on the borrower's behalf is a misrepresentation** and is never available.
>
> **The deposit still requires sourcing**, because an undisclosed loan deposited as savings is a hidden
> obligation that would make the ratios wrong.
>
> **What should happen:** escalate today rather than on the due date — a condition that cannot be
> satisfied is a problem on the day it is identified, and there is usually an alternative if there is
> time. Reach the borrower through every channel, tell him plainly that funding depends on these three
> items, and address the lock consequences honestly.

### Problem 8

A file is denied for insufficient documented income. The originator tells the borrower it was "the
appraisal," delays the notice a week while shopping the file to two other lenders, sends a notice
saying only "does not meet our credit standards," and never dispositions a second application from
another borrower who stopped responding two months ago.

**Identify every failure.**

> **Solution.** **Four failures, and each is independently a problem.**
>
> **Misstating the reason.** Telling him it was the appraisal when he was denied for income is a
> misstatement he will act on — he may pay for a second appraisal or renegotiate a price for no reason —
> and **the notice will say otherwise**, so he will also learn the originator misled him.
>
> **Delaying the notice.** Adverse action notice must go out within a defined period. **A denial delayed
> while an originator shops the file is a compliance problem that helps no one**, and it does not become
> acceptable because the motive was to find a solution.
>
> **The generic reason fails the requirement's purpose.** The notice must contain **either the actual,
> specific reasons or a disclosure of the right to obtain them** — "does not meet our credit standards"
> tells the applicant nothing usable. Where a **consumer report** contributed, additional content is
> required, including the agency's identity and a statement that the agency did not make the decision
> and cannot supply the reasons.
>
> **The abandoned application is a fourth failure.** **"The borrower stopped responding" is not a
> disposition.** An application may be treated as **incomplete** under a prescribed framework, including
> notice requirements in defined circumstances — and an application quietly abandoned and never
> dispositioned is exactly the file an examiner samples.
>
> **What the originator may legitimately tell the borrower:** what the notice says, that he may request
> the specific reasons where the notice discloses that right, that he may obtain his report from the
> agency named, and that other lenders and programs exist. **He may not editorialize about the decision
> or promise another lender will approve him** — and **a denied file needs a complete record as much as
> a funded one.**

## Chapter summary

**Underwriting is a decision an originator does not make**, and telling a borrower what underwriting
will do makes it.

**Ability to repay is a determination on verified and documented information**, considering factors that
include the simultaneous loan and mortgage-related obligations — and for adjustable structures, a
payment computed on defined assumptions rather than a discounted start rate.

**Qualified mortgage is a category with feature and points-and-fees limits**, and non-QM is not
improper. **Business-purpose exclusions are why the classification discipline matters** — a
misclassified loan may be one for which the determination was never made.

**An automated finding is a recommendation on the data entered, not an approval.** "The system approved
it" is not a defense, and compensating factors and exceptions are the lender's determinations —
with exception patterns a fair lending issue.

**Lenders lend against the lower of value or price.** Supply an appraiser **information, never a
target**, use the reconsideration channel, and evidence the borrower's copy.

**Nothing about a low appraisal is automatic**, and the deposit question on a waived contingency is
legal.

**Title and hazard insurance cover different things and run in different directions.** Distinguish
exceptions, exclusions, and **requirements — which carry the deadlines** — and order the report first.

**Payment does not clear the record; a reconveyance does.** Recent construction raises relation-back
exposure, and a refinance with an existing second usually needs a subordination.

**Insurance is a first-week item in mapped hazard areas**, and force-placed coverage is the real
consequence of a lapse.

**Conditions are the work, not formalities.** Read them as written, respond completely, assign owners
and dates, escalate early — and never alter, backdate, sign for a borrower, or represent a condition
satisfied when it is not.

**Re-verification before funding is why post-approval conduct undoes approvals**, and the written
warning at application is the cheapest prevention available.

**Adverse action requires timely notice with the actual specific reasons or the right to obtain them.**
Deliver it promptly, state the real reason, editorialize about nothing, and disposition every
application — because "stopped responding" is not a disposition and a denied file needs as complete a
record as a funded one.
