## 1. Promissory notes

### 1.1 Two documents, two jobs

Every secured real estate loan involves at least two instruments, and confusing them is the single most common error in this material.

| Instrument | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Promissory note** | The **promise to repay**. It creates the debt and states its terms |
| **Security instrument** | Pledges the **property** as security for that debt |

> **The note is the debt. The security instrument is the remedy.**
>
> A note without a security instrument is an unsecured obligation — enforceable, but with no claim against the property. A security instrument without a note secures nothing, because there is no obligation to secure.

### 1.2 What the note contains

The principal amount, the interest rate and how it is determined, the payment amount and schedule, the maturity date, the parties, and the consequences of default.

**Parties:** the **maker** (or borrower) promises to pay; the **payee** (or holder) is entitled to receive.

### 1.3 Negotiability

A note meeting certain requirements is a **negotiable instrument**, transferable so that a subsequent holder may take it free of certain defenses the maker could have raised against the original payee.

Practically, this is why loans are sold. A borrower's obligation follows the note into the secondary market, and the borrower's payments may be directed to a servicer they never chose.

**A licensee's role is recognition, not analysis.** Whether a particular note is negotiable and what defenses survive are legal questions.

### 1.4 The clauses that matter

Several note and security instrument provisions come up constantly and are frequently misdescribed.

| Clause | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Acceleration** | On default, makes the entire balance immediately due. **Without it, a lender could only sue for missed payments** — which is why foreclosure depends on it |
| **Alienation (due-on-sale)** | Permits the lender to call the loan due on transfer of the property. This is why loans are generally not assumable without lender consent |
| **Prepayment** | Addresses whether and on what terms the borrower may pay early. A **prepayment penalty** is a charge for doing so, and its permissibility is regulated |
| **Late charge** | A charge for a payment received after a stated period |
| **Subordination** | An agreement to take a lower lien position than the instrument would otherwise hold |
| **Release** | In a blanket loan, permits individual parcels to be released as they are sold |

**Acceleration and alienation are the two students merge.** Acceleration responds to **default**; alienation responds to **transfer.** They do different work and are triggered by different events.

### 1.5 Straight and amortized notes

**A straight note** — sometimes called an interest-only or term note — provides for interest payments with the principal due at maturity. The principal does not reduce along the way.

**An amortized note** provides for payments that retire both interest and principal over the term, so the balance reaches zero at maturity. A **fully amortized** loan retires completely; a **partially amortized** loan leaves a **balloon** payment due at maturity.

### 1.6 Checkpoint

You should be able to state what each instrument does, distinguish acceleration from alienation, and explain what a balloon payment signals about the amortization.

## 2. Deeds of trust and mortgages

### 2.1 The California instrument

California residential lending overwhelmingly uses the **deed of trust**, and it involves **three** parties rather than two.

| Party | Role |
|---|---|
| **Trustor** | The borrower, who conveys title in trust as security |
| **Beneficiary** | The lender, for whose benefit the security is held |
| **Trustee** | A neutral third party holding the power of sale |

A **mortgage** by contrast involves two parties: **mortgagor** (borrower) and **mortgagee** (lender).

**The memory device that survives the exam:** the trus**tor** gives the security; the trus**tee** holds the power; the bene**ficiary** benefits.

### 2.2 Why the third party matters

The trustee holds a **power of sale** — the authority to sell the property on default without going to court.

That single feature is why California residential foreclosure is predominantly **nonjudicial**, and it drives everything in Section 6: the timeline, the borrower's reinstatement window, and the deficiency consequences.

### 2.3 Title theory and lien theory

Jurisdictions describe the security relationship differently. Some treat the security instrument as passing legal title to the lender until the debt is repaid; others treat it as creating a lien while the borrower retains title.

**California's deed of trust is generally treated as a lien for most practical purposes**, notwithstanding its form as a conveyance in trust. The borrower possesses, uses, and can transfer the property, subject to the security interest.

For a licensee the practical point is what it produces: **the borrower is the owner, and the lender holds a claim.**

### 2.4 Satisfaction and the reconveyance

When the debt is satisfied, the beneficiary directs the trustee to reconvey, and a **reconveyance** is recorded releasing the deed of trust.

**Payment does not clear the record; the recorded document does.** A seller who says a loan was paid off in 2016 has stated a fact about the debt and nothing about the title, and an unreleased deed of trust in a preliminary report requires a document — the same point Lessons 05 and 09 make from the title side.

### 2.5 Priority

**First, second, and junior** describe recording position rather than loan type. A "second" is simply a deed of trust recorded after another.

Priority determines **who is paid first from a limited fund and whose interest survives a foreclosure.** A junior lienholder wiped out by a senior foreclosure has lost its security whatever the merits of the debt.

The exceptions from Lesson 05 apply here: property tax liens generally take priority regardless of timing, mechanic's lien priority may relate back, and **subordination** moves a lienholder down voluntarily.

**Subordination is a significant decision, not a formality**, and it belongs to counsel — most often when a seller who carried financing is asked to move behind a construction lender.

### 2.6 Purchase money and seller financing

A **purchase money loan** is one used to acquire the property. California treats certain purchase money loans differently for deficiency purposes, addressed in Section 6.

**Seller financing** — where the seller carries all or part of the purchase price — creates a note and deed of trust in the seller's favor. It is legitimate and common, and it raises specific questions: priority, what happens if the buyer defaults, servicing, and whether any disclosure or regulatory requirement applies to the arrangement.

**A licensee arranging seller financing should route the structure to counsel** rather than assembling it from a form.

### 2.7 Other structures

**Assumption** — the buyer takes over the existing loan. Generally requires lender consent because of the alienation clause, and the seller may remain liable unless released by novation. **A seller who "let the buyer assume" and assumes he is released has assumed a novation he did not obtain.**

**Subject to** — the buyer takes title without assuming the loan, which remains the seller's obligation and remains secured by the property. It triggers the alienation clause and carries real risk for both parties.

**Blanket loan** — one loan secured by more than one parcel, typically with a release clause permitting individual parcels to be sold.

**Construction loan** — advanced in stages as work progresses, with its own priority concerns against mechanic's liens.

**Home equity line** — a revolving credit facility secured by the property.

### 2.8 Checkpoint

You should be able to name the three parties to a deed of trust, explain what the power of sale produces, and distinguish assumption from taking subject to.

## 3. Loan markets and sources

### 3.1 Primary and secondary

**The primary market** is where loans are originated — the lender and the borrower.

**The secondary market** is where existing loans are bought and sold between investors.

The secondary market's function is **liquidity**. A lender who can sell a loan recovers its capital and can lend again, so the supply of mortgage credit does not depend on any one lender's balance sheet.

Two consequences a licensee should be able to explain:

**Underwriting standards flow backward from the secondary market.** Lenders originate to standards that make loans saleable, which is why documentation requirements can feel disconnected from a particular borrower's obvious ability to pay.

**Servicing frequently transfers.** A borrower's loan may be sold and the servicing directed elsewhere, and the borrower's obligations follow the note.

### 3.2 Sources of financing

| Source | Character |
|---|---|
| **Depository institutions** | Banks and credit unions lending from deposits and other funds |
| **Mortgage bankers** | Originate and fund loans, frequently selling them into the secondary market |
| **Mortgage brokers** | Arrange loans between borrowers and lenders; do not lend their own funds |
| **Private and hard money lenders** | Individual or fund lenders, typically shorter term and higher rate, often asset-focused |
| **Seller financing** | The seller carries all or part of the price |
| **Government-backed programs** | An agency insures or guarantees the lender, supporting terms qualifying borrowers might not otherwise obtain |

### 3.3 Government-backed programs, and three things licensees misstate

Government-backed programs share a structure: **an agency insures or guarantees the lender against certain losses**, reducing risk and supporting terms — notably lower down payments.

- **The government is not the lender.** Private lenders make the loans.
- **The loans bear interest** on ordinary terms.
- **They are not restricted to new construction**, and not to first-time buyers as a general matter.

Government programs commonly carry **property condition requirements**, which matters to a seller evaluating an offer: a property with deferred maintenance may not satisfy them. **That is a closing-probability question, not a preference.**

### 3.4 Conventional financing and mortgage insurance

A **conventional loan** is one not insured or guaranteed by a government agency. Where the down payment is below a program threshold, **mortgage insurance** is commonly required, adding to the monthly cost and protecting the lender rather than the borrower.

Students consistently get that last point wrong. **Mortgage insurance protects the lender.** The borrower pays for it, and receives access to financing at a lower down payment in exchange.

### 3.5 The Federal Reserve and rates, in outline

Monetary policy influences the cost and availability of credit, and mortgage rates respond to broader capital market conditions rather than being set by any single institution.

**What a licensee should take from this:** rates move for reasons outside any participant's control, and **predicting them is not within the license.** "Rates will be lower in the spring" is a prediction stated as certainty, on which a client may act.

### 3.6 What a licensee may say about financing

**May:** explain the general characteristics of common program types; explain what preapproval is; explain how the appraisal interacts with the loan; explain how a credit affects the transaction; recommend the buyer speak with more than one lender; explain what tends to make an offer read as strong.

**May not:** recommend a specific program as suitable for this buyer; predict what a lender will approve; predict the direction of rates; advise on structuring the buyer's finances to qualify; or suggest anything be omitted or characterized differently on an application.

**And never accept anything of value for referring a buyer to a settlement service provider.** Referrals happen; payment for them does not, and disclosure does not cure it.

### 3.7 Checkpoint

You should be able to explain what the secondary market does and why it shapes underwriting, and state who mortgage insurance protects.

## 4. Interest, amortization, and points

### 4.1 Simple interest

Real estate loans generally use **simple interest** — interest computed on the outstanding principal, not on accumulated interest.

> **Interest = Principal × Rate × Time**

> **A $400,000 loan at 6% for one year:** 400,000 × 0.06 = **$24,000** annual interest.
>
> **One month's interest:** $24,000 ÷ 12 = **$2,000.**

### 4.2 How an amortized payment splits

Each payment on an amortized loan covers the interest accrued since the last payment, and the remainder reduces principal.

> **$400,000 at 6%, monthly payment $2,398.20.**
>
> **Month 1:** interest = 400,000 × 0.06 ÷ 12 = **$2,000.00.** Principal = 2,398.20 − 2,000.00 = **$398.20.** New balance: **$399,601.80.**
>
> **Month 2:** interest = 399,601.80 × 0.06 ÷ 12 = **$1,998.01.** Principal = **$400.19.** New balance: **$399,201.61.**

**The pattern is the lesson.** Early payments are almost entirely interest; the principal portion grows each month while the interest portion shrinks. This is why a borrower five years into a thirty-year loan has repaid far less principal than the elapsed fraction suggests — and why a seller's payoff surprises him.

### 4.3 Points

**One point equals one percent of the loan amount.**

**Discount points** are paid to reduce the interest rate. **Origination points** are a charge for making the loan. Both are quoted the same way and do different things.

> **2 points on a $400,000 loan** = 400,000 × 0.02 = **$8,000.**

**The break-even calculation** is the useful one:

> Paying 2 points ($8,000) reduces the payment by $140 per month.
>
> $8,000 ÷ $140 = **57 months**, about 4 years 9 months.
>
> A borrower who expects to hold the loan longer than that comes out ahead; one who expects to sell or refinance sooner does not.

**A licensee may explain the arithmetic. Whether this borrower should pay points depends on his plans, tax situation, and alternatives — which is a lender and advisor question.**

### 4.4 Rate structures

| Structure | Characteristic | The borrower's exposure |
|---|---|---|
| **Fixed rate** | Rate and payment fixed for the term | None to rate movement; pays for that certainty |
| **Adjustable rate** | Rate changes per an index plus a margin, within caps | Payment changes over time |
| **Interest-only period** | Payments cover interest only for a period | Principal is not retired during it |
| **Balloon** | Payments do not retire principal by maturity | A large sum due at maturity |

**For an adjustable-rate loan, the rate at each adjustment is the index plus the lender's margin, subject to the note's caps** — not the lender's discretion, and not the borrower's credit at that time.

**Caps** limit movement: a periodic cap limits change at any single adjustment; a lifetime cap limits total movement over the loan's life.

### 4.5 Nominal and effective rates

The **nominal rate** is the rate stated in the note. The **effective rate** reflects the actual cost including points and certain fees, which is why a loan with a lower stated rate can cost more.

**The annual percentage rate** is the disclosed measure designed to make loans comparable by including certain costs beyond interest. It is a comparison tool with defined contents, and a licensee should point a borrower to it rather than computing or interpreting it for him.

### 4.6 Impounds

Many loans require an **impound** or escrow account, into which the borrower pays monthly amounts toward property taxes and insurance, which the servicer then pays when due.

Two practical consequences: the monthly obligation is larger than principal and interest alone, and an **impound shortage** after a tax or insurance increase produces a payment increase on a loan the borrower believed was fixed. Explaining that in advance prevents an angry call the licensee will otherwise receive.

### 4.7 Checkpoint

You should be able to compute a month's interest and the principal split, compute points and a break-even, and explain what determines an adjustable rate at adjustment.

## 5. Loan-to-value and qualifying ratios

### 5.1 Loan-to-value

**LTV = loan amount ÷ value**, where value is the **lower of appraised value and purchase price.**

> **A $920,000 purchase with a $736,000 loan:** 736,000 ÷ 920,000 = **80% LTV.**
>
> **If the appraisal comes in at $900,000**, the lender lends against $900,000. At 80%: 900,000 × 0.80 = **$720,000** — $16,000 less than planned.

**That single rule — lenders lend against the lower of value or price — explains every consequence of a low appraisal**, and it is worth memorizing in exactly that form.

The buyer's options are then: bring the additional funds, renegotiate the price, negotiate another resolution, or cancel if a contingency permits and its procedure is followed.

**What does not happen automatically:** the price does not adjust to the appraised value by operation of any loan program, the loan is not denied, and **no one is entitled to a second appraisal as of right.**

### 5.2 Combined loan-to-value

Where more than one loan is secured by the property, **CLTV** measures the total.

> A $920,000 property with a $736,000 first and a $46,000 second:
> (736,000 + 46,000) ÷ 920,000 = 782,000 ÷ 920,000 = **85% CLTV.**

### 5.3 Equity

**Equity = value − liens.** It changes with value, with principal reduction, and with new borrowing — which is why a homeowner who takes a home equity line has converted equity into debt without any change in the property.

### 5.4 Qualifying ratios

Lenders measure a borrower's capacity with ratios. The two conventional ones:

**The front-end (housing) ratio** = housing expense ÷ gross monthly income. Housing expense conventionally includes principal, interest, taxes, and insurance — **PITI** — plus association dues where applicable.

**The back-end (total debt) ratio** = total monthly obligations ÷ gross monthly income, including the housing expense plus other recurring debt.

> **Gross monthly income $9,000.** Proposed PITI $2,520. Car payment $480, student loan $300, credit minimums $120.
>
> **Front-end:** 2,520 ÷ 9,000 = **28%.**
> **Back-end:** (2,520 + 480 + 300 + 120) ÷ 9,000 = 3,420 ÷ 9,000 = **38%.**

**Thresholds vary by program, by compensating factors, and over time.** A licensee should be able to compute the ratios and explain what they measure, and should **never state a threshold as a rule** — that is a lender question, and stating it is the kind of confident error that costs a buyer a property.

### 5.5 The three numbers, restated

From the Practice course, because it belongs to the mathematics:

**What the buyer can borrow** — the lender's number.
**What cash is available** — down payment, closing costs, reserves, moving costs, and the immediate costs of occupying a home. Buyers routinely plan to the down payment and forget the rest.
**What the buyer is willing to spend** — often lower than the first, and **the number that should drive the search.**

A licensee who searches to the lender's maximum is showing properties the buyer will feel unable to afford.

### 5.6 What jeopardizes an approval

Advise the buyer of these **in writing at the start of escrow**, because by the time they surface it is usually late:

| Buyer action | Effect |
|---|---|
| Opening new credit, financing furniture or a car | Changes the debt-to-income analysis the approval rested on |
| Changing or leaving a job | Employment is a condition; a change may end the approval |
| Large unexplained deposits | Requires sourcing; may look like undisclosed borrowed funds |
| Moving money between accounts | Complicates asset verification |
| Paying off debt with funds needed for closing | Reduces verified cash to close |
| Missing a payment on any obligation | Affects credit at the re-check |

**A short written note at the start of escrow prevents more failed transactions than any other single communication a licensee sends.**

### 5.7 Occupancy is a material fact

Occupancy — owner-occupied, second home, or investment — affects pricing, program eligibility, and which consumer protections apply. It is therefore a material fact the lender relies on.

**A buyer who asks a licensee to record owner-occupancy on a property he intends to rent is asking for participation in loan fraud.** Decline plainly, explain why, and record the loan as it actually is. Whether he can afford the payment either way is beside the point: the misstatement is about obtaining terms on a false representation of the risk being priced.

### 5.8 Checkpoint

You should be able to compute LTV, CLTV, and both qualifying ratios, and explain why the lower of value or price governs.

## 6. Default, foreclosure, and deficiency

### 6.1 What default is

Default is a failure to perform an obligation under the note or the security instrument. Non-payment is the common form; others include failure to pay taxes, failure to maintain insurance, and unauthorized transfer triggering the alienation clause.

**Acceleration is what makes default consequential.** Without an acceleration clause a lender could pursue only missed payments; with one, the entire balance becomes due.

### 6.2 The two paths

| | Nonjudicial foreclosure | Judicial foreclosure |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | The trustee's **power of sale** | A court action |
| Speed | Faster | Slower |
| Court involvement | None | Yes |
| Deficiency judgment | **Generally not available** | May be available, subject to limits |
| Redemption after sale | **Generally none** | A statutory redemption period may apply |

**The trade-off is the point.** A lender proceeding nonjudicially gets speed and gives up the deficiency; a lender proceeding judicially may preserve a deficiency claim and accepts a slower, costlier process. **In California residential practice, nonjudicial foreclosure predominates.**

### 6.3 The nonjudicial sequence, in outline

The process is statutory and its details are legal. In outline:

1. **Default occurs**, and the beneficiary elects to proceed.
2. **A notice of default is recorded**, beginning the statutory period.
3. **A reinstatement period runs**, during which the borrower may generally cure by paying the arrearage and permitted costs — **not the accelerated balance.**
4. **A notice of sale is recorded, published, posted, and mailed**, after the applicable period.
5. **The trustee's sale occurs**, and title passes by **trustee's deed.**

**The reinstatement window is the point most misunderstood by borrowers.** For a period, curing the arrearage restores the loan; after that window closes, the borrower's route is generally **redemption before sale** — paying the full accelerated amount — which is a materially different sum.

California also imposes borrower-protective requirements on servicers in the residential foreclosure context, including contact and single-point-of-contact obligations. **The specifics are legal, and a licensee's role is to know they exist and route the borrower to counsel and to a HUD-approved housing counselor.**

### 6.4 Deficiency

A **deficiency** is the shortfall where the sale proceeds do not satisfy the debt. California restricts deficiency judgments substantially:

- **Generally unavailable after a nonjudicial sale**, whatever the loan type.
- **Restricted for purchase money loans** in defined circumstances — protection connected to the loan having been used to acquire the property.
- **Subject to limits even in judicial foreclosure**, including valuation-based constraints.

**Whether a deficiency is available in a specific case is a legal question with significant consequences**, and it is exactly the question a distressed client will ask a licensee. The answer is a referral.

### 6.5 Alternatives to foreclosure

| Alternative | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Reinstatement** | Curing the arrearage within the window |
| **Forbearance or modification** | The lender agrees to pause, restructure, or change terms |
| **Short sale** | A sale for less than the balance, with lienholder approval |
| **Deed in lieu of foreclosure** | A voluntary conveyance to the lender |

**Short sales and deeds in lieu carry credit, tax, and deficiency consequences that are legal and tax questions.** A licensee who tells a distressed seller that a short sale "won't hurt as much" or that a deficiency "gets wiped out" has given advice in the client's most consequential moment, on a question the licensee cannot answer.

**And a deed in lieu does not automatically extinguish junior liens**, which is one reason a lender may decline one.

### 6.6 Junior liens and the sale

A senior foreclosure generally extinguishes junior liens as claims against the property — **while the underlying debts may survive as personal obligations**, subject to the deficiency limits above.

That is why priority matters and why a junior lienholder monitors senior loans: a second wiped out by a first's foreclosure has lost its security.

### 6.7 The licensee working with distressed sellers

Distressed transactions concentrate every risk in this course, and three practices control most of it:

1. **Refer early and repeatedly.** Counsel for the legal consequences, a tax professional for the tax consequences, and a HUD-approved housing counselor for the process. Do it in writing.
2. **Never predict an outcome** — approval of a short sale, forgiveness of a deficiency, the credit effect, or the timeline.
3. **Recognize the fraud patterns.** Distressed situations attract equity-stripping schemes, rescue arrangements, and transfers that leave the owner worse off. A licensee who sees one escalates to the broker and does not participate, however the request is framed.

### 6.8 Two loans, compared honestly

> **A borrower is offered two structures on the same purchase.**
>
> - **Loan A:** fixed rate for the full term, no points, higher rate.
> - **Loan B:** fixed for an initial period and adjustable afterward, two points paid at closing, lower initial rate.
>
> **The comparison a licensee is not qualified to perform**, and the analysis that shows why it matters anyway.
>
> **Section 4's mechanics supply the vocabulary.** Points are paid now to reduce the rate later, which makes the comparison a question about **how long the borrower holds the loan.** The shorter the holding period, the worse the points look; the longer it is, the better.
>
> **Section 4's amortization supplies the second half.** Early payments on an amortizing loan are mostly interest, so **a borrower who sells in four years has paid down very little principal** — which means the difference between the two structures is nearly all rate and points rather than equity.
>
> **The adjustable feature is the third variable, and it is the one presented worst.** The initial payment on Loan B is the number the borrower will be shown. **It is the one figure guaranteed to be true for the shortest time**, and the meaningful number is the worst case: what the payment becomes if the rate adjusts to its maximum.
>
> **What determines the right answer**, and none of it is on the rate sheet: how long the borrower expects to hold the property, how stable his income is, what reserves he holds, and what he would do if the payment rose to its maximum.
>
> **What the licensee does.**
>
> **He explains the mechanics** — what points are, what amortization means early in a loan, what an adjustment does — because these are concepts, and Section 4 exists so that he can.
>
> **He tells the borrower to ask the lender for both structures on a common basis**, including the worst-case payment on Loan B and a total cost over the holding period the borrower actually expects.
>
> **He asks the questions the rate sheet does not** — holding period, income stability, reserves — because those are the facts that decide it and nobody else in the transaction is asking.
>
> **He records the conversation.**
>
> **What he does not do.** Recommend either loan, characterize one as cheaper, tell the borrower "you can always refinance," or opine that rates will move in a direction. **The first three are the lender's work and the fourth is a prediction stated as a plan** — which is, across this entire course, the single most damaging sentence available in a financing conversation.

### 6.9 Checkpoint

You should be able to compare the two foreclosure paths on speed and deficiency, explain the reinstatement window, and state what a licensee says to a distressed client asking about a deficiency.

| **Nonjudicial foreclosure** | Sale under the power of sale. Faster; **generally no deficiency and generally no post-sale redemption.** |
| **Judicial foreclosure** | A court action. Slower; a deficiency may be available subject to limits, and a statutory redemption period may apply. |
| **Reinstatement** | Curing the arrearage and permitted costs within the statutory window — **not the accelerated balance**. |
| **Redemption before sale** | Paying the full accelerated amount after the reinstatement window closes. |
| **Deficiency** | The shortfall where sale proceeds do not satisfy the debt. Substantially restricted in California, and always a legal question. |
| **Short sale** | A sale for less than the balance, requiring lienholder approval, with credit, tax, and deficiency consequences for counsel. |

## Practice problems

### Problem 1

A seller carrying financing asks his agent whether the buyer can "just take over the loan," and
whether the seller will still owe anything. Separately, the buyer proposes taking title "subject to"
the existing first, and the agent says the two arrangements are the same thing.

**Correct the agent and answer the seller.**

> **Solution.** **They are not the same thing, and the difference is who owes.**
>
> **Assumption:** the buyer takes over the loan. It generally requires **lender consent** because of the
> **alienation (due-on-sale) clause** — and critically, **the seller remains liable unless released by
> novation.** A seller who "let the buyer assume" and assumes he is released has assumed a novation he
> did not obtain.
>
> **Subject to:** the buyer takes title **without assuming**. The loan remains the seller's obligation
> and remains secured by the property — so the seller has transferred the property, kept the debt, and
> lost control of whether it gets paid. It also triggers the alienation clause, with real risk for both
> parties.
>
> **What the licensee does:** explain the mechanism, and **route the structure to counsel.** Whether the
> seller is released, and what the lender may do on transfer, are legal questions.

### Problem 2

A note contains an acceleration clause and an alienation clause. The borrower misses three payments;
separately, another borrower transfers the property to her adult son. A licensee says both clauses do
the same thing and that a lender "can always call the loan."

**Distinguish them.**

> **Solution.** **They respond to different events and do different work.**
>
> **Acceleration responds to default.** On default it makes the **entire balance immediately due** —
> and without it, a lender could pursue only the missed payments. That is why foreclosure depends on
> it: the lender is enforcing an accelerated debt, not three payments.
>
> **Alienation responds to transfer.** It permits the lender to call the loan due when the property is
> transferred, which is why loans are generally **not assumable without lender consent.** The transfer
> to the son triggers this clause, not the acceleration clause.
>
> **"A lender can always call the loan" is wrong.** The lender's rights come from the clauses actually
> in the instrument and the events that trigger them.

### Problem 3

Compute for a $400,000 loan at 6% with a monthly payment of $2,398.20: (a) the first month's interest
and principal split; (b) the second month's; (c) the cost of 2 points; (d) the break-even if those
points reduce the payment by $140 per month.

**Then state what the pattern in (a) and (b) explains.**

> **Solution.** (a) Interest = 400,000 × 0.06 ÷ 12 = **$2,000.00.** Principal = 2,398.20 − 2,000.00 =
> **$398.20.** Balance: **$399,601.80.**
>
> (b) Interest = 399,601.80 × 0.06 ÷ 12 = **$1,998.01.** Principal = **$400.19.** Balance:
> **$399,201.61.**
>
> (c) 400,000 × 0.02 = **$8,000.** One point is one percent of the **loan amount** — not the price.
>
> (d) 8,000 ÷ 140 = **57 months**, about 4 years 9 months. A borrower holding longer comes out ahead;
> one selling or refinancing sooner does not.
>
> **The pattern:** early payments are almost entirely interest, and the principal portion grows only
> slowly. That is why a borrower five years into a thirty-year loan has repaid far less principal than
> the elapsed fraction suggests — and it is the arithmetic behind the **payoff surprise** a seller gets
> at closing.
>
> **On (d):** a licensee may explain the arithmetic. **Whether this borrower should pay points** depends
> on his plans, tax situation, and alternatives — a lender and advisor question.

### Problem 4

A buyer with an adjustable-rate loan asks his agent what his rate will be at the first adjustment. The
agent says it depends on the lender and on whether the buyer's credit has improved, and adds that
rates "will definitely be lower by spring."

**Correct each statement.**

> **Solution.** **All three are wrong.**
>
> **The rate at adjustment is the index plus the lender's margin, subject to the note's caps.** Not the
> lender's discretion.
>
> **Not the borrower's credit at that time** either — the margin was set when the loan was made.
>
> **Caps limit the movement:** a **periodic cap** limits change at any single adjustment; a **lifetime
> cap** limits total movement over the loan's life. Those are in the note, and the borrower should read
> them.
>
> **The rate prediction is outside the license.** Mortgage rates respond to broader capital market
> conditions and are not set by any single institution. "Rates will be lower in the spring" is a
> prediction stated as certainty, on which a client may act.
>
> Worth adding while the subject is open: an **impound shortage** after a tax or insurance increase
> raises the payment on a loan the borrower thought was fixed. Explaining that in advance prevents an
> angry call.

### Problem 5

A $920,000 purchase with 20% down. The appraisal comes in at $900,000. The buyer's agent tells him
the price will adjust automatically to the appraised value, that the loan is now denied, and that he
can demand a second appraisal.

**Compute the shortfall and correct all three statements.**

> **Solution.** **Lenders lend against the lower of appraised value and purchase price** — so against
> $900,000. At 80%: 900,000 × 0.80 = **$720,000**, against the $736,000 planned. **A $16,000 shortfall
> in loan proceeds.**
>
> **All three statements are wrong.** The price does **not** adjust to appraised value by operation of
> any loan program. The loan is **not** denied. And **no one is entitled to a second appraisal as of
> right.**
>
> **The buyer's actual options:** bring the additional $16,000, renegotiate the price in whole or part,
> negotiate another resolution such as a credit or a term change, or cancel if a contingency permits and
> its procedure is followed.
>
> **The rule worth memorizing in exactly that form** — the lower of value or price — explains every
> consequence of a low appraisal.

### Problem 6

Gross monthly income $9,000. Proposed PITI $2,520. Car payment $480, student loan $300, credit card
minimums $120. The buyer's preapproval says $1.1 million; his comfortable payment supports about
$860,000. A relative has offered $100,000 of help.

**Compute both ratios, and address the three numbers and the gift.**

> **Solution.** **Front-end:** 2,520 ÷ 9,000 = **28%.**
> **Back-end:** (2,520 + 480 + 300 + 120) ÷ 9,000 = 3,420 ÷ 9,000 = **38%.**
>
> **A licensee computes and explains the ratios and never states a threshold as a rule.** Thresholds
> vary by program, by compensating factors, and over time — that is a lender question, and stating one
> is the kind of confident error that costs a buyer a property.
>
> **Search to $860,000, not $1.1 million.** The three numbers are what he can borrow, what cash is
> available — down payment, closing costs, reserves, moving costs, and the immediate costs of occupying
> a home, which buyers routinely forget — and **what he is willing to spend**, which is the number that
> should drive the search. Searching to the lender's maximum shows him properties he will feel unable
> to afford.
>
> **On the $100,000: confirm with the lender first.** Ask him to confirm how the assistance would be
> treated and documented, do not build the search around the higher figure until confirmed, and do not
> contact the relative directly. **A gift and a loan are treated differently**, because an undisclosed
> loan is a hidden obligation that undermines the lender's capacity analysis.

### Problem 7

Two weeks into escrow a buyer finances $9,000 of furniture, moves $22,000 from a relative into his
checking account, and pays off his car with part of his down payment funds. He also asks the licensee
to record the property as owner-occupied, though he intends to rent it, noting he can afford either
payment.

**Assess each act.**

> **Solution.** **All three financial moves attack the file the approval rested on**, and the occupancy
> request is a different category entirely.
>
> **Financing furniture** changes the debt-to-income analysis. **The $22,000 deposit** requires sourcing
> and may look like undisclosed borrowed funds. **Paying off the car with closing funds** solves one
> ratio by breaking another — it reduces verified cash to close.
>
> Underlying all three: an approval rests on the file **as underwritten**, and lenders commonly
> **re-verify credit and employment before funding.** The written start-of-escrow warning — do not open
> credit, change jobs, or move large sums without telling your lender first — prevents more failed
> transactions than any other communication a licensee sends. Also on that list: leaving a job and
> missing a payment on any obligation.
>
> **The occupancy request is loan fraud, and affordability is beside the point.** Occupancy affects
> pricing, program eligibility, and which consumer protections apply, and is therefore **a material fact
> the lender relies on.** The misstatement is not about whether he will pay; it is about obtaining terms
> on a false representation of the risk being priced. Decline plainly, explain why, and record the loan
> as it actually is.

### Problem 8

A distressed homeowner two payments behind asks her agent: can she still save the house, will a short
sale be approved, will she owe the difference, and would a deed in lieu clear her second mortgage. The
agent tells her a short sale "won't hurt as much," that the deficiency "gets wiped out in California,"
and that a deed in lieu will clear everything.

**Correct the advice and state the correct posture.**

> **Solution.** **Every answer the agent gave was a legal or tax conclusion, and at least two are wrong
> as stated.**
>
> **On saving the house:** for a period after a notice of default is recorded, a **reinstatement**
> window lets the borrower cure by paying the **arrearage and permitted costs — not the accelerated
> balance.** After that window closes, the route is generally **redemption before sale**, paying the
> full accelerated amount, which is a materially different sum. That distinction is the one borrowers
> misunderstand most.
>
> **On approval:** never predicted. A short sale requires lienholder approval.
>
> **On deficiency:** California restricts deficiency judgments substantially — **generally unavailable
> after a nonjudicial sale**, and restricted for **purchase money loans** in defined circumstances. But
> whether a deficiency is available **in her case** is a legal question with significant consequences,
> and a short sale's deficiency treatment depends on what the lienholders agree to. "It gets wiped out"
> is not an answer a licensee may give.
>
> **On the deed in lieu: it does not automatically extinguish junior liens** — which is one reason a
> lender may decline one, and it means her second may survive.
>
> **The correct posture:** refer early and repeatedly, **in writing** — counsel for the legal
> consequences, a tax professional for the tax consequences, and a HUD-approved housing counselor for
> the process. Predict nothing. And recognize that distressed situations attract equity-stripping and
> rescue schemes: a licensee who sees one escalates to the broker and does not participate, however the
> request is framed.

## Chapter summary

**The note is the debt; the security instrument is the remedy.** A note without security is unsecured;
security without a note secures nothing.

**Acceleration responds to default; alienation responds to transfer.** They do different work.

**California uses a three-party deed of trust**, and the trustee's **power of sale** is what produces
nonjudicial foreclosure, the reinstatement window, and the deficiency consequences.

**Payment does not clear the record; a recorded reconveyance does.**

**Priority determines who is paid first and whose interest survives foreclosure**, and subordination is
a decision for counsel rather than a formality.

**Assumption leaves the seller liable without a novation; taking subject to leaves the debt entirely
with the seller.** Route both to counsel.

**The secondary market supplies liquidity and shapes underwriting backward**, which is why
documentation requirements can feel disconnected from an obvious ability to pay.

**Government-backed does not mean government-lent**, and mortgage insurance **protects the lender**
while the borrower pays for it.

**Interest is simple: principal × rate × time.** Each amortized payment covers accrued interest first,
which is why early payments are nearly all interest and why the payoff surprises sellers.

**One point is one percent of the loan.** Compute the break-even; leave the decision to the borrower
and the lender.

**An adjustable rate is index plus margin within the note's caps** — never lender discretion, never the
borrower's later credit — and never predict the direction of rates.

**Lenders lend against the lower of appraised value and purchase price.** That sentence explains every
consequence of a low appraisal, and nothing about the resolution is automatic.

**Compute the ratios; never state a threshold as a rule.** Search to what the buyer is willing to
spend, not the lender's maximum.

**Send the start-of-escrow warning in writing** about credit, employment, and moving funds — and refuse
any request to misstate occupancy, whatever the buyer can afford.

**Nonjudicial foreclosure trades the deficiency for speed; judicial foreclosure trades speed for a
possible deficiency and a redemption period.** Reinstatement cures the arrearage; redemption requires
the accelerated balance.

**With distressed clients, refer early and in writing, predict nothing, and recognize the fraud
patterns.**
