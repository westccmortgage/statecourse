## 1. Value, price, and cost

### 1.1 Three different things

Students merge these constantly, and every valuation error downstream begins here.

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Value** | What the property is **worth** — an opinion, formed as of a date, for a stated purpose |
| **Price** | What someone **actually paid**. A historical fact |
| **Cost** | What it took to **create** the improvements. Also a historical fact |

> **Value is an opinion; price and cost are facts. All three can differ, and frequently do.**

A property can cost $900,000 to build, be worth $700,000, and sell for $760,000 — all at the same time, without anything being wrong.

### 1.2 Market value

The appraisal standard is **market value**, and its definition contains conditions that do the work.

Market value is, in outline, the most probable price a property should bring in a competitive and open market under conditions requisite to a fair sale, where:

- **Buyer and seller are each typically motivated** — neither is forced.
- **Both are well informed** and acting in their own interests.
- **A reasonable exposure time** is allowed in the open market.
- **Payment is in cash or its equivalent**, with financing terms not affecting the price.
- **The price is unaffected by special financing or concessions.**

**Each condition excludes something.** A forced sale, an uninformed buyer, a two-week marketing period, or seller-paid concessions inflating the number each produce a price that is not market value.

That is why a distressed sale may be evidence of a transaction and poor evidence of value, and why the analysis in Section 3 asks whether a comparable reflects **this** market.

### 1.3 The elements of value

Four elements must be present for value to exist, remembered as **DUST**:

| Element | What it requires |
|---|---|
| **D** — Demand | Someone wants it, **with purchasing power** |
| **U** — Utility | It satisfies a need or use |
| **S** — Scarcity | It is in limited supply |
| **T** — Transferability | Ownership can be transferred |

**Demand without purchasing power is not demand.** That single qualification explains why desire alone does not create value, and why affordability constraints move markets.

**Transferability is the one students forget.** A property nobody can convey — clouded title, unresolved authority — has impaired value for that reason alone.

### 1.4 The economic principles

These recur throughout the approaches, and each has a practical application.

| Principle | What it says |
|---|---|
| **Supply and demand** | Price responds to their relationship in this market |
| **Substitution** | A buyer will pay no more than the cost of an equally desirable substitute. **The foundation of all three approaches** |
| **Highest and best use** | Value reflects the most productive legally permitted use — Section 2 |
| **Conformity** | Maximum value arises where a property conforms reasonably to its surroundings |
| **Progression** | A lesser property benefits from surrounding better ones |
| **Regression** | A better property is dragged down by surrounding lesser ones |
| **Contribution** | An improvement adds what the market pays for it, **not what it cost** |
| **Increasing and diminishing returns** | Successive investment adds value up to a point, then less than it costs |
| **Anticipation** | Value reflects expected future benefits |
| **Change** | Value is a snapshot; markets and neighborhoods move |
| **Balance** | Value is maximized where the factors of production are in equilibrium |
| **Externalities** | Factors outside the property affect its value |

### 1.5 Substitution is the engine

Every approach in this lesson is an application of substitution:

- **Sales comparison** — a buyer will pay no more than a similar property sold for.
- **Cost** — a buyer will pay no more than the cost of building an equivalent, less depreciation.
- **Income** — an investor will pay no more than a comparable income stream costs.

A student who holds substitution understands why there are three approaches and why they should converge.

### 1.6 Contribution is not cost

The most common client misconception, and one a licensee must be able to explain without giving offense:

> **Money spent on a property does not become value in the property. The market decides what an improvement contributes, and for specialized improvements the contribution is usually well below the cost.**

A $150,000 recording studio built into a garage in a neighborhood of family homes may contribute a fraction of that, because the pool of buyers who want it — and would pay for it — is small. That is **over-improvement relative to the neighborhood**, and it is a recognized phenomenon rather than a rhetorical device for arguing a seller down.

**The complement is also true:** some modest improvements contribute more than they cost, because they remove a specific buyer objection.

### 1.7 Checkpoint

You should be able to distinguish value, price, and cost; name the elements of value; and explain contribution to a seller without giving offense.

## 2. Highest and best use

### 2.1 The concept

**Highest and best use** is the reasonably probable use that produces the highest value. Appraisal of the land assumes it; appraisal of an improved property considers whether the existing improvement represents it.

### 2.2 The four tests

A use must satisfy all four, applied in order:

| Test | Question |
|---|---|
| **Legally permissible** | Is the use allowed — zoning, private restrictions, entitlements? |
| **Physically possible** | Can the site support it — size, shape, soils, access, utilities? |
| **Financially feasible** | Does it produce a positive return? |
| **Maximally productive** | Among the uses passing the first three, which produces the highest value? |

**The order matters.** A use that is not legally permissible is excluded before feasibility is considered, however profitable it would be.

**And "legally permissible" has two halves**, from Lessons 05 and 06: zoning **and** recorded private restrictions, with the more restrictive governing. Zoning permitting a use does not authorize it where the CC&Rs prohibit it.

### 2.3 Interim use

Where the highest and best use is not yet feasible, the current use may be an **interim use** — economically reasonable now, expected to change.

A single-family home on a parcel in a corridor transitioning to commercial is the standard example: the house is the interim use, and the land may be worth more than the improved property.

### 2.4 When the improvement subtracts

> **Where the value of the land as if vacant exceeds the value of the improved property, the improvement has negative contribution** — its removal cost is a deduction rather than an addition.

This is counterintuitive and real. A functionally obsolete building on a valuable site can make the property worth less than the bare land.

### 2.5 Why a licensee needs this

Three situations:

**Pricing a property whose use is changing.** A licensee who prices a transitioning property as a house may be leaving the land value on the table — or, in the other direction, may be pricing land value into a property whose entitlement does not support it.

**Advising on improvements.** A seller asking whether to add a fourth bedroom is asking a contribution and conformity question.

**Recognizing where the analysis exceeds the license.** Highest and best use for a development site is appraisal and entitlement work. A licensee who tells a buyer the parcel's highest and best use is a fourplex has made a legal, physical, and financial determination in one sentence.

### 2.6 Checkpoint

You should be able to apply the four tests in order and explain when an improvement has negative contribution.

## 3. The sales comparison approach

### 3.1 What it does

The sales comparison approach estimates value by analyzing **closed sales of comparable properties**, adjusting each for its differences from the subject, and reconciling the adjusted indications.

It is the primary approach for residential property, because residential buyers behave by substitution — they compare.

### 3.2 The direction of adjustment

The most-missed mechanic in valuation, and worth stating in a form that removes ambiguity.

> **Adjustments are made to the comparable, never to the subject.**
>
> The question each adjustment answers is: *what would this comparable have sold for if it had been like the subject?*

Therefore:

- If the comparable is **superior**, its price is adjusted **downward**.
- If the comparable is **inferior**, its price is adjusted **upward**.

The memory aid: **CBS — Comparable Better, Subtract.** And its complement: comparable worse, add.

### 3.3 A worked grid

Subject: 1,800 sq ft, 3 bed, 2 bath, 6,000 sq ft lot, 2-car garage, updated kitchen, no view.

| | Comp A | Comp B | Comp C |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Sale price | $845,000 | $902,000 | $810,000 |
| Closed | 2 months ago | 5 months ago | 1 month ago |
| Living area | 1,800 | 1,980 | 1,700 |
| Bed / bath | 3 / 2 | 4 / 2 | 3 / 2 |
| Lot | 6,100 | 6,000 | 5,400 |
| Garage | 2-car | 2-car | 1-car |
| Kitchen | Updated | Updated | Original |
| **Adjustments** | | | |
| Market conditions | — | +$9,000 | — |
| Living area | — | −$27,000 | +$15,000 |
| Bedroom count | — | −$10,000 | — |
| Lot size | −$1,000 | — | +$4,000 |
| Garage | — | — | +$12,000 |
| Kitchen | — | — | +$20,000 |
| **Net adjustment** | −$1,000 | −$28,000 | +$51,000 |
| **Adjusted value** | **$844,000** | **$874,000** | **$861,000** |

Read what each line does:

- **Comp B closed five months ago in a rising market**, so it is adjusted **up** — it would sell for more today.
- **Comp B is larger and has a fourth bedroom**, both superior, so both are adjusted **down** toward the subject.
- **Comp C has an original kitchen and a one-car garage**, both inferior, so both are adjusted **up**.

### 3.4 Reconciliation

Three adjusted values — $844,000, $874,000, $861,000 — are **not averaged.** Averaging treats a comparable requiring $51,000 of adjustment as exactly as reliable as one requiring $1,000, which it is not, and silently discards that information.

Reconciliation weights by reliability:

1. **Adjustment size**, gross as well as net. A small net can conceal a large gross, and reliability tracks gross.
2. **Recency.**
3. **Similarity in the characteristics driving this market.**

A defensible conclusion: *the supported range is approximately $845,000 to $870,000, most probable near $850,000 to $855,000, weighted toward Comp A as the least-adjusted and most similar indicator.*

**A range, a most-probable point, and a stated reason for the weighting** — not a single number presented as fact.

### 3.5 Where adjustment amounts come from

Adjustment values are derived, in order of preference:

1. **Paired sales analysis** — comparing sales differing in one characteristic to isolate its contribution.
2. **Market-derived rates** — an established rate within this submarket, applied to modest differences only.
3. **Cost with depreciation** — for a specific improvement, recognizing that contribution rarely equals cost.
4. **Reasoned judgment**, disclosed as such.

The last is legitimate when the first three are unavailable, provided it is **identified as judgment rather than presented as measurement.**

### 3.6 What each data category proves

| Category | What it proves | What it does not |
|---|---|---|
| **Closed sales** | What buyers actually paid | Current conditions, if the market moved |
| **Pending sales** | Where the market is moving now | The final price; terms can change |
| **Active listings** | What sellers are asking; the competition | **Value** — asking prices are hopes |
| **Expired and withdrawn** | What the market rejected | Much alone, but a useful ceiling indicator |

**Active listings never establish value.** A seller can ask any number, and three homes listed at a price for over ninety days without an offer are evidence the market has **declined** to pay it three separate times.

### 3.7 Common errors

| Error | Why it is wrong |
|---|---|
| Adjusting the subject instead of the comparable | Reverses the entire logic |
| Averaging adjusted values | Treats unreliable indicators as equal to reliable ones |
| Discarding every comparable with a difference | Leaves nothing to analyze; differences are what adjustments exist for |
| Applying a price-per-square-foot rate across large differences | Contribution per square foot is not linear across a large gap |
| Many small adjustments reaching a target | The grid becomes an argument for a predetermined number |
| Automatic exclusion or non-adjustment of a distressed sale | Whether it reflects this market is an **analytical** question |

### 3.8 Checkpoint

You should be able to adjust in the correct direction, reconcile without averaging, and say what an active listing proves.

## 4. The cost approach

### 4.1 What it does

The cost approach estimates value as:

> **Land value (as if vacant, at highest and best use) + cost of improvements new − accrued depreciation = indicated value**

It applies substitution directly: a buyer will pay no more than the cost of acquiring a site and building an equivalent improvement.

### 4.2 Reproduction and replacement cost

**Reproduction cost** is the cost to construct an **exact replica**, including any obsolete features.

**Replacement cost** is the cost to construct a building of **equivalent utility** using current materials and methods.

**Replacement cost is generally the more useful measure**, because a buyer does not want the obsolete features. Reproduction cost matters for historic and unusual structures.

### 4.3 Cost estimating methods

| Method | How |
|---|---|
| **Square foot (comparative)** | Applying a cost per square foot from comparable construction. Fast, and the common approach |
| **Unit-in-place** | Costing each component — foundation, framing, roofing — installed |
| **Quantity survey** | Itemizing every material and labor cost. The most detailed and least practical |

### 4.4 Depreciation: the three categories

**Depreciation** here means loss in value from any cause, and it comes in three forms. Distinguishing them is the examinable and practical skill.

| Type | Source | Curable? |
|---|---|---|
| **Physical deterioration** | Wear, age, deferred maintenance | Often curable — worn carpet, a failed roof. Sometimes not — structural aging |
| **Functional obsolescence** | Something about the **improvement itself** — poor layout, one bathroom, an outdated system, an over-improvement | Sometimes curable |
| **External (economic) obsolescence** | Something **outside the property** — a new freeway, a declining neighborhood, an adjacent nuisance | **Generally incurable**, because the owner cannot change it |

**Curable** means the cost to cure is justified by the value it adds. **Incurable** means it is not — either because the fix costs more than it returns or because it cannot be fixed at all.

**External obsolescence is the one to recognize by its source.** If the cause is off the property, the owner cannot cure it, and the loss is real.

### 4.5 A worked cost approach

> **Subject:** 2,000 sq ft home, 12 years old, on a lot worth $340,000 as if vacant.
> Replacement cost new: $260 per square foot.
> Estimated total economic life: 60 years. Effective age: 12 years.
> Additional functional obsolescence for a single bathroom: $18,000.
> External obsolescence from a new arterial behind the lot: $25,000.

**Cost new:** 2,000 × $260 = **$520,000.**

**Physical deterioration, straight-line (age-life):** effective age ÷ total economic life = 12 ÷ 60 = **20%.**
$520,000 × 0.20 = **$104,000.**

**Total accrued depreciation:** $104,000 + $18,000 + $25,000 = **$147,000.**

**Indicated value:** $340,000 + $520,000 − $147,000 = **$713,000.**

Note that **land is not depreciated.** Depreciation applies to improvements only.

### 4.6 Effective age versus actual age

**Actual age** is years since construction. **Effective age** reflects condition and updating.

A well-maintained, updated 30-year-old house may have an effective age of 12. A neglected 12-year-old house may have an effective age of 25. **The age-life computation uses effective age**, which is why the same chronological age produces different depreciation.

### 4.7 Where the approach is strong and weak

**Strongest:** new or nearly new construction, where depreciation is minimal and the estimate is close to actual cost. Special-purpose properties with no comparable sales — a school, a church, a fire station. Insurance valuation, where the question is replacement rather than market.

**Weakest:** older improvements, where depreciation must be estimated and the estimate carries the whole answer. Properties where land value is itself hard to establish.

**The recurring misuse:** treating the cost approach as a floor. A property is not worth what it cost to build, and telling a seller "you have $900,000 in it, so it's worth $900,000" is the contribution error at building scale.

### 4.8 Checkpoint

You should be able to run the cost approach with all three depreciation types and explain why external obsolescence is generally incurable.

## 5. The income capitalization approach

### 5.1 What it does

The income approach estimates value from the income a property produces. It is the primary approach for investment property, because investors buy income streams.

### 5.2 Building the income statement

The sequence, and each line matters:

| Line | Computation |
|---|---|
| **Potential gross income (PGI)** | All income at full occupancy |
| **− Vacancy and collection loss** | An allowance, market-derived |
| **= Effective gross income (EGI)** | |
| **− Operating expenses** | Fixed, variable, and reserves for replacement |
| **= Net operating income (NOI)** | |

**Two exclusions students get wrong, and they are the examinable ones:**

> **Debt service is not an operating expense**, and **depreciation is not an operating expense.**

NOI measures the property's performance, independent of how any particular owner financed it or what their tax position is. Two buyers with different loans buy the same NOI.

**Reserves for replacement are included** — the annualized allowance for roofs, systems, and other items that will need replacing. Omitting them overstates NOI and therefore overstates value.

### 5.3 The IRV relationship

> **I = R × V** — Income equals Rate times Value
>
> **V = I ÷ R** — Value equals Income divided by Rate
> **R = I ÷ V** — Rate equals Income divided by Value

The **capitalization rate** expresses the relationship between a property's NOI and its value, and it is derived from the market — from sales of comparable income properties where both NOI and price are known.

### 5.4 Worked problems

> **A property produces NOI of $84,000. Comparable sales indicate a 6% capitalization rate.**
>
> V = 84,000 ÷ 0.06 = **$1,400,000.**

> **A property sold for $1,250,000 with NOI of $81,250. What rate does it indicate?**
>
> R = 81,250 ÷ 1,250,000 = **6.5%.**

> **Full income statement.** 12 units at $2,100 monthly. Vacancy and collection 5%. Operating expenses $118,000 including reserves. Market cap rate 5.75%.
>
> PGI = 12 × 2,100 × 12 = **$302,400.**
> Less vacancy: 302,400 × 0.05 = 15,120. **EGI = $287,280.**
> Less expenses: 287,280 − 118,000 = **NOI = $169,280.**
> V = 169,280 ÷ 0.0575 = **$2,943,999**, say **$2,944,000.**

### 5.5 The inverse relationship

> **Rate and value move in opposite directions for a given income.**

> The same $169,280 NOI at 5.0% = **$3,385,600.** At 7.0% = **$2,418,286.**

A two-point movement in the rate changes the indicated value by nearly a million dollars on identical income. **That is why the rate's derivation matters more than the arithmetic**, and why a licensee should never select a cap rate to reach a number.

A **higher cap rate** signals higher perceived risk or lower expected growth; a **lower rate** signals the opposite.

### 5.6 Gross rent and gross income multipliers

A simpler screening tool.

> **GRM = sale price ÷ gross monthly rent** (residential)
> **GIM = sale price ÷ gross annual income** (commercial)

> A property sold for $840,000 with gross monthly rent of $6,000. GRM = 840,000 ÷ 6,000 = **140.**
> Applying that to a subject renting for $6,400: 6,400 × 140 = **$896,000.**

**The limitation is the lesson.** A multiplier uses **gross** income and therefore ignores expenses entirely. Two properties with identical gross rents and very different expense structures produce identical multiplier indications and very different values. It is a screening tool, not a valuation.

### 5.7 Reconciling the three approaches

An appraiser applies the approaches that fit the property and **reconciles** them — weighting by reliability and relevance, not averaging.

| Property type | Typically weighted toward |
|---|---|
| Single-family residence | **Sales comparison** |
| New construction | Cost, supported by sales comparison |
| Apartment or commercial | **Income capitalization** |
| Special-purpose with no sales | Cost |

Reconciliation is judgment, stated with its reasoning — the same discipline as reconciling comparables within the sales comparison approach.

### 5.8 Checkpoint

You should be able to build an income statement excluding debt service and depreciation, solve IRV in all three directions, and explain what a multiplier ignores.

## 6. Appraisal independence and CMA limits

### 6.1 The boundary

| | Comparative market analysis | Appraisal |
|---|---|---|
| Prepared by | A real estate licensee | A licensed or certified appraiser |
| Purpose | To advise a client on pricing or offer strategy | An independent opinion of value for a defined use, commonly lending |
| Standards | Professional practice; not appraisal standards | Professional appraisal standards |
| Independence | Advises a client the licensee represents | **Independent of the parties** |
| May be called an appraisal | **No** | Yes |

**The difference is not the data used.** A licensee's analysis and an appraiser's report may rely on the same closed sales. The difference is **who prepares it, under what standards, with what independence, and for what purpose.**

### 6.2 What a licensee may and may not do

**May:** prepare a comparative market analysis; give an opinion of value to advise a client; explain how the opinion was formed; discuss what an appraisal is likely to consider.

**May not:** hold the analysis out as an appraisal; use it where an appraisal is required; represent that it satisfies a lender's valuation requirement; or perform an appraisal without the required license or certification.

### 6.3 Appraiser licensing, in outline

California licenses and certifies appraisers at levels tied to the complexity and value of property an appraiser may appraise, administered by the state's appraiser regulatory agency. Federally related transactions carry their own requirements.

A licensee's practical takeaway: **appraiser qualification is a real credential with levels**, and a valuation for a lending purpose requires an appraiser rather than a licensee, however experienced the licensee.

### 6.4 Independence

**A licensee may provide genuinely relevant information to an appraiser through the proper channel** — a list of sales the licensee believes relevant, information about condition and improvements, a copy of the contract.

**A licensee may not communicate a target.** "The deal dies without another $25,000" is exactly what independence rules exist to prevent, and a value that moves after that conversation is unreliable for the purpose the appraisal serves.

**The distinction is between supplying information and supplying a target** — and requests to "take another look" that arrive with a number attached are the pattern to refuse, **including when the request comes from your own client.**

### 6.5 When the appraisal disagrees

A lender's appraisal below the contract price is, first, a **financing event** rather than a verdict on the licensee's analysis. Lenders lend against the lower of appraised value and purchase price.

The options are practical rather than automatic: the buyer brings additional funds; the parties renegotiate; the parties negotiate another resolution; or the transaction is cancelled if a contingency permits and its procedure is followed.

**What does not happen automatically:** the price does not adjust to the appraised value by operation of any loan program, the loan is not denied, and no one is entitled to a second appraisal as of right.

**Where the appraisal contingency has been removed**, the practical options remain but the contractual rights are limited — and whether the buyer may cancel and what happens to his deposit is a **legal question** for counsel, not for the licensee.

### 6.6 Appraisal bias

Valuation is a judgment, and judgment is where bias operates. Concerns about appraisal bias — including in the treatment of neighborhoods and of the people occupying properties — are a recognized issue, and Lesson 14 takes up the fair housing framework.

Two points here. **Objective, market-derived adjustment support is the structural control**, because a derived adjustment can be examined and an impression cannot. And **a licensee who believes an appraisal reflects bias should escalate to the broker** and follow the reconsideration process available through the lender — not confront the appraiser and not advise the client on a claim.

### 6.7 What makes a licensee's opinion defensible

The test is **reconstructability as of the date given**: could someone reviewing this later see what data was used, what adjustments were made, what reasoning connected them, and when?

What the file should contain:

1. The comparables used, with data and sources, **as of the date of the analysis**.
2. The adjustment grid, with the direction and basis of each adjustment.
3. The reconciliation, including what was weighted and why.
4. Comparables **considered and rejected**, with the reason.
5. The market conditions evidence — months of supply, days on market, direction.
6. The recommendation, as a **range with a most-probable point**.
7. The date and the licensee who prepared it.
8. The written advice given, and the client's decision.

**What is not on that list:** that the property eventually sold near the estimate, which is luck rather than method; that the client signed an acknowledgment, which proves delivery rather than soundness; and that the brokerage's template was used, which proves formatting.

### 6.8 When the client chooses a different number

Price is the client's decision; the analysis and advice are the licensee's obligation. Where a seller lists above the supported range, the file needs the analysis, the written advice, the client's decision, and any agreed revisit strategy.

**What must not happen:** the client's reasoning being adopted as the brokerage's opinion of value, or the analysis being quietly revised upward to match the list price.

> **An analysis that follows the price rather than informing it is worse than no analysis.** It documents nothing and looks, later, like an opinion manufactured to fit.

### 6.10 A worked reconciliation

> **Three approaches, one property**: a forty-year-old single-family residence in an established neighborhood, owner-occupied, being valued for a listing decision.
>
> - **Sales comparison** indicates a range, with the three closest comparables clustering and one outlier requiring large gross adjustment.
> - **Cost approach** indicates a figure well above the comparison range.
> - **Income capitalization** indicates a figure well below it.
>
> **The reconciliation is not an average**, and Section 3's reconciliation discussion makes the point. It is a judgment about **which approach the market for this property actually uses.**
>
> **Why sales comparison dominates here.** Buyers of owner-occupied single-family homes decide by comparison to other homes. **The approach mirrors the actual decision process of the actual buyers**, which is the criterion.
>
> **Why the cost approach comes in high, and why that is expected.** Section 4's method estimates replacement cost less depreciation plus land. **Depreciation on a forty-year-old improvement is the hardest element to estimate**, and it is systematically understated — physical wear is visible, functional obsolescence is easy to miss, and external obsolescence is frequently ignored entirely. **A high cost indication on an older property usually means the depreciation estimate is light**, not that the property is worth more than comparable sales say.
>
> **Why the income approach comes in low, and why it is nearly irrelevant here.** Section 5's method converts income to value. **Owner-occupied homes in most markets sell for more than their rent supports**, because the buyers are not buying income. Applying the approach produces an accurate answer to a question no participant in this market is asking.
>
> **The outlier comparable.** Section 3's adjustment discussion governs: a comparable requiring large gross adjustment is a weak comparable regardless of where its adjusted figure lands. **It is given little weight, and the reasoning is stated rather than the comparable being quietly dropped.**
>
> **The reconciliation, written.** Primary weight to sales comparison, supported by the three clustering comparables; the cost approach as a check with its depreciation limitation stated; the income approach reported and given minimal weight with the reason. **A range, with a most-probable figure inside it, and the reasoning for each on the page.**
>
> **Why this matters for a licensee who will never appraise.** Section 6.7's requirement is that a licensee's own opinion be defensible, and defensibility is exactly this: **the reasoning is visible, the weight given to each indication is explained, and a reader who disagrees can see where.** An opinion that states a number and shows nothing is not defensible even when it is right — **and Section 6.1's boundary means the licensee's opinion is a comparative market analysis, never an appraisal**, however carefully it was constructed.

### 6.11 Checkpoint

You should be able to state the appraisal-CMA boundary, distinguish supplying information from supplying a target, and list what makes an opinion reconstructable.

| **Reconciliation** | Weighting indications by reliability to reach a range and a most-probable point. **Not averaging.** |
| **Paired sales analysis** | Deriving an adjustment by comparing sales differing in one characteristic. The preferred source. |
| **Active listings** | Evidence of asking prices and competition — **never of value**. |
| **Reproduction versus replacement cost** | Cost of an exact replica including obsolete features; cost of equivalent utility using current methods. **Replacement is generally the more useful measure.** |
| **Physical deterioration** | Loss from wear, age, and deferred maintenance. |
| **Functional obsolescence** | Loss from something about the **improvement itself** — layout, one bathroom, an outdated system, an over-improvement. |
| **External obsolescence** | Loss from something **outside the property**. **Generally incurable**, because the owner cannot change it. |
| **Curable / incurable** | Whether the cost to cure is justified by the value it adds. |
| **Effective age** | Age as reflected by condition and updating. **The age-life computation uses effective age**, not actual age. |
| **Land is not depreciated** | Depreciation applies to improvements only. |
| **Potential gross income** | All income at full occupancy. |
| **Effective gross income** | PGI less vacancy and collection loss. |
| **Net operating income** | EGI less operating expenses **including reserves for replacement** — and **excluding debt service and depreciation**. |
| **IRV** | I = R × V; V = I ÷ R; R = I ÷ V. |
| **Capitalization rate** | The relationship between NOI and value, **derived from the market**. Rate and value move in opposite directions for a given income. |
| **Gross rent multiplier** | Price ÷ gross monthly rent. A screening tool that **ignores expenses entirely**. |
| **Comparative market analysis** | A licensee's opinion of value to advise a client. **Not an appraisal and may never be called one.** |
| **Appraiser independence** | A licensee may supply **information** to an appraiser and never a **target** — including at a client's request. |
| **Reconstructability** | The test of a defensible opinion: could a reviewer later see the data, the adjustments, the reasoning, and the date. |

## Practice problems

### Problem 1

A seller says the house "cost $900,000 to build four years ago plus $150,000 for the recording studio
in the garage, so it's worth at least $1,050,000." Comparable closed sales support $760,000.

**Explain the analysis without giving offense.**

> **Solution.** **Value, price, and cost are three different things.** Value is an **opinion** formed as
> of a date; price and cost are historical **facts**. A property can cost $900,000 to build, be worth
> $700,000, and sell for $760,000 — all at once, without anything being wrong.
>
> **Contribution is the concept for the studio.** Money spent on a property does not become value in
> the property; the market decides what an improvement contributes. Here the pool of buyers who want a
> recording studio — **and would pay for one** — is small, and the studio also consumed the garage,
> which most buyers in a neighborhood of family homes do want. That is **over-improvement relative to
> the neighborhood**, a recognized phenomenon rather than a device for arguing him down.
>
> **Say the complement honestly in the same conversation:** the relationship runs both ways, and some
> modest improvements contribute more than they cost because they remove a specific buyer objection.
>
> **And the cost approach is not a floor.** "You have $1,050,000 in it, so it's worth that" is the
> contribution error at building scale.

### Problem 2

A parcel in a corridor transitioning to commercial holds a 60-year-old single-family house. Zoning now
permits neighborhood commercial; the recorded CC&Rs from the original 1958 tract restrict the parcel
to residential use. A licensee tells the owner the highest and best use is a retail building.

**Apply the tests and assess the advice.**

> **Solution.** **The four tests run in order, and the analysis stops at the first.**
>
> **Legally permissible** has two halves: zoning **and** recorded private restrictions, with the more
> restrictive governing. Zoning permits commercial; the CC&Rs prohibit it. Unless the restrictions can
> be addressed, retail is **not legally permissible** — and the analysis never reaches physically
> possible, financially feasible, or maximally productive, however profitable retail would be.
>
> **The advice is also outside the license.** Telling an owner the highest and best use is a retail
> building makes a legal, physical, and financial determination in one sentence. Highest and best use
> for a development site is appraisal and entitlement work.
>
> **What is true and worth saying:** the house may be an **interim use** — economically reasonable now
> and expected to change — and if the land as if vacant is worth more than the improved property, the
> improvement has **negative contribution** and its removal cost is a deduction. Both are reasons to
> get an appraiser and counsel involved rather than to price the property as a house by default.

### Problem 3

Adjust each toward a subject of 1,800 sq ft, 3 bed, 2 bath, updated kitchen: (a) a comparable of
1,980 sq ft with 4 bedrooms; (b) a comparable with an original kitchen and a one-car garage; (c) a
comparable that closed five months ago in a rising market.

**State the direction and the reason for each.**

> **Solution.** Every adjustment answers one question: *what would this comparable have sold for if it
> had been like the subject?* **Adjustments are made to the comparable, never the subject.**
>
> (a) **Downward, twice.** Larger living area and an extra bedroom are both **superior** — comparable
> better, subtract.
>
> (b) **Upward, twice.** An original kitchen and a one-car garage are both **inferior** — comparable
> worse, add.
>
> (c) **Upward.** The market has risen since it closed, so it would sell for more today. Note this is a
> **market conditions** adjustment, driven by whether the market changed rather than by the calendar —
> a five-month-old sale in a flat market may need none.
>
> **CBS: Comparable Better, Subtract.**

### Problem 4

Three comparables produce adjusted values of $844,000 (net adjustment $1,000), $874,000 (net $28,000),
and $861,000 (net $51,000 gross, and the most recent sale). A licensee averages them to $859,667 and
presents it as the value. He also cites three active listings at $1.3 million that have sat 94, 112,
and 130 days.

**Correct both.**

> **Solution.** **Averaging treats the $51,000-adjustment comparable as exactly as reliable as the
> $1,000 one** — it is not, and the average silently discards that information.
>
> **Reconciliation weights by reliability:** adjustment size, **gross as well as net** since a small net
> can conceal a large gross; then recency; then similarity in what drives this market.
>
> A defensible conclusion: **a supported range of roughly $845,000 to $870,000, most probable near
> $850,000 to $855,000, weighted toward the least-adjusted and most similar indicator.** A range, a
> point, and a stated reason — not a single number presented as fact.
>
> **The active listings prove asking prices, not value.** A seller can ask anything. What these three
> additionally prove is that **the market has declined to pay $1.3 million three separate times**, over
> three months each. Citing them as support inverts what they show.

### Problem 5

Compute the cost approach: 2,000 sq ft home, effective age 12, total economic life 60, land worth
$340,000 as if vacant, replacement cost new $260 per sq ft, $18,000 of functional obsolescence for a
single bathroom, and $25,000 of external obsolescence from a new arterial behind the lot. The owner
asks whether he can cure the arterial problem by landscaping.

**Answer both.**

> **Solution.** **Cost new:** 2,000 × $260 = **$520,000.**
> **Physical deterioration (age-life):** 12 ÷ 60 = **20%** × $520,000 = **$104,000.**
> **Total accrued depreciation:** 104,000 + 18,000 + 25,000 = **$147,000.**
> **Indicated value:** $340,000 + $520,000 − $147,000 = **$713,000.**
>
> Note that **land is not depreciated** — depreciation applies to improvements only. And the age-life
> computation uses **effective age**, not actual age: a well-maintained updated 30-year-old house may
> have an effective age of 12, while a neglected 12-year-old house may have an effective age of 25.
>
> **On the arterial: no.** **External obsolescence arises outside the property**, which is exactly why
> it is **generally incurable** — the owner cannot change it. Landscaping may soften the experience and
> will not remove the arterial. The single bathroom, by contrast, is **functional obsolescence**
> arising from the improvement itself and may be curable if the cost to cure is justified by the value
> it adds.

### Problem 6

A 12-unit building rents at $2,100 per unit monthly. Vacancy and collection loss 5%. Operating
expenses $118,000 including reserves. The market cap rate is 5.75%. The owner's accountant produced a
"net income" of $141,000 by deducting debt service of $96,000 and adding back $23,000 of depreciation
from the tax return.

**Compute the value correctly and explain the accountant's error.**

> **Solution.** **PGI** = 12 × 2,100 × 12 = **$302,400.**
> Less vacancy 5% = 15,120. **EGI = $287,280.**
> Less operating expenses 118,000. **NOI = $169,280.**
> **V = 169,280 ÷ 0.0575 = $2,944,000** (rounded).
>
> **The accountant mixed two different measures.** **Debt service is not an operating expense**, and
> **depreciation is not an operating expense.** NOI measures **the property's** performance,
> independent of how any particular owner financed it or what their tax position is — two buyers with
> different loans buy the same NOI.
>
> **Reserves for replacement, by contrast, belong in.** Omitting them overstates NOI and therefore
> overstates value.
>
> Using his $141,000 figure at the same rate would indicate $2,452,174 — nearly half a million dollars
> off, from a definitional error rather than an arithmetic one.

### Problem 7

The same $169,280 NOI is capitalized by three parties at 5.0%, 5.75%, and 7.0%. Separately, a licensee
values a fourplex by applying a GRM of 140 derived from a nearby sale, without examining either
property's expenses.

**Show the value spread and state what the multiplier ignores.**

> **Solution.** **At 5.0%:** 169,280 ÷ 0.05 = **$3,385,600.**
> **At 5.75%:** **$2,944,000.**
> **At 7.0%:** 169,280 ÷ 0.07 = **$2,418,286.**
>
> **Nearly a million dollars of spread on identical income.** Rate and value move in **opposite
> directions**, and that sensitivity is why **the rate's derivation matters more than the arithmetic**
> — and why a licensee must never select a cap rate to reach a number. A higher rate signals higher
> perceived risk or lower expected growth; a lower rate the opposite.
>
> **The GRM ignores expenses entirely**, because it uses **gross** income. Two properties with identical
> gross rents and very different expense structures — one with all utilities paid by the owner, one
> without — produce identical multiplier indications and materially different values.
>
> **It is a screening tool, not a valuation.**

### Problem 8

A listing agent's analysis supports $845,000 to $870,000. The seller lists at $1,020,000. Two weeks
later the agent revises her written analysis upward to match the list price, tells a buyer's agent the
price "reflects our brokerage's opinion of value," and emails the lender's appraiser three sales with
a note that "the deal dies without another $25,000."

**Assess each act.**

> **Solution.** **Listing at the seller's number was correct.** Price is the client's decision; the
> analysis and advice are the licensee's obligation. She discharged both.
>
> **The revision is the failure, and it is worse than having no analysis.** An analysis that follows the
> price rather than informing it documents nothing and looks, later, like an opinion manufactured to
> fit. What the file needed instead: the comparables with sources **as of the date**, the adjustment
> grid with direction and basis, the reconciliation and its weighting, **comparables rejected and why**,
> the market conditions evidence, the range with a most-probable point, the date, the written advice,
> and the seller's decision.
>
> **Representing the list price as the brokerage's opinion of value adopts the seller's reasoning as the
> brokerage's** — the one thing the file must not do.
>
> **The email mixes the permitted with the prohibited.** Sending relevant sales through the proper
> channel is **information** and is fine. "The deal dies without another $25,000" is a **target**, and a
> value that moves after that conversation is worthless for the purpose the appraisal exists to serve —
> including when the request serves her own client.

## Chapter summary

**Value is an opinion; price and cost are facts**, and market value's conditions — typical motivation,
informed parties, reasonable exposure, cash-equivalent payment — each exclude something.

**DUST names the elements**, and demand without purchasing power is not demand.

**Substitution is the engine of all three approaches**, and **contribution is not cost**.

**Highest and best use is tested in order** — legally permissible, physically possible, financially
feasible, maximally productive — and "legally permissible" means zoning **and** private restrictions.
Where land as if vacant exceeds improved value, the improvement subtracts.

**Adjust the comparable, never the subject.** Comparable better, subtract.

**Reconcile, do not average.** Weight by adjustment size — gross as well as net — recency, and
similarity, and state a range with a most-probable point and a reason.

**Closed sales prove price; pending sales show direction; active listings never establish value.**

**The cost approach is land plus cost new less accrued depreciation**, with land never depreciated and
**effective age** driving the age-life computation. Physical, functional, and external — and external
is generally incurable because its cause is off the property.

**NOI excludes debt service and depreciation and includes reserves.** Two buyers with different loans
buy the same NOI.

**IRV in all three directions**, with rate and value moving in opposite directions — which is why the
rate's derivation matters more than the arithmetic.

**Multipliers ignore expenses** and are a screening tool.

**A CMA is not an appraisal and may never be called one.** The difference is not the data but the
preparer, standards, independence, and purpose.

**Supply an appraiser information, never a target** — including at your own client's request. A low
appraisal is a financing event, and nothing about the resolution is automatic.

**Defensibility is reconstructability**, and an analysis quietly revised to match a list price is worse
than no analysis at all.
