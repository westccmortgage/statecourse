## 1. Taxation, eminent domain, police power, and escheat

### 1.1 Fee simple absolute is not absolute against government

Lesson 03 established that fee simple absolute is the greatest estate the law recognizes. It is nonetheless subject to four government powers, conventionally remembered as **PETE**:

| Power | What it does | Compensation |
|---|---|---|
| **P** — Police power | Regulates use for public health, safety, morals, and general welfare | **None** |
| **E** — Eminent domain | Takes property for public use | **Just compensation required** |
| **T** — Taxation | Levies on property to fund government | None; it is a charge, not a taking |
| **E** — Escheat | Property passes to the state where an owner dies with no heirs and no will | None |

**The compensation column is the distinction that matters.** Regulation under the police power reduces what an owner may do without paying him; eminent domain takes and pays.

### 1.2 Police power

The broadest of the four, and the source of zoning, building codes, subdivision regulation, environmental controls, health and safety requirements, and rent regulation where a jurisdiction has adopted it.

The power is exercised by state and local government. Its exercise must serve a legitimate public purpose and must not be arbitrary — but within those limits it can substantially reduce what an owner may do, and the owner is not compensated.

> A parcel zoned for commercial use is rezoned residential, and its market value falls by half. **That is regulation, not a taking**, in the ordinary case. The owner has not lost the property; he has lost uses.

Where regulation goes so far as to deprive an owner of substantially all economically beneficial use, the question of a **regulatory taking** arises. Whether a particular regulation crosses that line is a legal question of real complexity, and a licensee's role is to recognize the concept, never to advise that a client has a claim.

### 1.3 Eminent domain

The power to take private property for public use upon payment of just compensation. The process is called **condemnation**.

Two situations a licensee should be able to name:

**Partial taking.** Where only part of a parcel is taken, compensation may address both the part taken and **severance damages** to what remains — for example, where the remainder is left an unusable shape or loses its access.

**Inverse condemnation.** Where government action effectively takes or damages property without a formal proceeding, and the owner brings the claim rather than the government.

**A pending or threatened condemnation is a material fact.** It affects value, marketability, and the buyer's plans, and it is disclosed. Whether the owner has a claim, what compensation is due, and how to respond are legal questions for counsel.

### 1.4 Taxation

Property taxation is taken up in Lesson 13. For present purposes:

- Property taxes become a **lien on the property**, generally with priority over other liens regardless of when they attached.
- **Special assessments** fund improvements benefiting particular property, and a buyer inherits the remaining obligation.
- Unpaid property taxes can ultimately lead to sale of the property under the applicable process.

### 1.5 Escheat

Where a person dies owning property with no will and no heirs who take under the law of intestate succession, the property passes to the state. It is uncommon in residential practice and appears mainly as a concept and occasionally in a chain of title.

### 1.6 What a licensee actually does with this section

Three things:

1. **Recognize which power is operating.** A rezoning is police power; a road widening that takes twelve feet of frontage is eminent domain.
2. **Treat pending government action as a material fact** — a proposed rezoning, an announced project, a pending condemnation.
3. **Refer the legal questions.** Whether compensation is owed, whether a regulation has gone too far, and what a taking is worth are all outside the license.

### 1.7 Checkpoint

You should be able to name the four powers, state which requires compensation, and distinguish regulation from a taking on a set of facts.

## 2. Zoning and general plans

### 2.1 The planning hierarchy

California land use is organized in layers, and each answers a different question.

| Layer | Question it answers |
|---|---|
| **General plan** | What does this jurisdiction intend this area to become? |
| **Specific plan** | How will that intent be implemented in this particular area? |
| **Zoning ordinance** | What uses and development standards apply to this parcel **now**? |
| **Entitlements and permits** | What has this particular parcel actually been approved for? |

**The general plan is the long-range policy document**, and California requires local general plans to address specified elements — land use, circulation, housing, conservation, open space, noise, and safety among them. **Zoning is generally required to be consistent with the general plan.**

For a licensee the operative point is that the general plan describes intent while zoning controls today, and a buyer asking "will this area change?" is asking a general plan question, answered by the jurisdiction rather than by the licensee.

### 2.2 What a zoning ordinance controls

**Use** — what may occur on the parcel: residential, commercial, industrial, agricultural, mixed, and the subcategories within each.

**Development standards** — the physical envelope: setbacks from each property line, maximum height, lot coverage, floor area ratio, minimum lot size, parking requirements, and open space.

Both halves matter. A parcel zoned to permit a second unit may still be unable to accommodate one within the setbacks, the height limit, and the parking requirement. **Permitted use and physical feasibility are different questions**, and clients routinely conflate them.

### 2.3 The relief mechanisms

Where a proposal does not fit the ordinance, three mechanisms are commonly available, and they are frequently confused.

| Mechanism | What it addresses |
|---|---|
| **Variance** | Relief from a **development standard** — a setback, a height limit — usually requiring a hardship arising from the property itself |
| **Conditional use permit** | Permission for a **use** the ordinance allows in that zone subject to review and conditions |
| **Rezoning** | A change to the ordinance itself, moving the parcel to a different zone |

> **The distinction to hold:** a variance addresses the envelope; a conditional use permit addresses the use; a rezoning changes the rule.

All three are **discretionary**. Nobody is entitled to one, and a licensee who tells a buyer a variance "won't be a problem" has predicted a discretionary decision — which Lesson 13 of the Practice course identifies as a prediction stated as certainty.

### 2.4 Nonconforming use

A **legal nonconforming use** is one that was lawful when established and no longer conforms because the ordinance changed. It is commonly permitted to continue — often described as being "grandfathered."

Three limits that surprise owners:

- **Expansion is generally restricted.** The right is usually to continue, not to enlarge.
- **Discontinuance can end it.** Many ordinances terminate the right after a period of abandonment.
- **Destruction can end it.** Rebuilding after substantial destruction may not be permitted.

**And a use that was never lawful is not a nonconforming use.** It is an unpermitted use, which is a different and worse problem — and "it's been like that for thirty years" does not convert one into the other.

### 2.5 Recorded restrictions again

From Lesson 05, restated because it belongs here too: **zoning and recorded private restrictions are independent, and the more restrictive generally governs in practice.** Zoning permitting a use does not authorize it where the CC&Rs prohibit it. A buyer must satisfy both.

### 2.6 What a licensee may say

**May:** identify the current zoning designation with its source; explain that zoning governs use and development standards; explain that variances, conditional use permits, and rezonings exist and are discretionary; direct the buyer to the planning counter; and confirm that the buyer investigated before his contingency expired.

**May not:** state that a use is permitted; predict that an application will be approved; interpret setback or height provisions for the buyer's project; or characterize an existing condition as legally nonconforming.

> **A buyer asks: "Can I build a second unit here?"**
>
> **Correct:** "That's two questions — what the zoning allows and whether the lot can physically fit it within setbacks, height, and parking, plus whatever the recorded restrictions say. The city's planning counter answers the first two, and I'll get you the CC&Rs for the third. Let's get both before your contingency expires, and I'll note we discussed it."
>
> **Incorrect:** "The zoning allows it, so you're fine."

### 2.7 Checkpoint

You should be able to distinguish a variance from a conditional use permit, explain the limits on a nonconforming use, and answer a buildability question correctly.

## 3. Subdivision and building controls

### 3.1 The two subdivision statutes, revisited

From Lesson 04, because it is the land use half that matters here:

**The Subdivision Map Act** governs the **creation** of parcels — the tentative and final map process, the conditions a local agency may impose, dedications, and required improvements. It answers whether a parcel may lawfully be created and sold.

**The Subdivided Lands Law** is the DRE-administered consumer protection statute governing the **sale** of subdivided interests, including the public report requirement before certain subdivided property may be offered.

The recurring practice point: **a separate tax bill is not evidence of a lawful split.** Where a parcel's creation is in question, it goes to the local agency, to title, and to counsel — before it is marketed as a separate building site, because marketing it as one is a factual claim about entitlement.

### 3.2 Building codes and permits

Building codes set minimum standards for construction, and work generally requires a **permit** and **inspections** culminating in a final approval.

What a permit record establishes and does not:

| Establishes | Does not establish |
|---|---|
| That a permit was issued for described work | That the work was completed |
| That inspections were performed, where finaled | That the work was done well |
| The scope as permitted | That subsequent work was permitted |
| A date | That current condition matches the permit |

**A permit that was issued but never finaled is a real problem** — an open permit can complicate a sale, and the obligation to close it out generally follows the property.

### 3.3 Unpermitted work

This is among the most common problems in California residential transactions, and licensees mishandle it in both directions.

**The facts to establish:** what work was done, when, whether a permit was issued, whether it was finaled, and whether the work is habitable and safe as built.

**What the licensee does:**

1. **Disclose what is known**, including what the seller said and what the licensee observed.
2. **Recommend permit research** at the jurisdiction — which is a public record and is where the answer actually lives.
3. **Do not include unpermitted area in advertised square footage** without disclosing its status.
4. **Recommend the buyer investigate what legalization would require**, since the cost can be substantial and sometimes the work cannot be legalized at all.
5. **Document the recommendation and the buyer's response.**

**What the licensee does not do:** state that the work is legal because it looks fine, characterize the risk as low, predict what the city would require, or advise the seller to say nothing because "everyone does it."

> **The asymmetry worth teaching:** unpermitted work does not automatically make a property unsaleable, and it does automatically make it a disclosure item. Licensees fail on both sides — some conceal it, some describe it as a disaster. The professional response is to make it visible and investigable.

### 3.4 Certificates and clearances

Depending on the jurisdiction and the property type, a transfer may trigger local requirements — retrofit obligations for smoke and carbon monoxide devices and water conservation fixtures, occupancy or resale inspections, sewer lateral requirements, or point-of-sale reports.

**These are local and they vary.** A licensee working across city lines who assumes uniformity will eventually miss one, and a missed point-of-sale requirement can delay a closing.

### 3.5 Accessibility in new construction

Certain multifamily housing is subject to accessible design and construction requirements, and California standards can exceed the federal baseline. A licensee marketing new construction should not represent that a property meets accessibility requirements without confirming it, and should direct specific questions to the developer, the architect, or the jurisdiction.

### 3.6 Checkpoint

You should be able to state what a permit record establishes, handle unpermitted work correctly, and explain why local transfer requirements need to be checked jurisdiction by jurisdiction.

## 4. Environmental hazards and disclosure

### 4.1 The licensee's role, stated once for all of them

For every hazard in this section the role is the same three steps:

1. **Disclose what is known**, including the licensee's own observations.
2. **Recommend appropriate investigation** by a qualified professional, matched to the property.
3. **Do not evaluate.**

A licensee who tells a buyer that visible growth "is just surface mold, it'll clean off" has made an assessment they are not qualified to make, on which the buyer may rely.

### 4.2 Age-triggered: lead-based paint

For target housing built **before 1978**, the federal lead-based paint requirements apply. They are triggered by the **age of the housing**, not by whether children will occupy it, and licensees routinely misstate them in both directions.

| The obligation is | The obligation is not |
|---|---|
| Disclose **known** lead-based paint and hazards | Test the property |
| Provide **available records and reports** | Create records that do not exist |
| Provide the required **pamphlet** | Certify the property is free of lead |
| Give the buyer an **opportunity to conduct an assessment** | Remediate before transfer |

### 4.3 The other common hazards

**Asbestos.** Present in older construction in insulation, flooring, roofing, and other materials. Generally of most concern when disturbed. Evaluation and abatement are specialist work.

**Radon.** A naturally occurring soil gas that can accumulate indoors. Testing is inexpensive relative to the transaction and is the only way to know.

**Mold.** Requires moisture, which means the underlying question is usually where the water is coming from. A licensee should disclose visible growth and moisture evidence as **observations** and recommend evaluation — never characterize the type or the risk.

**Methamphetamine contamination.** Where a property was used for manufacture, remediation obligations and notice requirements can apply. A licensee learning of a history routes it immediately.

**Underground storage tanks.** Common on older and rural property. Leaking tanks create soil and groundwater contamination questions and potentially substantial liability.

**Soil and groundwater contamination.** Arising from prior industrial or agricultural use, adjacent operations, or historic fill. On any property with an industrial or agricultural history, the question belongs at intake.

**Formaldehyde, PCBs, and other building-material issues**, which appear in particular construction eras and product types.

### 4.4 Proximity and off-site conditions

Some conditions are not on the property at all: a neighboring industrial use, an agricultural operation with spraying or odor, a rail corridor, a flight path, a former landfill, a high-voltage corridor.

These raise the same discipline. **Disclose what is known, point the buyer to objective sources, and do not characterize.** Whether an odor from an adjacent dairy is tolerable is the buyer's judgment to make with accurate information — not the licensee's to make for him.

### 4.5 The environmental site assessment, in outline

For commercial and some other transactions, environmental due diligence is conducted through a phased assessment: a **Phase I** records and site reconnaissance review to identify recognized environmental conditions, followed where warranted by a **Phase II** involving sampling.

A licensee should know the terms, know that these are specialist reports, and know that they take time — which puts them at the front of the due diligence queue rather than the back.

### 4.6 Checkpoint

You should be able to state the lead-based paint obligations correctly in both directions, and give the correct response to a buyer asking whether visible mold is serious.

## 5. Water conservation and natural-hazard context

### 5.1 Natural hazard disclosure

California requires disclosure of whether a property lies within certain mapped hazard zones — flood, fire, seismic, and related designations. The disclosure is commonly prepared by a third-party report provider.

What it does and does not do:

- **Does:** tell the buyer the property is within a mapped zone, so he can investigate what that means for insurance, construction requirements, and risk.
- **Does not:** certify the property is safe, predict whether an event will occur, or substitute for the buyer's own investigation.

**It applies based on mapping, not on the property's event history.** A property that has never flooded in the seller's twenty years of ownership is disclosed as within a flood zone if it is mapped in one, and a buyer's view that the mapping is outdated changes nothing about the obligation.

### 5.2 Why it matters more than buyers assume

Hazard zone status drives consequences buyers regularly discover late:

**Insurance availability and cost.** In some California areas this is now the determining factor in whether a transaction closes at all.

**Construction and improvement requirements**, which can materially affect renovation plans.

**Financing**, where lenders require particular coverage.

**Disclosure obligations the buyer inherits** as a future seller.

> **The single most preventable failure in this area:** insurance quotes obtained at the end of the contingency period rather than at the beginning. A buyer who learns in week one that coverage will cost far more than assumed can act. A buyer who learns it two days before closing generally cannot.

### 5.3 Seismic context

California's seismic mapping includes zones related to fault rupture and to seismic hazards such as liquefaction and landslide. Older residential construction may present retrofit questions — foundation bolting, cripple wall bracing, chimney condition.

The licensee's role is the familiar one: disclose what is known and what is mapped, recommend evaluation by a qualified professional, and route the engineering questions.

### 5.4 Fire hazard severity

Mapped fire hazard severity zones carry consequences including defensible space obligations, construction and material requirements in some areas, and — most acutely in current California practice — insurance availability.

A licensee listing or selling in a mapped fire area should treat insurance as a **first-week item**, not an administrative closing task.

### 5.5 Water: supply, rights, and conservation

Three distinct topics that clients merge.

**Supply.** Where does the water come from — municipal, district, mutual water company, private well, shared well? For rural property this is the question that determines whether the property is usable as intended.

**Rights.** Riparian, appropriative, and groundwater rights are the specialist area from Lesson 02. Recognize, ask, refer.

**Conservation requirements.** California transactions increasingly involve water conservation obligations, including fixture requirements that can be triggered at transfer, and local restrictions on landscaping and use.

**Groundwater management** adds a layer on rural and agricultural property: a parcel within a managed basin may face pumping constraints that affect what can be grown or built.

### 5.6 The rural intake questions

For rural, agricultural, or unimproved property, at the listing appointment:

1. What is the water source, and is it documented?
2. If a well: production, quality, and date of the last test?
3. Is any water right appurtenant to the parcel, and is it documented?
4. Is the property within a groundwater management area?
5. Are there shared-facility agreements — a shared well, a pipeline, an access easement?
6. Is the supply sufficient for the buyer's intended use?
7. What is the septic situation, and when was it last inspected?
8. Is access legal and documented, or is it by long-standing custom?

That last question closes the loop with Lesson 05: a rural parcel reached by a road nobody has a recorded right to use has an access problem the buyer will discover after closing.

### 5.7 Checkpoint

You should be able to explain why hazard disclosure applies on mapping rather than history, and list the rural intake questions.

## 6. Public and private land-use due diligence

### 6.1 The two halves

Every land use question has a public half and a private half, and satisfying one does not satisfy the other.

| Public | Private |
|---|---|
| General plan and zoning | Recorded CC&Rs and restrictions |
| Permits and building code | Association rules and architectural approval |
| Subdivision and parcel legality | Easements burdening the parcel |
| Environmental and hazard requirements | Private agreements — shared wells, access, maintenance |
| Local transfer requirements | Leases and occupancy agreements |

**A buyer must clear both columns.** The most common failure is checking the public half, receiving a "yes," and treating the question as answered.

### 6.2 The sequence

Order the work by **how long it takes**, not by how important it seems. The slow items are the ones that fail transactions.

**Week one:**
- Order the preliminary report and the underlying documents.
- Order association documents where applicable.
- Obtain insurance quotes where the property is in a mapped hazard area.
- Begin permit research at the jurisdiction.
- Order the natural hazard report.

**Week two:**
- General and specialist inspections, matched to the property.
- Zoning and entitlement questions at the planning counter.
- Survey where a boundary trigger is present.
- Environmental assessment where the property's history calls for one.

**Ongoing:**
- Track every item against the contingency deadline in writing.
- Confirm receipt of each report and the buyer's review.
- Escalate anything that will not arrive in time, while there is still time to act.

### 6.3 The questions that identify the work

A short intake interrogation identifies most of what a particular property needs:

- **What does the buyer intend to do with it** beyond live in it?
- **What is the property's history** — industrial, agricultural, prior uses, prior construction?
- **What is visibly present** — additions, conversions, outbuildings, slopes, retaining walls, wells, tanks, solar?
- **What is adjacent** — commercial, agricultural, rail, flight path, corridor?
- **What is mapped** — fire, flood, seismic?
- **What is recorded** — easements, restrictions, association?

Notice that the buyer's **intent** is the first question. A buyer who plans to build, subdivide, run a business, or keep animals needs due diligence a buyer who plans to move in does not.

### 6.4 What defensible due diligence looks like in the file

- The buyer's stated intentions, recorded at intake.
- Each investigation recommended, with the date.
- Each report ordered, with the date ordered and the date received.
- The buyer's confirmation that he received and reviewed each.
- Recommendations to consult the jurisdiction, a specialist, or counsel — with the buyer's response.
- Anything that could not be obtained in time, and what was done about it.

**The last item is the one licensees omit.** A report that never arrived, with no record of the escalation, looks in hindsight exactly like a report nobody ordered.

### 6.5 The recurring failure

Not that a licensee gave wrong land use advice. That a licensee **gave land use advice at all** — and the confident answer arrived faster than the correct referral would have.

The questions in this lesson are answered by the planning counter, the building department, the recorded documents, the specialist, and counsel. The licensee's professional contribution is knowing which question goes where, and getting it there while the buyer can still act on the answer.

### 6.6 Checkpoint

You should be able to build a due diligence sequence for a specific property, justify the ordering by lead time, and state what the file must show.

## Practice problems

### Problem 1

A city rezones a commercially zoned parcel to residential and the owner's market value falls by half.
Separately, the city takes twelve feet of frontage from an adjacent parcel for a road widening,
leaving the remainder without its former driveway access. A licensee tells the first owner he has "a
taking claim worth half his value."

**Identify the power in each and assess the advice.**

> **Solution.** **The rezoning is police power** — regulation of use for public health, safety, and
> general welfare, and **no compensation is owed** in the ordinary case. The owner has not lost the
> property; he has lost uses. Regulation reducing value substantially is still regulation.
>
> **The road widening is eminent domain**, exercised through condemnation, requiring **just
> compensation.** Because only part was taken and the remainder lost its access, compensation may
> address both the part taken and **severance damages** to what remains.
>
> **The advice is wrong and outside the license.** Whether regulation goes so far as to deprive an
> owner of substantially all economically beneficial use — a **regulatory taking** — is a legal question
> of real complexity, and telling a client he has a claim worth a specific amount is both a legal
> conclusion and a valuation of it.
>
> **What the licensee actually does:** recognize which power is operating, treat pending government
> action as a **material fact** to disclose, and refer the legal questions.

### Problem 2

A buyer asks whether he can build a second unit. Zoning permits second units in the district. The lot
is 40 feet wide with 5-foot side setbacks, a 25-foot height limit, and a two-space parking
requirement. The CC&Rs prohibit more than one dwelling per lot. His agent says "the zoning allows it,
so you're fine."

**Identify everything the answer missed.**

> **Solution.** **Three separate questions, and the agent answered one of them.**
>
> **Permitted use is not physical feasibility.** Zoning controls both use and the **development
> standards** — setbacks, height, coverage, parking, open space. A 40-foot lot with 5-foot side
> setbacks leaves 30 feet of buildable width, and the parking requirement has to land somewhere. The
> zoning may permit a unit the lot cannot hold.
>
> **The CC&Rs prohibit it**, and private restrictions and zoning are independent. **The more restrictive
> generally governs** — zoning permission does not authorize what the recorded restrictions forbid, and
> the buyer must satisfy both.
>
> **The correct answer:** *"That's two questions on the public side — what the zoning allows and whether
> the lot can physically fit it within setbacks, height, and parking — plus whatever the recorded
> restrictions say. The city's planning counter answers the first two; I'll get you the CC&Rs for the
> third. Let's have both before your contingency expires, and I'll note we discussed it."*

### Problem 3

Three owners want to do things the ordinance does not currently allow: one wants to build within the
required rear setback because of an unusual lot shape; one wants to operate a childcare facility in a
residential zone where the ordinance lists it as subject to review; one wants his industrial parcel
treated as commercial. A licensee tells each that the approval "won't be a problem."

**Name the correct mechanism for each and assess the reassurance.**

> **Solution.** **A variance** for the setback — relief from a **development standard**, usually
> requiring a hardship arising from the property itself, which an unusual lot shape may supply.
>
> **A conditional use permit** for the childcare facility — permission for a **use** the ordinance
> already allows in that zone subject to review and conditions.
>
> **A rezoning** for the industrial parcel — a change to the ordinance itself, moving the parcel to a
> different zone.
>
> **The distinction to hold:** a variance addresses the envelope, a conditional use permit addresses the
> use, a rezoning changes the rule.
>
> **All three are discretionary, and nobody is entitled to one.** "Won't be a problem" predicts a
> discretionary decision — a prediction stated as certainty, on which a buyer may commit hundreds of
> thousands of dollars.

### Problem 4

A converted garage has been used as a bedroom for thirty years. The seller says it is grandfathered.
Permit records show a permit issued in 1994 for a garage conversion, never finaled. The listing
advertises 2,340 square feet including the converted space.

**Assess each element.**

> **Solution.** **"Grandfathered" is being used for the wrong concept.** A **legal nonconforming use**
> was lawful when established and no longer conforms because the ordinance changed. **A use that was
> never lawful is not a nonconforming use** — it is unpermitted, which is a different and worse
> problem, and thirty years does not convert one into the other.
>
> **The open permit is a real problem, not a technicality.** A permit issued but never finaled can
> complicate the sale, and the obligation to close it out generally follows the property to the buyer.
> Note also what the record does establish and does not: it shows a permit was issued for described
> work and gives a date. It does **not** establish that the work was completed, done well, or that
> current condition matches the permit.
>
> **The square footage is a misrepresentation as stated.** Unpermitted area is not included in
> advertised living area without disclosing its status.
>
> **What the licensee does:** disclose what is known including the seller's statement and the licensee's
> observations; recommend permit research at the jurisdiction, which is public record; recommend the
> buyer investigate what legalization would require, since the cost can be substantial and some work
> cannot be legalized at all; and document. **What the licensee does not do:** say it is legal because
> it looks fine, characterize the risk, predict what the city will require, or advise the seller to stay
> quiet.

### Problem 5

A 1962 house is being sold to a buyer with no children. There is visible dark growth under a bathroom
window and a rusted vent pipe in the rear yard the seller says served "an old oil tank, probably still
down there." The agent tells the buyer lead rules do not apply without children, that the growth is
"just surface mold," and that old tanks are common in the neighborhood.

**Correct all three.**

> **Solution.** **Lead-based paint obligations are triggered by the age of the housing**, not by whether
> children will occupy it — pre-1978 target housing is covered. And the obligation is misstated in the
> other direction too: **disclose what is known**, provide available records and reports, provide the
> required **pamphlet**, and give the buyer an **opportunity to conduct an assessment.** It does not
> require testing, creating records that do not exist, certifying the property lead-free, or
> remediating.
>
> **"Just surface mold" is an assessment the licensee is not qualified to make**, on which the buyer may
> rely. Mold requires moisture, so the underlying question is where the water is coming from. Disclose
> the **observation** — visible growth and its location — and recommend evaluation.
>
> **An underground storage tank is not made harmless by being common.** Leaking tanks create soil and
> groundwater contamination questions and potentially substantial liability. Disclose what the seller
> said, recommend specialist investigation, and route it.
>
> **The rule covering all three:** disclose what is known including your own observations, recommend
> appropriate investigation by a qualified professional, and **do not evaluate.**

### Problem 6

A property has never flooded in the seller's twenty-two years but sits in a mapped flood zone and a
mapped fire hazard severity zone. The buyer says the flood mapping is "obviously outdated." The
licensee plans to have the buyer get insurance quotes after the inspection contingency closes.

**Correct the buyer and assess the plan.**

> **Solution.** **The disclosure applies on mapping, not on event history.** A property that never
> flooded is disclosed as within a flood zone if it is mapped in one, and the buyer's view of the
> mapping's currency changes nothing. The disclosure tells him he is in a mapped zone so he can
> investigate what that means; it does **not** certify safety, predict an event, or substitute for his
> own investigation.
>
> **The insurance plan is the single most preventable failure in this area.** Hazard zone status drives
> insurance availability and cost — in some California areas now the determining factor in whether a
> transaction closes at all — along with construction requirements, financing conditions, and the
> disclosure obligations the buyer inherits as a future seller.
>
> **Insurance is a first-week item in a mapped fire or flood area, not an administrative closing task.**
> A buyer who learns in week one that coverage will cost far more than assumed can act; one who learns
> it two days before closing generally cannot.

### Problem 7

A buyer is purchasing 20 rural acres to plant an orchard and build a house. The property has a well, a
septic system, and is reached by a gravel road across a neighbor's land that the seller says "everyone
has always used."

**List the questions and identify the most serious issue.**

> **Solution.** **The access is the most serious issue**, and it is the one dressed up to sound
> harmless. "Everyone has always used it" describes a **custom, not a right.** A rural parcel reached
> by a road nobody has a recorded right to use has an access problem the buyer discovers after closing —
> and long permitted use raises a prescriptive question that is a legal doctrine to route, not a
> conclusion to assert.
>
> **The rural intake questions:** What is the water source, and is it documented? For the well —
> production, quality, and the date of the last test? Is any water right appurtenant to the parcel and
> documented? Is the property within a **groundwater management area**, where pumping constraints could
> affect what can be grown? Are there shared-facility agreements — shared well, pipeline, access
> easement? Is the supply sufficient for **the buyer's intended use**, which here means irrigating an
> orchard rather than serving a household? What is the septic situation and when was it last inspected?
> And is access legal and documented, or by custom?
>
> **What transfers:** the buyer's **intent** is the first due diligence question. A buyer planning to
> plant and build needs investigation a buyer planning to move in does not.

### Problem 8

A buyer intends to build. On day eleven of a fourteen-day contingency, the association documents have
not arrived, the permit research has not started, no survey was ordered despite a fence that appears
to cross the rear line, and the preliminary report was received that morning. The agent's file
contains a list of properties shown and the executed contract.

**Diagnose the sequencing and state what the file should have shown.**

> **Solution.** **Everything slow was started last.** Order due diligence by **lead time**, not by
> apparent importance — the slow items are the ones that fail transactions.
>
> **Week one** should have carried: the preliminary report and its underlying documents; association
> documents; insurance quotes if the property is in a mapped hazard area; permit research at the
> jurisdiction; and the natural hazard report. **Week two:** inspections matched to the property;
> zoning and entitlement questions at the planning counter, which this buyer needs because he intends
> to build; the survey, triggered here by a fence apparently crossing a line; and environmental
> assessment if the property's history called for one.
>
> **And the two halves were never separated.** Public — general plan and zoning, permits, parcel
> legality, hazard requirements, local transfer requirements. Private — CC&Rs, association rules and
> architectural approval, easements burdening the parcel, private agreements, leases. **A buyer must
> clear both columns**, and the common failure is getting a "yes" from the public half and treating the
> question as answered.
>
> **What the file should show:** the buyer's stated intentions recorded at intake; each investigation
> recommended with its date; each report ordered with the date ordered and received; the buyer's
> confirmation of receipt and review; recommendations to consult the jurisdiction, a specialist, or
> counsel with the buyer's responses; and **anything that could not be obtained in time and what was
> done about it.** That last item is the one licensees omit — and a report that never arrived, with no
> record of the escalation, looks in hindsight exactly like a report nobody ordered.

## Chapter summary

**Four government powers limit fee simple absolute**, and the compensation column separates them:
police power regulates without paying, eminent domain takes and pays, taxation charges, escheat
collects where there are no heirs.

**Regulation that reduces value is not a taking** in the ordinary case. Whether it has gone far enough
to become one is a legal question to recognize and refer, never to advise on.

**Pending government action is a material fact** — proposed rezonings, announced projects, threatened
condemnation.

**The general plan states intent; zoning controls today; entitlements record what a parcel has actually
been approved for.** Zoning governs use **and** development standards, and permitted use is not the
same question as physical feasibility.

**Variance for the envelope, conditional use permit for the use, rezoning to change the rule** — all
discretionary, and none is a formality.

**A nonconforming use may continue but generally not expand, and may be lost.** A use that was never
lawful is not nonconforming, and time does not cure it.

**Zoning and recorded restrictions are independent, and the more restrictive governs.**

**A permit record establishes issuance and a date** — not completion, quality, or current condition. An
open permit follows the property.

**Unpermitted work is a disclosure item, not automatically a disaster.** Disclose, recommend permit
research and a legalization inquiry, keep it out of advertised square footage without disclosure, and
never characterize the risk.

**Local transfer requirements vary by jurisdiction** and must be checked rather than assumed.

**For every environmental hazard the role is the same:** disclose what is known including your own
observations, recommend appropriate investigation, and do not evaluate. Lead-based paint is triggered
by housing age, not by children.

**Natural hazard disclosure applies on mapping, not event history**, and its most consequential
downstream effect is insurance — a first-week item in mapped areas, never a closing task.

**Water is three questions**: supply, rights, and conservation. On rural property, ask all eight intake
questions — and treat "everyone has always used that road" as an access problem, not an answer.

**Every land use question has a public half and a private half**, and clearing one does not clear the
other.

**Sequence due diligence by lead time**, document what was recommended, ordered, received, and
reviewed — and document what did not arrive and what you did about it.

**The recurring failure is not wrong land use advice. It is land use advice at all.**
