## 1. Freehold and leasehold estates

### 1.1 What an estate is

An **estate** is the nature and extent of a person's interest in land. It answers two questions: what rights does the holder have, and for how long?

The primary division is by **duration**:

| | Duration | Nature |
|---|---|---|
| **Freehold estate** | Indefinite — potentially forever, or for a life | An interest in the land itself |
| **Leasehold estate** | A fixed or terminable period | A right of possession, with the owner retaining the reversion |

The practical difference: a freehold holder owns; a leasehold holder possesses. Both are real property interests, and both can be bought, sold, and encumbered.

### 1.2 The four leasehold estates

Leaseholds are classified by **how they end**, which is the useful way to hold them.

| Estate | Duration | How it ends |
|---|---|---|
| **Estate for years** | A definite period stated in the lease | Automatically, at the stated end. **No notice required.** |
| **Periodic estate** | Renews period to period — month to month | By notice from either party, per the required period |
| **Estate at will** | Indefinite, with the consent of both | When either party ends it, subject to statutory notice |
| **Estate at sufferance** | The tenant holds over after the right ends | By the landlord's action; the tenant is a holdover, not a trespasser |

**The name misleads on the first one.** An "estate for years" is defined by having a **definite ending date**, not by lasting years. A six-month lease and a ten-day lease are both estates for years.

### 1.3 The distinctions that matter in practice

**Estate for years versus periodic estate: notice.** An estate for years expires on its stated date without anyone giving notice. A periodic tenancy continues until someone gives the required notice. A licensee who advises a landlord that a fixed-term lease "needs a 30-day notice to end" has described the wrong estate.

**Estate at sufferance is not trespass.** The holdover tenant entered lawfully. That is why the landlord's remedy is a legal process rather than self-help, and why changing the locks is unlawful no matter how clear the tenant's obligation to leave.

**A leasehold survives a sale.** The buyer of a leased property takes subject to the lease on its terms. This is why existing leases and their terms are an intake item, and why "the tenant will be out by closing" needs to be verified against the lease rather than accepted.

### 1.4 The reversion

When a leasehold ends, possession returns to the owner. That future interest is the **reversion**, and it is what the landlord holds during the tenancy.

The landlord's estate is not suspended by the lease. It is a fee simple subject to a leasehold, which is why the landlord can sell, mortgage, and devise it while the tenant is in possession.

### 1.6 Why the estate concept controls everything downstream

A principles course opens with estates because **the estate held determines what can be done with the property**, and every later topic assumes the answer.

| What the estate determines | Where it surfaces later |
|---|---|
| **What can be conveyed** | A seller cannot convey more than he holds. A life tenant conveying "the property" conveys a life estate measured by his life, and the buyer finds out later. |
| **What can be encumbered** | A lender's security is limited by the estate pledged, which is why a lender's title requirements probe the estate rather than accepting a description. |
| **Who must sign** | Concurrent owners, spouses, trustees, and entity signatories — Sections 3, 4, and 6 of this lesson are all versions of this one question. |
| **What happens at death** | Survivorship, probate, or the terms of an instrument. **The difference between two forms of concurrent ownership is invisible on the property and total in effect.** |
| **What a creditor can reach** | Which affects who is willing to lend and on what terms. |
| **What the occupant may do** | Alter, encumber, lease, or let deteriorate — the life tenant's limits in Section 2.4 are the clearest case. |
| **How long it lasts** | Perpetual, for a life, for a term, or until a condition occurs. |

**Two conclusions.**

**The most consequential fact about a property is frequently invisible.** Two identical houses on the same street can be held under estates with completely different consequences for conveyance, financing, and inheritance, and **nothing about the property reveals it.** The estate is established from documents, which is why the intake practice in Section 6.5 asks for them rather than for a description.

**"Who owns it?" is the wrong question.** The professional question is **"what interest is held, by whom, and who has authority to convey it?"** — three questions, frequently with three different answers, and a transaction that assumes they collapse into one is the transaction that fails at title clearance.

> **The failure this prevents.** A listing taken from a person who describes himself as the owner, marketed, and put under contract — after which title clearance reveals a co-owner, a life estate, a trust with a successor trustee requirement, or an entity whose authority document says something other than what the signer said. **Every one of those is discoverable at intake with a question and a document**, and every one of them is expensive at day nineteen.

### 1.7 Checkpoint

You should be able to name a leasehold estate from a description of how it terminates, and state what happens to a lease when the property is sold.

## 2. Fee simple and life estates

### 2.1 Fee simple absolute

**Fee simple absolute** is the greatest estate the law recognizes: ownership of indefinite duration, freely transferable during life and at death, with the full bundle of rights.

It is the default. A deed conveying property without limiting language conveys a fee simple absolute, and most residential transactions transfer exactly this.

Fee simple is still subject to the four government powers taken up in Lesson 06, and to private restrictions taken up in Lesson 05. "Absolute" describes the estate's duration and transferability, not freedom from regulation.

### 2.2 Defeasible fees

A fee can be granted subject to a condition, so that it may end if the condition is broken. These are called **defeasible** fees, and California practice encounters them mainly in older chains of title and in conveyances to public or charitable uses.

The practical point for a licensee is narrow and important: **a deed containing conditional language is a title matter, not a curiosity.** It goes to title and to counsel, and the buyer needs to understand it before the contingency expires. Whether a condition is enforceable, and what happens if it is broken, is a legal question.

### 2.3 Life estates

A **life estate** is ownership measured by a lifetime rather than by a fixed period.

| Party | Interest |
|---|---|
| **Life tenant** | Owns and possesses for the measuring life |
| **Remainderman** | Takes when the measuring life ends, where the grantor named a third party |
| **Reversion holder** | Takes when the measuring life ends, where the interest returns to the grantor |

A variant worth naming: a life estate **pur autre vie** is measured by the life of someone other than the holder. The holder may die first, in which case the estate passes to the holder's successors until the measuring life ends.

### 2.4 What a life tenant may and may not do

**May:** possess and use the property, receive its income, and transfer whatever interest they hold — which is the life estate, and no more.

**May not:** commit **waste** — conduct that permanently impairs the property to the detriment of the remainderman. Neglecting maintenance, removing valuable timber, or allowing the property to deteriorate can all be waste.

**Must generally:** maintain the property, pay taxes and ordinary carrying charges, and preserve the remainderman's interest.

### 2.5 The transaction problem

A life tenant can only sell what they own, and what they own ends when the measuring life ends. A buyer purchasing from a life tenant alone receives an interest that terminates on someone else's death.

> **The professional response:** where title shows a life estate, a conveyance in fee requires the life tenant **and** the remainderman or reversion holder to join. This is an authority question of exactly the kind Lesson 01 described, and it is verified against the instrument, not assumed.

A licensee who takes a listing from a life tenant without identifying the remainderman has taken a listing that cannot deliver a fee.

### 2.6 Checkpoint

You should be able to say who must sign to convey a fee where a life estate exists, and explain what waste is.

## 3. Joint tenancy and tenancy in common

### 3.1 Concurrent ownership

Where two or more people own the same property at the same time, they hold it concurrently. California's principal forms are **tenancy in common**, **joint tenancy**, and the community property forms taken up in Section 4.

### 3.2 The operative comparison

| | Tenancy in common | Joint tenancy |
|---|---|---|
| **Shares** | May be unequal | Equal |
| **Right of survivorship** | **No** | **Yes** |
| **On death** | The share passes by will or intestate succession | The interest passes to the surviving joint tenants |
| **Transferable during life** | Yes, freely | Yes — but transfer **severs** the joint tenancy as to that interest |
| **Creation** | The default where two or more take without specifying | Requires express language creating it |
| **Number of owners** | Any number | Two or more |

**The survivorship difference is the whole point**, and it is what makes vesting an estate planning question rather than a form selection.

### 3.3 The four unities

Joint tenancy traditionally requires four unities, conventionally remembered as **PITT**:

- **Possession** — each holds an undivided right to possess the whole.
- **Interest** — equal interests.
- **Time** — the interests vested at the same time.
- **Title** — the interests arose from the same instrument.

Tenancy in common requires only unity of **possession**. That single requirement is why tenants in common may hold unequal shares acquired at different times from different sources.

### 3.4 Severance

A joint tenant who transfers their interest during life **severs** the joint tenancy as to that interest, which becomes a tenancy in common.

> Three joint tenants each hold a one-third interest. One sells to an outsider.
>
> **The buyer holds a one-third interest as a tenant in common.** The remaining two continue as joint tenants **between themselves** as to their two-thirds, with survivorship operating between them and not as to the outsider's third.

Severance is a recurring examination topic and a recurring practical surprise, because the transferring joint tenant has changed the survivorship expectations of people who did not agree to the change.

### 3.5 Partition

Where concurrent owners cannot agree, any of them may generally seek **partition** — a court-supervised division of the property, either physically or by sale with division of the proceeds.

This is a legal remedy, and a licensee's role is to recognize it as the answer to "we can't agree what to do with the house" and refer it. Licensees are frequently asked to take a listing where the co-owners disagree; the authority question must be answered before the listing is signed, not during escrow.

### 3.6 What the licensee actually does

Vesting has ownership, survivorship, tax, and estate consequences, and **advising which to choose is outside the license.** The licensee's obligations are:

1. **Explain that the choice matters** and that changing it later is neither free nor simple.
2. **Refer to legal and tax counsel before escrow prepares the deed.**
3. **At intake on a listing, confirm how title is held and who must sign** — against the record, not from the seller's description.

> Two unmarried siblings say they want the survivor to take the whole property automatically, **and** each to be able to leave her share to her own children.
>
> Those goals point in different directions. Survivorship and the ability to devise a share are not both satisfied by a single form. Resolving that tension is estate planning, and it belongs to counsel — not to the licensee, and not to escrow's deadline for the deed instruction.

### 3.8 A worked ownership intake

> **Six listing appointments in one week.** At each, the licensee asks the Section 6.5 questions: **how is title currently held, may I see the document, and who must sign to convey?**
>
> **1. "My wife and I own it."** Two people, married, and the form of ownership is a question with several possible answers — Section 4.2's concepts are engaged, and the answer affects survivorship, creditors, and taxation. **The licensee does not advise on which form is better** and does not assume which one applies. He obtains the vesting from the record.
>
> **2. "It's in my trust."** Section 6.2 governs. **The trustee conveys, and the question is who currently holds that office** — which the trust instrument answers and the seller's description does not. A successor trustee acting after a death or incapacity is a different situation with its own documentary requirements. **The licensee obtains the documentation the title company and counsel will require, and routes the legal questions.**
>
> **3. "My brother and I inherited it."** Two owners, and the form of their concurrent ownership determines what happens if one dies before closing and whether one can act alone. **Both sign.** How the property passed to them — by will, by trust, by operation of law — may also determine whether an administration is required, which is a legal question with a timeline attached to it.
>
> **4. "It's owned by our LLC."** Section 6.3. **The entity conveys, through whoever its governing documents authorize**, and the authority is established from the documents rather than from the person's title on a business card. **A member is not automatically authorized.**
>
> **5. "I have power of attorney for my father."** Section 6.4. **The instrument's scope is what matters** — whether it reaches real property, whether it is currently effective, and whether any conditions on it have been satisfied. **This is a legal question and it goes to counsel and the title company early**, because a defect discovered at closing cannot be repaired at closing. **And where the principal's capacity is in question, Lesson 15 of the practice course applies:** speak with the principal directly, document what you observed, and escalate.
>
> **6. "I bought it with my partner but it's really mine — she just helped with the loan."** **Two different claims: what the record says and what the seller believes the arrangement was.** The record controls who must sign. **What the parties intended between themselves is a legal question the licensee does not resolve**, and a listing taken on the seller's characterization is a listing that will fail.
>
> **What the week produced.** Six appointments, six documents requested, two matters routed to counsel before listing. **None of the six sellers described their situation inaccurately on purpose.** Each described it the way people describe ownership in conversation, which is by relationship rather than by interest — and Section 1.6's point is that those are not the same thing.

### 3.9 Checkpoint

You should be able to state the operative differences between the two forms, explain what severance does to a three-party joint tenancy, and say what you would do when a client asks which to choose.

## 4. Community property orientation

### 4.1 What the system is

California is a **community property** state. In outline, property acquired by a married person during marriage is generally community property, and property acquired before marriage or by gift or inheritance during marriage is generally that spouse's separate property.

**This section is an orientation, not a guide.** The rules governing characterization, transmutation, commingled assets, and dissolution are complex, fact-dependent, and squarely legal. What a licensee needs is enough to recognize the issue at intake and to route it correctly.

### 4.2 The three concepts to hold

**Community property.** Generally, property acquired during marriage other than by gift or inheritance. Each spouse generally holds an equal interest.

**Separate property.** Generally, property owned before marriage, or acquired during marriage by gift, devise, or inheritance — together with the rents, issues, and profits of separate property.

**Community property with right of survivorship.** A form California recognizes that combines community property characterization with survivorship. It has both tax and estate consequences, and whether it fits a particular couple is a question for their advisors.

### 4.3 The intake question that matters

The licensee's operative concern is authority to convey.

> **Where community property is involved, both spouses generally must join in a conveyance or encumbrance of real property.**

That single proposition drives the intake practice:

1. **Ask marital status** as part of confirming authority, in the same neutral way as every other intake question.
2. **Check how title is held** against the record.
3. **Where the record and the answer do not reconcile**, stop and escalate rather than proceeding.
4. **Where a spouse is not on title but may have an interest**, that is a title and legal question — route it to title and to counsel before the listing is signed.

A listing signed by one spouse where the other has an interest is the same category of failure as a listing signed by a beneficiary rather than a trustee: it cannot deliver what it promises, and it surfaces in escrow where the cost is highest.

### 4.4 Dissolution and separation

Where a marriage is being dissolved, authority to convey may be affected by court orders, and the timing of a sale may be constrained. A licensee working on a sale during a dissolution should expect that **counsel is already involved** and should coordinate rather than improvise.

What the licensee must not do is advise either spouse on their rights, characterize property as community or separate, or predict how a court will divide it. Each of those is a legal conclusion.

### 4.5 A note on neutrality

Questions about marital status at intake exist to establish authority to convey. They are asked of every client in the same way, recorded, and used for that purpose. A licensee who asks selectively, or who uses the answer for anything other than the authority analysis, has created a fair housing problem alongside the compliance one.

### 4.6 Checkpoint

You should be able to state the general rule about joining in a conveyance, and describe what you do when the record and the client's description of title do not match.

## 5. Condominiums and common interest developments

### 5.1 What a common interest development is

A **common interest development** combines individually owned interests with a shared interest in common areas, governed by a set of recorded documents and administered by an association.

The recurring forms:

| Form | What the owner owns |
|---|---|
| **Condominium** | An individual unit — often defined by interior surfaces — plus an undivided interest in the common areas |
| **Planned development** | A separate lot, plus membership in an association owning or managing common areas |
| **Stock cooperative** | Shares in a corporation owning the building, plus a right of occupancy |
| **Community apartment** | An undivided interest in the whole, plus an exclusive right to occupy a unit |

The differences matter for financing, for what is being conveyed, and for how the owner's obligations arise.

### 5.2 The governing documents

Ownership in a common interest development comes with a body of private law the buyer is agreeing to be bound by:

- **The declaration** — recorded covenants, conditions, and restrictions establishing the scheme and the use restrictions.
- **Bylaws** — how the association operates and is governed.
- **Rules and regulations** — adopted by the association, and easier to change than the declaration.
- **The budget and reserve study** — what the association plans to spend and what it has set aside.
- **Meeting minutes** — where problems appear before they appear anywhere else.
- **Financial statements and assessment history.**
- **Pending or threatened litigation**, including construction defect claims.

### 5.3 Why this belongs in a principles course

Because the documents impose obligations that bind the buyer regardless of zoning and regardless of what the seller told him:

**Use restrictions.** Whether he may rent the unit, and on what terms. Whether he may keep animals. What he may do to the exterior, the patio, or the windows. Whether he may run a business from it.

**Assessments.** The regular assessment, and the association's power to levy **special assessments** — which can be substantial and which a buyer inherits.

**Reserves.** An association with inadequate reserves against known future work has a special assessment in its future, whether or not one has been declared.

**Litigation.** Construction defect litigation affecting the building is a material fact, and it can also affect the availability of financing.

### 5.4 The timing point

The buyer must have the documents **in time to review them within the contingency period** — which means requesting them promptly rather than late.

This is one of the slow items. An association that takes three weeks to produce documents will destroy a fourteen-day review contingency that was ordered on day ten. A licensee who orders the documents at the start has given the buyer a real review; one who orders them late has given him a formality.

### 5.5 What a licensee does and does not do

**Does:** identify that the property is in a common interest development; order or ensure the documents are ordered immediately; confirm the buyer received them; recommend he read the declaration, the budget and reserves, the minutes, and any litigation disclosure; and document the recommendation.

**Does not:** summarize the declaration for the buyer, characterize the reserves as adequate, predict whether a special assessment will be levied, or advise whether litigation is serious. Each of those exceeds the license and invites reliance.

> **A buyer asks: "Are the reserves okay?"**
>
> **Correct:** "That's outside what I can assess, and it's exactly what to have reviewed before your contingency expires — the reserve study and the budget are in the packet, and a CPA or your attorney can tell you what they show. I'll note that we discussed it."
>
> **Incorrect:** "They look fine to me — this association seems well run."

### 5.7 What a buyer in a common interest development is actually buying

Section 5.3 explains why this belongs in a principles course. This section makes the point concrete, because the gap between what a buyer thinks he is buying and what he is buying is wider here than anywhere else in residential real estate.

**The buyer is acquiring four things at once:**

1. **An interest in a unit or a lot**, defined by an instrument rather than by the walls he can see. **What is his, what is common, and what is exclusive-use common area is a documentary question**, and it determines who repairs what.
2. **An undivided interest in common area**, with its condition, its deferred maintenance, and its future costs.
3. **Membership in an association**, with governing documents that bind him and a board that can act.
4. **A share of an ongoing financial obligation** — regular assessments, and special assessments that have not happened yet.

**What the documents answer, and what each one is for:**

| The document | The question it answers |
|---|---|
| **Declaration and restrictions** | What may and may not be done — rentals, pets, alterations, business use, parking, occupancy |
| **Bylaws and rules** | How the association operates and what it may enforce |
| **Budget and reserve study** | **Whether the association can pay for what is coming** — the single most predictive document in the package |
| **Financial statements** | Present condition, delinquency rates, and whether assessments are being collected |
| **Minutes** | What the board has been discussing, which is frequently where a future special assessment first appears |
| **Insurance information** | What the association covers and, more importantly, what it does not |
| **Litigation disclosures** | Pending matters, which can affect both value and financing availability |

**Three points a licensee should make to every buyer, in writing.**

**Order the documents on day one.** Section 5.4's timing point. **The buyer's review period is what remains after delivery**, and a package ordered on day six against a seven-day delivery obligation leaves a buyer reading hundreds of pages in a weekend.

**Read the reserve study and the minutes.** Buyers read the pet rules and skip these two. **A thinly funded reserve is a special assessment that has not been announced yet**, and the minutes are where it is visible first.

**Confirm what financing requires.** Some programs impose conditions on the development itself — owner-occupancy ratios, litigation status, budget requirements — and **a buyer can be approved while the development is not.**

> **Section 5.5's limit governs all of it.** The licensee delivers the documents, recommends they be reviewed with counsel, and **does not interpret the restrictions.** Whether a provision permits a particular use is a legal question about a recorded instrument, and it is asked of licensees constantly.

### 5.8 Checkpoint

You should be able to name what a buyer must review, explain why the documents must be ordered early, and answer a reserves question correctly.

## 6. Trust and entity ownership basics

### 6.1 Why this section exists

Because the most common and most expensive intake failure in residential practice is **unverified authority to convey**, and trusts and entities are where it happens.

The failure has a consistent shape: someone with an obvious connection to the property — the occupant, the person paying the taxes, a beneficiary, an officer — signs a listing they have no authority to sign. It surfaces in escrow, where unwinding costs the most.

### 6.2 Trust ownership

Where title is held in a trust, the **trustee** holds legal title and may convey **only within the authority the trust document grants.**

| Party | Role |
|---|---|
| **Trustor / settlor** | Created the trust and transferred property into it |
| **Trustee** | Holds legal title and administers the trust; **signs** |
| **Beneficiary** | Holds the beneficial interest; **does not sign** |

What must be verified at intake:

1. **Who the current trustee is** — trustees change, and the person who was trustee three years ago may not be.
2. **That the trust authorizes sale of real property.**
3. **What the trust requires of the trustee in doing so** — some require consents or notices.
4. **That title is actually held in the trust**, against the record.

Occupancy, payment of taxes, and beneficial interest are each evidence of connection to the property, and **none establishes authority to sell it.**

### 6.3 Entity ownership

Where title is held by a corporation, limited liability company, partnership, or other entity, authority comes from the entity's governing documents and from whatever authorization those documents require.

The verification questions:

- **Does the entity exist and is it in good standing?**
- **Who is authorized to act for it** — and does the governing document require a resolution, a manager's consent, or the signature of more than one person?
- **Does the authorization cover this transaction**, including its terms?

A signature block reading "President" is not verification. It is a claim to be verified against the entity's documents.

### 6.4 Other authority situations

**Probate and decedents' estates.** The personal representative acts within what the appointment permits, and the process may require court involvement. Verify the appointment.

**Conservatorship and guardianship.** Authority comes from the court order, which may limit what may be done and may require approval.

**Power of attorney.** The instrument must currently be valid and must expressly authorize conveyance of real property. A general power of attorney signed years ago is not self-evidently sufficient, and whether it is, is a legal question.

**Co-owners.** All of them, unless the record or an instrument establishes otherwise.

### 6.5 The intake practice

> **Verify authority against a source at intake, and stop and escalate where it is unclear rather than postponing the question.**

Ten minutes with the trust document, the operating agreement, or the letters of appointment at the listing appointment prevents a failure that would otherwise appear in escrow with an accepted offer, a buyer with a loan approval, and no way to deliver title.

And where the answer is genuinely unclear, the licensee's role is not to resolve it. It is to route it to title and to counsel **before the listing is signed.**

### 6.6 Checkpoint

You should be able to list what you would verify at a listing appointment where title is held in a trust, and explain why a beneficiary's signature is insufficient.

## Practice problems

### Problem 1

Classify each and state how it ends: (a) a twelve-month lease expiring March 31; (b) a month-to-month
tenancy; (c) a tenant who stayed past March 31 without a new agreement; (d) an occupancy both parties
may end at any time. A landlord asks whether he must give thirty days' notice to end (a).

**Answer.**

> **Solution.** (a) An **estate for years** — defined by a definite ending date, not by lasting years;
> a six-month lease and a ten-day lease are both estates for years. It ends **automatically on March
> 31 without notice.** So the landlord's question is answered no, and a licensee who tells him a
> 30-day notice is required has described a different estate.
>
> (b) A **periodic estate**, which continues until someone gives the required notice.
>
> (c) An **estate at sufferance.** The tenant entered lawfully and is a **holdover, not a trespasser** —
> which is exactly why the remedy is a legal process and why changing the locks is unlawful however
> clear the obligation to leave.
>
> (d) An **estate at will**, subject to statutory notice.

### Problem 2

A buyer is purchasing a fourplex. Three units are on twelve-month leases with eight months remaining;
the seller says the tenants "will be out by closing."

**What does the buyer take, and what should the licensee do?**

> **Solution.** **A leasehold survives a sale.** The buyer takes subject to the leases on their terms —
> the leases do not end because the property changed hands, and the buyer steps into the landlord's
> position with a **reversion** eight months out.
>
> The seller's statement is a representation to be **verified against the leases**, not accepted. Ask
> for the leases themselves, the deposits held and their amounts, any notices already given, and any
> written agreement to vacate. "They'll be out" is not a lease term.
>
> **What transfers:** existing leases and their terms are an intake item on any occupied property,
> because they determine what is actually being conveyed.

### Problem 3

A licensee takes a listing from a man who has lived in and maintained a house for eleven years. Title
shows a life estate in him, with a remainder to his niece.

**What can he convey, and who must sign?**

> **Solution.** **He can convey only what he owns**, which is an interest that terminates when he dies.
> A buyer purchasing from him alone receives an estate that ends on someone else's death — which is
> not what any residential buyer thinks he is buying.
>
> **To convey a fee, the life tenant and the remainderman must both join.** The niece is not a
> formality; she holds a present interest in the property's future.
>
> **What transfers:** this is the Lesson 01 authority question in another costume. Verify against the
> instrument, and a licensee who takes this listing without identifying the remainderman has taken a
> listing that cannot deliver a fee.
>
> Also worth knowing while he holds it: the life tenant must not commit **waste** — neglecting
> maintenance, removing valuable timber, or allowing deterioration to the remainderman's detriment.

### Problem 4

Three siblings hold a property as joint tenants, each with a one-third interest. One sells her
interest to an outside investor.

**Describe the resulting ownership.**

> **Solution.** **The sale severs the joint tenancy as to that interest.**
>
> The investor holds a **one-third interest as a tenant in common.** He has no survivorship rights, and
> none run against him.
>
> The two remaining siblings continue as **joint tenants between themselves** as to their combined
> two-thirds, with survivorship operating between them only.
>
> **Why this is a recurring surprise:** the transferring joint tenant unilaterally changed the
> survivorship expectations of two people who never agreed to the change. Nothing required their
> consent, and nothing notified them.
>
> If the three later cannot agree what to do with the property, any of them may generally seek
> **partition** — a court-supervised division physically or by sale. That is a legal remedy to
> recognize and refer, and the authority question must be settled **before** a listing is signed rather
> than during escrow.

### Problem 5

Two unmarried siblings buying together tell the licensee they want the survivor to take the whole
property automatically, **and** each to be able to leave her share to her own children. Escrow needs
the vesting instruction tomorrow.

**Respond.**

> **Solution.** **Name the tension and refer it — do not choose.**
>
> Those two goals point in different directions. **Survivorship** means the surviving owner takes;
> **the ability to devise a share** means each can direct her interest elsewhere. Joint tenancy
> delivers the first and defeats the second; tenancy in common delivers the second and defeats the
> first. No single form satisfies both, and resolving it is **estate planning, not form selection.**
>
> The licensee may explain that the choice matters and carries ownership, survivorship, tax, and estate
> consequences, and that changing it later is neither free nor simple. The licensee may **not** advise
> which to choose.
>
> **The referral goes to legal and tax counsel before escrow prepares the deed** — and escrow's
> deadline is a scheduling pressure, not a reason to answer a question outside the license.

### Problem 6

A married man signs a listing alone. He says the house was bought during the marriage but title is in
his name only, that his wife "has nothing to do with it," and that they are in the middle of a
dissolution. He asks the licensee whether the house is community property and what his wife is
entitled to.

**What does the licensee do?**

> **Solution.** **Stop, verify, and escalate — and answer neither question.**
>
> The operative concern is authority to convey. Where community property is involved, **both spouses
> generally must join** in a conveyance or encumbrance of real property, and title in one name does not
> resolve whether the other has an interest. Property acquired during marriage other than by gift or
> inheritance is generally community property — but characterization is fact-dependent and squarely
> legal.
>
> **What the licensee does:** confirm how title is held against the record; where the record and the
> client's description do not reconcile, stop rather than proceeding; and route the interest question
> to title and to counsel **before the listing is signed.** In a dissolution, expect that authority may
> be affected by court orders and that counsel is already involved — coordinate rather than improvise.
>
> **What the licensee must not do:** characterize property as community or separate, advise either
> spouse on their rights, or predict how a court will divide it. All three are legal conclusions.
>
> **And a note on how the question is asked:** marital status is an intake question about authority,
> asked of every client the same way and used only for that purpose. Asking selectively creates a fair
> housing problem alongside the compliance one.
>
> **What transfers:** a listing signed by one spouse where the other has an interest is the same
> failure as a listing signed by a beneficiary rather than a trustee — it cannot deliver what it
> promises, and it surfaces in escrow where the cost is highest.

### Problem 7

A buyer is fourteen days into a review contingency on a condominium. The association documents were
ordered on day ten and have not arrived. When they do, the buyer asks his agent whether the reserves
are adequate and whether the construction defect litigation mentioned in the minutes is serious.

**Assess the timing and answer the questions.**

> **Solution.** **The timing failure came first.** Association documents are a **slow item** and belong
> at the front of the queue. An association taking three weeks to produce a packet destroys a
> fourteen-day contingency ordered on day ten. Ordered at the start, the buyer gets a real review;
> ordered late, he gets a formality.
>
> **Neither question may be answered.** "That's outside what I can assess, and it's exactly what to
> have reviewed before your contingency expires — the reserve study and budget are in the packet, and a
> CPA or your attorney can tell you what they show. The litigation disclosure is the same: it's a legal
> question, and it can also affect financing availability. I'll note that we discussed both."
>
> Saying "they look fine to me, this association seems well run" characterizes reserves the licensee
> cannot evaluate, on which the buyer will rely.
>
> **What he must review:** the declaration and its use restrictions — rental limits, animals,
> exteriors, business use — which bind him regardless of zoning; the budget and **reserves**, since
> inadequate reserves against known work mean a **special assessment** in the future whether or not one
> has been declared; the minutes, where problems appear first; the assessment history; and any pending
> or threatened litigation.

### Problem 8

A woman calls to list her late mother's house. She has lived there twelve years, pays the taxes, and
is one of three beneficiaries of the trust that holds title. She produces a trust document from 2011
naming her mother as trustee. Separately, an LLC-owned property is offered by a man whose signature
block reads "President."

**State what must be verified in each.**

> **Solution.** **Neither person has yet shown authority to convey.**
>
> **On the trust:** the **trustee** signs, and only within the authority the trust document grants. A
> **beneficiary does not sign**, however many there are and however clearly they benefit. Occupancy,
> payment of taxes, and beneficial interest are each evidence of connection to the property and **none
> establishes authority to sell it.** Verify: who the **current** trustee is — the 2011 document names
> someone now deceased, so successor trustee provisions and any acceptance must be examined; that the
> trust authorizes sale of real property; what the trust requires of the trustee in doing so, since
> some require consents or notices; and that title is actually held in the trust, against the record.
>
> **On the LLC:** a signature block reading "President" is a **claim to be verified**, not
> verification — and an LLC does not ordinarily have a president at all, which is itself a signal.
> Verify: that the entity exists and is in good standing; who is authorized to act for it under the
> operating agreement, and whether a resolution, a manager's consent, or more than one signature is
> required; and that the authorization covers this transaction including its terms.
>
> **What transfers:** verify authority against a source at intake, and stop and escalate where it is
> unclear rather than postponing. Ten minutes at the listing appointment prevents a failure that
> otherwise appears in escrow with an accepted offer, a buyer with loan approval, and no way to deliver
> title.

## Chapter summary

**An estate answers what rights and for how long.** Freeholds are of indefinite duration in the land
itself; leaseholds are possession for a period, with the owner holding the reversion.

**Leaseholds are best held by how they end.** An estate for years expires on its date **without
notice**; a periodic estate requires notice; an estate at will ends at either party's election subject
to statutory notice; and an estate at sufferance is a holdover who entered lawfully — which is why
self-help is unlawful.

**A leasehold survives a sale.** Verify existing leases rather than accepting assurances about them.

**Fee simple absolute is the default** and remains subject to government powers and private
restrictions. Conditional language in a deed is a title matter for counsel.

**A life tenant owns for a lifetime, may not commit waste, and can convey only what they hold.** A fee
requires the life tenant and the remainderman to join.

**Joint tenancy has equal shares and survivorship and requires express language; tenancy in common
allows unequal shares, has no survivorship, and is the default.** A lifetime transfer by a joint tenant
**severs** as to that interest while survivorship continues among the others.

**Vesting is an estate planning question.** Explain that it matters, refer it to counsel before the
deed is prepared, and confirm at intake how title is actually held.

**California is a community property state, and both spouses generally must join** in a conveyance.
Ask marital status neutrally as an authority question, check the record, and stop where they do not
reconcile.

**A common interest development brings a body of private law with it.** Use restrictions,
assessments, reserves, minutes, and litigation all bind or affect the buyer — and the documents are a
slow item that must be ordered early enough for the review to be real.

**Verify authority to convey against a source.** Trustee, not beneficiary; the entity's governing
documents, not a signature block; the appointment, the court order, the power of attorney's actual
terms, and all co-owners. Where it is unclear, stop and escalate before the listing is signed.
