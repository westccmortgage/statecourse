## 1. Deed requirements

### 1.1 What a deed is

A **deed** is the written instrument by which an owner conveys an interest in real property to another. The parties have specific names:

- **Grantor** — the party conveying.
- **Grantee** — the party receiving.

A memory device that survives the examination: the gran**tor** gives; the gran**tee** takes.

### 1.2 The requirements

A valid deed must generally:

| Requirement | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Be in writing** | The Statute of Frauds applies to conveyances |
| **Identify a competent grantor** | Legally capable, and with authority to convey |
| **Identify the grantee** | Sufficiently to be ascertainable |
| **Describe the property** | Adequately — **not** by street address alone |
| **Contain granting language** | Words showing present intent to convey |
| **Be signed by the grantor** | The grantee's signature is generally not required |
| **Be delivered and accepted** | Section 3 — and the requirement most often misunderstood |

### 1.3 What a deed does not require

Students and clients believe several things a deed does not need, and each misbelief causes real problems.

**Consideration is not required for validity.** A deed may be a gift. Consideration matters for other purposes — including whether a party qualifies as a bona fide purchaser under the recording system — but a deed reciting "love and affection" can be perfectly valid.

**The grantee need not sign.** The grantor conveys; the grantee's acceptance is required but is generally presumed where the conveyance is beneficial.

**Recording is not required for validity.** A deed transfers title between grantor and grantee on delivery and acceptance, recorded or not. **Recording protects against third parties** — it does not create the transfer.

**Notarization is not required for validity**, but it is required in practice: a deed must generally be acknowledged before a notary to be recorded. So an unacknowledged deed may be valid and unrecordable, which is a problem the grantee will discover later.

**A perfect legal description is not required if the property is otherwise adequately described**, but adequacy is a legal question and the practice is to use the recorded legal description exactly.

### 1.4 The granting clause

The words showing intent to convey — "grant," "convey," "quitclaim." In California the word **"grant"** carries statutory significance, discussed in Section 2, which is why deed type follows from the operative word rather than from the document's title.

### 1.5 The habendum and other clauses

**The habendum clause** — historically "to have and to hold" — defines the extent of the estate conveyed. Where it conflicts with the granting clause, the resolution is a legal question.

**Reservations and exceptions** carve something out: a reserved life estate, reserved mineral rights, an excepted strip. These are the "except" and "reserving" phrases from Lesson 04, and dropping one while copying describes a different parcel.

**Conditions and restrictions** may appear, and conditional language in a deed is a title matter for counsel, not a curiosity.

### 1.6 The licensee's practice

1. **Take the legal description from a recorded document**, not from a prior listing.
2. **Copy it exactly and completely**, including recording references and every reservation, exception, and "together with."
3. **Never substitute an address or APN.**
4. **Escrow prepares the deed**; the licensee confirms the vesting instruction came from the client after the client consulted counsel.
5. **Route conditional or unusual language** to title and to counsel.

### 1.7 Checkpoint

You should be able to list the requirements of a valid deed and name four things a deed does not require.

## 2. Grant deed and quitclaim deed

### 2.1 The spectrum

Deeds differ in **what the grantor warrants** about the title being conveyed. That is the organizing idea; the names follow from it.

| Deed | What the grantor warrants |
|---|---|
| **General warranty deed** | The broadest warranties, extending back through prior ownership. Uncommon in California residential practice |
| **Grant deed** | Limited implied warranties (Section 2.2). **The standard California residential instrument** |
| **Quitclaim deed** | **Nothing.** Conveys whatever interest the grantor has, which may be none |
| **Trustee's deed** | Given following a trustee's sale under a deed of trust |
| **Sheriff's deed** | Given following an execution sale |
| **Gift deed** | A conveyance for love and affection rather than value |
| **Deed in lieu of foreclosure** | A borrower conveying to the lender to avoid foreclosure |

### 2.2 The grant deed's implied warranties

By use of the word **"grant,"** a California grantor impliedly warrants two things unless the deed says otherwise:

1. **That the grantor has not previously conveyed the same estate** to another.
2. **That the estate is free from encumbrances made or suffered by the grantor** — not by prior owners.

**Both warranties are limited to the grantor's own period of ownership.** A grant deed does not warrant against a defect created by someone who owned the property in 1974.

That limit is exactly why title insurance exists in California residential practice: the deed's warranties do not reach the risks the buyer actually faces.

### 2.3 The quitclaim deed

A quitclaim conveys **whatever interest the grantor has, with no warranty that the grantor has any.**

Its legitimate uses are specific and common:

- **Clearing a cloud on title** — a possible claimant releases whatever interest they might have.
- **Transfers between related parties** where warranties are unnecessary.
- **Correcting a defect** in a prior conveyance.
- **Releasing an easement or other interest.**

**What a quitclaim does not do:** it does not clear liens against the property, and it does not make the grantee's title better than the grantor's was. A buyer receiving a quitclaim in an arm's-length purchase should understand that if the grantor owned nothing, the buyer received nothing.

> **A licensee must not advise a client to accept a quitclaim, or to give one, as a solution to a title problem.** Whether a quitclaim resolves a particular cloud is a legal question, and quitclaims are also used to accomplish frauds precisely because they warrant nothing.

### 2.4 Deed types encountered after default

**A trustee's deed** conveys title following a trustee's sale. A buyer at such a sale takes what the sale conveys — which may extinguish junior liens but not senior ones — and the property is generally sold without the protections of an ordinary transaction.

**A deed in lieu of foreclosure** transfers title to the lender voluntarily. It does not automatically extinguish junior liens, which is the reason a lender may decline one, and its consequences for the borrower are legal and tax questions.

Both belong to Lesson 11's default material. Here the point is recognition: **a chain of title containing one of these tells a story the buyer's counsel and title company should read.**

### 2.5 Reconveyance

Not a transfer of ownership but of a security interest: the **reconveyance** is the recorded document releasing a deed of trust when the debt is satisfied.

**Payment of the debt does not clear the record; the recorded document does.** A seller who says a loan was paid off years ago has stated a fact about the debt and nothing about the title, and an unreleased deed of trust in a preliminary report requires a document.

### 2.6 Checkpoint

You should be able to state what a grant deed impliedly warrants and its limit, and explain what a quitclaim does and does not accomplish.

## 3. Delivery and acceptance

### 3.1 The requirement that surprises everyone

> **A deed transfers title on delivery and acceptance. Signing does not transfer title. Notarizing does not transfer title. Recording does not transfer title.**

A deed that is signed, notarized, and sitting in a drawer has conveyed nothing.

### 3.2 What delivery means

Delivery is not primarily physical. It is the grantor's **present intent to pass title now**, manifested by conduct.

The recurring evidence of intent:

| Evidence | Weight |
|---|---|
| Handing the deed to the grantee | Strong |
| Recording the deed | Raises a presumption of delivery |
| Delivering to escrow with instructions to record on closing | Delivery on the conditions being met |
| Handing it to a third party for the grantee | Depends on the instructions and the grantor's control |
| Keeping it, with instructions to record after death | **Generally not effective delivery** — the grantor retained control and intended a testamentary transfer |

**The failure mode is intent, not custody.** A grantor who hands over a deed while saying "this takes effect when I die" has not manifested present intent to pass title now, and the attempted transfer generally fails as an ineffective testamentary act.

### 3.3 Delivery to escrow

Escrow supplies the ordinary mechanism. The grantor deposits the deed with instructions to deliver on the conditions being satisfied. That is a **conditional delivery**, and it is one of the two elements — with a binding contract — that make an arrangement an escrow at all.

Critically, **once delivered into escrow under a binding contract, the grantor generally cannot unilaterally recall it.** The escrow holder is not the grantor's agent for that purpose.

### 3.4 Acceptance

The grantee must accept. Acceptance is generally presumed where the conveyance is beneficial, and it can be shown by conduct — recording, taking possession, paying taxes, encumbering the property.

Acceptance matters most where it is refused. A grantee can decline a conveyance, and there are real reasons to: contaminated property carrying remediation liability, property carrying assessments exceeding its value, or an unwanted interest in a dispute.

### 3.5 What a void delivery cannot be cured by

**Recording does not cure a defective delivery.** A recorded deed raises a presumption of delivery, and a presumption can be rebutted. A deed recorded by a grantee who took it from a drawer without the grantor's intent to convey did not become effective by being recorded.

This is also the mechanism behind deed fraud: a forged or improperly delivered deed appears in the record and looks like a conveyance. **A forged deed is void and conveys nothing, no matter how many transfers follow it** — which is why the title industry treats forgery as a category of loss distinct from an ordinary defect.

### 3.6 The licensee's role

Delivery and acceptance questions arise in specific situations a licensee should be able to spot:

- A client says a relative "signed the house over" years ago but nothing was recorded and nothing changed.
- A deed appears in the record that the seller says he never signed.
- A parent wants to deed the property to a child "but keep it until I die."
- A grantee wants to refuse a conveyance.

**In every one, the licensee's role is the same:** recognize, stop, escalate to the broker, and route it to title and to counsel. Whether a delivery was effective is a legal conclusion with enormous consequences.

### 3.7 Checkpoint

You should be able to explain why a signed and notarized deed may convey nothing, and identify a delivery that fails for lack of present intent.

## 4. Recording and constructive notice

### 4.1 What the recording system is for

California maintains a public record of documents affecting real property. The system exists to answer one question:

> **Who has notice of what?**

Recording does not make a document valid, and failing to record does not make it invalid between the parties. **Recording protects the recorded party against third parties.**

### 4.2 The three kinds of notice

| Type | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Actual notice** | The person in fact knows |
| **Constructive notice** | The law charges the person with knowing, because it was recorded |
| **Inquiry notice** | Facts that would prompt a reasonable person to investigate |

**Constructive notice is the system's engine.** A recorded document is knowable by anyone who looks, so the law treats everyone as knowing it. That is why a buyer cannot claim to be surprised by a recorded easement he never read.

**Inquiry notice is the one that surprises people**, because nothing was recorded. Someone visibly in possession who is not the record owner, a well-worn path across the rear of a lot, an occupant paying rent to a person other than the seller — each is a fact that would prompt investigation, and a purchaser may be charged with what the investigation would have revealed.

### 4.3 The bona fide purchaser

The recording system's protection generally runs to a **bona fide purchaser**: one who takes **for value**, **in good faith**, and **without notice** of the prior interest — actual, constructive, or inquiry.

Each element does work:

- **For value** is why a donee is treated differently from a buyer, and one reason consideration matters even though a deed does not require it for validity.
- **In good faith** excludes a party who knew of the problem.
- **Without notice** includes what the records showed and what an inquiry would have revealed.

### 4.4 Priority

The general rule: **first in time, first in right**, as measured by recording, for those entitled to the system's protection.

The exceptions from Lesson 05, restated because they belong to the recording material:

| Exception | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Property tax liens** | Generally take priority regardless of when they attached |
| **Mechanic's liens** | Priority may relate back to when work commenced |
| **Subordination agreement** | A lienholder agrees to take a lower position |
| **Purchase-money priority** | Certain purchase-money security interests receive favored treatment |

**Priority determines who is paid first from a limited fund and whose interest survives a foreclosure.** A junior lienholder wiped out by a senior foreclosure has lost its security whatever the merits of the debt — which is why a recording date on a preliminary report is not administrative detail.

### 4.5 The chain of title and the abstract

**The chain of title** is the successive recorded conveyances tracing ownership. A **break in the chain** — a conveyance from someone who never appears as a grantee — is a defect requiring resolution.

**A wild deed** is a recorded document outside the chain: because a searcher following the chain would not find it, recording it may not give constructive notice.

**An abstract of title** is a summary of the recorded documents affecting a parcel. In modern California residential practice the preliminary report and the title policy have largely replaced reliance on an abstract, but the vocabulary persists.

### 4.6 What is recorded and what is not

**Recorded:** deeds, deeds of trust, reconveyances, easements, declarations of restrictions, judgment abstracts, mechanic's liens, lis pendens, maps, and many other instruments.

**Frequently not recorded, and therefore invisible to a records search:** leases, occupancy agreements, unrecorded easements arising by implication or prescription, side agreements between parties, and personal property arrangements.

**That gap is what inquiry notice covers**, and it is why physical inspection of the property is a separate obligation from reading the title report.

### 4.8 The recording statute, and what "void as against" means

Section 4.1 said that recording gives constructive notice and protects a purchaser. **Civil Code § 1214** is the section that does it, and its wording repays close reading because it is a race-notice rule expressed as a rule of voidness:

> *"Every conveyance of real property or an estate for years therein, **other than a lease for a term not exceeding one year**, is **void as against any subsequent purchaser or mortgagee** of the same property, or any part thereof, **in good faith and for a valuable consideration, whose conveyance is first duly recorded**, and as against any judgment affecting the title, unless the conveyance shall have been duly recorded prior to the record of notice of action."*

**Unpack it as four conditions a later buyer must satisfy to defeat an earlier unrecorded conveyance.**

| Condition | The words | What it excludes |
|---|---|---|
| **Subsequent** | *"any subsequent purchaser or mortgagee"* | Someone who took first cannot use the section |
| **In good faith** | *"in good faith"* | A buyer with actual notice of the earlier deed |
| **For value** | *"for a valuable consideration"* | A donee; an heir; a gift |
| **First recorded** | *"whose conveyance is first duly recorded"* | A later buyer who also failed to record |

**All four, together.** This is California's race-notice system in a sentence: the later purchaser must be *both* without notice *and* first to record. Neither alone is enough.

**"Void as against" is narrower than "void."** The unrecorded deed is perfectly good between the grantor and the grantee. It fails only against a person the section describes. **A grantee who never records still owns the property against the whole world except a subsequent good-faith purchaser for value who records first** — which is exactly the person most likely to appear.

**The lease carve-out is a practical one.** A lease for a term not exceeding one year is outside the section. Short residential tenancies are not recorded and were never expected to be, and a buyer of occupied property takes subject to what possession discloses regardless.

**And the tail reaches judgments:** *"and as against any judgment affecting the title, unless the conveyance shall have been duly recorded prior to the record of notice of action."* An unrecorded deed can lose to a judgment in litigation the grantee knew nothing about.

### 4.9 Why possession still matters after recording

§ 1214 protects a purchaser *"in good faith."* Good faith is defeated by notice, and **notice is not only what the records show.**

**Constructive notice comes from two places.** The recorded chain of title, and **the fact of possession**. A person in open, visible, exclusive possession of property puts a prospective purchaser on inquiry: the purchaser is charged with knowing what a reasonable inquiry of that occupant would have revealed.

**This is why a walk-through is a title procedure and not only a condition procedure.** An occupant who is not the seller is a question. A tenant with a five-year unrecorded lease, a relative with an unrecorded life estate, a buyer under an unrecorded contract of sale — **none appears in the records and each appears at the property.**

**The practical instruction for a licensee is one sentence long:** if anyone other than the seller is in possession, find out in writing who they are and on what basis, and give the answer to escrow and to the title company before closing.

### 4.10 A worked priority problem

> **1 March.** Owner deeds the property to Buyer A for $700,000. A takes the deed home and does not record it.
> **14 March.** Owner deeds the same property to Buyer B for $720,000. B has never heard of A, searches title and finds nothing, and records on 15 March.
> **20 March.** A records.

**B prevails.** B is a subsequent purchaser, in good faith (no actual or constructive notice — nothing was recorded and, on these facts, nobody was in possession), for valuable consideration, whose conveyance was first duly recorded. A's earlier deed is *"void as against"* B under § 1214.

**Change one fact.** Suppose A had moved in on 2 March and was living there when B inspected.

**Now B loses**, and not because of the recording dates. A's open possession is inquiry notice; B is charged with what an inquiry of the occupant would have revealed, which is A's deed. **B is no longer a purchaser "in good faith,"** and the first condition of § 1214 fails. The recording race is irrelevant because B cannot get past the good-faith requirement.

**Change a different fact.** Suppose the 14 March transfer was a gift to Owner's nephew, who recorded immediately.

**A prevails.** The nephew is subsequent and first to record and may well be in good faith, but he did not take *"for a valuable consideration."* § 1214 does not protect him.

**The lesson a licensee should carry from all three:** recording is decisive only when the other three conditions are met, and the two that fail most often in real life are **good faith, defeated by someone living in the house**, and **value, defeated by a family transfer.** Both are visible before closing to anyone who looks.

### 4.11 The disclosure article, and the waiver that is void

One more section belongs in a chapter about what passes with the transfer. **Civil Code § 1102** opens the residential transfer disclosure article:

> *"(a) Except as provided in Section 1102.2, this article applies to any transfer by sale, exchange, real property sales contract as defined in Section 2985, **lease with an option to purchase**, any other **option to purchase**, or **ground lease coupled with improvements** of any single-family residential property.*
>
> *(b) For purposes of this article, the definitions contained in Chapter 1 (commencing with Section 10000) of Part 1 of Division 4 of the Business and Professions Code shall apply.*
>
> *(c) **Any waiver of the requirements of this article is void as against public policy.**"*

**Subdivision (a) is broader than "sale."** A lease with an option, a bare option, and a ground lease coupled with improvements are all inside the article. A transaction structured to avoid a sale does not thereby avoid the disclosure obligation.

**Subdivision (b) is the cross-reference from Assignment 01 Section 5.8** — the Civil Code borrowing the Business and Professions Code's definitions. A reader of the disclosure article who does not know that is reading with a piece missing.

**Subdivision (c) is one short sentence and it settles a great deal.** *"Any waiver of the requirements of this article is void as against public policy."* A seller cannot contract out of it. A buyer cannot agree to give it up, however sophisticated they are and however much they are paid for the concession. **A licensee presented with an "as-is, no disclosures" arrangement should know that the words do not do what the parties think they do.**

### 4.12 Checkpoint

You should be able to explain what recording does and does not accomplish, define the three kinds of notice, and state the elements of a bona fide purchaser.

## 5. Title insurance and preliminary reports

### 5.1 What a preliminary report is

A **preliminary report** reports the condition of title as shown in the records and states **the terms on which the title insurer would be willing to issue a policy**, including the exceptions it would take.

Three things it is not:

- **Not a guarantee** that title is clear.
- **Not the policy.** It states terms; the policy is the contract.
- **Not a survey.** It addresses record title, not the physical property.

### 5.2 Exceptions, exclusions, requirements

Three categories a licensee must distinguish:

| Category | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Exception** | An item the policy **will not insure against**. The buyer bears that risk unless it is otherwise addressed |
| **Exclusion** | A category the policy form does not cover at all — governmental regulation such as zoning, eminent domain, matters created or agreed to by the insured |
| **Requirement** | Something the insurer needs before issuing — a reconveyance, a resolution, a release, evidence of authority |

**The requirements are the items with deadlines attached**, and the ones most likely to delay a closing.

### 5.3 What the policy does

Title insurance **indemnifies the insured against covered losses from title defects, within the policy's terms and excluding what it excepts.** It also typically obligates the insurer to defend covered claims.

Two features distinguish it from other insurance:

**It looks backward, not forward.** Other insurance covers future events; title insurance covers defects that already exist in the record or the chain, discovered later.

**The premium is generally paid once**, at closing, for coverage that continues.

### 5.4 Owner's and lender's policies

The **owner's policy** insures the buyer's interest. The **lender's policy** insures the lender's lien and its priority.

They insure **different interests**, which is why both commonly exist — and why a buyer who declines an owner's policy because "there's a title policy on the loan" has not economized on a duplicate. He has left his own interest uninsured.

**Extended coverage** may address matters a standard policy excepts, including some survey matters, typically requiring a survey or other evidence. Whether to obtain it is the buyer's decision, made informed.

### 5.5 What title insurance does not cover

The list is as important as the coverage:

- **Zoning and governmental regulation** — excluded.
- **Physical condition of the property.**
- **Boundary location**, under a standard policy — the survey exception.
- **Matters the insured created or agreed to.**
- **Matters known to the insured and not disclosed to the insurer.**
- **Anything the policy excepted.**

> A buyer asks whether the neighbor's fence is on the line and the agent says the title report "came back clean."
>
> **That answer is wrong.** The report addresses record title, and boundary location is exactly what the standard policy excepts. **Nothing in the report speaks to where the fence is.** A survey does.

### 5.6 Reading the report item by item

| Item | Correct handling |
|---|---|
| Utility easement along a boundary | Obtain the document — width, location, permitted use. Can prevent structures the buyer intends |
| Recorded CC&Rs | Obtain and review; bind the buyer regardless of zoning |
| Unreleased deed of trust the seller says was paid | A recorded **reconveyance** must be obtained |
| Mechanic's lien | Route to escrow and title; priority may relate back |
| Judgment against a similarly named person | Route to title; do not assume it is or is not the seller |
| Lis pendens | A serious cloud; route to counsel |
| A requirement for a corporate resolution or trustee evidence | The authority verification from Lesson 03, with a deadline |

**Three responses wrong in every case:** treating an item as routine because most properties have one; asking title to "remove" an exception; and predicting an easement's or restriction's effect on the buyer's plans.

### 5.7 The timing point

The preliminary report and the underlying documents belong at the **front** of the transaction queue, with association documents and hazard-area insurance quotes.

An exception discovered late — an easement across the building site, an unreleased lien requiring a document from a lender that no longer exists — takes time to resolve. A buyer who receives the report on day three has options; one who receives it on day twelve of a fourteen-day review has a formality.

### 5.9 What the two policies cover, side by side

Section 5.4 named owner's and lender's policies. The difference is not a matter of degree, and a buyer who declines an owner's policy on the ground that "the lender has one" has misunderstood what they were declining.

| | Owner's policy | Lender's policy |
|---|---|---|
| **Insured** | The buyer | The lender |
| **Amount** | The purchase price | The loan amount |
| **Duration** | As long as the insured holds an interest, and beyond for warranties of title | Until the loan is paid |
| **Behaviour over time** | Stays at the purchase price | **Declines as the loan amortises** |
| **On a claim** | Pays the owner | Pays the lender; the owner recovers nothing |

**The lender's policy does not protect the buyer at all.** If a title defect surfaces and there is no owner's policy, the lender is made whole and **the buyer bears the entire loss of their equity** — the down payment, every principal payment made, and the property.

**And an owner's policy is the cheapest insurance in the transaction on a per-dollar basis**, because it is issued once for a single premium and never renewed.

### 5.10 Reading Schedule B, and the four exception types

A preliminary report is not a policy. It is an **offer** to issue a policy on stated terms, and the terms are the exceptions in Schedule B. Four types appear, and they call for different responses.

**Monetary liens.** Deeds of trust, judgments, tax liens, mechanics liens. **These are cleared at closing** — paid from proceeds and released. Their presence is a payoff question, not a title question.

**Easements and rights of way.** Utility easements, access easements, drainage. **These do not clear.** They travel with the land, and the question is whether they affect the buyer's intended use. A utility easement across the back of a lot is ordinary; the same easement across the only buildable area is a different property from the one the buyer thinks they are buying.

**Covenants, conditions and restrictions.** Recorded restrictions on use, and in a common interest development the declaration. These do not clear either, and they are frequently the most consequential document in the file.

**Survey and boundary exceptions.** *"Any facts an accurate survey would disclose."* **This exception is the title company declining to insure the boundaries**, and it is standard on the basic policy. A buyer who wants the boundaries insured is buying extended coverage and, usually, providing a survey.

**The habit that catches problems:** read Schedule B against the buyer's stated plans. A buyer who intends to build a workshop, park a boat, run a home business, add a second unit, or fence the perimeter is a buyer for whom a specific exception may matter, and **the licensee is the only person who knows both the plan and the report.**

### 5.11 A worked exception

> A buyer intends to build a detached garage on the north side of the lot. Schedule B, item 7: *"An easement for ingress, egress and public utilities over the northerly 12 feet of said land, as granted to Southern California Edison Company by instrument recorded [date], in Book [x], Page [y] of Official Records."*

**Nothing about this clears at closing**, and no amount of money paid at escrow makes it go away.

**Three actions, in order.** Obtain the recorded instrument itself — the report gives its book and page, and **the report's one-line description is a summary, not the grant.** Compare its width and location against a plot plan or survey. Then determine whether the intended garage sits inside the twelve feet.

**If it does**, the buyer's plan is not feasible as conceived, and the options — relocating the structure, seeking a quitclaim or partial release from the utility, or withdrawing — are questions for the utility, the city and counsel respectively.

**The licensee's whole job here is timing.** Every step above is available in the first week of escrow and impossible in the last. **The failure mode is not missing the exception; it is reading it, understanding it, and forwarding it without connecting it to what the buyer said they wanted to do.**

### 5.12 Checkpoint

You should be able to explain what an exception means, distinguish it from an exclusion and a requirement, and state what title insurance does not cover.

## 6. Voluntary and involuntary transfer

### 6.1 The two categories

**Voluntary transfer** occurs with the owner's intent: sale, gift, dedication, and transfer at death by will.

**Involuntary transfer** occurs without it: intestate succession, escheat, eminent domain, foreclosure, execution sale, tax sale, adverse possession, and transfers by operation of natural forces.

### 6.2 Transfer at death

**Testate** — the decedent left a valid will. Real property passes as the will directs, generally through probate unless a mechanism avoids it.

**Intestate** — no valid will. Property passes by **intestate succession** to heirs in the order the law specifies. That order is statutory and its application is legal work.

**Escheat** — no will and no heirs who take. The property passes to the state.

**Probate** is the court-supervised process of administering a decedent's estate. Sales during probate may require court involvement and may follow particular procedures — which is why the personal representative's authority must be verified against the appointment, exactly as Lesson 03 required.

**Mechanisms that can avoid probate** — a trust holding title, survivorship forms of ownership, and certain statutory instruments — have different consequences and are estate planning matters for counsel.

### 6.3 Adverse possession

Title acquired through possession meeting statutory requirements. The elements are conventionally described as possession that is **open and notorious, hostile to the true owner's interest, actual, exclusive, and continuous** for the statutory period — and in California, **payment of taxes** is also required.

Two points for a licensee:

**The tax payment requirement is the practical filter.** It defeats most casual claims, because a person occupying without paying the taxes generally cannot satisfy it.

**It is a legal doctrine, never a licensee's conclusion.** A client who says "we've used that strip for twenty years, so it's ours" has stated a claim requiring legal analysis — and the neighbor who says "they've used it so long there's nothing I can do" has stated the mirror image. Both go to counsel.

Prescriptive **easements** arise from the same family of doctrines and produce a use right rather than ownership.

### 6.4 Transfer by natural forces

**Accretion** — the gradual addition of land by deposit — generally benefits the owner. **Erosion** is its gradual counterpart. **Avulsion** — a sudden change, such as a river changing course in a storm — generally does not shift boundaries. **Reliction** is land exposed by a permanent recession of water.

These matter on waterfront and watercourse property and are boundary questions for a surveyor and counsel.

### 6.5 Involuntary transfer after default

**Foreclosure** — judicial or nonjudicial — transfers title following default under a security instrument, and is the subject of Lesson 11.

**Execution sale** follows a judgment, transferring title by sheriff's deed.

**Tax sale** follows unpaid property taxes under the applicable process.

Each produces a deed type carrying its own limitations, and each appears in a chain of title as a signal that the buyer's counsel and title company should look closely.

### 6.6 Dedication

**Dedication** is a private owner devoting land to public use, accepted by the public body. It appears most often on subdivision maps, where streets and easements are offered for dedication as a condition of approval.

A licensee reading a recorded map should recognize dedicated areas as land the owner no longer controls — which occasionally surprises a buyer who measured the lot to the pavement.

### 6.7 What ties this lesson together

Every mechanism in this lesson answers one of three questions, and a licensee who keeps them separate will handle unfamiliar situations correctly:

1. **Did title actually transfer?** — the deed requirements, and delivery and acceptance.
2. **Who has notice, and whose interest prevails?** — recording and priority.
3. **What risk is insured, and what is not?** — the policy, its exceptions, and its exclusions.

A licensee who can place a problem into one of those three, and route it accordingly, is doing the job. A licensee who answers it is not.

### 6.8 A worked title problem

> **A licensee takes a listing. The preliminary report reveals:**
>
> - Title vested in a person who died two years ago.
> - A deed from that person to the current occupant, **signed and notarized but never recorded.**
> - A deed of trust recorded eighteen months ago, signed by the occupant.
> - A lien recorded against the deceased owner's name eleven months ago.
>
> **The occupant says: "My uncle gave me the house before he died. I have the deed."**
>
> **What each element means, applying this lesson.**
>
> **An unrecorded deed can still be effective between the parties.** Section 3 establishes that delivery and acceptance are what transfer title, not recording. **The occupant may well own the property.**
>
> **Recording is about notice to the world.** Section 4. **An unrecorded deed does not give constructive notice**, which means subsequent parties dealing with the record owner may take without knowledge of it — and the consequences depend on rules this course states and does not resolve on particular facts.
>
> **The eleven-month-old lien is the problem the unrecorded deed created.** It was recorded against a person the record still showed as the owner, after the deed was signed and before it was recorded. **Whether it attaches is exactly the question the recording system exists to answer**, and it is a legal question with real stakes.
>
> **The deed of trust signed by the occupant** was presumably underwritten on some showing of ownership, which raises its own questions about what the lender was given.
>
> **The delivery question is live.** Section 3.2's requirements — **was the deed delivered during the uncle's lifetime, with intent to pass title presently?** "He gave it to me" describes a belief. A deed found in a drawer after death is a different fact pattern from a deed handed over with intent, and the difference determines whether title passed at all or whether the property is part of the estate.
>
> **What the licensee does.**
>
> **He does not take the listing on the occupant's description.** Lesson 03's intake questions apply with force: how is title held, may I see the document, who must sign.
>
> **He routes it — to counsel and to the title company — before marketing.** **Every one of the questions above is legal**, and the title company will not insure until they are resolved.
>
> **He records what he was told and when**, and he does not repeat the occupant's characterization of ownership in any advertisement.
>
> **What he must not do.** Conclude that the unrecorded deed settles it, record the deed himself, tell the occupant the lien "won't matter because the deed is older," or market the property while the questions are open. **The fourth is the one that turns a solvable title problem into a transaction with a buyer whose deadlines are running.**
>
> **Why this lesson puts it here.** Sections 1 through 5 supply every concept required to see the issues — deed requirements, delivery, recording, notice, priority, and what a preliminary report is. **What they do not supply is the answer**, and knowing the difference is the professional skill.

### 6.10 Involuntary transfer, and the four routes a title can move without a signature

Section 6.3 introduced involuntary transfer. The routes are worth having distinctly, because each leaves a different mark in a title chain and a licensee will meet all four.

| Route | What happens | What appears in the chain |
|---|---|---|
| **Foreclosure** | The security instrument's power is exercised; the property is sold | A trustee's deed upon sale, or a sheriff's deed after judicial sale |
| **Eminent domain** | A public agency takes for public use, paying just compensation | A final order of condemnation, or a deed in lieu |
| **Adverse possession** | Long, open, hostile, exclusive possession under the statutory conditions, **including payment of taxes** | Nothing — until a quiet title judgment records it |
| **Escheat** | An owner dies with no heirs and no will | A state proceeding and its judgment |

**And two more that move title without a conveyance from the owner:**

**Death.** By will, or by intestate succession, or — where the vesting so provides — automatically to a surviving joint tenant under Civil Code § 683.

**Court order.** A dissolution judgment, a partition, a judgment of specific performance, a bankruptcy trustee's sale.

**The one worth pausing on is adverse possession**, because California adds a requirement that surprises people who learned the doctrine elsewhere: **the claimant must have paid the property taxes** for the statutory period. That requirement is why successful adverse possession claims against a well-run owner are rare — the taxes go to the record owner's address and get paid — and why the claims that do succeed tend to involve strips of land nobody was separately assessing.

### 6.11 Reading a chain of title for the things that matter

A licensee does not examine title. A licensee does read preliminary reports and does look at chains, and there are four patterns worth recognising.

**A conveyance for nominal consideration.** A deed reciting $10 or "love and affection" is usually a family transfer, and family transfers raise the questions Assignment 03 Section 4.8 describes: was it interspousal, was it a gift with reassessment consequences, and did everyone who needed to sign, sign.

**A quitclaim deed in the recent chain.** A quitclaim conveys whatever interest the grantor has, **with no warranty that they have any.** One appearing in a chain is not a defect; it is a question — what was it clearing up, and did it work?

**A deed out of a trust, or into one.** Trust transfers are ordinary. What matters is whether the person who signed had authority under the trust instrument, which is a title officer's question and a real one.

**A gap.** A conveyance from a grantor who does not appear to have acquired title in the chain. Usually an unrecorded intermediate deed, sometimes a name change, occasionally something worse. **This is exactly what Civil Code § 1214 is about**, and it is why the recording system exists.

**A worked chain.**

> 1998: Grant deed to **Robert and Elaine Chen, husband and wife as joint tenants.**
> 2014: **Quitclaim deed** from Elaine Chen to Robert Chen.
> 2019: Grant deed from **Robert Chen, a single man**, to the **Chen Family Trust**.
> 2026: The trustee lists the property. The trustee is Robert Chen's daughter.

**Four questions, each answerable and none by the licensee.**

**Did the 2014 quitclaim sever the joint tenancy and convey Elaine's interest?** A conveyance by one joint tenant generally severs, and a quitclaim conveys what the grantor had. The instrument presumably did both — **and whether it was part of a dissolution, and whether any dissolution order affected it, is not visible in a chain.**

**"A single man" in 2019 is a change of status that should be consistent** with the 2014 transfer. If Robert Chen had remarried before 2019, the community property questions in Assignment 03 Section 4.7 arise.

**Does the trust instrument authorise this trustee to sell?** The daughter's authority comes from the trust, and the title company will want to see it — a certification of trust at minimum.

**Is Robert Chen alive?** A successor trustee acting suggests a death or an incapacity, and if a death, whether the trust was funded correctly with this property matters.

**What the licensee does with all of that:** send the preliminary report and the chain to escrow and the title officer **at listing**, with the specific question *"can you confirm the trustee has authority to convey and that no further signatures are required?"* — and get the answer in writing before the property goes on the market. **Four weeks of marketing on a property that cannot convey is four weeks nobody gets back.**

### 6.12 Checkpoint

You should be able to distinguish voluntary from involuntary transfer, state the adverse possession elements including California's tax requirement, and place a title problem into one of the three questions.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **Deed** | The written instrument conveying an interest in real property. The gran**tor** gives; the gran**tee** takes. |
| **Deed requirements** | Writing, competent grantor, ascertainable grantee, adequate property description, granting language, grantor's signature, and **delivery and acceptance**. |
| **What a deed does not require** | Consideration for validity, the grantee's signature, recording, or notarization for validity — though acknowledgment is generally required to **record**. |
| **Granting clause** | The words showing present intent to convey. In California, **"grant"** carries statutory significance. |
| **Habendum clause** | Defines the extent of the estate conveyed. |
| **General warranty deed** | The broadest warranties, extending through prior ownership. Uncommon in California residential practice. |
| **Grant deed** | The standard California residential instrument, impliedly warranting that the grantor has not previously conveyed the same estate and that the estate is free of encumbrances **made or suffered by the grantor** — limited to the grantor's own ownership. |
| **Quitclaim deed** | Conveys whatever interest the grantor has, **warranting nothing**. Does not clear liens and cannot make title better than the grantor's. |
| **Trustee's deed** | Given following a trustee's sale under a deed of trust. |
| **Deed in lieu of foreclosure** | A borrower's voluntary conveyance to the lender. Does not automatically extinguish junior liens. |
| **Reconveyance** | The recorded document releasing a deed of trust when the debt is satisfied. **Payment does not clear the record; the document does.** |
| **Delivery** | The grantor's **present intent to pass title now**, manifested by conduct. Not primarily physical, and not accomplished by signing, notarizing, or recording. |
| **Failed testamentary delivery** | A deed handed over or held with instructions to take effect at death — generally ineffective, because the grantor retained control. |
| **Conditional delivery** | Deposit into escrow with instructions to deliver on conditions being met. One of the two elements making an arrangement an escrow. |
| **Acceptance** | Required of the grantee, generally presumed where beneficial, and refusable for real reasons — contamination liability, assessments exceeding value. |
| **Forged deed** | **Void**, conveying nothing regardless of how many transfers follow. |
| **Actual notice** | The person in fact knows. |
| **Constructive notice** | Knowledge the law charges a person with because a document was recorded. The system's engine. |
| **Inquiry notice** | Knowledge charged from facts that would prompt investigation — an occupant who is not the record owner, a worn path, rent paid to someone else. |
| **Bona fide purchaser** | One who takes **for value**, **in good faith**, and **without notice**. The party the recording system protects. |
| **First in time, first in right** | The general priority rule, measured by recording, subject to tax lien priority, mechanic's lien relation-back, subordination, and purchase-money priority. |
| **Chain of title** | The successive recorded conveyances tracing ownership. A break is a defect. |
| **Wild deed** | A recorded document outside the chain, which a searcher following the chain would not find — so recording it may not give constructive notice. |
| **Preliminary report** | A report of record title stating the terms on which a policy would issue. Not a guarantee, not the policy, not a survey. |
| **Exception** | An item the policy will not insure against. |
| **Exclusion** | A category the policy form does not cover at all. |
| **Requirement** | Something the insurer needs before issuing — the item with a deadline attached. |
| **Backward-looking coverage** | Title insurance covers defects already existing in the record or chain, discovered later, for a premium generally paid once. |
| **Survey exception** | Why a standard policy does not insure boundary location. |
| **Testate / intestate / escheat** | Death with a valid will; without one, passing by intestate succession; or to the state where there are no heirs. |
| **Adverse possession** | Title by possession that is open and notorious, hostile, actual, exclusive, and continuous for the statutory period — and in California, **with payment of taxes**. |
| **Accretion / erosion / avulsion / reliction** | Gradual deposit; gradual loss; sudden change, which generally does not shift boundaries; and land exposed by permanent recession of water. |
| **Dedication** | A private owner devoting land to public use, accepted by the public body. Common on subdivision maps. |

## Practice problems

### Problem 1

A deed recites consideration of "love and affection," is signed only by the grantor, was never
recorded, and was never notarized. A licensee tells the grantee it is "not a real deed."

**Assess each element.**

> **Solution.** **The licensee is wrong on all four, and right about one practical consequence he did
> not identify.**
>
> **Consideration is not required for validity.** A deed may be a gift. Consideration matters
> elsewhere — including whether someone qualifies as a **bona fide purchaser** under the recording
> system — but a deed reciting love and affection can be perfectly valid.
>
> **The grantee need not sign.** The grantor conveys; the grantee's acceptance is required and is
> generally presumed where the conveyance is beneficial.
>
> **Recording is not required for validity.** A deed transfers title between grantor and grantee on
> **delivery and acceptance**, recorded or not. Recording protects against **third parties**; it does
> not create the transfer.
>
> **Notarization is not required for validity** — but a deed must generally be acknowledged before a
> notary to be **recorded**. So this deed may be valid and **unrecordable**, which is the real problem
> the grantee will discover later, and the one the licensee missed.
>
> Whether it is effective is a legal conclusion in any event. Route it.

### Problem 2

A seller offers a quitclaim deed, saying it will "clear up" a recorded judgment lien and an unreleased
1998 deed of trust. The buyer's agent says a quitclaim is fine since the seller obviously owns the
property.

**Assess.**

> **Solution.** **A quitclaim conveys whatever interest the grantor has, warranting nothing** — and it
> does **not clear liens against the property.** The judgment lien and the unreleased deed of trust
> survive it untouched.
>
> **The 1998 deed of trust requires a recorded reconveyance.** Payment of the debt does not clear the
> record; the document does.
>
> **The agent's reasoning is backwards.** A quitclaim is risky precisely because it makes no
> representation about what the grantor owns — if the grantor owned nothing, the buyer receives
> nothing. In an arm's-length purchase the buyer normally receives a **grant deed**, which impliedly
> warrants that the grantor has not previously conveyed the same estate and that it is free of
> encumbrances **made or suffered by the grantor.**
>
> Note the limit that explains title insurance: those warranties reach only the grantor's own period of
> ownership. A grant deed warrants nothing about a defect created in 1974.
>
> **And a licensee must not advise a client to give or accept a quitclaim as a solution to a title
> problem.** Whether it resolves a particular cloud is legal, and quitclaims are used to accomplish
> frauds precisely because they warrant nothing.

### Problem 3

A father signs and notarizes a deed to his daughter, puts it in his safe, and tells her "this is
yours when I'm gone." He dies four years later. She finds the deed and records it.

**Did title transfer?**

> **Solution.** **Generally not — and recording it did not fix anything.**
>
> **A deed transfers title on delivery and acceptance.** Signing does not. Notarizing does not.
> Recording does not.
>
> **Delivery is not primarily physical; it is the grantor's present intent to pass title now.** "This is
> yours when I'm gone" is the opposite: he retained the deed and retained control, and manifested an
> intent to transfer at death. That is an attempted **testamentary** transfer, which generally fails
> because it was not made with the formalities a will requires.
>
> **Recording raises a presumption of delivery — and a presumption can be rebutted.** A deed recorded by
> a grantee who took it from a drawer without the grantor's present intent to convey did not become
> effective by being recorded.
>
> **What the licensee does:** recognize the pattern, stop, escalate to the broker, and route it to title
> and to counsel. Whether delivery was effective is a legal conclusion with enormous consequences — and
> this property may in fact pass through the father's estate instead.

### Problem 4

A buyer's title search is clean. On the property he notices a tenant in the guest house paying rent to
someone other than the seller, and a gravel path worn across the rear of the lot to the neighbor's
barn. The seller calls the tenant "a friend staying temporarily."

**What notice is in play, and what should happen?**

> **Solution.** **Inquiry notice — the kind that surprises buyers, because nothing was recorded.**
>
> **Constructive notice** is the system's engine: a recorded document is knowable by anyone who looks,
> so everyone is treated as knowing it. But **leases, occupancy agreements, unrecorded easements arising
> by implication or prescription, and side agreements are frequently not recorded** and are therefore
> invisible to a records search.
>
> **That gap is what inquiry notice covers.** An occupant who is not the record owner, rent paid to a
> third party, and a worn path to a neighbor's structure are textbook facts that would prompt a
> reasonable person to investigate — and a purchaser may be charged with **what the investigation would
> have revealed**, recorded or not.
>
> **This also threatens bona fide purchaser status**, which requires taking for value, in good faith,
> and **without notice** — actual, constructive, or inquiry.
>
> **What should happen:** investigate rather than accept the explanation. Ask for any written agreement
> with the occupant, route the path to a survey and to counsel, and do not treat "a friend staying
> temporarily" as a description of a possessory interest. **Physical inspection is a separate obligation
> from reading the title report**, precisely because of this gap.

### Problem 5

A buyer asks whether the neighbor's fence is on the line. His agent says the title report "came back
clean" and that the owner's policy will cover it if there is a problem. The buyer also declines an
owner's policy because the lender is getting one.

**Correct all three.**

> **Solution.** **"Clean" answers a question he did not ask.** A preliminary report and a policy address
> **record title** — what the records show about ownership and encumbrances. They are **not surveys**,
> and the standard policy **excepts** matters a survey would disclose. **Nothing in the report speaks to
> where the fence is.**
>
> **The policy will not cover it either.** Boundary location under a standard policy is the survey
> exception, and an **exception is an item the policy will not insure against.** Also outside coverage:
> zoning and governmental regulation, physical condition, matters the insured created or agreed to, and
> matters known to the insured and not disclosed.
>
> **Declining the owner's policy is not economizing on a duplicate.** The owner's policy insures the
> **buyer's interest**; the lender's insures the **lender's lien and its priority.** They insure
> different interests, which is why both exist — and declining leaves his own interest uninsured.
>
> **What to say:** the report doesn't address that, a survey does, here is how to get one before the
> contingency expires — and **extended coverage** may address some survey matters, typically requiring a
> survey or other evidence, which is his decision to make informed.

### Problem 6

A preliminary report shows: recorded CC&Rs; a utility easement along the rear line where the buyer
plans to build; an unreleased deed of trust from 2016; a judgment against a similarly named person;
a lis pendens; and a requirement for evidence of the trustee's authority. It arrives on day twelve of
a fourteen-day review.

**Handle each, and state what went wrong first.**

> **Solution.** **The timing failure came first.** The preliminary report and its underlying documents
> belong at the **front** of the queue, with association documents and hazard-area insurance quotes. An
> unreleased lien needing a document from a lender that may no longer exist, discovered on day twelve,
> leaves a formality where a review should have been.
>
> **CC&Rs:** obtain and review; they bind the buyer regardless of zoning.
>
> **The easement:** obtain the document — width, location, permitted use. It may prevent exactly what
> he is buying the lot to build. Do **not** call it routine, ask title to remove it, or predict its
> effect on his plans.
>
> **The 2016 deed of trust:** a recorded **reconveyance** must be obtained.
>
> **The similar-name judgment:** route to title. A judgment lien is general and reaches everything the
> debtor owns in the county — do not assume it is or is not the seller.
>
> **The lis pendens:** a serious cloud giving notice that pending litigation may affect anyone acquiring
> an interest. Route to counsel immediately.
>
> **The trustee evidence is a requirement, not an exception** — something the insurer needs before
> issuing, and therefore **the item with a deadline attached.** It is the Lesson 03 authority
> verification arriving through the title company.

### Problem 7

A client says her family has farmed a 40-foot strip beyond her fence line for twenty-two years, so it
is "obviously ours now." The adjoining owner's agent says there is nothing his client can do about it.
Neither family has ever paid taxes on the strip.

**Assess both positions.**

> **Solution.** **Both agents have stated legal conclusions, and both may be wrong — but the tax fact
> is decisive against the claim.**
>
> Adverse possession requires possession that is **open and notorious, hostile, actual, exclusive, and
> continuous** for the statutory period — **and in California, payment of taxes.** Nobody paid taxes on
> the strip.
>
> **The tax requirement is the practical filter.** It defeats most casual claims exactly like this one,
> because a person occupying without paying the taxes generally cannot satisfy it.
>
> **Neither agent may say so as a conclusion.** "It's obviously ours" and "there's nothing you can do"
> are the mirror images of the same error, and the elements are fact-intensive.
>
> Worth naming alongside it: **prescriptive easements** arise from the same family of doctrines and
> produce a **use right rather than ownership** — so a claim that fails as adverse possession may still
> raise a use question.
>
> **Both positions go to counsel**, and the boundary itself goes to a surveyor.

### Problem 8

Three chains of title cross a licensee's desk: one containing a trustee's deed from 2019; one where a
conveyance appears from a grantor who never appears as a grantee; and one where a subdivision map
shows a 15-foot strip along the frontage offered for dedication, which the buyer measured as part of
his lot.

**Identify the issue in each and place it in the lesson's three questions.**

> **Solution.** **The trustee's deed** signals a completed foreclosure. A buyer at a trustee's sale
> takes what the sale conveyed — which may have extinguished junior liens but not senior ones — and the
> property was sold without an ordinary transaction's protections. It is a **"who has notice and whose
> interest prevails"** question: the buyer's counsel and title company should read what the sale did to
> the surrounding interests.
>
> **The conveyance from a stranger to the chain is a break in the chain** — a defect requiring
> resolution. If the instrument sits entirely outside the chain it may be a **wild deed**, which a
> searcher following the chain would not find, so recording it may not have given constructive notice.
> That is a **"did title actually transfer"** question.
>
> **The dedicated strip is land the owner no longer controls.** Dedication is a private owner devoting
> land to public use, accepted by the public body, and it appears routinely on subdivision maps where
> streets and easements are offered as a condition of approval. The buyer measured to the pavement and
> bought less than he thinks. That is a **"what risk is insured and what is not"** question — and a
> description and survey question besides.
>
> **What transfers:** every problem in this lesson is one of three — did title transfer, who has notice
> and whose interest prevails, and what is insured. A licensee who can place a problem and route it is
> doing the job. One who answers it is not.

## Chapter summary

**A deed requires a writing, a competent grantor, an ascertainable grantee, an adequate description,
granting language, the grantor's signature, and delivery and acceptance.**

**It does not require consideration, the grantee's signature, recording, or notarization for
validity** — though acknowledgment is generally needed to record, so an unacknowledged deed may be
valid and unrecordable.

**Deeds differ by what the grantor warrants.** A California grant deed impliedly warrants no prior
conveyance of the same estate and no encumbrances made or suffered by the grantor — **limited to the
grantor's own ownership**, which is precisely why title insurance exists.

**A quitclaim warrants nothing, clears no liens, and cannot make title better than the grantor's.**
Never advise giving or accepting one as a title solution.

**Title transfers on delivery and acceptance.** Signing, notarizing, and recording do not transfer
title, and a deed held with instructions to take effect at death generally fails.

**Recording raises a presumption of delivery that can be rebutted**, and a forged deed is void no
matter how many transfers follow.

**Recording answers who has notice.** Constructive notice comes from the record; **inquiry notice**
comes from facts on the ground — and the unrecorded world of leases, occupancies, and implied easements
is exactly why physical inspection is a separate obligation.

**The recording system protects the bona fide purchaser** — for value, in good faith, without notice.

**First in time, first in right**, subject to tax lien priority, mechanic's lien relation-back,
subordination, and purchase-money priority. Priority decides who is paid and whose interest survives
foreclosure.

**A preliminary report states the terms on which a policy would issue.** Distinguish **exceptions**
(risks not insured), **exclusions** (categories outside the policy), and **requirements** (the items
with deadlines). Order it early.

**Title insurance looks backward**, covers defects already in the record or chain, and does not cover
zoning, physical condition, boundary location under a standard policy, or what the insured created or
concealed. Owner's and lender's policies insure different interests.

**Voluntary transfer occurs with intent; involuntary transfer does not** — intestate succession,
escheat, eminent domain, foreclosure, execution and tax sales, adverse possession, and natural forces.

**Adverse possession in California requires payment of taxes**, which defeats most casual claims — and
it is never a licensee's conclusion.

**Every problem in this lesson is one of three questions:** did title transfer, who has notice and
whose interest prevails, and what is insured. Place it, and route it.
