## 1. Creation of agency

### 1.1 What agency is

**Agency** is the relationship in which one person is authorized to act on behalf of another in dealings with third parties.

| Party | Role |
|---|---|
| **Principal** | The person represented. Also called the client |
| **Agent** | The person authorized to act for the principal |
| **Third party** | A participant who is not the agent's principal. In residential practice, the other side |

In California residential practice, **the broker is the agent** and the salesperson acts for the broker. Section 5 develops why that matters.

### 1.2 The three ways agency arises

| Method | How |
|---|---|
| **Express agreement** | A listing agreement, representation agreement, or other engagement — written or oral |
| **Conduct (implication)** | The licensee acted as an agent and the consumer reasonably relied |
| **Ratification** | The principal accepts the benefit of acts performed on their behalf, adopting them after the fact |

**Conduct is the route that surprises licensees**, and it is the reason the agency disclosure belongs early rather than at the offer.

> A homeowner and a licensee have talked for five weeks. He has advised her on pricing, recommended repairs, and told her what he thinks buyers will accept. Nothing is signed and he has not been paid. He now represents a buyer making an offer on her home and says no agency existed.
>
> **He may be wrong.** Agency can arise from conduct, and five weeks of advice she reasonably relied on looks like the conduct of an agent. Payment is not what creates duties, and the absence of a signed agreement does not prevent the relationship.
>
> The consequence is not merely that he cannot claim a commission. He may have owed her fiduciary duties throughout, and representing the buyer without disclosure and consent breaches duties actually owed.

### 1.3 What does not create agency

**Payment does not.** A buyer's agent compensated from the seller's side still represents the buyer. Who pays and who is represented are separate questions, and this must be explained to clients rather than assumed.

**A signed form alone does not, where conduct contradicts it.** A form naming one relationship while the licensee behaves as the other party's advisor creates exactly the contradiction that later gets litigated.

### 1.4 Scope of authority

Agency comes with a scope, and a licensee should know its limits.

**Actual authority** is what the principal has granted, expressly or by implication from what the engagement requires.

**Apparent authority** arises where the principal's conduct leads a third party reasonably to believe the agent has authority the agent does not in fact have. It can bind the principal, which is why loose statements about what a licensee may commit to are dangerous.

**What a residential licensee ordinarily may not do without specific authority:** accept an offer on the client's behalf, sign documents for the client, or bind the client to terms.

### 1.5 Checkpoint

You should be able to name the three ways agency arises and explain why the disclosure belongs before the relationship has been operating.

## 2. Fiduciary duties

### 2.1 What a fiduciary duty is

A **fiduciary** owes the highest duties the law imposes in a commercial relationship: to act in the principal's interest rather than the fiduciary's own.

The conventional list, with the memory device **OLD CAR**:

| Duty | What it requires |
|---|---|
| **O** — Obedience | Follow the principal's instructions — **lawful instructions only** |
| **L** — Loyalty | Place the principal's interests ahead of the agent's own |
| **D** — Disclosure | Disclose material facts known to the agent |
| **C** — Confidentiality | Protect the principal's confidential information |
| **A** — Accounting | Account for all funds and property belonging to the principal |
| **R** — Reasonable care and diligence | Perform with the skill of a competent licensee |

### 2.2 Obedience and its limit

Obedience extends to **lawful** instructions and no further. An instruction to conceal a material defect, to refuse to show property to a category of buyer, or to misrepresent competing offers is not an instruction to be followed.

> A principal instructs her agent not to show the property to a category of buyers. The agent believes refusing will cost her the listing.
>
> **Required:** refuse the instruction, explain that it would be unlawful discrimination, escalate to the broker, and address whether the listing can continue.
>
> **Three failures:** following it while documenting that it came from the client — documentation does not transfer responsibility for the act; following it informally by scheduling around those buyers — which achieves the same exclusion; and withdrawing quietly without explanation — which leaves the client free to repeat the instruction with the next licensee.

### 2.3 Loyalty

Loyalty means surfacing conflicts rather than silently resolving them in the agent's favor. The recurring breaches:

- Acting as an undisclosed principal — buying the client's property, or having an interest in the buyer.
- Accepting an undisclosed benefit from a third party.
- Steering the client toward an outcome that pays the agent more.
- Representing a competing interest without disclosure and consent.

### 2.4 Disclosure, and the confidentiality boundary

This is the most consequential line in agency practice, and it falls in the same place every time.

> **Confidentiality protects the principal's negotiating position. It never authorizes concealing a material fact about the property.**

| Protected as confidential | Must be disclosed |
|---|---|
| The seller's lowest acceptable price | A known material defect |
| The buyer's maximum | The condition of the property |
| The client's motivation and timeline | Facts affecting value or desirability |
| The client's financial position | The licensee's own observations |

> A seller's agent learns the foundation was underpinned after movement six years ago. The seller says the work was done properly, produces no records, and instructs the agent not to mention it because "it's fixed."
>
> **Disclosure controls.** The instruction cannot be followed. Confidentiality protects the negotiating position, not concealment of a material physical condition — and the adequacy of a repair with no records is exactly what a buyer would want to evaluate. Careful silence while avoiding an affirmative false statement is still concealment.

### 2.5 Reasonable care and diligence

The duty breached most often, and the one that requires no dishonesty at all.

A licensee who repeats an unverified square footage figure, misses a contingency deadline, fails to deliver a disclosure, or does not perform the visual inspection has breached this duty without lying to anyone. **Most claims against licensees are negligence claims.**

### 2.6 What is owed to non-clients

Non-clients are not unprotected. Every licensee owes **honesty, fair dealing, and disclosure of material facts** to everyone in the transaction — the other party, their agent, lenders, escrow, appraisers.

> **"I don't represent them" answers the wrong question.** It correctly identifies whose interests the licensee advances. It says nothing about whether the licensee may mislead them.

The distinction is whose interests you advance, not whether you owe anything.

### 2.7 Which duty was breached

Section 2.1 through 2.6 state the duties. **The skill a principles course is trying to build is recognizing which duty a given failure breaches**, because the answer determines the analysis, the remedy, and frequently whether the conduct is negligence or something worse.

| The conduct | The duty breached |
|---|---|
| Failing to present a competing offer to the seller | **Disclosure** |
| Telling a buyer's agent the lowest price the seller would take | **Confidentiality** |
| Buying the client's property through a relative without saying so | **Loyalty** |
| Missing a contingency deadline | **Reasonable care** |
| Following an instruction not to show the property to a category of buyers | **The obedience limit** — the instruction was unlawful, and following it is the licensee's own violation |
| Depositing an earnest money check into an operating account | **Accounting** |
| Recommending a vendor who pays a fee, without disclosing it | **Loyalty**, and the conflict framework |
| Preparing a pricing analysis from three comparables in a market with twenty | **Reasonable care** |
| Concealing a known material defect at the seller's instruction | **Honesty to non-clients**, and the obedience limit |
| Substituting the licensee's judgment on price for the seller's | **Obedience** — the decision was the principal's |
| Telling a buyer client "the seller will take 700" without knowing | **Reasonable care**, and it is a misrepresentation |
| Continuing to use a former client's confidential information | **Confidentiality**, which survives termination — Section 6.8 |

**Two observations worth carrying.**

**Reasonable care is the most frequently breached and the least dramatic.** Section 2.5 notes it can be breached without dishonesty, and most rows on any real list of claims are careless rather than dishonest. **A licensee who does careful work is protected from most of this table without ever facing an ethical dilemma.**

**Several rows breach two duties at once**, and the second is usually the more serious. Concealing a defect at the seller's instruction breaches honesty to the buyer **and** describes the licensee performing an unlawful instruction. Recommending an undisclosed paid referral breaches loyalty **and** engages a separate framework with its own consequences.

> **The diagnostic question that resolves most cases:** whose interest was subordinated, and to what? **If the answer is "the principal's, to the licensee's," it is loyalty. If it is "the principal's, to nobody's — it was just done badly," it is care.** If it is "a non-client's, to the principal's," it is the honesty duty in Section 2.6, and that duty does not bend to fiduciary loyalty.

### 2.8 Checkpoint

You should be able to name the six duties, state the confidentiality-disclosure boundary, and explain why a careless licensee can breach a fiduciary duty without any dishonesty.

## 3. Seller, buyer, and dual agency

### 3.1 The representation forms

| Form | The brokerage represents |
|---|---|
| **Seller's agent** | The seller only |
| **Buyer's agent** | The buyer only |
| **Dual agent** | Both parties in the same transaction |

A brokerage becomes a dual agent when it represents both sides — including where **two different salespersons** of the same brokerage represent the two parties, because the brokerage is the agent.

### 3.2 Dual agency requires informed written consent from both

Not one. Not implied. Not obtained afterwards.

**Informed** means each party understands what dual agency means and what it limits. **Written** means documented. **From both** means each party consents on their own behalf. And the consent must **precede** the brokerage acting on both sides rather than follow it.

### 3.3 What dual agency does and does not permit

The single most misunderstood point in agency, and the source of real breaches:

> **Consent to dual agency permits the brokerage to represent both parties. It does not convert either party's confidential negotiating position into shared information.**

> Both parties sign a dual agency consent. During negotiation the seller privately tells the broker the lowest number he would take. The buyer asks the broker directly what the seller will accept.
>
> **The broker may say nothing about the seller's floor** without the seller's permission.
>
> Two wrong answers worth naming: *both are clients so both are entitled to what the broker knows* — being a client entitles a party to loyalty about their own interests, not to the other client's confidences; and *disclosing both sides' limits keeps it balanced* — that does not cure the breach, it commits it twice.
>
> A vague answer conveying the same information is still a disclosure. The form of words does not matter.

### 3.4 What a dual agent can still do

Dual agency is not paralysis. The brokerage can still deliver information, prepare documents, coordinate the transaction, disclose material facts, and advise each party on matters not involving the other's confidences.

What it cannot do is advocate one party's negotiating position against the other's, or move confidential information across.

### 3.5 The structural tension

A dual agent owes loyalty to two parties whose interests on price are directly opposed. That tension is why consent must be informed — the party is agreeing to a relationship in which the agent cannot advocate for them the way a single-side agent would.

A licensee explaining dual agency badly — "it just means we handle both sides, it's more efficient" — has obtained a signature rather than consent.

### 3.6 Checkpoint

You should be able to state what dual agency requires, what consent does and does not permit, and why assigning a second salesperson does not avoid it.

## 4. California agency disclosure

### 4.1 The three steps

California's agency disclosure operates in three steps that occur at **three different times**:

| Step | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Disclose** | Provide the statutory disclosure describing the relationship options and the duties in each |
| **Elect** | The relationship is chosen |
| **Confirm** | The relationship actually elected is recorded, in or with the purchase agreement |

### 4.2 The timing

| Role | When the disclosure must be provided |
|---|---|
| **Seller's agent** | **Before the listing agreement is signed** |
| **Buyer's agent** | **As soon as practicable before the buyer signs an offer** |
| **Agent presenting an offer to the seller** | **Before or at the time the offer is presented** |

**Every one of these is the same principle: the disclosure precedes the decision it informs.**

A student who holds that principle can resolve unfamiliar timing questions without memorizing a table. Apply it and the answers fall out: the seller should know whose interests the presenting agent serves **while evaluating the offer**, not afterward.

### 4.3 Why late delivery is not cured by delivery

> A buyer's agent presents an offer. The agency disclosure reaches the listing side the following week, after acceptance, and is signed and dated then.
>
> **The file now contains a signed disclosure and is still defective.** The document exists; the protection it was meant to give does not, because the seller made the decision without it.

This is the shape of every timing defect in the course. The remedy for a late disclosure is not a later signature.

### 4.4 The confirmation

The agency confirmation records which relationship was actually elected. Two failure modes appear in file audits:

**A confirmation contradicting the earlier disclosure** — naming the brokerage as buyer's agent while the listing file shows seller's agent, with no dual agency consent anywhere. That is not a documentation problem. It describes the brokerage acting on both sides without either party's consent, and it is the most serious finding in a typical residential audit.

**A confirmation left blank or unchecked**, which leaves the file unable to show what relationship existed.

### 4.5 The disclosure is not the agreement

A recurring confusion: the agency disclosure describes the relationship options and the duties; the listing or representation agreement creates the engagement and its terms. They are different documents doing different work, and providing one does not accomplish the other.

**The agency disclosure is required regardless of whether a representation agreement is signed.** A licensee who requires a signed representation agreement before showing property is stating a brokerage policy, which is legitimate — but the disclosure obligation does not depend on it.

### 4.6 The dates, and what they prove

The three steps in Section 4.1 exist as a sequence, and **their dates are what a file audit examines** — not their presence.

**The chain, with what each date has to precede:**

| Step | Its date must precede |
|---|---|
| **Disclosure to the seller** | The listing agreement. The seller must know what the relationship is before he commits to it. |
| **Disclosure to the buyer** | The advice. Section 4.2's timing runs from the outset of the working relationship, not from the offer. |
| **Disclosure to the other party** | The presentation of the offer. |
| **Election** | Recorded in the agreement itself. |
| **Consent to dual agency**, where applicable | **The brokerage acting on both sides.** Consent dated after the conduct is a record of the problem. |
| **Confirmation** | Nothing — it is the last link, and its job is to be **consistent with the four above.** |

**Three defects a file audit finds, in descending order of seriousness:**

**1. A confirmation that contradicts the file.** A purchase agreement confirming the brokerage as buyer's agent while the listing file shows it as seller's agent, with no consent anywhere, **describes an undisclosed dual agency** — a breach of duties actually owed rather than a documentation failure.

**2. A disclosure dated after the agreement.** The document exists and the protection it was designed to give never operated.

**3. A signature with no evidence of delivery.** Section 4.3 establishes that late delivery is not cured by delivery. **A file that cannot show when a disclosure reached the party cannot show that it was timely**, and the burden of that ambiguity does not fall on the consumer.

**Why the buyer side fails more often than the listing side.** A listing agreement forces the paperwork; **nothing forces it on the buyer side.** A licensee can work with a buyer for six weeks, give advice throughout, and produce a disclosure on the day the offer is written — and the file will contain a properly executed document that proves nothing about the six weeks.

> **The one habit that closes all three defects:** date everything at the moment it happens, evidence delivery rather than relying on signature, and **read the chain forward as a sequence** rather than checking off documents as a set. If the dates do not run in order, something happened out of order — and that is worth knowing while the transaction can still respond to it.

### 4.7 Checkpoint

You should be able to state when each disclosure is due, explain why a signature after the decision does not cure lateness, and identify what an audit finding of a contradictory confirmation means.

## 5. Broker supervision and salesperson authority

### 5.1 The brokerage is the agent

Stated in Lesson 01 and applied here:

> **The salesperson's client is the broker's client. The listing is the brokerage's contract. The salesperson's compensation comes from the broker.**

What follows:

- **Cancellation of a listing is a broker-level decision** governed by the agreement, not the individual salesperson's to grant.
- **The transaction file belongs to the brokerage**, whoever created the documents.
- **A second salesperson on the other side does not avoid dual agency**, because the brokerage is the agent.
- **A salesperson may not contract in her own name** for licensed activity.

### 5.2 The supervisory duty

The responsible broker must exercise reasonable supervision. Discharged by a system:

1. **Written policies** covering the conduct required.
2. **File review**, close enough in time to catch problems before they mature.
3. **Advertising review** before publication.
4. **Trust fund controls**, including reconciliation.
5. **Training**, particularly for new licensees.
6. **An escalation path** a salesperson can actually use.

**A policy nobody follows is not supervision**, and it is not a defense for anyone — not for the broker who wrote it and not for the salesperson who departed from it.

**Independent contractor status does not reduce any of this.** It characterizes the tax and compensation relationship and answers a different question.

### 5.3 Supervision from the receiving end

Licensees encounter supervision failures from below, and the correct responses are worth stating:

| Situation | Response |
|---|---|
| A manager tells you to conceal an error | **Escalate to the responsible broker.** The instruction does not protect you |
| Office practice deviates from the written policy | Raise it; a policy nobody follows protects no one |
| You are asked to handle work beyond your competence with no support | Say so in writing and ask for supervision or reassignment |

**Following an improper instruction and documenting that it came from someone else does not transfer responsibility for the act. It documents that you knew.**

### 5.4 Delegation and unlicensed assistants

Unlicensed assistants may perform administrative work. They may not perform licensed activity — soliciting, negotiating, showing property, or discussing terms — and they may not be compensated for referrals.

The boundary is not the job title. It is the activity.

### 5.5 Vicarious responsibility

The broker answers for the salesperson's conduct in the course of the agency, which is why the supervisory system exists and why brokerages care about advertising approval, file review, and policy compliance in ways that can feel bureaucratic to a new licensee.

The principal may also bear responsibility for the agent's conduct within the scope of the agency, which is one reason a seller cannot instruct an agent into unlawful conduct and stand clear of it.

### 5.6 Checkpoint

You should be able to explain why a listing is not the salesperson's to cancel, describe what real supervision consists of, and state the correct response to an improper instruction from a manager.

## 6. Compensation, conflicts, and termination

### 6.1 Compensation is negotiable

**Brokerage compensation is set independently by each brokerage and negotiated with the client.** It is not standard, not customary, and not set by any association or listing service.

Describing a rate as standard is both factually wrong and an antitrust exposure. The correct sentence:

> "Compensation is negotiable between you and our brokerage. There is no standard rate, and no rate set by any association or listing service. Here is what our brokerage charges and what it covers."

### 6.2 Source of payment does not determine representation

Restated because clients and licensees both get it wrong: **a buyer's agent compensated from the seller's side still represents the buyer.** The arrangement must be explained rather than assumed, along with what happens if the seller's side offers less than the agreed compensation or offers none.

### 6.3 Conflicts of interest

Any interest that could reasonably influence the licensee's advice:

- A referral fee or thing of value from a service provider.
- An ownership or financial interest — including a relative's — in a recommended provider.
- A bonus contingent on a sale or a price threshold.
- The licensee's own interest, or a relative's, in a property.
- Representation of another party with a competing interest.
- A personal relationship a client would want to know about.

### 6.4 Disclosure must arrive in time to matter

> **Disclose before the client relies on the advice the conflict affects.**

| Inadequate approach | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| "The client is free to choose someone else" | The client cannot weigh a recommendation without knowing what the recommender receives |
| "I'll disclose if they ask" | The client does not know to ask |
| "I won't let it influence me" | A private intention is not something the client can evaluate |
| "I'll disclose after acceptance" | Information after the decision has no value |
| "The fee comes from the seller, not the buyer's pocket" | Who pays does not change that the advice carries an interest |

### 6.5 The referral fee prohibition is different

A conflict is ordinarily **managed by disclosure**. One category is not:

> **A licensee may not give or accept a fee, kickback, or thing of value for the referral of settlement service business, and may not split a fee where no services were performed. Disclosure does not cure it.**

This reaches arrangements that feel like ordinary business courtesy — an escrow company paying per file, a lender covering marketing costs in exchange for referrals, a warranty company paying per policy.

Where the brokerage has an **ownership interest** in a provider it refers to, that is an **affiliated business arrangement** carrying its own disclosure and conditions.

### 6.6 The licensee as principal

Where a licensee buys or sells for their own account, or for a relative or an entity they have an interest in, the licensee's status must be disclosed. A licensee purchasing from a client, or holding an interest in a buyer of a client's property, occupies the highest-risk position in the business, and it requires disclosure, the client's informed consent, and the broker's involvement.

### 6.7 Termination of agency

Agency ends by:

| Method | How |
|---|---|
| **Completion** | The purpose is accomplished |
| **Expiration** | The stated term ends |
| **Mutual agreement** | Both parties agree |
| **Revocation** | The principal ends it |
| **Renunciation** | The agent ends it |
| **Operation of law** | Death or incapacity of a party, destruction of the property, or similar events |

**The power to terminate and the right to terminate without consequence are different.** A principal generally has the power to revoke, and exercising it may still breach the agreement and expose the principal to a claim. A licensee who tells a seller "you can cancel any time" has described the power and omitted the consequence — and has given a legal opinion about the agreement's terms.

### 6.8 What survives termination

**Confidentiality generally survives.** A licensee may not use or disclose a former principal's confidential information afterwards — including for a new client, and including after the property is relisted elsewhere.

> A listing expires unsold. Six months later the same licensee represents a buyer interested in the property, now listed with another brokerage. He knows the former seller's lowest acceptable price. **He may not use it.**
>
> A duty that evaporated when the engagement ended would not be worth having. This is why the obligation is described as surviving rather than lapsing.

**Compensation provisions may also survive**, including a protection clause applying to buyers identified during the term.

### 6.9 A worked agency audit

> **A brokerage reviews one closed file.** It contains:
>
> - **(a)** A listing agreement dated March 4. An agency disclosure signed by the seller dated March 7.
> - **(b)** A buyer working with the same brokerage since February, with no disclosure in the buyer file until April 19 — the date of the purchase agreement.
> - **(c)** A purchase agreement confirming the brokerage as **both** the listing and the selling brokerage.
> - **(d)** **No dual agency consent from either party.**
> - **(e)** An email dated April 12 from the buyer's salesperson to the buyer: "I talked to our listing side — I think they'd take 690."
> - **(f)** A referral fee received from a home warranty company, disclosed nowhere in the file.
> - **(g)** A commission disbursement showing the brokerage paid from both sides.
>
> **The findings, ranked.**
>
> **1. (c) and (d) together: an undisclosed dual agency.** Section 3.2 requires informed written consent from **both** parties, and there is none. **This is a breach of duties actually owed rather than a documentation failure**, and it is the finding that changes the character of the whole file.
>
> **2. (e) The confidential information moved between sides.** Section 3.3 states what dual agency does not permit, and this is the clearest possible violation of it: **the seller's negotiating position, communicated by the brokerage to the party negotiating against the seller.** It also proves item 1 was operating in fact rather than only on paper.
>
> **3. (f) The undisclosed referral fee.** Section 6.3 and Section 6.4. **Money received in connection with a recommendation the client relied on, with no disclosure at any point.** Unlike the timing failures below, this involves value moving without the client's knowledge.
>
> **4. (b) The disclosure that arrived at the offer.** Ten weeks of a working relationship, and the disclosure is dated the day of the purchase agreement. Section 4.3: **late delivery is not cured by delivery.** The document exists; nothing it was designed to accomplish occurred.
>
> **5. (a) The disclosure after the listing agreement.** Three days late, and the seller committed before being informed.
>
> **6. (g) The two-sided payment.** Section 6.2 establishes that source of payment does not determine representation, so **this is not itself a violation.** It is listed because it is the item an examiner uses to find item 1 — the disbursement is what makes the dual agency visible in thirty seconds.
>
> **What the brokerage does.** Records every finding with its date; preserves the file including item (e); escalates to counsel, because items 1, 2, and 3 are not internal training matters; determines what obligation exists to the parties, which is a legal question; and **changes the procedures that produced items (a), (b), and (d)** — because a brokerage whose buyer files have no disclosure step and whose system does not detect that both sides are its own will produce this file again.
>
> **What the brokerage does not do.** Produce a consent now, re-date item (b), or add a referral disclosure to the file today. **A documented gap is a compliance problem. A manufactured document is a different proceeding**, and every one of these findings is survivable in a way that fabrication is not.

### 6.10 The agency questions a licensee answers, and the ones he does not

This lesson has drawn several boundaries. Collecting them makes the working rule visible.

**What a licensee explains, and should be able to explain well:**

- **What the three disclosure steps are and when each happens.** Section 4.1 and Section 4.2.
- **What representation means** — that the brokerage owes the client fiduciary duties and owes everyone honesty, fair dealing, and disclosure of material facts.
- **That the brokerage, not the individual, is the agent.** Section 5.1, which surprises consumers and licensees alike.
- **That who pays does not determine who is represented.** Section 6.2, and consumers regularly assume the opposite.
- **What dual agency is, what it requires, and what it costs the parties** — specifically that no one will advocate against the other side on price. Sections 3.2 through 3.5.
- **That compensation is negotiable.** Section 6.1.
- **What conflicts exist in this transaction**, disclosed before the client acts. Section 6.4.

**What routes to counsel:**

- Whether a particular agency relationship arose on particular facts.
- The legal effect of a defective or late disclosure.
- Whether a duty was breached and what follows.
- What a representation agreement's provisions mean for the client's obligations.
- Whether the client can terminate, and what termination costs. Section 6.7 describes the mechanisms; **the application is legal.**

**What routes to the broker:**

- Any dual agency, at the moment it becomes foreseeable rather than at the offer.
- Any request to release a client from an agreement.
- Any instruction the licensee believes is unlawful. Section 2.2's limit.
- Any conduct by another licensee that engages a duty — including confidential information volunteered by the other side.
- Any compensation arrangement that varies from the brokerage's standard.

> **The organizing principle across all three lists.** A licensee explains **how the framework works**; counsel says **what it means for these facts**; the broker decides **what the brokerage does.** Almost every agency question a consumer asks fits one of those three, and a licensee who can sort them in the moment will handle correctly a great many situations he has never seen.

**And the one question that appears in all three lists at once**, which is why it is the hardest: *"Whose side are you on?"* **The explanation is the licensee's, the legal characterization is counsel's, and if the honest answer is uncomfortable, that discomfort is information the broker needs before the transaction goes further.**

### 6.11 Checkpoint

You should be able to state the negotiability sentence correctly, explain why the referral fee prohibition is not cured by disclosure, and say what survives the end of an agency.

| **The confidentiality boundary** | Confidentiality protects the **negotiating position**. It never authorizes concealing a material fact about the property. |
| **Reasonable care and diligence** | The duty breached most often, and breachable by carelessness alone with no dishonesty. |
| **What is owed to everyone** | Honesty, fair dealing, and disclosure of material facts. "I don't represent them" answers a different question. |
| **Dual agency** | One brokerage representing both parties — **including through two different salespersons**, because the brokerage is the agent. |
| **Informed written consent** | What dual agency requires from **both** parties, understanding what it means and limits, in writing, **before** the brokerage acts on both sides. |
| **What consent permits** | Representation of both parties. **Not** the movement of either party's confidential position to the other, and a vague answer conveying it is still a disclosure. |
| **Disclose, elect, confirm** | California's three-step agency disclosure, occurring at three different times. |
| **The timing principle** | The disclosure precedes the decision it informs — before the listing is signed, before the buyer signs an offer, and before or when the offer is presented. |
| **Agency confirmation** | The record of the relationship actually elected. A confirmation contradicting the earlier disclosure with no dual agency consent is the most serious ordinary audit finding. |
| **Reasonable supervision** | An affirmative duty discharged by a system: policies, file review, advertising review, trust fund controls, training, and a usable escalation path. |
| **Documenting an improper instruction** | Does **not** transfer responsibility for the act. It documents that you knew. |
| **Negotiable compensation** | Set independently by each brokerage and negotiated with the client. Never standard, customary, or set by an association or listing service. |
| **Source of payment** | Does not determine representation. A buyer's agent paid from the seller's side still represents the buyer. |
| **Conflict of interest** | Any interest that could reasonably influence the licensee's advice. Managed by disclosure that arrives **before** the client relies on the advice. |
| **Referral fee prohibition** | No fee, kickback, or thing of value for referring settlement service business, and no fee split where no services were performed. **Disclosure does not cure it.** |
| **Affiliated business arrangement** | Where the brokerage holds an ownership interest in a provider it refers to. Carries its own disclosure and conditions. |
| **Power versus right to terminate** | A principal generally has the power to revoke; exercising it may still breach the agreement. |
| **Survival of confidentiality** | Confidentiality generally survives the end of the agency — including for a new client and after the property is relisted. |

## Practice problems

### Problem 1

A homeowner and a licensee have talked for five weeks. He advised her on pricing, recommended
repairs, and told her what buyers would accept. Nothing is signed and he has not been paid. He now
represents a buyer offering on her home and says no agency existed with her.

**Is he right, and what is the consequence?**

> **Solution.** **He may well be wrong.** Agency can arise **from conduct**, and five weeks of advice
> she reasonably relied on looks like the conduct of an agent. **Payment is not what creates duties**,
> and the absence of a signed agreement does not prevent the relationship.
>
> **The consequence is not merely that he cannot claim a commission.** He may have owed her fiduciary
> duties throughout, and representing the buyer without disclosure and consent breaches duties actually
> owed.
>
> **What transfers:** this is precisely why the agency disclosure belongs early rather than at the
> offer. The relationship may already exist while the consumer is unaware of it — and the licensee
> frequently is too.

### Problem 2

A seller's agent learns the foundation was underpinned after movement six years ago. The seller says
the work was done properly, produces no records, and instructs the agent not to mention it because
"it's fixed." Separately, the seller tells her the lowest price he will accept, and the buyer's agent
asks her directly what the seller will take.

**Which instruction binds her, and what may she say about the price?**

> **Solution.** **The two facts fall on opposite sides of the same line, and the line is always in the
> same place.**
>
> **The foundation must be disclosed.** Confidentiality protects the seller's **negotiating position**,
> not concealment of a material physical condition — and the adequacy of a repair with no records is
> exactly what a buyer would want to evaluate. Obedience extends to **lawful** instructions only, so
> this one cannot be followed. She tells the seller it must be disclosed and why, involves the broker,
> and does not follow the instruction. Careful silence while avoiding an affirmative false statement is
> still concealment.
>
> **The lowest price is confidential** and stays confidential. She may say nothing about it without the
> seller's permission.
>
> **What transfers:** the confidentiality-disclosure boundary is the most consequential line in agency
> practice, and it resolves the majority of agency questions.

### Problem 3

A brokerage lists a home. A buyer the same brokerage has been working with wants to offer. Both parties
sign a dual agency consent. During negotiation the seller privately tells the broker his floor. The
buyer asks directly what the seller will accept. The broker considers three answers: telling him,
since both are clients; disclosing both sides' limits to keep it balanced; and giving a vague answer
that gets the point across.

**Assess each.**

> **Solution.** **All three breach the duty**, and the third is the one licensees think is safe.
>
> **Consent to dual agency permits the brokerage to represent both parties. It does not convert either
> party's confidential negotiating position into shared information.**
>
> *Both are clients so both are entitled to what I know* — being a client entitles a party to loyalty
> about their **own** interests, not to the other client's confidences.
>
> *Disclosing both sides' limits keeps it balanced* — that does not cure the breach, it commits it
> twice.
>
> *A vague answer* conveying the same information **is still a disclosure.** The form of words does not
> matter.
>
> **What the broker may say:** nothing about the seller's floor without the seller's permission. Dual
> agency is not paralysis — the brokerage can still deliver information, prepare documents, coordinate,
> disclose material facts, and advise each party on matters not involving the other's confidences. What
> it cannot do is advocate one negotiating position against the other or move confidences across.

### Problem 4

A buyer's agent presents an offer. The agency disclosure reaches the listing side the following week,
after acceptance, and is signed and dated then. The file also contains a confirmation naming the
brokerage as buyer's agent, while the listing file shows it as seller's agent, and there is no dual
agency consent anywhere.

**Rank the two findings and state the correct response.**

> **Solution.** **The contradiction is the more serious, and it is different in kind.** It describes the
> brokerage **acting on both sides without either party's informed written consent** — a breach of
> duties actually owed, not a documentation failure. In an ordinary residential file audit this is the
> most serious finding available.
>
> **The late disclosure is second.** It must be provided **before or at the time the offer is
> presented**, so the seller knows whose interests the presenting agent serves **while evaluating it**.
> The file now contains a signed disclosure and is still defective: the document exists, the protection
> it was meant to give does not, because the seller decided without it. **A later signature does not
> cure lateness.**
>
> **The correct response:** record both findings, escalate to the broker, and obtain anything that
> legitimately exists. **What must not happen** is creating the consent that was never obtained or
> re-dating the disclosure. A documented gap is survivable; a manufactured document is falsification.

### Problem 5

A seller wants to cancel her listing two weeks in. The salesperson who took it says he is willing to
release her. Separately, the brokerage's manager tells him to leave a square footage error
uncorrected because escrow closes in four days, and an unlicensed assistant has been calling
expired listings to solicit business, paid $300 per listing obtained.

**Assess all three.**

> **Solution.** **The listing is not his to release.** It is the **brokerage's contract** with the
> seller, and cancellation is a broker-level decision governed by the agreement's terms. The same
> principle determines who owns the file and why a second salesperson would not cure dual agency.
>
> **The manager's instruction must be escalated above him.** A licensee told to conceal a known error
> escalates to the **responsible broker** — the instruction does not protect her, and following an
> improper instruction while documenting that it came from someone else **does not transfer
> responsibility for the act. It documents that she knew.**
>
> **The assistant is performing licensed activity and being compensated for it.** Soliciting listings
> is licensed activity, and the boundary is **the activity, not the job title.** Unlicensed assistants
> may perform administrative work; they may not solicit, negotiate, show property, discuss terms, or be
> compensated for referrals.
>
> **What ties them together:** reasonable supervision is a system — policies, file review, advertising
> review, trust fund controls, training, and a usable escalation path. Two of these three failures are
> supervision failures at the manager level.

### Problem 6

A seller asks why the listing states a negotiated compensation figure rather than "the standard rate."
The licensee's brokerage also receives $300 per file from its preferred escrow company, and the
licensee's brother owns the home warranty provider she recommends. She plans to mention both after
the offer is accepted, noting that clients are always free to use someone else.

**Answer the seller and assess the two arrangements.**

> **Solution.** **The answer to the seller:** *"Compensation is negotiable between you and our
> brokerage. There is no standard rate, and no rate set by any association or listing service. Here is
> what our brokerage charges and what it covers."* Describing a rate as standard or customary is both
> factually wrong and an antitrust exposure.
>
> **The $300 per file is prohibited, not merely undisclosed.** A licensee may not give or accept a fee,
> kickback, or thing of value for referring settlement service business, or split a fee where no
> services were performed — and **disclosure does not cure it.** This is what makes it different from
> an ordinary conflict, and its resemblance to routine business courtesy is exactly why it catches
> people.
>
> **The brother's company is a conflict requiring disclosure before the client relies on the advice it
> affects.** Both of her justifications fail: "free to choose someone else" ignores that a client cannot
> weigh a recommendation without knowing what the recommender receives, and disclosure after acceptance
> arrives after the decision it should have informed.

### Problem 7

A listing expires unsold. Six months later the same licensee represents a buyer interested in the
property, now listed with another brokerage. He knows the former seller's lowest acceptable price and
that she needed to close by a certain date.

**May he use either fact?**

> **Solution.** **No, to both.** **Confidentiality generally survives the end of the agency.** He may
> not use or disclose a former principal's confidential information afterwards — including for a new
> client, and including after the property is relisted elsewhere.
>
> Her floor and her timeline are both negotiating-position information, protected for the same reason.
>
> **Why the rule takes this shape:** a duty that evaporated when the engagement ended would not be worth
> having. That is why the obligation is described as **surviving** rather than lapsing.
>
> Worth noting alongside it: **compensation provisions may also survive**, including a protection clause
> applying to buyers identified to the seller during the term.

### Problem 8

A seller under an exclusive listing tells her agent she is unhappy and wants out. The agent says
"you can cancel any time — just let me know." A week later the brokerage sends her an invoice for
marketing costs and asserts a compensation claim.

**What was wrong with the agent's statement?**

> **Solution.** **He conflated the power to terminate with the right to terminate without
> consequence.**
>
> A principal generally has the **power** to revoke an agency. Exercising it may nonetheless **breach
> the agreement** and expose the principal to a claim — which is exactly what happened here.
>
> **And the statement was a legal opinion about the agreement's terms**, given confidently by someone
> not entitled to give it, on which she acted.
>
> **What he should have done:** state that the decision is a broker-level one governed by the
> agreement's cancellation and compensation terms, escalate to the broker, and — a useful first step —
> find out what she actually wants. "I want to cancel" often means "nothing is happening and no one is
> telling me why," which is a communication problem with a different fix.
>
> Agency ends by completion, expiration, mutual agreement, revocation, renunciation, or operation of
> law. **None of those routes automatically extinguishes what the agreement says about compensation.**

## Chapter summary

**Agency arises by express agreement, by conduct, or by ratification.** Conduct is the route that
surprises licensees, and it is why the disclosure belongs early.

**Payment does not create or determine agency.** A buyer's agent paid from the seller's side still
represents the buyer.

**Six fiduciary duties — OLD CAR — run to the principal.** Obedience covers lawful instructions only,
and reasonable care is the duty most often breached, by carelessness alone.

**Confidentiality protects the negotiating position and never authorizes concealing a material fact.**
That single boundary resolves most agency questions.

**Honesty, fair dealing, and disclosure of material facts run to everyone.** "I don't represent them"
answers a different question.

**A brokerage is a dual agent when it represents both sides**, including through two salespersons, and
it requires informed written consent from both, **before** it acts on both sides.

**Consent permits representation, not disclosure.** Neither party's confidences cross, disclosing both
does not balance it, and a vague answer conveying the information is still a disclosure.

**Disclose, elect, confirm — at three different times**, each preceding the decision it informs. A
signature after the decision does not cure a late disclosure, and a confirmation contradicting the
disclosure with no consent is the most serious ordinary audit finding.

**The brokerage is the agent and the party.** The listing is not the salesperson's to cancel, the file
is the brokerage's, and a salesperson may not contract in her own name.

**Supervision is a system, not a manual**, and independent contractor status does not reduce it.
Documenting an improper instruction does not transfer responsibility — it documents that you knew.

**Compensation is negotiable and never standard.** Say the sentence correctly.

**Conflicts are managed by disclosure that arrives before reliance** — with one exception: paying or
accepting anything of value for a settlement service referral is prohibited outright, and disclosure
does not cure it.

**Agency ends six ways, and the power to terminate is not the right to do so without consequence.**
Confidentiality, and often compensation provisions, survive the ending.
