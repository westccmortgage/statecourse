## 1. Federal protected classes

### 1.1 The framework

Federal fair housing law prohibits discrimination in the sale, rental, and financing of housing, and in the provision of related services, on the basis of specified protected characteristics.

**The federal protected classes:**

| Class |
|---|
| **Race** |
| **Color** |
| **Religion** |
| **Sex** |
| **National origin** |
| **Familial status** |
| **Disability (handicap)** |

The first four and national origin came earlier; **familial status and disability were added later**, which is why some older material lists five.

### 1.2 The statutes to know by name

**The Civil Rights Act of 1866** prohibits racial discrimination in property transactions. Its significance is its reach: it is **not subject to the exemptions** that apply under later fair housing law, and it applies to all property.

**The Fair Housing Act** — Title VIII of the Civil Rights Act of 1968, amended in 1988 — is the principal federal housing discrimination statute, establishing the seven classes above and the enforcement framework.

**The Americans with Disabilities Act** addresses public accommodations and commercial facilities. It reaches a brokerage's own office as a place of public accommodation; it is not the statute governing accessibility in a private dwelling, which is the Fair Housing Act's domain.

**The Equal Credit Opportunity Act** governs discrimination in credit, addressed in Section 5.

### 1.3 What familial status protects

**Familial status** protects households with children under 18, persons who are pregnant, and persons in the process of obtaining custody of a child.

Consequences licensees regularly miss:

- **"Adults only" housing is generally prohibited**, and the exemptions for housing for older persons are **narrow and have specific requirements that must actually be satisfied.**
- **Occupancy standards that are set restrictively enough to exclude families with children** function as familial status discrimination however they are phrased.
- **Steering families toward particular buildings, floors, or areas** is steering.

### 1.4 What disability protection covers

Disability protection reaches physical and mental impairments that substantially limit major life activities, along with a record of such an impairment and being regarded as having one.

Three obligations flow from it, developed in Section 4: **reasonable accommodations**, **reasonable modifications**, and **accessible design and construction requirements** for certain multifamily housing.

**One clarification that prevents a common error:** current illegal drug use is generally not protected, while a record of addiction may be.

### 1.5 Exemptions, and why they matter less than licensees think

Federal fair housing law contains limited exemptions — for certain owner-occupied dwellings of a small number of units, certain single-family sales by an owner without a broker, and certain religious organizations and private clubs.

**Three reasons a licensee should not rely on them:**

1. **The exemptions generally do not apply where a real estate licensee is involved.** That is the point: they cover an owner acting alone, not a transaction with a broker in it.
2. **The 1866 Act's prohibition on racial discrimination is not subject to them.**
3. **California's protections are broader**, and a federal exemption does not create a state one.

> **A licensee who hears "this one is exempt" should treat that as a reason to escalate, not as a reason to proceed.**

### 1.6 Checkpoint

You should be able to name the seven federal classes, name the four statutes, and explain why exemptions are rarely available in a transaction involving a licensee.

## 2. California fair housing protections

### 2.1 The principle to carry

> **California protects the federal classes and adds further characteristics. A licensee who knows only the federal list will engage in conduct that is lawful federally and unlawful in California.**

That single sentence is the section's operative content, and it should shape every practice decision.

### 2.2 The California framework

Two bodies of California law do most of the work.

**The Fair Employment and Housing Act** is California's principal housing discrimination statute, covering the federal characteristics and adding others.

**The Unruh Civil Rights Act** prohibits discrimination by business establishments, and its reach extends beyond housing to business generally — which is why it can apply to conduct that would not fall under a housing-specific statute.

California's protections extend beyond the federal seven to characteristics including **marital status, sexual orientation, gender identity and gender expression, ancestry, source of income, age, genetic information, citizenship, immigration status, primary language, military and veteran status**, and others.

**The list is not static.** It has been added to repeatedly, which is the practical reason a licensee should not work from a memorized list at all.

### 2.3 Source of income

The addition licensees encounter most often in practice.

**An instruction to refuse applicants using housing assistance is an unlawful instruction.** The obligations from the agency lesson apply in full: refuse it, explain why, escalate to the broker, and address whether the engagement can continue.

Advertising and screening practices that exclude assistance recipients — "no vouchers," or a minimum income multiple applied to the full rent rather than the tenant's portion — fail for the same reason.

### 2.4 Why the practice does not depend on the list

Because the list grows, and because copy that describes people is a risk regardless of which characteristic it touches, the reliable practice is not memorization:

> **Describe the property. Do not describe the people who should live in it.**

Property-focused copy never requires reference to any protected characteristic at all, which is why it is safe against a list that changes. And **"it's not one of the federal seven" is not clearance** — it is the reasoning that produces California violations.

### 2.5 Enforcement

Complaints may be pursued through the state civil rights agency, through federal agencies, and through the courts. Remedies can include damages, injunctive relief, civil penalties, and attorney fees.

**A licensee's discipline exposure is separate and additional.** Discriminatory conduct is grounds for action against a license independent of any civil outcome, which means "the case settled" does not close the matter.

### 2.6 The obligation runs to everyone

Fair housing obligations are not limited to the consumer in front of the licensee.

> A licensee who accepts a seller's instruction to limit who sees a property has violated the obligation as to buyers he never met. A licensee whose marketing reaches a narrow audience by design has done the same.

The client relationship is one surface among several, and the harm to a person who never learned the property existed is exactly the harm the law addresses.

### 2.8 California's protected characteristics, in the statute

Section 2.1 said that California protects more characteristics than federal law. **Government Code § 12955** — the housing article of the Fair Employment and Housing Act — is where the list lives, and reading it is the only way to hold it accurately.

> *"It shall be unlawful:*
>
> *(a) For the owner of any housing accommodation to discriminate against or harass any person because of the **race, color, religion, sex, gender, gender identity, gender expression, sexual orientation, marital status, national origin, ancestry, familial status, source of income, disability, veteran or military status, or genetic information** of that person."*

**Set that against the federal list.** The federal Fair Housing Act, 42 U.S.C. § 3604, prohibits discrimination because of *race, color, religion, sex, familial status, or national origin*, with disability addressed separately at § 3604(f).

| Characteristic | Federal | California |
|---|---|---|
| Race, color, religion, sex, national origin, familial status | ✓ | ✓ |
| Disability | ✓ (§ 3604(f)) | ✓ |
| **Gender identity, gender expression** | — | ✓ |
| **Sexual orientation** | — | ✓ |
| **Marital status** | — | ✓ |
| **Ancestry** | — | ✓ |
| **Source of income** | — | ✓ |
| **Veteran or military status** | — | ✓ |
| **Genetic information** | — | ✓ |

**A licensee who knows only the federal list will act lawfully under federal law and unlawfully in California.** That is not a rhetorical flourish; it is the ordinary consequence of a longer list.

**And the section reaches the licensee, not only the owner.** Subdivision (i) makes it unlawful *"for any person or other organization or entity whose business involves **real estate-related transactions** to discriminate against any person in making available a transaction, or in the terms and conditions of a transaction"* on the listed grounds. Subdivision (g) reaches anyone who *"aid[s], abet[s], incite[s], compel[s], or coerce[s]"* any of it. **A licensee following an owner's unlawful instruction is inside subdivision (g).**

### 2.9 Source of income, and the two subdivisions that give it teeth

Of the California-only characteristics, **source of income** is the one that most often catches a licensee out, because the discriminatory sentence sounds like a business policy: *"we don't take vouchers."*

§ 12955 does not leave the point to inference. It addresses the mechanics of income screening directly:

> *"(n) To use a financial or income standard in the rental of housing that **fails to account for the aggregate income of persons residing together** or proposing to reside together on the same basis as the aggregate income of married persons residing together or proposing to reside together.*
>
> *(o)(1) In instances in which there is a **government rent subsidy**, to do either of the following: (A) Use a financial or income standard in assessing eligibility for the rental of housing **that is not based on the portion of the rent to be paid by the tenant**. (B)(i) Use a person's credit history … **without offering the applicant the option, at the applicant's discretion, of providing lawful, verifiable alternative evidence** of the applicant's reasonable ability to pay the portion of the rent to be paid by the tenant, including, but not limited to, government benefit payments, pay records, and bank statements."*

**Three operational rules come out of that.**

**Roommates' incomes aggregate the same way spouses' do.** A "three times the rent, per person" standard applied to unmarried co-applicants but not to married ones is inside subdivision (n).

**Where a subsidy exists, the income test runs against the tenant's own share of the rent.** A tenant paying $400 of a $2,400 rent is assessed against $400. Applying a three-times-rent rule to the full $2,400 is precisely what subdivision (o)(1)(A) prohibits.

**Credit history cannot be the only door.** Subdivision (o)(1)(B) requires that a subsidised applicant be **offered the option** of supplying alternative evidence of ability to pay. Not asked for it if they complain — offered it.

**And applying an unlawful standard uniformly does not cure it.** Consistency is a defence to a claim of disparate treatment; it is not a defence to a standard the statute prohibits outright.

### 2.10 Perception, association, and why intent is not the test

Two more features of the California scheme close the gaps that a well-meaning licensee might otherwise fall into.

**§ 12955(m)** provides that the listed characteristics include *"a **perception** that the person has any of those characteristics or that the person is **associated with a person** who has, or is perceived to have, any of those characteristics."*

**The Unruh Civil Rights Act, Civil Code § 51**, does the same for business establishments — and a real estate brokerage is one. **§ 51(e)(7)** extends every listed characteristic to *"(B) A **perception** that the person has any particular characteristic … (C) A **perception that the person is associated with a person** who has, or is perceived to have, any particular characteristic…"*

**What this means in practice is that being wrong is not a defence.** A licensee who treats an applicant differently because of an assumption about their religion has discriminated on the basis of religion whether or not the assumption was correct. A licensee who treats a tenant differently because of their partner's disability has discriminated on the basis of disability.

**And nothing in either statute requires a discriminatory motive.** The question is what the licensee did and on what basis, not what they intended by it. **This is the same point Assignment 14 of the Real Estate Practice course makes about implicit bias**, arriving from the other direction: the law does not ask whether the licensee meant well, because the consumer's access to housing is unaffected by what the licensee meant.

**The professional response, in one line:** apply written criteria uniformly, record what was applied, and route every instruction that would require you to do otherwise to the broker in writing — because § 12955(g) reaches the person who carries the instruction out.

### 2.11 Checkpoint

You should be able to name California's principal fair housing statutes, explain why the federal list is insufficient, and state the practice that does not depend on the list.

## 3. Prohibited conduct and advertising

### 3.1 The prohibited practices

| Practice | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Refusal to sell or rent** | Declining to deal on a protected basis |
| **Different terms or conditions** | Different price, deposit, terms, or services |
| **Steering** | Directing a person toward or away from areas, buildings, or units |
| **Blockbusting** | Inducing sales or rentals by representations about the entry of a protected group into an area |
| **Redlining** | Denying or varying the terms of financing or insurance by area |
| **Discriminatory advertising** | Copy or targeting indicating a preference, limitation, or discrimination |
| **Refusal of accommodation or modification** | Denying a required reasonable accommodation or modification |
| **Falsely denying availability** | Telling a person a unit is unavailable when it is |
| **Retaliation or interference** | Against a person exercising fair housing rights or assisting another |
| **Discouragement** | Deterring an inquiry or application rather than denying it |

### 3.2 The forms that are committed with good intentions

The obvious practices are the ones licensees already know not to do. The subtle ones are where violations actually occur.

| Form | Example |
|---|---|
| **Redirection** | "You'd be more comfortable over here" |
| **Curated balance** | Selecting a "representative mix" chosen by the licensee rather than by the buyer's criteria |
| **Characterization** | Describing residents rather than the property or the area's objective features |
| **Differential enthusiasm** | Showing everything, but presenting some properties warmly and others flatly |
| **Differential effort** | Prompt follow-up on some inquiries, slower on others |
| **Assumed suitability** | Deciding a two-story home "won't work" for a buyer with a disability and not showing it |
| **Soliciting preferences** | Asking which protected characteristics matter so they can be "honored" |
| **Discouragement** | "You probably wouldn't qualify" instead of taking the application |

**The last four are the ones committed with good intentions**, and they are prohibited conduct all the same. **Steering is defined by whose criteria drove the selection, not by the licensee's intent.**

**Discouragement deserves particular attention:** it achieves a denial while producing no record of one, which is exactly why it is hard to detect and dangerous to permit.

### 3.3 The advertising rule

> **Advertising may not indicate a preference, limitation, or discrimination based on protected characteristics, and may not express an intention to make one.**

The workable form:

> **Describe the property. Do not describe the people who should live in it.**

Copy that sorts people, however warmly intended, signals who is and is not welcome. That is the harm, and **intent is not a defense.**

### 3.4 Rewriting copy

| Problem copy | Why it fails | Property-focused rewrite |
|---|---|---|
| "Family-friendly neighborhood" | Familial status | "Cul-de-sac location; park two blocks away" |
| "Perfect for a young couple" | Age and familial status | "Two bedrooms, one bath, 940 square feet" |
| "Walking distance to St. Anne's parish" | Religion | "Walking distance to the town center" |
| "Not suitable for anyone with mobility issues" | Disability | "Second-floor unit; access by stairs, no elevator" |
| "Ideal for professionals, no students" | Sorts occupants | "Quiet building; on-site laundry; assigned parking" |
| "Mature adults only" | Age and familial status | Omit; the older-persons exemptions are narrow and must actually apply |
| "English-speaking building" | National origin, primary language | Omit entirely |
| "Great for empty nesters" | Familial status and age, softened | "Single-story, low-maintenance yard" |
| "No vouchers" | **Source of income** — California | Omit; screen on uniform written criteria |

**Every rewrite is more informative than the original.** "Second-floor unit; access by stairs, no elevator" tells a buyer with a mobility concern exactly what they need, **without deciding for them** whether the property will work.

### 3.5 Accessibility information belongs in the copy

A common overcorrection is to omit accessibility features entirely, on the theory that any mention of disability is prohibited. That withholds information consumers need.

> **The rule is about who the property is *for*, not about what the property *has*.**

**Do describe:** single-level layout, step-free entry, wide doorways, roll-in shower, elevator, grab bars, accessible parking.
**Do not characterize:** who the property is or is not suitable for.

### 3.6 Targeting is part of the advertisement

The most consequential digital point, and it is easy to miss because it does not appear in the copy.

Platforms permit advertisers to select who sees an advertisement — by age, by location, by inferred interests, by proxies for demographic characteristics.

> **Restricting who may see a housing advertisement can deny groups access to the housing opportunity, and that is the harm advertising rules exist to prevent.**

Neutral copy delivered selectively excludes as effectively as discriminatory wording. Two consequences: **targeting choices must be examined with the same care as the words**, and **using a platform's tool does not shift responsibility.** "The platform offered that option" is not an answer.

### 3.7 Imagery, disclaimers, and instructions

**Imagery communicates preference as effectively as words.** Marketing that consistently depicts one group can signal who is welcome.

**An equal housing statement does not cure discriminatory content.** A discriminatory advertisement with a fair housing logo is still a discriminatory advertisement.

**A client's instruction does not authorize it.** Documenting that copy came from the seller does not transfer responsibility, and publishing a softened version conveying the same preference is the same violation in gentler words. The professional response is to explain why the copy cannot run, offer the property-focused rewrite, and escalate if the client insists.

### 3.8 Checkpoint

You should be able to name the prohibited practices, rewrite copy that sorts people, and explain why targeting is part of the advertisement.

## 4. Reasonable accommodations and modifications

### 4.1 Two different requests

Using the wrong framework produces the wrong answer, and the error is extremely common.

| | Reasonable accommodation | Reasonable modification |
|---|---|---|
| What it changes | A **rule, policy, practice, or service** | The **physical premises** |
| Examples | Waiving a no-pet policy for an assistance animal; a reserved accessible parking space; permitting a live-in aide; accepting rent on a different date tied to benefit timing | Installing a grab bar; widening a doorway; adding a ramp; lowering a countertop |
| Who requests | The person with the disability, or someone on their behalf | The same |
| Analysis | Is it reasonable, and is it necessary because of the disability? | The same |

> **The classic error is evaluating an accommodation request under the ordinary policy** — treating a request to waive a no-pet rule as a pet question — which produces a denial that never engaged the actual standard.

### 4.2 Assistance animals are not pets

For fair housing purposes, an assistance animal is not a pet. Three consequences frequently mishandled:

1. **A no-pet policy does not, by itself, justify denying the request.**
2. **A pet deposit or pet rent may not be charged.** Charging one imposes a condition on a protected group that others do not bear.
3. **A request cannot be refused merely because the animal is not "certified."** Reliance on a certification requirement is a common and serious error.

The provider may still evaluate reasonableness, and **the resident remains responsible for damage the animal causes and for the animal's behavior.**

### 4.3 What "reasonable" means

Reasonableness is an actual analysis, not a formality, and it **can produce a denial.** Factors that legitimately bear on it include whether the request would impose an undue financial and administrative burden, whether it would fundamentally alter the nature of the operation, and whether an alternative would equally meet the need.

Two points about process:

- **The interactive process matters.** Where a request as made is not workable, the provider engages about alternatives rather than simply refusing.
- **Delay functions as denial.** A request that receives no response is effectively refused, and the absence of a decision is not a defense.

### 4.4 Modifications: who pays and what conditions apply

In private housing, a reasonable modification is generally made **at the requester's expense.** The provider may impose reasonable conditions — workmanlike performance, permits, and in some circumstances restoration when the tenancy ends.

**What the provider may not do is treat the request as an unauthorized alteration and refuse it on that basis.** That applies the lease framework to a fair housing question.

### 4.5 Verification, and its limits

Where a disability or the disability-related need is not obvious, a provider may generally seek information sufficient to establish that the person has a disability and that the requested accommodation is necessary because of it.

**What may not be sought is a diagnosis, medical records, or detail about the nature or severity of the condition.** The question is whether the need exists, not what the condition is.

### 4.6 Design and construction

Certain multifamily housing is subject to accessible design and construction requirements, and **California standards can exceed the federal baseline.**

A licensee marketing new construction should not represent that a property meets accessibility requirements without confirming it, and should direct specific questions to the developer, the architect, or the jurisdiction.

### 4.7 Accessibility in how service is delivered

The obligation is not limited to the property. It extends to how a licensee provides service:

- Offering information in a format a consumer can use.
- Not assuming a consumer's needs or capacities from an observation.
- **Not steering a consumer toward or away from properties based on an assumption about what would suit their disability.**

> **The consumer decides what works for them; the licensee provides accurate information about the property.**

That last point is the one licensees most often get wrong with the best intentions. Deciding on a buyer's behalf that a two-story house "won't work for him" and not showing it is steering, however kindly meant.

### 4.8 Checkpoint

You should be able to classify a request, state the standard applied, and identify what may and may not be asked in verification.

## 5. Fair lending and appraisal bias

### 5.1 The fair lending framework

**The Equal Credit Opportunity Act** prohibits discrimination in any aspect of a credit transaction on protected bases, and requires that an applicant who is denied credit receive a statement of the reasons or notice of the right to obtain one.

**The Fair Housing Act** separately prohibits discrimination in residential real-estate-related transactions, including lending.

**The Home Mortgage Disclosure Act** requires certain lenders to collect and report data on mortgage applications, which supports detection of lending patterns no individual decision reveals.

**The Community Reinvestment Act** addresses depository institutions' obligations to help meet the credit needs of the communities they serve.

### 5.2 Redlining and its modern forms

**Redlining** — denying or varying the terms of financing or insurance by area — is prohibited, and its historical operation is part of why present-day patterns exist.

Federally influenced appraisal and lending practice once graded neighborhoods for lending risk, with grades correlated to racial composition. **Areas graded poorly received less mortgage capital for decades**, which suppressed maintenance, values, and the accumulation of housing equity in exactly the neighborhoods already excluded.

Modern forms include marketing that reaches some areas and not others, branch and service distribution, and — increasingly — **algorithmic and data-driven decision-making that reproduces historical patterns without anyone intending it.**

### 5.3 Why this compounds

**Housing is the principal vehicle of wealth accumulation for most American families. Wealth built through housing is inherited, and inherited wealth becomes the down payment for the next generation.**

Exclusion from housing markets therefore does not end when the exclusionary practice ends. **It compounds** — which explains why equal treatment starting now does not by itself produce equal outcomes.

**What this does and does not imply for a licensee.** It does **not** imply that a licensee should attempt to correct history through their own decisions about who lives where — selecting properties for a buyer based on a protected characteristic is steering regardless of the intent behind it, including a corrective intent.

It **does** imply that a licensee should recognize inherited patterns rather than reading them as natural facts about places, refuse to transmit them as market wisdom, apply uniform practices, and give consumers objective information so they can apply their own criteria.

### 5.4 The licensee's role in lending

**May:** explain in general terms how loan program types work; explain what a preapproval is; recommend the buyer speak with more than one lender; and provide the same information and referrals to every consumer.

**May not:** steer a consumer toward or away from a lender or program on a protected basis; assume from an observation what a consumer can afford or qualify for; discourage an application; or participate in an arrangement that varies terms by area or by borrower characteristic.

> **"They probably wouldn't qualify" is not an assessment a licensee is entitled to make**, and acting on it is discouragement, which produces a denial with no record of one.

### 5.5 Appraisal bias

Valuation is a judgment, and judgment is where bias operates. Concerns about appraisal bias — including in the treatment of neighborhoods and of the people occupying properties — are a recognized issue, and both federal and California attention has increased.

**The structural control is objective, market-derived support.** A derived adjustment can be examined; an impression cannot. Comparable selection, adjustment derivation, and neighborhood description are each places where a stated basis constrains discretion.

### 5.6 The licensee's role with an appraisal

**May:** provide genuinely relevant information through the proper channel — sales the licensee believes relevant, information about condition and improvements, a copy of the contract.

**May not:** communicate a target. "The deal dies without another $25,000" is exactly what independence rules exist to prevent — including when the request comes from the licensee's own client.

**Where a licensee believes an appraisal reflects bias:** escalate to the broker and follow the reconsideration process available through the lender. **Do not confront the appraiser**, and do not advise the client that they have a claim — that is a legal conclusion.

### 5.7 Insurance

Discrimination in the provision of property insurance by area or by protected characteristic raises the same concerns as lending, and insurance availability is increasingly the practical constraint on transactions in mapped hazard areas.

A licensee's role is the familiar one: ensure the buyer investigates **early in the contingency period**, provide the same information and referrals to every consumer, and escalate anything that looks like a pattern rather than a pricing decision.

### 5.9 Appraisal bias, and why it reaches the licensee

Section 5.4 introduced fair lending. Appraisal is the part of it that a residential licensee is closest to, because the licensee is often the person who meets the appraiser at the property.

**§ 12955(i)(2)** puts appraisal inside the statute expressly. It is unlawful:

> *"For any person or other entity whose business includes **performing appraisals**, as defined in subdivision (b) of Section 11302 of the Business and Professions Code, **of residential real property to discriminate against any person in making available those services, or in the performance of those services**, because of race, color, religion, sex, gender, gender identity, gender expression, sexual orientation, familial status, source of income, disability, genetic information, veteran or military status, or national origin."*

**"Or in the performance of those services" is the operative phrase.** The prohibition is not only about refusing to appraise for someone; it reaches **how the appraisal is performed** — which comparables were selected, which neighbourhood boundaries were drawn, what adjustments were made.

**Why this is a mechanism rather than only a motive.** Assignment 14 of the Real Estate Practice course explains that comparable-sales analysis measures the accumulated effect of past exclusion accurately and thereby carries it forward. An appraiser selecting comparables "across the boulevard" rather than within a subject's own neighbourhood, or the reverse, is making a judgment about which market the property belongs to — **and where those boundaries were originally drawn by exclusion, an accurate measurement reproduces it.**

**What a licensee should and should not do at an appraisal.**

**Do provide factual information.** Recent comparable sales the appraiser may not have, a list of improvements with dates and permits, the property's own history. Providing data is proper and useful.

**Do not attempt to influence the conclusion.** Suggesting a value, mentioning what the deal needs, or characterising the neighbourhood is improper, and appraiser independence rules exist precisely to prevent it.

**Do not remove or add anything to the property to signal something about its occupants.** Advice of that kind — to depersonalise a home so the appraiser cannot tell who lives there — is sometimes given informally and is a symptom of the problem rather than a remedy for it. **A licensee who believes an appraisal was influenced by who lives in the house should escalate it, not manage around it.**

**Do know that a reconsideration process exists.** A borrower who believes an appraisal is inaccurate may request reconsideration through the lender, supported by specific comparable sales. **That is a factual process with a defined route**, and it is the answer to "the appraisal came in low" whatever the reason.

### 5.10 The documentation that answers a fair housing question

The recurring professional problem in fair housing is not knowing the rule. It is being able to show, months or years later, what was actually done.

**Four records, each cheap and each decisive.**

**Written selection criteria, applied uniformly.** For a rental: income standard, credit standard, references, occupancy. Written down before any applicant appears, applied to every applicant, with the evaluation recorded. **A criterion invented for one applicant is indefensible even if it is reasonable.**

**A property-showing log.** Which properties were shown, to whom, and on whose criteria they were selected. **The steering allegation is answered by a record showing the client's stated criteria and the properties matching them** — and it is unanswerable without one.

**Advertising copy and where it ran.** Every advertisement, retained, with its publication. § 12955(c) reaches notices, statements and advertisements indicating a preference, and the defence to an allegation is the copy itself.

**The written record of any instruction refused.** An owner who instructs a licensee to exclude a protected class has put the licensee inside § 12955(g) if the licensee complies. **The record of the refusal — the instruction, the refusal, the date, in writing to the broker — is what separates a licensee who declined from one who quietly went along.**

**And the framing that makes this a professional habit rather than defensive paperwork.** Every one of these four records also makes the work better: uniform criteria produce better tenants, a showing log produces better client service, retained copy produces better advertising. **They are not written for the complaint. They are the reason the complaint does not happen.**

### 5.11 Checkpoint

You should be able to name the fair lending statutes, explain why exclusion compounds, and state what a licensee does when an appraisal appears to reflect bias.

## 6. Complaints, documentation, and professional response

### 6.1 Structure beats effort

The central practical finding, and the reason this material belongs in a principles course rather than an ethics seminar:

> **Conscious effort to be fair does not reliably reduce the effects of bias. Structure does.**

Effort fails because it depends on noticing, and much of what produces unequal outcomes operates outside awareness. **Structure works because it removes the discretion in which bias operates.**

### 6.2 The practices that constrain discretion

| Practice | What it constrains |
|---|---|
| **Written criteria applied uniformly** | The decision itself, in screening and selection |
| **Uniform service scripts** | Differential treatment at first contact |
| **The consumer's criteria drive the search** | Whose judgment selects properties |
| **Objective sources for subjective questions** | The licensee substituting characterization for data |
| **Uniform showing and access protocols** | Selective supervision, and the judgments behind it |
| **Documented evaluations** | Makes the basis reviewable, which itself changes behavior |
| **Slowing decisions under time pressure** | Bias operates most strongly when fast |
| **Reviewing outcomes, not intentions** | Detects patterns no individual decision reveals |

### 6.3 Declining characterizations well

Licensees steer partly because declining is hard in the moment. Having language ready removes the difficulty. Each answer **declines the characterization, gives an honest reason, and leaves the consumer better equipped.**

> **"What kind of people live here?"**
> "I don't describe neighborhoods by who lives in them — that's not something I'm permitted to do, and honestly my impression wouldn't be worth much. Census data and the city's own information are public, and here's where to find them."

> **"Is this a safe area?"**
> "Safety isn't something I can characterize for you. Local law enforcement publishes incident data by area, and here's where. I'd also suggest visiting at different times of day."

> **"Which schools are good?"**
> "School quality is something to evaluate against your own priorities. The district publishes performance data — here's the link. I'd avoid ranking them, because what matters in a school varies a lot by family."

**What not to do instead:** refuse and change the subject, which leaves a real informational need to be answered somewhere less careful; answer "in general terms," since a general characterization is still a characterization; or **answer by showing rather than telling**, redirecting the search toward what you would have said.

### 6.4 The unlawful instruction

Write this out in advance, because it is the hardest thing in the lesson to do in the moment — the client is in front of you, the listing is at stake, and the instruction is usually delivered casually.

> "I can't do that, and I want to be straight with you about why. [Characteristic] is protected under California law, and screening or showing on that basis isn't something I'm able to do — it would put your listing and my license at risk. What I can do is [the lawful alternative addressing the underlying concern]. I'm also going to let my broker know we discussed this, because that's the process."

**Four components:** refuses plainly, explains without lecturing, offers a lawful alternative addressing the real concern, and escalates transparently.

**Three failures:** following it while documenting that it came from the client — documentation does not transfer responsibility for the act; complying informally by scheduling around those consumers — which achieves the same exclusion; and withdrawing quietly without explanation — which leaves the client free to repeat the instruction with the next licensee.

### 6.5 Responding to a complaint

When a consumer alleges discrimination, the first response frequently determines the trajectory:

1. **Acknowledge promptly.** Silence reads as evasion.
2. **Notify the broker immediately.**
3. **Preserve everything** — records, communications, drafts, texts, and the marketing as it ran.
4. **Respond factually**, without admitting or denying liability before the file has been reviewed.
5. **Do not offer a settlement or a fee reduction** on your own initiative.
6. **Report to the carrier** where the matter could become a claim.

**Why the wrong responses are wrong:** an immediate detailed defense commits a position before anyone reviewed the file; an apology accepting fault may be treated as an admission and can compromise coverage; a fee reduction commits the brokerage and rarely ends the matter; waiting for a formal demand hardens the matter and may make coverage notice late; and handling it quietly compounds the original problem with a supervision failure.

**Deleting a record that reflects poorly is far worse than the record.** It converts a defensible dispute about professional judgment into a question about integrity, and it is generally discoverable anyway.

### 6.6 Complaints that are not about you

A consumer who reports that another brokerage discriminated should be taken seriously:

- **Record what they report**, without characterizing it as established.
- **Inform them that enforcement agencies accept complaints**, and how.
- **Notify your broker.**

**What not to do:** investigate the other brokerage, tell the consumer such complaints rarely succeed, or take no action because the conduct was someone else's.

### 6.7 Outcome review

The practice most likely to detect a problem is also the least used: periodically look at **your own outcomes** rather than your intentions.

- Where are the properties you showed, mapped?
- How quickly did you respond to inquiries, grouped by source?
- Which applicants did you encourage, and which did you discourage?
- What does your marketing reach, and whom does it reach?

**Individual decisions rarely reveal anything. Patterns do** — and a licensee who reviews their own patterns finds problems while they are still correctable.

### 6.8 What the file must show

For a fair housing question, the record is the defense:

- The consumer's stated criteria, recorded at intake and updated.
- The search parameters used.
- Properties presented and shown, with dates.
- **Where a matching property was not shown, why.**
- Screening criteria and documented evaluations against them.
- Advertising as it ran, where, when, and with whose approval — **including the audience targeting.**
- Instructions declined, and the escalation.

**A list of properties shown, without the criteria that produced it, shows what happened without showing why.** The criteria are what make it defensible.

### 6.10 What happens when a complaint is filed, and what the licensee does

Section 6.4 described the complaint routes. Knowing the shape of a proceeding matters because the licensee's conduct in the first week after an allegation frequently determines the outcome.

**The routes, and they are not exclusive.**

| Route | Filed with | What it produces |
|---|---|---|
| **State civil rights agency** | California's civil rights department | Investigation, conciliation, and a determination; a right-to-sue |
| **Federal** | HUD, or a substantially equivalent state agency | Investigation and conciliation; referral for enforcement |
| **Court** | Directly, by the complainant | Damages, injunction, fees |
| **The Department of Real Estate** | Against the licence | Discipline under B&P §§ 10176 and 10177 |

**A single course of conduct can produce all four.** A licensee who assumes that resolving one disposes of the others has assumed something untrue, and the licence proceeding is frequently the last to arrive and the longest to resolve.

**What the licensee does in the first week.**

**Preserve everything, immediately.** Every record touching the transaction, including drafts, texts and voicemail. **The instinct to tidy is the single most damaging instinct available**, and a file that was altered after notice of a complaint is a different and far worse case.

**Notify the broker, in writing, the same day.** The broker has supervision obligations and errors-and-omissions coverage with notice requirements that can be forfeited by delay.

**Do not contact the complainant.** Not to explain, not to apologise, not to clarify. Every one of those is a communication that will be recounted.

**Do not draft a narrative from memory before reading the file.** Write from documents. A recollection contradicted by a dated record is worse than no recollection.

**Cooperate with the investigation through counsel.** An investigator's questions are proper and the answers matter; **the licensee who answers them alone, informally, and in a hurry is the one who creates the quotable sentence.**

### 6.11 Fair housing as a system rather than a rule

The examinable content of fair housing is a list of protected characteristics and prohibited acts. **The professional content is that neither is where failures happen.** Almost nobody in this business decides to discriminate. The failures come from discretion exercised in good faith and unevenly, and the remedy is structural.

**Four structures, each of which operates whether or not anyone is thinking about fair housing on a given day.**

**Written criteria, published and applied uniformly.** A standard written before the applicant appears cannot be adjusted to them.

**A uniform information script.** The same facts, in the same order, to every enquirer. **Steering is a difference in the information different consumers receive**, and a script removes the mechanism.

**Objective property selection.** Properties selected against the client's stated written criteria, with the criteria on file. Assignment 14 Section 6.9 explains why the showing log answers the allegation; the criteria are what the log is measured against.

**Outcome review.** Periodically, look at who was served and how — not to reach a conclusion, but because **a practice that has never looked at its outcomes has no idea what they are.**

**Why this belongs in a Principles course rather than only in Practice.** The Real Estate Practice course carries the SB 1495 components and the participatory practicum, and it goes further into implicit bias than this course does. **What a Principles student should leave with is the structural insight**: that a licensee's exposure under Government Code § 12955, the Unruh Civil Rights Act and B&P § 10177(l) does not depend on what they intended, and that the only reliable protection is a practice designed so that discretion has less room to operate.

**The closing thought, and it is the honest one.** A licensee who reads this assignment and concludes "I would never do that" has taken exactly the wrong lesson. **The licensees who generate fair housing complaints are, in the overwhelming majority, people who would say the same thing and mean it.** The question worth carrying is not whether you would discriminate. It is whether, if your practice were producing unequal outcomes, **you would have any way of knowing.**

### 6.12 Checkpoint

You should be able to produce the declining scripts, write the unlawful instruction response, state the complaint sequence, and describe what outcome review examines.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **The federal protected classes** | Race, color, religion, sex, national origin, familial status, disability. |
| **Civil Rights Act of 1866** | Prohibits racial discrimination in property transactions, **not subject to the later exemptions**, and applies to all property. |
| **Fair Housing Act** | Title VIII of the 1968 Act as amended, the principal federal housing discrimination statute. |
| **Americans with Disabilities Act** | Addresses public accommodations and commercial facilities — including a brokerage's own office. |
| **Familial status** | Households with children under 18, pregnant persons, and persons obtaining custody. Older-persons exemptions are **narrow and must actually apply**. |
| **FEHA** | California's principal housing discrimination statute, covering the federal characteristics and adding others. |
| **Unruh Civil Rights Act** | Prohibits discrimination by business establishments, reaching beyond housing. |
| **Source of income** | A California-protected characteristic. An instruction to refuse housing assistance is an unlawful instruction. |
| **"Not one of the federal seven"** | Not clearance. It is the reasoning that produces California violations. |
| **Steering** | Directing a person toward or away from areas, buildings, or units. **Defined by whose criteria drove the selection, not by intent.** |
| **Blockbusting** | Inducing sales or rentals by representations about the entry of a protected group into an area. |
| **Redlining** | Denying or varying financing or insurance terms by area. |
| **Discouragement** | Deterring an inquiry or application rather than denying it. Achieves a denial with **no record of one**. |
| **Assumed suitability** | Deciding on a consumer's behalf that a property will not work for them. Steering, committed with good intentions. |
| **Describe the property, not the people** | The rule that works because it never requires reference to any protected characteristic — and therefore survives a list that changes. |
| **Accessibility information** | Belongs in the copy. The rule governs who the property is **for**, not what it **has**. |
| **Audience targeting** | Part of the advertisement. Restricting who may see a housing advertisement denies access as effectively as the words, and a platform's tool does not shift responsibility. |
| **Equal housing statement** | Appropriate and expected. **Does not cure** discriminatory content. |
| **Reasonable accommodation** | A change to a **rule, policy, practice, or service** because of a disability. |
| **Reasonable modification** | A change to the **physical premises**, generally at the requester's expense with reasonable conditions. |
| **Assistance animal** | Not a pet. No pet deposit or pet rent, no certification requirement — and the resident remains responsible for damage and behavior. |
| **Interactive process** | Engaging about workable alternatives. **Delay functions as denial.** |
| **Verification limits** | Enough to establish the disability and the disability-related need — **not** diagnosis, records, or nature and severity. |
| **Equal Credit Opportunity Act** | Prohibits credit discrimination and requires reasons for adverse action. |
| **HMDA / CRA** | Mortgage application data reporting supporting pattern detection; and depository obligations to community credit needs. |
| **Compounding** | Why exclusion outlasts the practice: housing wealth is inherited and becomes the next generation's down payment. |
| **Corrective steering** | Selecting properties on a protected basis with a corrective intent. **Still steering.** |
| **Appraiser independence** | Supply **information**, never a **target** — including at your own client's request. |
| **Structure beats effort** | Effort depends on noticing; structure removes the discretion in which bias operates. |
| **Outcome review** | Examining your own patterns — showings mapped, response times by source, who was encouraged, whom your marketing reached. |

## Practice problems

### Problem 1

A seller tells a licensee that his fourplex is "exempt from all that fair housing stuff" because he
lives in one unit, and instructs the licensee not to rent to families with children or to anyone using
a housing voucher. He adds that he would never discriminate on race, so there is no real issue.

**Assess every element.**

> **Solution.** **The exemption claim fails first, and for three reasons.** Federal exemptions are
> limited and **generally do not apply where a real estate licensee is involved** — that is the point of
> them: they cover an owner acting alone. The **1866 Act's prohibition on racial discrimination is not
> subject to them** at all. And **California's protections are broader**; a federal exemption does not
> create a state one.
>
> **"This one is exempt" is a reason to escalate, not a reason to proceed.**
>
> **Excluding families with children is familial status discrimination**, federally and in California —
> and the older-persons exemptions are narrow and must actually be satisfied.
>
> **Excluding voucher holders is source of income discrimination**, protected in California. So is a
> minimum income multiple applied to the full rent rather than the tenant's portion.
>
> **And his framing is the error the whole lesson addresses.** Race is one of seven federal
> characteristics, and California protects far more. A licensee who reasons "it's not race, so it's
> fine" will engage in conduct that is unlawful.

### Problem 2

A licensee shows a buyer three homes in a different area than the two he asked about, tells him he'd
"be more comfortable over here," follows up on some inquiries within an hour and others in two days,
presents certain listings warmly and others flatly, and does not schedule a two-story listing for a
buyer with a mobility impairment because the stairs "won't work for him."

**Classify each and state what makes them all the same violation.**

> **Solution.** **All five are steering**, and **steering is defined by whose criteria drove the
> selection, not by the licensee's intent.**
>
> **Redirection** — showing a different area than the one asked about. **Differential effort** —
> allocating access unequally by response time, a distinction that cannot be defended afterward in
> neutral terms. **Differential enthusiasm** — shaping the selection as effectively as declining to show
> it. **Assumed suitability** — the two-story home, and the one committed with the best intentions.
>
> **On the last:** the consumer decides what works for them; the licensee provides accurate information
> about the property — here, that access is by stairs with no elevator. Deciding on his behalf and
> omitting the property is steering however kindly meant, and it is precisely why accessibility
> information belongs **in the copy** rather than being used to filter for him.
>
> **The defense is the record:** the buyer's stated criteria, the search parameters used, properties
> presented with dates, and **the reason where a matching property was not shown.** A list of properties
> shown without the criteria that produced it shows what happened without showing why.

### Problem 3

Rewrite each and say what makes the rewrite better: (a) "family-friendly neighborhood"; (b) "not
suitable for anyone with mobility issues"; (c) "mature adults only, no vouchers"; (d) "walking distance
to St. Anne's parish." The seller insists on (a) and offers to sign a statement that it was his idea.

**Answer.**

> **Solution.** (a) "Cul-de-sac location; park two blocks away." (b) "Second-floor unit; access by
> stairs, no elevator." (c) Omit both clauses; screen on uniform written criteria. (d) "Walking
> distance to the town center."
>
> **Every rewrite is more informative than the original.** (b) especially tells a buyer with a mobility
> concern exactly what they need to know **without deciding for them** whether the property will work —
> which is also the answer to the common overcorrection of omitting accessibility features entirely.
> The rule governs who the property is **for**, not what it **has**: describe the step-free entry, the
> roll-in shower, the elevator.
>
> **On (c):** "mature adults only" touches age and familial status and the older-persons exemptions are
> narrow; "no vouchers" is **source of income** discrimination in California.
>
> **The seller's offer is worthless and the licensee should say so plainly.** A client's instruction
> does not authorize the copy, **documenting that it came from him does not transfer responsibility**,
> and publishing a softened version conveying the same preference is the same violation in gentler
> words. Explain why it cannot run, offer the rewrite, and escalate if he insists.

### Problem 4

A licensee posts a listing with fully neutral copy and an equal housing logo, then boosts it with
targeting set to homeowners aged 25–45 in three ZIP codes. Her marketing images consistently depict one
group of people. She says the copy is clean so there is no exposure.

**Assess.**

> **Solution.** **The targeting is the serious problem, and it is easy to miss because it does not
> appear in the copy at all.**
>
> Platforms permit selection by age, location, inferred interests, and proxies for demographic
> characteristics. **Restricting who may see a housing advertisement can deny groups access to the
> housing opportunity — the exact harm advertising rules exist to prevent.** Age-based selection plus
> geographic proxies is that. **Neutral copy delivered selectively excludes as effectively as
> discriminatory wording**, and "the platform offered that option" is not an answer: using the tool does
> not shift responsibility from the advertiser.
>
> **The imagery is a second exposure.** Imagery communicates preference as effectively as words, and
> marketing that consistently depicts one group can signal who is welcome.
>
> **The logo cures nothing.** A discriminatory advertisement with a fair housing logo is still a
> discriminatory advertisement, and the same is true of a disclaimer stating that all are welcome.
>
> **Targeting choices must be examined with the same care as the words** — and the archive must record
> the audience, not only the creative.

### Problem 5

A tenant with a disability asks the property manager to waive the no-pet policy for her assistance
animal and to install grab bars in her bathroom at her own cost. The manager replies that the building
does not allow pets, that certification would be required in any event, that a $500 pet deposit would
apply, that the grab bars are an unauthorized alteration under the lease, and asks for her medical
records. Three weeks later he has not decided.

**Classify each request and identify every error.**

> **Solution.** **The animal is a reasonable accommodation** — a change to a rule or policy. **The grab
> bars are a reasonable modification** — a change to the physical premises, generally at the requester's
> expense, subject to reasonable conditions such as workmanlike performance and permits.
>
> **Six errors.** He **evaluated the accommodation under the ordinary pet policy** — the classic
> mistake, producing a denial that never engaged the actual standard. An **assistance animal is not a
> pet**, so a no-pet policy does not by itself justify refusal. **Certification may not be required.** A
> **pet deposit or pet rent may not be charged**, because it imposes a condition on a protected group
> others do not bear. **Treating the modification as an unauthorized alteration** applies the lease
> framework to a fair housing question. **Medical records may not be sought** — a provider may seek
> enough to establish the disability and the disability-related need, **not** a diagnosis, records, or
> the nature or severity of the condition.
>
> **And the delay is a seventh.** Reasonableness is a genuine analysis that **can produce a denial** —
> but the **interactive process** requires engaging about alternatives where a request as made is not
> workable, and **delay functions as denial.** The absence of a decision is not a defense.
>
> What remains true: the resident is responsible for damage the animal causes and for its behavior.

### Problem 6

A buyer's agent tells his client that a particular lender "probably wouldn't approve someone in your
situation" and steers him elsewhere without an application. Separately, the agent notices that a
lender's marketing appears to reach some neighborhoods and not others, and that an appraisal on a home
in a historically underinvested area came in far below his supported range.

**Address each.**

> **Solution.** **The first is discouragement**, and it is the form that produces a denial with **no
> record of one** — which is exactly why it is hard to detect and dangerous. **"They probably wouldn't
> qualify" is not an assessment a licensee is entitled to make**, and acting on it steers the consumer
> away from credit. What a licensee may do is explain in general terms how program types work, explain
> what a preapproval is, recommend speaking with more than one lender, and **provide the same
> information and referrals to every consumer.**
>
> **The marketing pattern is a modern redlining question.** Denying or varying financing or insurance
> terms by area is prohibited, and modern forms include marketing reach, service distribution, and
> **algorithmic decision-making that reproduces historical patterns without anyone intending it.**
> Escalate to the broker rather than concluding.
>
> **On the appraisal:** escalate to the broker and **follow the reconsideration process available
> through the lender.** Do **not** confront the appraiser, and do **not** advise the client they have a
> claim — a legal conclusion. And whatever the licensee sends must be **information** — relevant sales,
> condition, the contract — **never a target.**

### Problem 7

An owner tells a licensee, while signing the listing, that he does not want the property shown to a
particular category of buyer. The listing is worth several months of income.

**Write the response and name what each part accomplishes. Then identify three ways licensees fail
here.**

> **Solution.** *"I can't do that, and I want to be straight with you about why. That's protected under
> California law, and screening or showing on that basis isn't something I'm able to do — it would put
> your listing and my license at risk. What I can do is apply the same written criteria and the same
> showing protocol to everyone, which is what actually protects you if a decision is ever questioned.
> I'm also going to let my broker know we discussed this, because that's the process."*
>
> **Four components:** it **refuses plainly**, **explains without lecturing**, **offers a lawful
> alternative addressing the underlying concern**, and **escalates transparently** rather than behind
> the client's back.
>
> **Three failures.** Following it while documenting that it came from the client — **documentation does
> not transfer responsibility for the act.** Complying informally by scheduling around those buyers —
> which achieves the same exclusion. And withdrawing quietly without explanation — which leaves the
> client free to repeat the instruction with the next licensee.
>
> **Why this is written in advance:** it is the hardest thing in the lesson to do in the moment. The
> client is in front of you, the listing is at stake, and the instruction is delivered casually — exactly
> the conditions under which people improvise something softer and comply informally.
>
> **And the obligation runs to people he will never meet.** A licensee who accepts an instruction
> limiting who sees a property has violated it as to buyers he never met.

### Problem 8

A consumer emails alleging she was discouraged from applying for a rental. The licensee sends a
detailed defense within the hour, apologizes for "how it came across," offers to waive the application
fee, deletes a text thread with the owner that reads badly, and decides to tell his broker only if it
escalates. He also mentions that another brokerage did something similar to a client last month but
that "those complaints never go anywhere."

**State the correct sequence and address the other-brokerage report.**

> **Solution.** **Five errors, and the deletion is categorically the worst.**
>
> **The immediate defense** commits a position before anyone reviewed the file. **The apology** may be
> treated as an admission and can compromise coverage. **The fee waiver** commits the brokerage and
> rarely ends the matter. **Waiting to tell the broker** compounds the original problem with a
> supervision failure. And **deleting the thread converts a defensible dispute about professional
> judgment into a question about integrity** — and it is generally discoverable anyway.
>
> **The correct sequence:** acknowledge promptly, because silence reads as evasion; notify the broker
> immediately; **preserve everything** including drafts, texts, and the marketing as it ran with its
> targeting; respond factually without admitting or denying liability before the file is reviewed; offer
> no settlement on your own initiative; and report to the carrier where the matter could become a claim.
>
> **On the other brokerage:** **record what the consumer reported** without characterizing it as
> established, **inform her that enforcement agencies accept complaints** and how, and **notify the
> broker.** What he must not do is investigate the other brokerage, **tell her such complaints rarely
> succeed**, or take no action because the conduct was someone else's.
>
> **And note the separate exposure:** discriminatory conduct is grounds for action against a license
> independent of any civil outcome. "The case settled" does not close the matter.

## Chapter summary

**Seven federal protected classes, four statutes to know by name** — and the 1866 Act's racial
prohibition is not subject to the exemptions.

**Exemptions generally do not apply where a licensee is involved.** "This one is exempt" is a reason to
escalate.

**California protects the federal classes and adds many others**, including source of income, and the
list keeps growing — which is why the practice cannot be memorization.

**Describe the property; do not describe the people who should live in it.** Property-focused copy never
requires reference to any protected characteristic, so it survives a changing list — and "it's not one
of the federal seven" is not clearance.

**The prohibited practices include the subtle forms**, and redirection, curated balance,
characterization, differential enthusiasm and effort, assumed suitability, and discouragement are the
ones committed with good intentions. **Steering is defined by whose criteria drove the selection.**

**Accessibility information belongs in the copy**, because the rule governs who the property is for, not
what it has.

**Targeting is part of the advertisement**, imagery communicates preference, an equal housing statement
cures nothing, and a client's instruction authorizes nothing.

**Accommodations change rules; modifications change premises.** Evaluating an accommodation under the
ordinary policy is the classic error. Assistance animals are not pets, certification may not be
required, no pet charge may be imposed, verification may not seek diagnosis or records, and **delay
functions as denial.**

**Fair lending reaches credit, insurance, marketing reach, and algorithmic decisions**, and exclusion
**compounds** because housing wealth is inherited — which does not license corrective steering.

**Supply an appraiser information, never a target**, and escalate a suspected biased appraisal through
the broker and the lender's reconsideration process.

**Structure beats effort.** Written criteria, uniform scripts, consumer-driven search, objective
sources, uniform protocols, documented evaluations, slowing under pressure, and **outcome review** —
because patterns reveal what individual decisions never do.

**Have the declining scripts and the unlawful-instruction response written in advance.**

**On a complaint: acknowledge, notify the broker, preserve everything, respond factually, commit
nothing, notify the carrier** — and never delete an unfavorable record.

**The record is the defense**, and the criteria are what make a list of showings defensible.
