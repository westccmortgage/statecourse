## 1. Voluntary and involuntary liens

### 1.1 What an encumbrance is

An **encumbrance** is any right or interest in land held by someone other than the owner that affects the property's title or its use. It does not prevent ownership or transfer; it travels with the property and burdens whoever holds it.

The organizing distinction:

| Category | What it affects | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Money encumbrances (liens)** | **Title** — the property secures a debt | Deed of trust, tax lien, judgment lien, mechanic's lien |
| **Non-money encumbrances** | **Use** — what may be done with the property | Easements, recorded restrictions, encroachments |

That division carries most of the lesson. A lien is about who gets paid; an easement or restriction is about what may be done.

### 1.2 Voluntary and involuntary

A **voluntary lien** is created by the owner's agreement — most commonly a deed of trust securing a loan.

An **involuntary lien** arises by operation of law without the owner's consent. Taxes, judgments, and mechanic's liens are the recurring examples.

### 1.3 General and specific

A second axis, cutting across the first:

**A specific lien** attaches to a particular property — a deed of trust, a property tax lien, a mechanic's lien.

**A general lien** attaches to all of the debtor's property in the jurisdiction — a judgment lien, most federal tax liens.

The practical consequence: a general lien against a seller reaches every parcel he owns in the county, which is why a judgment against a person with a similar name must be resolved rather than assumed away.

### 1.4 The four-way classification

Most liens can be placed on both axes at once, and doing so is a useful examination and practice skill.

| Lien | Voluntary/involuntary | General/specific |
|---|---|---|
| Deed of trust | Voluntary | Specific |
| Property tax lien | Involuntary | Specific |
| Mechanic's lien | Involuntary | Specific |
| Judgment lien | Involuntary | General |
| Federal tax lien | Involuntary | General |
| Special assessment | Involuntary | Specific |

### 1.5 What a lien does and does not do

**Does:** secure a debt against the property, so the property can be reached if the debt is not paid; and generally must be satisfied or otherwise addressed before clear title passes.

**Does not:** prevent the owner from selling. A property with liens can be sold; the liens are ordinarily paid from the proceeds at closing, which is precisely why the payoff figures matter and why they exceed principal balances.

### 1.6 Checkpoint

You should be able to place a lien on both axes and explain the practical consequence of a general lien.

## 2. The common liens

### 2.1 Deed of trust

The dominant security instrument in California residential practice, taken up in detail in Lesson 11. For present purposes: it is a **voluntary, specific lien** created when the owner borrows and pledges the property.

It is removed from title by a **reconveyance** recorded when the debt is satisfied. **The debt being paid does not clear the record** — a recorded clearing document does. A seller who says a loan was paid off years ago has stated a fact about the debt, not about the title, and an unreleased deed of trust in the preliminary report must be cleared by document.

### 2.2 Property tax liens

Property taxes become a lien on the property. Two features make them exceptional:

**Priority.** Property tax liens generally take priority over other liens **regardless of when they attached**. This is the principal exception to the recording-order rules in Section 5, and it exists because the taxing power does not depend on the order in which private parties recorded.

**Attachment without notice to anyone.** They arise by operation of law on a schedule, not by a recorded instrument someone had to file.

**Special assessments** — levied for improvements benefiting particular property, such as a district's infrastructure — are also involuntary and specific, and a buyer inherits the remaining obligation.

### 2.3 Mechanic's liens

A **mechanic's lien** secures payment for labor or materials furnished for the improvement of real property. It is the lien licensees most often mishandle, for two reasons.

**Priority may relate back.** A mechanic's lien's priority may relate back to a time earlier than its recording — commonly tied to when work commenced or the improvement began — which means a lien recorded after a lender's deed of trust may nonetheless take priority over it. This defeats the ordinary "first recorded, first in right" intuition.

**It arises from work, not from a contract with the owner.** A subcontractor or supplier the owner never dealt with, and may never have heard of, can have lien rights arising from work on the property.

The statutory scheme includes notices and deadlines — preliminary notices, recording periods, and periods within which enforcement must begin. **The specifics are legal**, and a licensee's role is to recognize that recent construction on a property creates lien exposure, ensure it is routed to escrow, title, and counsel, and not to advise on whether a lien is valid or timely.

> **The intake question:** has any work been done on this property recently, by whom, and is everyone paid? A seller who renovated last spring and has an unpaid dispute with a contractor has a title problem that has not appeared yet.

### 2.4 Judgment liens

A money judgment can be made a lien on the debtor's real property in a county by recording an abstract of the judgment. It is **involuntary and general** — it reaches all of the debtor's real property in that county, including property acquired afterward while the lien is effective.

Two practical points:

**Similar names.** A judgment against a person whose name resembles the seller's appears in the title search and must be resolved through title, not assumed away by the licensee.

**A buyer's own judgments matter too.** A buyer with an outstanding judgment lien will find it attaching to the property he is about to buy, which is a financing problem discovered at the worst time.

### 2.5 Other encumbrances affecting title

**Federal and state tax liens**, which arise from unpaid taxes and are general.

**Lis pendens** — a recorded notice that litigation affecting title to the property is pending. It is not itself a lien, and it is a powerful cloud: it gives notice to anyone acquiring an interest that the outcome may affect them, which in practice stops transactions.

**Attachment**, a pre-judgment remedy securing a claim against property pending the outcome.

### 2.6 Homestead, briefly

California provides a homestead exemption protecting a portion of a homeowner's equity from certain creditors. Its scope, amount, and effect are legal questions, and it does not protect against a voluntary lien the owner granted or against tax liens.

The licensee's role is to recognize the term when a client raises it and to route it to counsel rather than explaining its effect on a particular creditor.

### 2.8 The homestead exemption, and the figure that changed

Section 2.3 listed the involuntary liens a judgment creditor can reach. The homestead exemption is what a judgment debtor keeps out of that reach, and California's is unusually large — large enough that a licensee who learned an older figure will be wrong by a factor of several.

**Code of Civil Procedure § 704.730** sets it:

> *"(a) The amount of the homestead exemption is **the greater of** the following:*
>
> *(1) The **countywide median sale price for a single-family home** in the calendar year prior to the calendar year in which the judgment debtor claims the exemption, **not to exceed six hundred thousand dollars ($600,000)**.*
>
> *(2) **Three hundred thousand dollars ($300,000)**.*
>
> *(b) The amounts specified in this section shall **adjust annually for inflation**, beginning on January 1, 2022, based on the change in the annual California Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers, **published by the Department of Industrial Relations**, for the most recent one-year period ending on December 31 preceding the adjustment, with each adjusted amount rounded to the nearest twenty-five dollars ($25)."*

**Four features of that section a licensee should hold onto, and one thing the section does not contain.**

**It is a floor and a ceiling, not a single number — and the two figures printed in subdivision (a) are base figures.** The structure is: the prior year's countywide median sale price for a single-family home, **capped** at the ceiling, or the **floor**, whichever is greater. A county median far below the floor is lifted to the floor; a median far above the ceiling is cut to the ceiling; a median between them is the answer. **What subdivision (a) prints as $300,000 and $600,000 are the amounts as enacted**, and subdivision (b) then adjusts both of them every January.

**It is county-specific.** The same debtor with the same equity is protected differently in Kern and in Marin. **This is one of the few places in California real property law where the answer depends on which county the property is in**, and it is worth remembering because most rules do not work that way.

**Both figures index annually, and the section does not print the adjusted result.** Subdivision (b) adjusts them each January, beginning 1 January 2022, by the change in the annual California Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers published by the Department of Industrial Relations, rounded to the nearest $25. **The code gives the base figures and the method; it does not give the answer.** A figure quoted from a course, a website, or a memory is stale by construction — and so, on its own, is the number printed in the section.

**Where each part of the answer has to come from.** This is the point at which most descriptions of the exemption go wrong, so it is worth setting out as a list rather than a sentence.

| Component | Where it comes from |
|---|---|
| Statutory base ceiling — $600,000 | § 704.730(a)(1), on the face of the code |
| Statutory base floor — $300,000 | § 704.730(a)(2), on the face of the code |
| The annual inflation adjustment | § 704.730(b) — the California CPI for All Urban Consumers published by the **Department of Industrial Relations**, for the one-year period ending 31 December, rounded to the nearest $25 |
| The **adjusted ceiling** | The base ceiling as adjusted each January since 2022. **Not printed in the section** |
| The **adjusted floor** | The base floor as adjusted each January since 2022. **Not printed in the section** |
| The countywide median | The **prior calendar year's** countywide median sale price for a single-family home in the county. **The section names no source for it** |
| **The exemption** | The greater of (the county median, capped at the adjusted ceiling) and the adjusted floor |

**Why this course prints no current dollar amount.** The Judicial Council publishes a list of current exemption amounts on form **EJ-156**, and Code of Civil Procedure **§ 703.150** is what requires it — but that duty runs to the exemptions in **§ 703.140(b)** and to the article beginning at **§ 704.010**, and the homestead exemption sits in the following article. **EJ-156, revision of 20 July 2026, carries no homestead figure**, and § 704.730 names no agency to publish one. A course that printed an adjusted amount would be printing a number it had computed rather than one an authority had published. **So this course teaches the calculation and names the inputs, and a licensee who needs the operative figure gets it from counsel or from the current published index — not from this page.**

**These figures are dramatically larger than the historical ones.** California's homestead exemption was for many years a set of fixed amounts in the tens of thousands of dollars, tiered by the debtor's age and household. **A licensee who learned those figures learned a repealed scheme.** This is Assignment 01 Section 5.4 in concrete form: currency is not a detail of legal research, it is the difference between a right answer and a confidently wrong one.

### 2.9 What the exemption does, and what it does not do

**What it does.** It protects a dollar amount of equity in a debtor's principal dwelling from a forced sale to satisfy a money judgment. If a creditor forces a sale, the exempt amount comes to the debtor before the judgment creditor is paid, and where the equity does not exceed the exemption, a forced sale generally cannot proceed at all because it would produce nothing for the creditor.

**What it does not do.**

**It does not defeat a consensual lien.** A deed of trust the owner signed is not affected. A homeowner cannot claim a homestead exemption against their own mortgage lender.

**It does not defeat a tax lien or a mechanics lien.** The exemption operates against enforcement of money judgments. Liens with their own statutory foundation — property taxes, federal tax liens, mechanics liens under the Civil Code's works-of-improvement provisions — are outside it.

**It does not prevent the judgment lien from attaching.** An abstract of judgment recorded against a debtor still attaches to the property and still appears on a title search. **The exemption is a shield at the sale, not a bar at the recording**, which is why a homestead-exempt owner who sells voluntarily still deals with the judgment lien in escrow.

**It is not a substitute for advice.** Whether a particular debtor may claim it, on which property, and against which creditor, is a question with procedural steps and deadlines attached. A licensee's job is to recognise the issue on a title report and route it, not to reassure a seller that they are protected.

### 2.10 A worked title problem

> A seller's preliminary report shows: a first deed of trust with a payoff of $610,000; an abstract of judgment recorded in 2023 for $92,000; and a property tax lien of $6,400. The property is in escrow at $915,000. The county median sale price for the prior calendar year was $655,000.

**The exemption figure, worked in the order § 704.730 sets it out.**

1. **The statutory base figures.** A $600,000 ceiling and a $300,000 floor, on the face of subdivision (a).
2. **The annual inflation adjustment.** Subdivision (b) adjusts both every January since 2022 by the California CPI, rounded to the nearest $25. Call the results the **adjusted ceiling** and the **adjusted floor**. Neither is printed in the code.
3. **The countywide median component.** The prior calendar year's countywide median sale price for a single-family home — **$655,000** on these facts.
4. **Cap the median.** The median counts only up to the adjusted ceiling.
5. **Compare with the floor.** The exemption is the greater of that capped figure and the adjusted floor. Here the capped figure is far above any adjusted $300,000, so the floor does not govern.
6. **The result.** The exemption in this county is **the adjusted ceiling**, unless the adjustments since 2022 have carried that ceiling above $655,000, in which case it is **$655,000**.

**What the licensee could not do with the code alone.** Every step but one is on the page. **Step 2 is not** — and step 2 is what turns the printed figures into the operative ones. The answer here is $600,000 only if every annual adjustment since 2022 happened to be zero. **A licensee who reads § 704.730 and answers "$600,000" has read the statute and still given a wrong number**, which is Assignment 01 Section 5.4's warning about currency in the place it is easiest to trip over.

**What that means here — and it is not what a seller hopes.** The property is being sold **voluntarily**, in escrow, at a price sufficient to pay everything. The homestead exemption governs what a judgment creditor may force; it does not entitle a seller to convey clear title while leaving a recorded judgment lien on the property. **Escrow will require the abstract of judgment to be paid or released**, because the buyer's lender and the title insurer will not accept it.

Order of payment from the $915,000, in the ordinary case:

| Item | Amount | Why |
|---|---:|---|
| Property tax lien | $6,400 | Property taxes take priority over everything |
| First deed of trust | $610,000 | Consensual, recorded first |
| Abstract of judgment | $92,000 | Recorded later, but must be cleared to convey insurable title |
| Costs and commission | — | Per the contract |
| **To seller** | balance | — |

**The instructive part is what the seller believed.** Told that California's homestead exemption runs to several hundred thousand dollars and that their equity is under it, a seller may conclude the judgment "doesn't count." It counts in escrow. **The exemption answers a question about forced sale that this transaction never asked**, and a licensee who does not know the difference will pass the seller's misunderstanding along.

### 2.11 Checkpoint

You should be able to explain why a mechanic's lien defeats the first-recorded intuition, and what clears a paid deed of trust from title.

## 3. Easements and licenses

### 3.1 What an easement is

An **easement** is a non-possessory right to use another's land for a specified purpose. The holder may use; the holder may not possess or exclude.

It is a **non-money encumbrance** — it affects use rather than securing a debt — and it runs with the land, binding successive owners.

### 3.2 Appurtenant and in gross

| | Easement appurtenant | Easement in gross |
|---|---|---|
| Benefits | A **parcel** — the dominant tenement | A **person or entity** |
| Burdens | Another parcel — the servient tenement | A parcel |
| On transfer | Passes with the dominant parcel automatically | Depends on the grant; commercial ones are generally transferable |
| Typical example | A driveway easement over a neighbor's lot | A utility company's line easement |

**The dominant tenement is benefited; the servient tenement is burdened.** Students confuse these constantly, and the memory aid is that the servient parcel *serves* the other.

An easement appurtenant **passes with the dominant parcel whether or not it is mentioned in the deed**, which is why the "together with" language in Lesson 04 matters for clarity rather than for creation — and why a landlocked parcel's access must be traced in the records rather than assumed from the description.

### 3.3 How easements are created

| Method | How it arises |
|---|---|
| **Express grant** | The servient owner grants it in writing |
| **Express reservation** | A grantor conveys land and reserves an easement over it |
| **Implication** | Arising from the circumstances of a division of land, where prior use was apparent and continuous |
| **Necessity** | Arising where a parcel would otherwise be landlocked by the division that created it |
| **Prescription** | Arising from long use that meets the legal requirements |
| **Dedication** | A private owner devoting land to public use, accepted by the public body |
| **Condemnation** | Acquired by a government body exercising eminent domain |

The last several are legal doctrines with requirements a licensee cannot evaluate. **Recognition is the skill**; whether a prescriptive or implied easement exists is a question for counsel.

### 3.4 The licensee's practical problem

The recurring situation is not exotic:

> The preliminary report shows a ten-foot utility easement along the rear property line. The buyer intends to build a workshop at the rear of the lot.

**What the licensee does:** obtain the underlying document, ensure the buyer reviews it — width, location, permitted use, maintenance obligations — and route the effect on his plans to counsel and to the jurisdiction, before the contingency expires.

**What the licensee does not do:** call the easement routine because most properties have one; ask the title company to "remove" it, which misunderstands what an exception is; or predict that the workshop "should be fine."

An easement along the rear line can prevent exactly the structure this buyer is buying the property to build. That is not a formality.

### 3.5 Licenses

A **license** is permission to use another's land that is generally **revocable** and does not create an interest in the land.

| | Easement | License |
|---|---|---|
| Interest in land | Yes | No |
| Revocable | Generally not | Generally yes |
| Runs with the land | Yes | No |
| Requires writing | Generally yes | Not necessarily |

The neighbor who has been allowed to park in the driveway for years has, in the ordinary case, a license — not an easement. But long use can raise a prescriptive claim, which is why "we've always let them" is a sentence a licensee should route rather than resolve.

### 3.6 Termination

Easements can end by express release, by merger of the dominant and servient parcels in one owner, by expiration of a stated term, by abandonment where the requirements are met, or by the purpose becoming impossible.

**Non-use alone generally does not terminate an easement.** A client who says "nobody has used that road in twenty years, so it's gone" has stated a conclusion the law usually does not reach. Route it.

### 3.7 A worked easement problem

> **The property.** A residential lot. The preliminary report lists: a public utility easement along the rear ten feet, recorded 1958; an easement for ingress and egress in favor of the adjoining parcel, recorded 1974, described as a twenty-foot strip along the northern boundary; and a drainage easement in favor of the county, recorded 1991.
>
> **The buyer intends to build a detached workshop in the rear yard and to fence the north side.**
>
> **What each easement means for his plan.**
>
> **The utility easement.** Section 3.2's easement in gross — it benefits a utility rather than a neighboring parcel. **A structure built within it is a structure built where someone else has a recorded right of access**, and the practical consequence is not theoretical: excavation, repair, or replacement can require removal.
>
> **The ingress and egress easement.** Appurtenant, benefiting the adjoining parcel, and **it runs with the land** — meaning it does not end because the current neighbor is friendly or because the strip is not currently used. **The fence the buyer plans would obstruct a recorded right of access**, which is the fact pattern of a neighbor dispute and possibly of an injunction.
>
> **The drainage easement.** Benefiting a public entity, with its own scope defined by the instrument.
>
> **What the licensee does — and Section 3.4 defines the boundary precisely.**
>
> **He obtains the recorded instruments**, not the one-line summaries in the report. **The summaries state that an easement exists; the instruments state what it permits, where exactly it runs, and what it prohibits.** This is the single most valuable thing a licensee does with a preliminary report and the thing most often skipped.
>
> **He tells the buyer his stated plans are affected by items in the report**, identifies which ones, and recommends in writing that he review them with counsel and, for location, obtain a survey.
>
> **He recommends the buyer confirm with the jurisdiction** what may be built where, because Lesson 06's public controls operate independently of these private ones — **and a plan can be defeated by either.**
>
> **What the licensee does not do.**
>
> **He does not interpret the instruments.** Whether the 1974 easement permits a gate, whether the 1958 easement has been abandoned by non-use, and whether anything about the arrangement has changed by operation of law are **legal questions**, and Section 3.6's termination doctrines are exactly the kind of analysis a licensee must not perform for a client.
>
> **He does not reassure.** "That easement is ancient, nobody uses it" is the sentence that appears in the complaint.
>
> **The professional summary.** **The buyer is buying a lot on which he cannot do two of the things he intends without answering questions first.** He is entitled to know that before he removes his contingencies, and the licensee's contribution is that he learns it on day three rather than after closing.

### 3.9 How easements are created, and how they end

Section 3.2 defined the easement. Its creation and termination are where the practical questions sit, because an easement nobody granted in writing is the one that surprises a buyer.

**Creation.**

| Method | How it arises | What a licensee sees |
|---|---|---|
| **Express grant or reservation** | The owner grants it, or reserves it when conveying | A recorded instrument; a Schedule B exception |
| **Implication** | A prior use, apparent and continuous, reasonably necessary, existing when a parcel was divided | Nothing recorded — a driveway or a sewer line crossing a neighbour |
| **Necessity** | A parcel is landlocked by a division of formerly common ownership | A parcel with no frontage on any road |
| **Prescription** | Open, notorious, hostile, continuous use for the statutory period | A neighbour who has used the driveway for twenty years |
| **Dedication** | An owner offers land for public use and it is accepted | Streets and public ways on a subdivision map |

**The last three create rights nobody recorded**, which is why the standard title policy excepts *"any facts an accurate survey would disclose"* and why Assignment 09 Section 4.9 insists that possession is notice.

**Termination.**

**Express release.** The dominant owner releases it, in writing, recorded.

**Merger.** The dominant and servient tenements come into the same ownership. An easement over one's own land is not an easement — and note the consequence: **if the parcels are later separated again, the easement does not automatically revive.**

**Abandonment.** Non-use alone is generally not enough; there must be conduct showing intent to abandon.

**Expiration.** Where the instrument sets a term or a condition that has occurred.

**End of necessity.** An easement by necessity ends when the necessity does — when the landlocked parcel gains other access.

### 3.10 A worked easement problem

> A buyer is purchasing a rural parcel. The preliminary report shows a recorded 20-foot access easement from the county road across a neighbouring parcel. On inspection, the actual driveway runs about 60 feet to the north of where the recorded easement is described, and has evidently done so for many years — there is mature landscaping along it and a culvert under it.

**The buyer has two different access questions and only one of them is answered by the recorded easement.**

**The recorded easement is real** and gives a legal right of access — over ground that has no road on it. Building a driveway within the recorded easement would mean grading a new route, and the neighbour's landscaping and outbuildings may be in the way.

**The driveway in use is not within the recorded easement.** Its legal basis, if any, would be prescription, implication, or an unrecorded agreement — none of which appears in the report and none of which a licensee can determine.

**Three actions, in order.** Obtain the recorded easement instrument and read its description. Have a surveyor locate both the recorded easement and the existing driveway on the ground. **Then send the result to counsel**, because whether the buyer may keep using the existing driveway is a legal question with a factual foundation that only the survey supplies.

**What makes this urgent rather than interesting.** If the buyer's use of the actual driveway rests on prescription against the neighbour, **the purchase itself may affect it**, and a neighbour who has tolerated one family's use for twenty years may take a different view of a new owner. **The time to find out is during the contingency period.**

**The failure mode to avoid** is the reassuring summary: *"there's a recorded easement, you have access."* That sentence is true, is what the report says, and completely misses what is actually happening on the ground.

### 3.11 Checkpoint

You should be able to identify the dominant and servient parcels, distinguish an easement from a license, and state the correct handling of an easement shown in a preliminary report.

## 4. Deed restrictions and covenants

### 4.1 What they are

**Private restrictions** limit what may be done with land, created by agreement and recorded so that they bind successive owners.

They appear as conditions or restrictions in individual deeds, and — far more commonly in modern practice — as a recorded **declaration of covenants, conditions, and restrictions** governing a subdivision or common interest development.

### 4.2 Private restrictions versus zoning

This distinction is worth stating precisely, because clients get it wrong in a costly direction.

| | Private restrictions | Zoning |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Private agreement, recorded | Government, by ordinance |
| Enforced by | Owners, or the association | The jurisdiction |
| Changed by | The procedure in the instrument, often requiring owner approval | The land use process |
| Effect on each other | **Neither overrides the other** | |

> **The controlling proposition: where zoning and a recorded restriction differ, the more restrictive generally governs in practice.**
>
> Zoning permitting a duplex does not authorize one where the recorded restrictions prohibit it. A restriction permitting a second unit does not authorize one where zoning prohibits it. A buyer must satisfy both.

A licensee who tells a buyer the property is "zoned for it, so you're fine" has answered half the question.

### 4.3 What restrictions commonly cover

Use — residential only, no business, no short-term rental. Building — setbacks, height, materials, minimum size, architectural approval. Appearance — paint, landscaping, fencing, visible vehicles. Occupancy and animals. Rental limits, including caps on the number of rented units, which can affect both an investor buyer and financing availability.

### 4.4 Enforcement

Private restrictions are enforced by those entitled to enforce them — other owners in the scheme, or the association. Remedies commonly include injunctive relief and, in an association context, the association's own enforcement mechanisms.

**Restrictions that violate law are not enforceable.** Racially restrictive covenants, discussed in Lesson 14, still appear in the recorded language of older title chains and are unenforceable and void. Their presence in a chain of title is not a defect in the seller's title, and California provides a process for their restrictive language to be addressed.

A licensee encountering such language should recognize what it is, not repeat it, and route the question of what may be done about it.

### 4.5 What a licensee may and may not do

**May:** identify that restrictions exist; obtain the recorded documents; ensure the buyer receives and reviews them; point out that they bind him regardless of zoning; recommend counsel where their effect on his plans matters; and document the recommendation.

**May not:** summarize the declaration for the buyer as though the summary were the document; opine on whether a particular use is permitted; predict whether an association will approve something; or characterize a restriction as unenforceable.

> **A buyer asks: "Can I run my business out of the garage?"**
>
> **Correct:** "There are two separate questions — what the city allows and what the recorded restrictions allow, and you have to satisfy both. Here are the CC&Rs; the use article starts on page eleven. The city's planning counter answers the zoning half. I'd get both answers before your contingency expires, and I'll note that we discussed it."
>
> **Incorrect:** "It's a quiet business, nobody will care."

### 4.6 Checkpoint

You should be able to explain why zoning permission is not sufficient, and answer a permitted-use question correctly.

## 5. Encroachments, priority, and recording

### 5.1 Encroachments

An **encroachment** is an improvement extending onto another's land — a fence, an eave, a driveway, a retaining wall, a structure.

Encroachments raise a mixture of questions no licensee can resolve: whose land it is on, whether the encroaching owner has acquired rights through long use, whether removal can be compelled, and what the effect is on marketability.

**The licensee's obligations:** disclose what is observed; recommend a survey where the facts call for one; recommend counsel where a claim may exist; document; and ensure it happens before the contingency expires.

**What the licensee must not do:** state where the boundary is, characterize the encroachment as minor, predict whether the neighbor has a claim, advise moving a fence to resolve it, or tell a buyer that decades of use have settled the question.

### 5.2 The recording system

California maintains a public record of documents affecting real property, and the system exists to answer one question: **who has notice of what?**

| Type of notice | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Actual notice** | The person in fact knows |
| **Constructive notice** | The law charges the person with knowing, because it was recorded |
| **Inquiry notice** | Facts that would prompt a reasonable person to investigate — such as someone visibly in possession who is not the record owner |

**Recording gives constructive notice to the world.** That is the whole mechanism: a recorded document is knowable by anyone who looks, so the law treats everyone as knowing it.

**Inquiry notice deserves attention** because it is the one that surprises people. A buyer who sees an occupant who is not the seller, or a well-worn path across the rear of the lot, has facts that would prompt investigation — and may be charged with what the investigation would have revealed, recorded or not.

### 5.3 Priority

The general rule: **first in time, first in right** — as measured by recording, for those entitled to the recording system's protection.

The exceptions are what matter in practice:

| Exception | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Property tax liens** | Generally take priority regardless of when they attached |
| **Mechanics liens** | Priority is measured from **commencement of the work** rather than from the lien's own recording date — ahead of what attached after the work began, behind what was already attached (Civil Code § 8450) |
| **Subordination agreement** | A lienholder agrees to take a lower position than it would otherwise hold |
| **Purchase-money priority** | Certain purchase-money security interests receive favored treatment |

**Subordination is the one licensees encounter most.** A construction lender frequently requires the seller who carried financing to subordinate, moving the seller behind the construction loan. Whether to agree is a significant decision with real consequences, and it belongs to counsel — not to the licensee explaining that "it's standard."

### 5.4 Why priority determines outcomes

Priority determines who is paid first from a limited fund and whose interest survives a foreclosure. A junior lienholder wiped out by a senior foreclosure has lost its security, whatever the merits of the debt.

That is why the recording date on the preliminary report is not administrative detail. **It is the order in which the property's value is claimed.**

### 5.5 What escrow and title actually do with this

Escrow obtains payoff demands and clears what must be cleared; title determines what it will insure and what it will except. Neither advises the parties, and neither decides a dispute.

A licensee's contribution is to surface issues early — the recent construction, the similar-name judgment, the unreleased deed of trust — so there is time to resolve them, and to route the questions rather than answering them.

### 5.7 Priority worked as a payment waterfall

Section 5.3 stated the priority rule — first in time, first in right, subject to statutory exceptions. This section runs it as arithmetic, because that is how it is experienced.

**The two exceptions that outrank recording order.**

**Property tax liens.** **Revenue and Taxation Code § 2192.1** puts it without qualification: every tax declared a lien on real property, and every public improvement assessment declared by law to be a lien, *"have priority over all other liens on the property, regardless of the time of their creation"* — over *"any recognizance, deed, judgment, debt, obligation, or responsibility"* the property may become charged with. A first deed of trust recorded in 2004 is junior to a 2026 property tax lien, and no recording date changes that.

**Mechanics liens are measured from commencement of the work.** **Civil Code § 8450** states the rule and its limit in one sentence:

> *"(a) A lien under this chapter, other than a lien provided for in Section 8402, has priority over a lien, mortgage, deed of trust, or other encumbrance on the work of improvement or the real property on which the work of improvement is situated, that (1) attaches **after commencement of the work of improvement** or (2) was unrecorded at the commencement of the work of improvement and of which the claimant had no notice.*
>
> *(b) Subdivision (a) is subject to the exception provided for in Section 8452."*

**Read what the section gives the lien priority over, because it is a closed list.** An encumbrance that **attached after commencement of the work**; or one that was **unrecorded when the work commenced** and of which the claimant had no notice. That is all of it. **An encumbrance already attached and of record when the first bulldozer arrived is on neither branch, and the mechanics lien does not reach it.**

**So relation-back is a measuring point, not a promotion to first position.** The lien's priority is taken from the day work commenced instead of the day the claim of lien was recorded. It moves the lien back in time; it does not move it to the front of the line. A lien whose work commenced in 2023 competes as a 2023 interest — ahead of everything that attached in 2024 and after, and behind everything already attached in 2019 and 2022.

**This is why a construction lender inspects the site before recording.** **A lender who records after work has begun has attached after commencement, is inside § 8450(a)(1), and may find itself junior to a lien not recorded for months.** A lender who records before anyone breaks ground is outside the subdivision entirely. It is also why title companies ask about recent work: the question is not whether a lien is on record, it is whether one can still arrive with an earlier date than the buyer's lender.

**Two limits the section names, and a licensee who quotes subdivision (a) alone has quoted half a rule.** § 8450(a) excepts *"a lien provided for in Section 8402"* — the lien of a person who provides work for a **site improvement** — whose priority is set separately by **§ 8458**. And § 8450(b) makes the whole rule subject to **§ 8452**, under which a mortgage or deed of trust that would otherwise be subordinate takes priority over a lien for work provided after the recording of a payment bond that refers to it and is in an amount not less than 75 percent of its principal. Neither operates in the ordinary example below.

> **The waterfall.** A property sells at a forced sale for **$742,000**, with five liens recorded against it.

| Recorded | Lien | Amount |
|---|---|---:|
| 2019 | First deed of trust | $520,000 |
| 2022 | Second deed of trust (HELOC, full line) | $150,000 |
| 2024 | Abstract of judgment | $88,000 |
| 2025 | Mechanics lien (work commenced 2023) | $61,000 |
| 2026 | Property taxes | $9,300 |

**Order of payment, and why:**

| Order | Lien | Why it sits here | Paid | Running balance |
|---|---|---|---:|---:|
| 1 | Property taxes 2026 | Priority over all other liens regardless of the time of their creation — R&T § 2192.1 | $9,300 | $732,700 |
| 2 | First deed of trust 2019 | Attached four years before the work commenced — on neither branch of § 8450(a) | $520,000 | $212,700 |
| 3 | Second deed of trust 2022 | Attached a year before the work commenced — on neither branch of § 8450(a) | $150,000 | $62,700 |
| 4 | Mechanics lien — measured from **2023** | Ahead of what attached after commencement | $61,000 | $1,700 |
| 5 | Abstract of judgment 2024 | Attached after the 2023 commencement — § 8450(a)(1) | **$1,700 of $88,000** | $0 |

**Three results worth sitting with.**

**Relation-back did not move the mechanics lien to the front, and nothing in § 8450 would have let it.** The lien was recorded last of the five and is paid fourth. The 2019 first deed of trust and the 2022 HELOC were both attached and of record in 2023 when the work commenced, so neither *"attaches after commencement"* nor was *"unrecorded at the commencement,"* and the section reaches neither. **A licensee who remembers relation-back as "the mechanics lien comes first" will get this backwards, and the mistake is expensive in exactly the case where it matters.**

**What relation-back actually did is visible in one line.** The mechanics lien was recorded in 2025 and the abstract of judgment in 2024, so on recording order alone the judgment would be paid first. It is not. The lien is measured from 2023, the judgment attached after that, and § 8450(a)(1) puts the lien ahead of it. **Change one fact — commencement in 2025 rather than 2023 — and the judgment creditor is paid in full and the contractor takes the $1,700.**

**The judgment creditor recovers $1,700 of $88,000 and the lien is extinguished by the sale.** Junior liens are wiped by a senior foreclosure; the debt survives as a personal obligation where the anti-deficiency statutes do not bar it, but the security is gone.

### 5.8 What a licensee does with a preliminary report

The waterfall above is invisible until someone reads Schedule B, and reading it is a licensee's job in the sense that **noticing** is the licensee's job.

**Four things to look for, in order.**

**Anything the seller did not mention.** A second deed of trust, an abstract of judgment, a mechanics lien, a lis pendens, a bankruptcy. Sellers routinely forget encumbrances that were resolved informally and never released of record.

**Anything with a date close to the present.** A recent recording is a live situation. A mechanics lien recorded last month means a contractor is unpaid **now**, and there may be more coming from other trades on the same job.

**Anything naming a person who is not the seller.** A judgment against a former owner with the same surname; a lien against a business the seller once operated. Some of these are genuine and some are name-match errors, and **the difference is resolved by a title officer, not by the licensee's judgment about which it looks like.**

**Anything about boundaries, access or easements.** These do not get paid off at closing. They travel with the land, and they change what the buyer is buying.

**And one habit that costs nothing.** **Send the preliminary report to the buyer with the items numbered and a sentence asking which they want explained.** It converts a document that is routinely unread into a document with a record of delivery — and if the buyer later says nobody told them about the easement, the email is the answer.

### 5.9 Checkpoint

You should be able to explain constructive and inquiry notice, state the priority rule and its principal exceptions, and describe the correct handling of an encroachment.

## 6. Title exceptions and risk allocation

### 6.1 What a preliminary report is

A **preliminary report** reports the condition of title as shown in the records and states **the terms on which the title insurer would be willing to issue a policy**, including the exceptions it would take.

Three things it is not:

- **Not a guarantee** that title is clear.
- **Not the policy.** It states terms; the policy is the contract.
- **Not a survey.** It addresses record title, not the physical property.

### 6.2 What an exception means

The single most useful sentence in this lesson:

> **An exception is an item the policy will not insure against. The buyer bears that risk unless it is otherwise addressed.**

That is why exceptions are read rather than skimmed, and why the underlying recorded documents must be obtained. An exception naming a recorded easement tells the buyer that an easement exists; only the document tells him where it runs, how wide it is, and what it permits.

### 6.3 Exclusions and requirements

Two related concepts:

**Exclusions** are categories the policy form does not cover at all — commonly including governmental regulation such as zoning, the exercise of eminent domain, and matters created or agreed to by the insured.

**Requirements** are what the insurer needs before issuing — a reconveyance, a resolution, a release, evidence of authority.

A licensee reading a preliminary report should distinguish the three: **exceptions** are risks not insured, **exclusions** are categories outside the policy, and **requirements** are things that must be done before closing. The requirements are the ones with deadlines attached.

### 6.4 Reading the report item by item

| Item | Correct handling |
|---|---|
| Utility easement along the rear line | Obtain the document; width, location, permitted use. Can prevent structures the buyer intends |
| Recorded CC&Rs | Obtain and review; bind the buyer regardless of zoning |
| Unreleased deed of trust the seller says was paid | A recorded reconveyance must be obtained; recollection does not clear the record |
| Mechanic's lien | Route to escrow and title; note that priority may relate back |
| Judgment against a similarly named person | Route to title for resolution; do not assume it is or is not the seller |
| Lis pendens | Recognize as a serious cloud; route to counsel |
| Item the buyer does not understand | Refer |

**Three responses that are wrong in every case:** treating an item as routine because most properties have one; asking the title company to "remove" an exception; and predicting the practical effect of an easement or restriction on the buyer's plans.

### 6.5 Owner's and lender's policies

The **owner's policy** insures the buyer's interest. The **lender's policy** insures the lender's lien and its priority. They insure different interests, which is why both commonly exist, and why a buyer who declines an owner's policy because "there's a title policy on the loan" has misunderstood what he is not buying.

**Extended coverage** may address matters a standard policy excepts, including some survey matters, typically requiring a survey or other evidence. Whether to obtain it is the buyer's decision, informed.

### 6.6 The timing point

The preliminary report and the underlying documents belong at the front of the transaction queue, with the association documents and the hazard-area insurance quotes.

An exception discovered late — an easement across the building site, an unreleased lien requiring a document from a lender that no longer exists — takes time to resolve. A buyer who receives the report on day three has options; one who receives it on day twelve of a fourteen-day review has a formality.

### 6.7 A worked preliminary report

> **The report** on a suburban residence, in the order the items appear:
>
> 1. **General and special taxes for the current fiscal year**, a lien not yet due and payable.
> 2. **A supplemental assessment.**
> 3. **A bond or assessment district obligation.**
> 4. **Covenants, conditions and restrictions recorded 1964**, with a note that any provision restricting on a protected basis is void and unenforceable.
> 5. **An easement for public utilities**, 1964.
> 6. **A deed of trust**, 2019, securing an original amount, in favor of a named lender.
> 7. **A second deed of trust**, 2021, in favor of a different lender.
> 8. **A notice of a pending action** affecting the property, recorded four months ago.
> 9. **A judgment against a person whose name is similar to the seller's.**
> 10. **Requirements**: satisfactory evidence of the authority of the parties, and a statement regarding parties in possession.
>
> **How a licensee reads this, applying Sections 6.2 through 6.4.**
>
> **Items 1 through 3 are cost items**, and the buyer's monthly figure has probably not included the third. **Section 6.4's discipline is to read every item rather than the ones that look important.**
>
> **Item 4 is a private restriction**, and it is separate from zoning. Section 4.2. **The unenforceable-provision note is standard and does not make the remaining provisions unenforceable** — which is precisely the misreading a licensee must not encourage. **Obtain the document; route its interpretation.**
>
> **Item 5** is located from the instrument rather than assumed.
>
> **Items 6 and 7 are monetary liens** expected to be paid at closing. **Two deeds of trust means two payoff demands**, and Lesson 10 of the practice course explains why the good-through date on each matters.
>
> **Item 8 is the most serious item on the report.** A recorded notice of a pending action puts the world on notice of a claim affecting the property. **It is not cleared by paying it**, its resolution depends on the underlying matter, and it can affect insurability and financing. **This is escalated to the broker and the title officer immediately** — and it raises a disclosure question for the seller that the licensee does not answer alone.
>
> **Item 9 is the similar-name judgment.** Section 5.3's priority rules make this consequential if it is the seller. **The title company resolves it through its process**, and the licensee's contribution is flagging it early rather than discovering on day twenty-five that clearance takes time.
>
> **Item 10's requirements are not exceptions.** Section 6.3's distinction. **They are conditions the title company will insist on before issuing**, and the authority requirement is Lesson 03's ownership question arriving from the title side.
>
> **What the licensee does with the whole document.** Deliver it promptly with delivery evidenced; **obtain the underlying recorded instruments for items 4 and 5**; flag items 8, 9, and 10 to escrow, the broker, and the client in writing; recommend review with counsel; and record what was recommended and when.
>
> **What he does not do.** Rank the items by seriousness for the client as legal advice, tell the buyer item 8 "will probably clear," characterize the 1964 restrictions, or forward the report with "let me know if you have questions." **Section 6.6's timing point is the reason:** a report delivered without the recommendation that it be reviewed has been delivered in a way that does not produce a review.

### 6.8 Checkpoint

You should be able to explain what an exception means, distinguish exceptions from exclusions and requirements, and state the correct response to each item on a preliminary report.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **Encumbrance** | A right or interest held by someone other than the owner, affecting title or use. Travels with the property. |
| **Money encumbrance (lien)** | An encumbrance securing a debt against the property. |
| **Non-money encumbrance** | An encumbrance affecting use — easements, recorded restrictions, encroachments. |
| **Voluntary lien** | Created by the owner's agreement, typically a deed of trust. |
| **Involuntary lien** | Arising by operation of law without consent — taxes, judgments, mechanic's liens. |
| **Specific lien** | Attaching to a particular property. |
| **General lien** | Attaching to all of the debtor's property in the jurisdiction, including property acquired later while effective. |
| **Reconveyance** | The recorded document clearing a satisfied deed of trust. **Payment of the debt does not clear the record** — a document does. |
| **Property tax lien priority** | Generally takes priority regardless of when it attached. The principal exception to first-in-time. |
| **Special assessment** | A levy for improvements benefiting particular property. Involuntary, specific, and inherited by the buyer. |
| **Mechanic's lien** | Securing payment for labor or materials improving real property. **Priority may relate back** to when work commenced, and rights can arise for parties the owner never dealt with. |
| **Judgment lien** | A money judgment made a lien on the debtor's real property in a county by recording an abstract. Involuntary and general. |
| **Lis pendens** | A recorded notice of pending litigation affecting title. Not a lien, and a powerful cloud. |
| **Homestead exemption** | Protection of a portion of a homeowner's equity from certain creditors. Does not protect against voluntary liens the owner granted or against tax liens. Effect is a legal question. |
| **Easement** | A non-possessory right to use another's land for a specified purpose. Runs with the land. |
| **Dominant tenement** | The parcel **benefited** by an easement appurtenant. |
| **Servient tenement** | The parcel **burdened** — it *serves* the other. |
| **Easement appurtenant** | Benefits a parcel and passes with it automatically on transfer. |
| **Easement in gross** | Benefits a person or entity rather than a parcel — a utility line easement. |
| **Easement by necessity** | Arising where a parcel would otherwise be landlocked by the division that created it. |
| **Easement by prescription** | Arising from long use meeting the legal requirements. A doctrine to recognize and refer. |
| **License** | Revocable permission to use another's land, creating no interest and not running with the land. |
| **Non-use** | Generally does **not** terminate an easement. |
| **Private restrictions (CC&Rs)** | Recorded limits on use binding successive owners. Neither overrides zoning nor is overridden by it — **the more restrictive generally governs.** |
| **Encroachment** | An improvement extending onto another's land. Disclose, recommend a survey, refer — never characterize. |
| **Constructive notice** | Knowledge the law charges a person with because a document was recorded. |
| **Inquiry notice** | Knowledge charged from facts that would prompt investigation — an occupant who is not the record owner, a worn path across the lot. |
| **First in time, first in right** | The general priority rule, measured by recording. |
| **Subordination agreement** | A lienholder's agreement to take a lower position than it would otherwise hold. A significant decision for counsel, not a formality. |
| **Preliminary report** | A report of record title stating the terms on which a policy would issue. Not a guarantee, not the policy, not a survey. |
| **Exception** | An item the policy **will not insure against**. The buyer bears that risk unless otherwise addressed. |
| **Exclusion** | A category the policy form does not cover at all — governmental regulation, eminent domain, matters created by the insured. |
| **Requirement** | Something the insurer needs before issuing. The items with deadlines attached. |

## Practice problems

### Problem 1

Classify each on both axes and state one practical consequence of the classification: (a) a deed of
trust; (b) a property tax lien; (c) a judgment lien; (d) a mechanic's lien.

**Answer.**

> **Solution.** (a) **Voluntary and specific.** Created by the owner's agreement and attached to this
> property. Consequence: it can be sold subject to, or paid from proceeds at closing — which is why the
> payoff demand, including accrued interest, is obtained rather than assumed from the balance.
>
> (b) **Involuntary and specific.** Consequence: it generally takes **priority regardless of when it
> attached**, which is the principal exception to first-in-time and exists because the taxing power
> does not depend on the order private parties recorded.
>
> (c) **Involuntary and general.** Consequence: it reaches **every parcel the debtor owns in the
> county**, including property acquired later while the lien is effective — which is why a buyer with an
> outstanding judgment discovers it attaching to the property he is buying, and why a judgment against
> a similarly named person must be resolved through title rather than assumed away.
>
> (d) **Involuntary and specific.** Consequence: its priority **may relate back** to when work
> commenced, so it can outrank instruments recorded before it.

### Problem 2

A seller renovated the kitchen and rebuilt the deck last spring. He is in an unresolved dispute with
the general contractor over the final $18,000, and says two subcontractors he never met have been
calling. The preliminary report is clean.

**What is the exposure, and what does the licensee do?**

> **Solution.** **A clean report is a snapshot, not a forecast.** Mechanic's lien exposure is exactly
> what a current report can fail to show.
>
> **Two features make this dangerous.** Priority **may relate back** to when work commenced, so a lien
> recorded next month can outrank a deed of trust recorded before it — which is why lenders care about
> recent construction. And lien rights arise **from work, not from a contract with the owner**: the
> subcontractors and suppliers the seller never dealt with can have rights of their own.
>
> **What the licensee does:** ask the intake question — *has any work been done recently, by whom, and
> is everyone paid?* — route it to escrow, title, and counsel immediately, and ensure it is addressed
> before the contingency expires.
>
> **What the licensee does not do:** advise whether a lien would be valid or timely, or reassure the
> buyer that the dispute is between the seller and his contractor. The statutory scheme's notices and
> deadlines are legal questions.

### Problem 3

A preliminary report shows a ten-foot utility easement along the rear property line. The buyer is
purchasing the lot specifically to build a workshop at the rear. The agent tells him easements are
routine, offers to ask title to remove it, and says the workshop should be fine.

**Assess all three responses.**

> **Solution.** All three are wrong, and the third is the one the buyer will rely on.
>
> **"Routine" is not an analysis.** Most properties have easements; this one may prevent exactly the
> structure the buyer is buying the property to build.
>
> **Title cannot "remove" an exception**, and asking misunderstands what an exception is: **an item the
> policy will not insure against.** It is not a blemish the insurer chose to note.
>
> **Predicting the effect on his plans is outside the license**, and it is a legal and land-use question
> at once.
>
> **What the licensee does:** obtain the underlying recorded document — width, location, permitted use,
> maintenance obligations — ensure the buyer reviews it, and route the effect on his plans to counsel
> and to the jurisdiction **before the contingency expires.** The exception names the easement; only the
> document says where it runs.

### Problem 4

A neighbor has parked in the seller's driveway for eleven years with permission. The seller says he
can revoke it any time. The buyer's agent says eleven years means the neighbor now has an easement.
Separately, an old access road across the property has not been used in twenty years, and the seller
says it is therefore gone.

**Assess each statement.**

> **Solution.** Three statements, and all three are conclusions a licensee must not deliver.
>
> **The permission is, in the ordinary case, a license** — revocable permission creating no interest in
> the land and not running with it. So far the seller's description is the usual one.
>
> **But "eleven years" raises a prescriptive claim**, which is a legal doctrine with requirements
> neither agent can evaluate — and permission itself bears on the analysis. Neither "it's just a
> license" nor "he has an easement now" is a statement a licensee can make.
>
> **Non-use alone generally does not terminate an easement.** Easements end by express release, merger,
> expiration of a stated term, abandonment where the requirements are met, or impossibility of purpose
> — not by twenty years of nobody driving on it.
>
> **What transfers:** "we've always let them" and "nobody has used it in years" are both sentences a
> licensee **routes** rather than resolves. Disclose the facts, obtain the documents, and refer.

### Problem 5

A buyer wants to add a second dwelling unit. The city's zoning permits it. The recorded CC&Rs prohibit
more than one dwelling per lot. The agent tells him he is "zoned for it, so you're fine," and adds that
the restriction is probably unenforceable because nobody has enforced it in years.

**Correct both.**

> **Solution.** **Zoning permission answers half the question.** Private restrictions and zoning come
> from different sources, are enforced by different parties, and are changed by different procedures —
> and **neither overrides the other.** In practice the **more restrictive governs**: zoning permitting a
> duplex does not authorize one where the recorded restrictions prohibit it. The buyer must satisfy
> both.
>
> **The enforceability opinion is a legal conclusion the licensee cannot give**, and it is the kind a
> buyer will spend money relying on. Restrictions are enforced by those entitled to enforce them —
> other owners in the scheme, or the association — and non-enforcement history is a legal argument, not
> a fact the licensee can assert.
>
> **The correct answer:** *"There are two separate questions — what the city allows and what the
> recorded restrictions allow, and you have to satisfy both. Here are the CC&Rs; the use article starts
> on page eleven. The city's planning counter answers the zoning half. Get both answers before your
> contingency expires, and I'll note that we discussed it."*

### Problem 6

A buyer notices a well-worn path crossing the rear of the lot and an occupant in the guest house who
is not the seller. The seller says the occupant is "just a friend staying temporarily" and there is
nothing recorded about the path.

**What kind of notice is in play, and what should happen?**

> **Solution.** **Inquiry notice** — the one that surprises people, because nothing was recorded.
>
> The recording system answers who has notice of what. **Actual notice** is knowing in fact.
> **Constructive notice** is what the law charges a person with because a document was recorded — a
> recorded document is knowable by anyone who looks, so everyone is treated as knowing it. **Inquiry
> notice** arises from facts that would prompt a reasonable person to investigate.
>
> An occupant who is not the record owner and a worn path across the lot are the textbook facts. The
> buyer may be charged with **what a reasonable investigation would have revealed**, recorded or not —
> which could include an unrecorded lease, a claimed prescriptive right, or an occupancy the seller
> cannot terminate on schedule.
>
> **What should happen:** the facts are disclosed and investigated rather than explained away. Ask for
> any written agreement with the occupant, route the path to a survey and to counsel, and do not accept
> "just a friend" as a description of a possessory interest.

### Problem 7

A seller carrying financing on a parcel is asked to sign a subordination agreement so a construction
lender can record in first position. His agent tells him it is standard and to go ahead. Separately,
the buyer declines an owner's title policy because "the lender is getting a title policy anyway."

**Assess both.**

> **Solution.** **Subordination is a significant decision, not a formality.** The seller is agreeing to
> take a **lower position than he would otherwise hold**, behind a construction loan — and priority
> determines who is paid first from a limited fund and whose interest survives a foreclosure. A junior
> lienholder wiped out by a senior foreclosure has lost its security whatever the merits of the debt.
> Whether to sign belongs to **counsel**, and "it's standard" is the single most expensive sentence a
> licensee can say about it.
>
> **The owner's and lender's policies insure different interests.** The lender's policy insures the
> lender's lien and its priority — **not the buyer's ownership.** A buyer who declines an owner's policy
> has not economized on a duplicate; he has left his own interest uninsured.
>
> Worth adding while the subject is open: **extended coverage** may address matters a standard policy
> excepts, including some survey matters, typically requiring a survey or other evidence. Whether to
> obtain it is the buyer's decision, made informed.

### Problem 8

A preliminary report received on day twelve of a fourteen-day review shows: recorded CC&Rs; an
unreleased deed of trust the seller says was paid in 2016; a judgment against a person with a name
similar to the seller's; a lis pendens; and a requirement for a corporate resolution.

**Handle each, and state what went wrong before any of it.**

> **Solution.** **The timing failure came first**, and it is the one that made everything else harder.
> The preliminary report and its underlying documents belong at the **front** of the queue with the
> association documents and hazard-area insurance quotes. An exception discovered on day twelve — an
> unreleased lien needing a document from a lender that may no longer exist — leaves a formality where
> a review should have been.
>
> **The CC&Rs:** obtain and review; they bind the buyer regardless of zoning.
>
> **The unreleased deed of trust:** a recorded **reconveyance** must be obtained. Payment of the debt
> does not clear the record; a document does, and the seller's recollection is not that document.
>
> **The similar-name judgment:** route to title for resolution. Do not assume it is or is not the
> seller — a judgment lien is general and reaches everything the debtor owns in the county.
>
> **The lis pendens:** recognize it as a serious cloud. It is not itself a lien, but it gives notice
> that pending litigation may affect anyone acquiring an interest, which in practice stops
> transactions. Route to counsel immediately.
>
> **The resolution:** this is a **requirement**, not an exception — something the insurer needs before
> issuing, and therefore the item with a deadline attached. Distinguish the three throughout:
> **exceptions** are risks not insured, **exclusions** are categories outside the policy, and
> **requirements** must be satisfied before closing.

## Chapter summary

**An encumbrance affects title or use.** Liens secure debts; easements, restrictions, and encroachments
govern what may be done.

**Classify liens on two axes** — voluntary or involuntary, general or specific — and read the practical
consequence off the classification.

**A lien does not prevent a sale**; it is ordinarily paid from proceeds, which is why payoff figures
exceed principal balances.

**Payment does not clear the record.** A recorded reconveyance does.

**Property tax liens generally take priority regardless of timing, and mechanic's lien priority may
relate back** — the two exceptions that defeat the first-recorded intuition. Recent construction is an
intake question.

**An easement is a non-possessory right that runs with the land.** The servient parcel serves the
dominant one; appurtenant easements pass with the dominant parcel automatically. Non-use generally does
not terminate one, and long permitted use is a doctrine to route rather than resolve.

**A license is revocable, creates no interest, and does not run with the land** — but "we've always let
them" belongs to counsel.

**Zoning and recorded restrictions are independent, and the more restrictive governs.** "Zoned for it,
so you're fine" answers half the question, and enforceability is never the licensee's call.

**Encroachments are disclosed, surveyed, and referred — never characterized.**

**Recording answers who has notice.** Constructive notice comes from the record; **inquiry notice**
comes from facts on the ground that would prompt investigation.

**Priority determines who is paid first and whose interest survives foreclosure**, which is why
subordination is a decision for counsel rather than a standard formality.

**An exception is a risk the policy will not insure against.** Read exceptions, obtain the underlying
documents, distinguish exceptions from exclusions and requirements, and never treat an item as routine,
ask for its removal, or predict its effect.

**Order the report early.** Late discovery converts a review into a formality.

## Required applied assignment

Prepare a one-page file memorandum for the applied scenario presented with this assignment. Classify each encumbrance on both axes, state how each arose and what would remove it, identify the priority questions and any exception to first-in-time that applies, distinguish the exceptions from the exclusions and requirements on the report given, state what you would obtain and in what order and by what deadline, identify what you would route to title, to a surveyor, and to counsel, and state what you would decline to characterize and why. The instructor version contains the scoring rubric.

## Knowledge check and remediation

Complete the five-item lesson quiz in the secure learning platform. The lesson mastery threshold is 80 percent — four of the five items. An unsuccessful attempt opens targeted remediation: an alternate explanation of the concepts, a second worked scenario, and a different five-item set covering the same concepts. The 80 percent threshold is a StateCourse instructional policy proposed for DRE review; it is not represented as a DRE-mandated score.

Completion requires all six instructional segments, the applied memorandum, the quiz submission, and any triggered remediation. Assignment completion does not waive the separate final examination, minimum elapsed-time, identity, proctor, or approval requirements.
