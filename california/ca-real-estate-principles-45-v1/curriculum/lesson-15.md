## 1. Lease formation and tenant interests

### 1.1 What a lease conveys

A lease conveys a **leasehold estate** — possession for a period — while the owner retains the **reversion.** Lesson 03 classified the four leasehold estates by how each terminates; this section addresses the agreement that creates one.

**The parties:** the **lessor** (landlord) conveys possession; the **lessee** (tenant) receives it.

### 1.2 The elements

A lease should identify the parties, describe the premises, state the term, state the rent and when it is payable, and reflect the parties' agreement on the operative terms.

**Writing.** Under the Statute of Frauds, **a lease exceeding one year must generally be in writing.** A shorter lease may be enforceable orally — and the professional practice is to write every lease, because the boundary is not obvious in the moment and because an oral lease proves nothing about its terms.

### 1.3 The operative terms

| Term | What it governs |
|---|---|
| **Term and renewal** | Duration, and whether and how it extends |
| **Rent** | Amount, due date, place and method, late charges |
| **Security deposit** | Amount, purpose, and the accounting on termination |
| **Use** | What the premises may be used for |
| **Maintenance and repair** | Which party is responsible for what |
| **Utilities** | Who pays |
| **Entry** | The landlord's right, purpose, notice, and timing |
| **Alterations** | What the tenant may change, and restoration |
| **Assignment and subletting** | Whether permitted, and on what conditions |
| **Default and remedies** | What happens on breach |
| **Subordination and attornment** | The lease's position relative to financing, and to a successor owner |

### 1.4 Assignment and subletting

Students merge these, and the difference has consequences.

| | Assignment | Sublease |
|---|---|---|
| What transfers | The **entire remaining interest** | **Less than the entire** remaining interest |
| Relationship created | The assignee deals with the landlord | The subtenant deals with the original tenant |
| Original tenant's liability | **Generally remains liable** unless released | Remains liable in full |

**In both cases the original tenant generally remains liable**, which is the point tenants miss. A tenant who "assigned the lease and walked away" has usually not walked away from anything.

### 1.5 Habitability

A residential landlord must maintain the property in a habitable condition. The obligation covers the elements making a dwelling fit to live in — weather protection, plumbing, hot and cold water, heating, electrical, sanitary facilities, and structural integrity, among others.

Two features shape the response:

1. **It is not waivable by agreement.** A lease clause purporting to relieve the landlord does not.
2. **It runs continuously**, not only at move-in.

Where the obligation goes unmet, a tenant may have remedies. **The exposure grows with delay, and a documented prompt response is the defense.**

### 1.6 Quiet enjoyment and entry

The tenant holds a **possessory right**, and entry requires:

1. **A permitted purpose** — necessary repairs, agreed services, showing the unit, permitted inspection, or emergency.
2. **Proper notice**, in the manner and within the time the law requires.
3. **A reasonable time** of day.
4. **Documentation** that notice was given.

**What does not substitute for notice:** the tenant being home, the owner's authorization, the manager holding a key, or the convenience of a contractor's schedule.

### 1.7 Termination and self-help

Termination and enforcement procedures are formal and unforgiving. **A good reason delivered by a defective notice fails**, and the landlord starts over, having lost weeks and improved the tenant's position.

The elements that must be right: the **type** of notice, its **content**, the **period** it gives, **who is named**, how it is **served**, and the **proof of service**. Impeccable documentation of the tenant's conduct does not cure a defect in any of them.

> **Self-help is prohibited.** Changing locks, removing doors, shutting off utilities, or removing belongings to force a tenant out is unlawful, with **no exception** for a tenant who is badly behaved, not paying, or apparently gone — and where a unit appears abandoned there is a legal process for that too.

**Retaliation** is separately prohibited. Enforcement action close in time to a tenant's complaint or assertion of a right can look retaliatory, and the protection is a **documented, independent basis** predating or unconnected to the protected activity — which is another reason the maintenance log matters.

### 1.8 A lease survives a sale

The buyer of a leased property takes **subject to the lease on its terms.** "The tenants will be out by closing" is a representation to verify against the leases, not to accept — along with the deposits held and their amounts, notices already given, and any written agreement to vacate.

### 1.9 Checkpoint

You should be able to distinguish assignment from subletting, state the entry requirements, and explain why a defective notice defeats a good reason.

## 2. Property management agreements

### 2.1 Management is licensed activity

Managing real property for another for compensation is licensed activity. It is performed **for and under the employing broker**, the funds handled are **trust funds**, and the records are **the brokerage's records.**

Management also differs from sales in a way that shapes the risk: **it is continuous.** A sale ends. A tenancy runs for years, generating funds monthly, decisions weekly, and exposure continuously. **A defect in a sales file is one transaction; a defect in a management practice repeats every month across every unit.**

### 2.2 What the agreement establishes

| Term | What it governs |
|---|---|
| **Parties** | The owner and the **brokerage** |
| **Property** | Exactly which units are managed |
| **Term and termination** | Duration, notice to terminate, what happens on termination |
| **Scope of authority** | What the manager may do without asking, and what requires owner approval |
| **Spending authority** | The approval threshold, and emergency authority |
| **Compensation** | Management, leasing, renewal, and any other fees |
| **Trust funds** | Where funds are held and how they are accounted for |
| **Reporting** | What the owner receives and how often |
| **Owner obligations** | Funding reserves, maintaining insurance, providing disclosures |
| **Insurance and indemnity** | Coverage each party maintains |
| **Fair housing** | The parties' obligations, and the manager's authority to refuse unlawful instructions |

### 2.3 Unsigned authority is a structural defect

A manager acting without a signed agreement is acting **without established authority.** That is not a paperwork gap:

- The scope of authority is undefined, so every decision is arguable.
- The compensation is unenforceable.
- The handling of funds is not authorized in writing.
- The owner can dispute any action taken.

**An audit finding two owners with no signed agreement has found a defect of the same order as commingling**, not a filing oversight.

### 2.4 Spending authority and emergencies

The most practically important term is the approval threshold, paired with **emergency authority.**

Emergencies do not wait for approval. A burst pipe, a failed heater in cold weather, a security failure — these require action, and the agreement should say so, define what counts, and require prompt notice afterward.

**A manager without written emergency authority faces a genuine dilemma:** act and be second-guessed, or wait while damage compounds and a habitability obligation runs. **Settling it in the agreement removes the dilemma.**

### 2.5 Tenant selection

The defensible practice, and the only one that survives review:

1. **Written criteria**, established before applications are taken.
2. **Objective standards** — income relative to rent, credit history, rental history, verifiable identity, lawful occupancy standards.
3. **Applied identically to every applicant.**
4. **Documented** — how each application was evaluated against each criterion.
5. **Disclosed** to applicants before they apply, along with any application fee.

**Uniformity is not merely fair; it is what makes a decision explainable afterward in neutral terms.**

The failures from Lesson 14 apply in full: impression-based selection, compatibility judgments, referral preference, differential strictness, and **discouragement** — telling an applicant they probably would not qualify instead of taking the application, which produces a denial with no record of one.

### 2.6 Maintenance triage

Not every request is equal, and a manager who treats them uniformly responds too slowly to the ones that matter.

| Category | Examples | Response |
|---|---|---|
| **Emergency** | No water, no heat in cold weather, sewage backup, gas odor, electrical hazard, security failure, flooding | Immediate; emergency authority applies |
| **Habitability** | Failed plumbing, non-functioning heat, pest infestation, mold, roof leak | Prompt, documented |
| **Ordinary** | Appliance malfunction, cosmetic damage, minor fixtures | Scheduled within a reasonable period |
| **Cosmetic or improvement** | Repainting by preference, upgrades | Owner decision |

**A loss of water is not routine maintenance.** Requiring a written request or routing through the owner may be part of the process; **neither justifies delaying the response.**

### 2.7 Owner reporting

The manager holds and handles the owner's money. **Reporting is how the accounting duty is discharged**, which is why it must be **regular and itemized** rather than summary. An accounting that cannot be verified is not an accounting, and owner preference for less detail does not change the obligation.

Monthly: rent collected by unit with dates; delinquencies and status; itemized disbursements with supporting documentation; fees charged; reserve balance and trust funds held; **security deposits held by tenant**; maintenance requests and responses; and vacancy and leasing activity. Annually: a summary and tax reporting information.

> **An owner unreported to for four months is not an administrative lapse.** The owner has been unable to verify the handling of their own funds for a third of a year, the trust records have gone that long without external scrutiny, and if there is an error nobody has been positioned to find it.

### 2.8 Checkpoint

You should be able to state what a management agreement must establish, explain why unsigned authority is structural, and triage a set of maintenance requests.

## 3. Trust funds and records

### 3.1 What a trust fund is

**Money received in the course of licensed activity that belongs to someone else is a trust fund.**

In practice that includes: purchase deposits, rents collected for owners, security deposits, owner-provided reserves, and application fees held pending processing.

### 3.2 The three obligations

1. **Separation.** Trust funds go into a trust account or neutral depository, kept **separate from the brokerage's own funds.**
2. **Records.** A complete accounting of every receipt and disbursement, **identifying whose funds they are.**
3. **Timeliness.** Funds are placed as required, without being held personally in the interim.

**A deposit check held in a desk drawer over a weekend is a trust fund handling problem**, whatever the intention — and the requirement runs to the broker.

### 3.3 Commingling and conversion

**Commingling** is mixing trust funds with the brokerage's own funds — including depositing them into the wrong account.

**Conversion** is using trust funds for a purpose other than the one for which they are held. It is the more serious offense and is treated as such.

> **Commingling is complete when the funds are deposited in the wrong account, regardless of how carefully the total is tracked and regardless of whether anyone loses money.**

### 3.4 Why an internal ledger does not cure commingling

The most common defense a licensee offers is that funds in the operating account are tracked separately in the accounting system. It does not work, for a structural reason worth teaching:

> **Funds in a brokerage operating account are exposed to the brokerage's creditors, to its operating losses, and to ordinary business risk. A ledger entry does not protect the money. Separation is what protects it, and the record is what proves the separation was maintained.**

### 3.5 Shortages and overages

| Finding | What it means | Correct response |
|---|---|---|
| **Shortage** | Someone else's money is missing | Cure **immediately** from the broker's own funds; document the cause; reconcile |
| **Overage** | The records do not accurately reflect whose funds are held | Investigate and document the source; **the broker may not simply keep it** |

**Neither is a rounding matter.** There is no tolerance threshold, and neither may be deferred to the next reconciliation.

> A manager discovers he deposited a tenant's rent into the operating account by mistake. The correct response is to correct it immediately, **document the error and the correction**, and notify the broker so the trust records reconcile and the cause is addressed. Correcting it quietly without a record is its own problem: **trust accounting is judged by the record as much as by the balance.**

### 3.6 Reconciliation

The trust account is reconciled on a regular cycle, comparing **three** balances: the bank balance, the book balance, and **the total of the individual beneficiary ledgers.** All three must agree.

> **A reconciliation that balances bank against book but never against the beneficiary ledgers will not detect the error that matters most** — money in the account belonging to a different beneficiary than the records say. The total is right the whole time, which is exactly why it goes unnoticed.

### 3.7 Security deposits

Deposits are the tenant's funds held for defined purposes, and California regulates their handling and their return.

- **Hold them as trust funds**, separate from the brokerage's funds.
- **Document condition at move-in**, thoroughly, with dated photographs. **A deposit dispute is decided on the difference between move-in and move-out condition, and a manager without move-in documentation has no case.**
- **Distinguish damage from ordinary wear and tear.** Wear and tear is not deductible.
- **Account in writing** on termination, itemizing deductions with supporting documentation, within the required time.
- **Return the balance** within the required period.

**Deposit accounting is the single most common source of tenant complaints**, and nearly all of it is prevented by move-in documentation and an itemized written accounting.

### 3.8 Records generally

Transaction and management records belong to the **brokerage**, and the responsible broker answers for them whoever created them. They are retained for the required period, and **cancelled and failed transactions are retained too** — disproportionately the ones that later produce disputes.

**A licensee leaving a brokerage may not unilaterally take files**, redacted or not. What may be taken is governed by brokerage policy and by client direction, and that question should be settled in writing in the office policy long before anyone departs.

### 3.9 Checkpoint

You should be able to explain why a ledger does not cure commingling, name the three balances a reconciliation must agree, and state what wins a deposit dispute.

## 4. California licensing and discipline

### 4.1 The license structure

A **broker** may conduct licensed activity independently and may employ salespersons. A **salesperson** may conduct licensed activity **only for and under a responsible broker.**

**The brokerage is the party** to listings, representation agreements, leases, and management agreements. The salesperson performs the work.

What follows, restated because it recurs in every audit: cancellation of a listing is a broker-level decision; the file belongs to the brokerage; a second salesperson on the other side does not cure dual agency; **and a salesperson may not contract in her own name for licensed activity** — a common and serious property management finding.

### 4.2 Supervision

The responsible broker must exercise **reasonable supervision**, discharged by a system:

1. **Written policies.**
2. **File review**, close enough in time to catch problems before they mature.
3. **Advertising review** before publication.
4. **Trust fund controls**, including reconciliation.
5. **Training**, particularly for new licensees.
6. **An escalation path** a salesperson can actually use.

**A policy nobody follows is not supervision**, and it is not a defense for anyone. **Independent contractor status does not reduce it** — that characterization addresses the tax and compensation relationship and answers a different question.

### 4.3 Unlicensed activity

Licensed activity is defined by **what is done and whether compensation is expected**, not by a job title. Unlicensed assistants may perform administrative work; they may not solicit, negotiate, show property, discuss terms, or be compensated for referrals.

A licensee may accept compensation for licensed activity **only from their responsible broker**, and may not act for more than one responsible broker at a time.

### 4.4 What produces discipline

| Category | Typical conduct |
|---|---|
| **Misrepresentation** | Stating as fact something unverified or untrue, or concealing a material fact |
| **Trust fund violations** | Commingling, conversion, shortages, inadequate records |
| **Failure to supervise** | A broker who did not review files, enforce policy, or notice a pattern |
| **Unlicensed activity** | Performing licensed acts, or compensating an unlicensed person for them |
| **Discrimination** | Conduct violating fair housing obligations |
| **Secret profit or undisclosed conflict** | Taking a benefit the principal did not know about |
| **Dishonest dealing** | Conduct demonstrating unfitness, including outside a transaction |
| **Falsification** | Backdating, altering, or creating records |

**Most of these do not require anyone to have set out to defraud.** Careless misrepresentation and inadequate trust fund records are the ordinary cases.

### 4.5 The disciplinary process, in outline

Investigation, an accusation, an opportunity for hearing, and a decision that may **revoke, suspend, restrict, or condition** a license, or impose other discipline. Related conduct may also carry civil and criminal exposure.

**A licensee contacted by the Department should notify the broker immediately, preserve records, and not respond independently or informally.** Cooperation is expected; **improvised** cooperation without review is where licensees make statements they cannot support.

### 4.6 The Real Estate Recovery Account

Funded by license fees, the account may pay a consumer who obtains a **judgment against a licensee for fraud, misrepresentation, deceit, or conversion of trust funds** where the judgment cannot be collected from the licensee.

Two features: it is a **last resort**, available after the consumer has pursued the licensee; and **payment from it affects the licensee's license until the account is reimbursed.**

### 4.7 Errors and omissions coverage

E&O responds to claims from professional services **within its terms and limits**, and is commonly conditioned on **prompt notice of a potential claim.**

Three consequences: **late reporting can forfeit coverage**; it does not transfer responsibility for the underlying conduct; and **intentional acts are generally excluded** — coverage protects against mistakes, not against decisions to deceive.

### 4.8 What to report to the broker, promptly

Any error affecting a client's decision or a transaction's terms; any client dissatisfaction that could become a claim; any allegation of misrepresentation, discrimination, or mishandling; any contact from another party's counsel; any regulatory contact; any suspected fraud; any wire fraud incident **or near-miss**; any request to alter or backdate a record; and any unlawful instruction from a client.

**The bias should be toward reporting.** A report that turns out unnecessary costs a conversation. An unreported matter that becomes a claim costs the coverage and the credibility.

### 4.9 Checkpoint

You should be able to name the discipline categories, state what E&O does and does not do, and describe what the recovery account is and is not.

## 5. Material fact disclosure

### 5.1 The standard

> **A fact is material if it would affect a reasonable buyer's decision to buy or the price they would pay.**

Measured against a reasonable buyer — not the seller's view of importance, not how often the condition appears, and not whether the buyer could have seen it.

### 5.2 The two independent duties

**The seller** discloses known material facts affecting the value or desirability of the property, principally through the statutory transfer disclosure statement supplemented by what the property, its age, and its location require.

**The licensee** conducts a reasonably competent and diligent **visual inspection** of the reasonably and normally accessible areas and discloses material facts it reveals.

**Neither discharges the other, and a professional inspection discharges neither.**

### 5.3 Completing and handling the seller's disclosure

Four rules, each preventing a specific failure:

1. **The seller completes the seller's disclosure.** A licensee who fills it in misattributes the statement.
2. **Every item is answered.** A blank is ambiguous; **"unknown" is an honest, available, and complete answer.**
3. **Uncertain items are not answered "no."** Marking "no" asserts knowledge the seller does not have.
4. **Amend when new information arrives.**

**And where the licensee knows something the disclosure omits**, they raise it with the seller and the broker. **A licensee who delivers a disclosure they know omits a material condition has adopted the omission** — the line separating a compliant licensee from a complicit one.

### 5.4 Observation versus inference

Record what was observed, not what it means:

- **Observation:** "Brown staining approximately three feet across on the ceiling of the northwest bedroom; paint in that area appears newer than the surrounding ceiling."
- **Not a conclusion:** "There was a roof leak that has been repaired."

The first is what the licensee observed and is qualified to state. The second is an inference the licensee is not qualified to make, and it may be wrong in a way the buyer relies on.

### 5.5 Where else material facts arise

| Source | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Physical condition** | Defects, repairs, systems, hazards |
| **Legal condition** | Liens, boundary disputes, litigation, permit status, unpermitted work |
| **Use constraints** | Zoning, association restrictions, easements |
| **Location** | Mapped hazard zones, environmental conditions, proximity |
| **Transaction facts** | Pending government action, an assessment, association litigation |
| **The licensee's own knowledge** | From any source, including a prior transaction |

### 5.6 What the law limits

**California imposes specific limits on the disclosure of a prior occupant's death and on disclosure relating to a prior occupant's illness.** The limits exist for a reason worth teaching: without them, disclosure practice would operate as a channel for discrimination and would attach a permanent penalty to properties for reasons unrelated to their condition.

**Do not volunteer. Do not misrepresent. Escalate.** The precise boundary is a legal question with real consequences on both sides, and it is not one to resolve at a showing.

### 5.7 What must never be represented as fact

- A **prediction** — that values will rise, that a permit will issue, that a variance will be granted.
- A **legal conclusion** — that a use is permitted, that an encroachment is unimportant, that a party may cancel.
- A **specialist's assessment** — that a crack is cosmetic, that a repair was adequate, that mold is harmless.
- **Anything unverified that cannot be attributed.**

The professional treatment of unverified information is to **attribute it and flag it**: "the seller reports the roof was replaced in approximately 2019; this has not been verified" — never to launder it into apparent fact by repeating it, and never to copy a figure from a prior listing as though it had been verified.

### 5.8 Delivery

**Content is half the obligation. Delivery, and evidence of when it occurred, is the other half**, because rights run from delivery.

For each disclosure the file shows **what** was delivered, **to whom**, **when**, **by what authorized method**, and the **evidence.**

**Late delivery is not cured by delivery.** Where a disclosure arrives after the buyer removed contingencies, rights running from delivery are not extinguished because he acted earlier without the information — and neither receipt before closing nor a closing extension addresses his right to respond. The obligation is to raise it immediately.

### 5.9 The three-part test

When unsure what to do about something:

1. **Do I know it?** If yes, the licensee's own obligation is engaged, whatever the seller does.
2. **Would a reasonable buyer consider it important?** If yes, it is material.
3. **Am I qualified to evaluate it?** If no, disclose the observation, recommend the specialist, and do not characterize.

**"Disclose everything" is not the rule.** The rule is: **disclose what is material and known, attribute what is unverified, decline what the law limits, and evaluate nothing.**

### 5.10 Checkpoint

You should be able to apply the three-part test, distinguish observation from inference, and explain why late delivery is not cured by delivery before closing.

## 6. Integrated California transaction audit

### 6.1 The chronology

A transaction is a sequence in which **each step supplies the information the next step requires.** The test when something seems out of order:

> **Does this decision have, in front of it, the information it was meant to be made with?**

| # | Step | Supplies |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | Agency disclosure | The consumer's understanding of the relationship |
| 2 | Listing or representation agreement | Authority to act, and its terms |
| 3 | Pricing analysis and advice | The basis for the client's price decision |
| 4 | Seller disclosure preparation | The material facts, before pressure exists |
| 5 | Marketing | Exposure, on compliant copy |
| 6 | Offer and negotiation | The terms |
| 7 | Acceptance | The contract, and the deadline clock |
| 8 | Disclosure delivery | What the buyer needs before committing |
| 9 | Investigation | What the buyer needs before removing contingencies |
| 10 | Contingency removal | The buyer's commitment, informed |
| 11 | Loan and appraisal | The financing |
| 12 | Final verification | Condition confirmed while leverage exists |
| 13 | Signing, funding, recording, disbursement | The transfer |
| 14 | Possession | Control |
| 15 | Audit and retention | The record |

**Every common sequencing error is a decision made before its information arrived.**

### 6.2 The audit standard

> **Reconstructability: could a person who was not there determine what happened, when, and on what basis?**

### 6.3 The audit sequence

1. **Completeness** — every required document present, **including for the parts that did not go smoothly.**
2. **Dates** — everything dated; delivery evidenced where timing matters.
3. **Consistency** — names, dates, amounts, and property descriptions reconcile.
4. **Chronology** — the order makes sense against the contract and the disclose-before-decide principle.
5. **Nothing improper present** — no unexecuted draft mistaken for final, no superseded figure presented as current.
6. **Retention** — filed where it will be found, for the required period, **including cancelled and failed transactions.**

**Keep superseded versions.** The sequence of versions is frequently what shows when a term changed and who agreed — the question a dispute usually asks.

### 6.4 A worked audit, ranked

> A closed file contains:
>
> - **(a)** An agency disclosure signed and dated three days **after** the listing agreement.
> - **(b)** A confirmation naming the brokerage as buyer's agent, while the listing file shows seller's agent, with **no dual agency consent** anywhere.
> - **(c)** An email agreeing to move the closing date, with **no signed amendment.**
> - **(d)** A contingency removal, signed but **undated.**
> - **(e)** A transfer disclosure with **two items left blank.**
> - **(f)** A referral fee disclosure dated the day of closing, for a warranty the buyer bought six weeks earlier.
> - **(g)** Marketing copy reading "great family neighborhood."
>
> **Ranked by seriousness:**
>
> **1. (b) The undisclosed dual agency.** Different in kind. It describes the brokerage acting on both sides without either party's informed written consent — a breach of duties actually owed, not a documentation failure.
>
> **2. (g) The fair housing copy.** Also different in kind: it sorts people rather than describing the property, and intent is not a defense.
>
> **3. (d) The undated removal.** Rights ran from it and the file cannot show when. The defect a dispute is most likely to turn on.
>
> **4. (a) Disclosure after the agreement.** The seller committed before being informed. The document exists; the protection does not.
>
> **5. (f) Late conflict disclosure.** Delivered after the buyer acted — the one thing a conflict disclosure must not be.
>
> **6. (c) Unsigned date change.** The operative contract still says the original date.
>
> **7. (e) Blanks in the disclosure.** Ambiguous about whether the items were addressed.
>
> **The response:** record each finding, escalate to the broker, obtain what legitimately exists, and address the **processes** that produced them.

### 6.5 The line

> **What must not happen:** dating the removal now, completing the blanks now, producing an amendment now with a matching date, or creating the consent that was never obtained.
>
> **A documented gap is a compliance problem a brokerage can survive.**
>
> **A manufactured document is falsification.** It converts a defensible situation into a question about integrity, and it ends careers.

Corrections are made **visibly**, with the original preserved and the change attributable. **A record that can be silently altered is not a record.**

### 6.6 The course in four questions

When you meet a situation this course did not cover, four questions will usually produce the right answer:

1. **Whose decision is this?** Price, terms, and whether to proceed belong to the client. The analysis and the advice belong to you. Legal effect belongs to counsel.
2. **Whose duty is engaged?** Fiduciary duties run to the principal; honesty, fair dealing, and disclosure of material facts run to everyone.
3. **Does this decision have the information it requires?** If not, something is out of order.
4. **What will the file show?** If the answer is "nothing," create the record now, while it is contemporaneous and true.

### 6.7 Checkpoint

You should be able to take an unfamiliar problem, apply the four questions, and produce both the action and the record it requires.

| **Why a ledger does not cure it** | Funds in an operating account are exposed to the brokerage's creditors and losses. A ledger entry does not protect money; **separation does.** |
| **Shortage / overage** | Missing money, cured immediately from the broker's funds; or records not reflecting whose funds are held, investigated and never simply kept. No tolerance threshold. |
| **Three-way reconciliation** | Bank, book, and **the total of individual beneficiary ledgers** — all three must agree. |
| **Move-in documentation** | What wins a deposit dispute, which turns on the difference between move-in and move-out condition. |
| **Ordinary wear and tear** | Not deductible from a security deposit. |
| **Reasonable supervision** | A system: policies, file review, advertising review, trust fund controls, training, and a usable escalation path. Not reduced by independent contractor status. |
| **Real Estate Recovery Account** | A last-resort fund for uncollectable judgments for fraud, misrepresentation, deceit, or conversion — with consequences for the licensee's license until reimbursed. |
| **Errors and omissions coverage** | Responds within terms and limits on **prompt notice**; excludes intentional acts; transfers no responsibility. |
| **Materiality** | Whether a fact would affect a reasonable buyer's decision or the price they would pay. |
| **Adopting the omission** | Delivering a disclosure the licensee knows omits a material condition. |
| **Observation versus inference** | What the licensee saw, stated as such — never the conclusion about what it means. |
| **Death and illness limits** | California's constraints, existing so disclosure practice does not become a channel for discrimination. Do not volunteer, do not misrepresent, escalate. |
| **Attribution** | "The seller reports approximately 2019; this has not been verified." Never laundering an unverified figure into apparent fact. |
| **Rights run from delivery** | Why an undated disclosure proves the wrong thing, and why receipt before closing does not cure late delivery. |
| **The three-part test** | Do I know it? Would a reasonable buyer consider it important? Am I qualified to evaluate it? |
| **Reconstructability** | Could a person who was not there determine what happened, when, and on what basis. |
| **Documented gap versus manufactured document** | A compliance problem a brokerage survives; versus falsification, which ends careers. |
| **The four questions** | Whose decision, whose duty, does this decision have its information, and what will the file show. |

## Practice problems

### Problem 1

A tenant on a two-year lease transfers her entire remaining interest to a friend and moves out,
telling the landlord she is "done." Another tenant lets a roommate take one bedroom for six months.
The landlord asks whether either original tenant still owes rent.

**Classify each and answer.**

> **Solution.** **The first is an assignment** — transfer of the **entire remaining interest**, with the
> assignee dealing directly with the landlord. **The second is a sublease** — less than the entire
> interest, with the subtenant dealing with the original tenant.
>
> **In both cases the original tenant generally remains liable unless released**, which is the point
> tenants miss. **A tenant who "assigned the lease and walked away" has usually not walked away from
> anything.**
>
> Whether either transfer was permitted at all depends on the lease's assignment and subletting
> provision, and whether the landlord's consent was required or given.
>
> Note also that this two-year lease **had to be in writing** — the Statute of Frauds reaches leases
> exceeding one year — which is what makes the terms provable at all.

### Problem 2

A tenant reports Friday morning that the unit has had no water since Thursday night. The manager tells
him to submit a written request and that the plumber's next routine visit is Tuesday. On Tuesday he
lets the plumber in without notice because the tenant is home. Eleven days after the tenant complained
to the city about the delay, the manager serves a termination notice naming one of two tenants on the
lease, left under the door.

**Assess each act.**

> **Solution.** **A loss of water is an emergency**, at the top of the triage list with no heat in cold
> weather, sewage backup, gas odor, electrical hazard, security failure, and flooding. **Emergency
> authority exists precisely so this does not wait for approval.** Habitability is **not waivable** and
> runs **continuously**; requiring a written request may be part of the process but **does not justify
> delaying the response** — and the exposure grows with the delay.
>
> **The entry is unlawful.** It requires a permitted purpose, proper notice in the required manner and
> time, a reasonable hour, and documentation. **The tenant being home does not substitute for notice**
> — nor does the owner's authorization, holding a key, or the contractor's schedule.
>
> **The notice is defective on two elements** — correct parties named, and correct service with proof.
> **A good reason delivered by a defective notice fails**, and the landlord starts over having lost
> weeks and improved the tenant's position.
>
> **And the timing creates a retaliation appearance.** The protection is a **documented, independent
> basis** predating or unconnected to the protected activity — which here does not exist, because the
> only documented conduct is the manager's own delay.

### Problem 3

An audit finds two owners with no signed management agreement, one owner unreported to for four months,
security deposits in the brokerage operating account, and a salesperson signing leases in her own name.
The broker says nothing has gone wrong and no one has complained.

**Assess each and the broker's defense.**

> **Solution.** **Four independent defects, and none depends on harm having occurred.**
>
> **Unsigned agreements are structural, not clerical:** scope undefined so every decision is arguable,
> compensation unenforceable, fund handling unauthorized in writing, and every action disputable. This
> sits alongside commingling, not alongside filing.
>
> **The unreported owner** has been unable to verify the handling of her own money for a third of a
> year, the trust records have gone that long without external scrutiny, and if there is an error nobody
> has been positioned to find it. **Reporting is how the accounting duty is discharged** — regular and
> itemized, and an accounting that cannot be verified is not an accounting.
>
> **The deposits are commingled**, complete on deposit.
>
> **The salesperson may not contract in her own name** for licensed activity. The lease is the
> **brokerage's**, executed for the broker.
>
> **The absence of complaints is not evidence of compliance.** It usually means the problems have not
> surfaced yet — and in a management practice, which repeats monthly across every unit, they will.

### Problem 4

A manager deposits a month's rents and three security deposits into the operating account, pointing out
that his software tracks each beneficiary to the cent and the total is correct. Two months later a
reconciliation that matched bank to book to the penny turns out to have concealed a deposit credited to
the wrong tenant's ledger. A $340 overage also appears, which the broker proposes to keep as a fee.

**Address all three.**

> **Solution.** **Commingling was complete on deposit**, regardless of the tracking and regardless of
> whether anyone lost money. The structural reason the ledger fails: **funds in an operating account are
> exposed to the brokerage's creditors, operating losses, and ordinary business risk. A ledger entry
> does not protect the money — separation does**, and the record proves the separation was maintained.
>
> **The reconciliation missed the third balance.** It must compare **bank, book, and the total of the
> individual beneficiary ledgers** — all three agreeing. Bank-to-book alone cannot detect money held for
> a **different beneficiary** than the records say, because the total was right the whole time. That is
> exactly why it went unnoticed for two months.
>
> **The overage may not be kept.** An overage means the records do not accurately reflect whose funds
> are held; it is **investigated and documented**, and the broker may not simply keep it. A **shortage**
> is cured **immediately from the broker's own funds** with the cause documented. Neither is a rounding
> matter, neither has a tolerance threshold, and neither waits for the next reconciliation.
>
> And when the original error was found, the correct response was to correct it immediately, **document
> the error and the correction**, and notify the broker — because **trust accounting is judged by the
> record as much as by the balance.**

### Problem 5

A tenancy ends. The manager withholds the entire $2,400 deposit for carpet replacement and interior
repainting, sending a one-line note five weeks later. There is no move-in condition record. The
departing licensee who managed the property also takes copies of all her management files, with tenant
personal information redacted.

**Assess both.**

> **Solution.** **The deposit handling fails on every element.** A deposit dispute is decided on **the
> difference between move-in and move-out condition**, and a manager without move-in documentation
> **has no case** — which is the entire reason move-in documentation exists. **Ordinary wear and tear is
> not deductible**, and carpet wear and repainting after a tenancy are the standard examples. The
> accounting must be **itemized in writing with supporting documentation**, delivered within the
> required time, with the balance returned within the required period. A one-line note five weeks later
> is none of that.
>
> Nearly all deposit complaints — the most common source of tenant complaints — are prevented by two
> practices: **dated photographic documentation at move-in and move-out, and an itemized written
> accounting.**
>
> **The file removal is not hers to make.** Records belong to the **brokerage**, and the responsible
> broker answers for them whoever created them. **Redaction addresses a different risk and does not
> change who owns the record.** What may be taken is governed by brokerage policy and client direction —
> a question that should be settled in writing in the office policy long before anyone departs.

### Problem 6

Previewing a listing, an agent notices ceiling staining with newer paint. The seller says a pipe leaked
two years ago, was repaired, and there is "nothing to disclose because it's fixed." The disclosure comes
back with two blanks and one uncertain item marked "no." The agent completes the blanks from what the
seller told him verbally, writes in his own disclosure that "there was a roof leak that has been
repaired," and copies "roof replaced 2019" from the prior listing into the marketing sheet.

**Identify every error.**

> **Solution.** **Six, and two duties that exist independently.**
>
> **The leak must be disclosed by the seller.** A repaired condition is still material because **the
> adequacy of the repair is exactly what a buyer evaluates** — and here there are no records.
>
> **The blanks must be answered by the seller**, with **"unknown"** where that is the truth. A blank is
> ambiguous; "unknown" is honest and complete.
>
> **The item marked "no" asserts knowledge the seller does not have.**
>
> **The agent completing the form misattributes the statement** — the seller completes the seller's
> disclosure.
>
> **"There was a roof leak that has been repaired" is an inference** the agent is not qualified to make
> and that the buyer may rely on. The correct entry is the **observation**: "brown staining approximately
> three feet across on the northwest bedroom ceiling; paint in that area appears newer than the
> surrounding ceiling."
>
> **"Roof replaced 2019" launders an unverified figure** from a prior listing into apparent fact. The
> professional treatment attributes and flags it.
>
> **And the agent's own visual inspection duty is independent** of the seller's disclosure and of any
> professional inspection. **A licensee who delivers a disclosure knowing it omits a material condition
> has adopted the omission.**

### Problem 7

A buyer asks a licensee whether the prior owner died in the house. Separately, the licensee is asked by
a client whether a crack in the foundation is "cosmetic," whether the city will approve a variance, and
whether values in the area will rise. A disclosure is delivered nine days after the buyer removed
contingencies, and the listing agent proposes a one-week closing extension if he objects.

**Handle each.**

> **Solution.** **The death question is legally constrained.** California limits disclosure of a prior
> occupant's death and of a prior occupant's illness — limits existing so disclosure practice does not
> become a channel for discrimination or attach permanent penalties unrelated to condition. **Do not
> volunteer. Do not misrepresent. Escalate.** The precise boundary is a legal question with real
> consequences on both sides, and it is not one to resolve at a showing.
>
> **The other three are each things that must never be represented as fact:** a **specialist's
> assessment** (the crack), a **prediction about a discretionary decision** (the variance), and a
> **prediction** (values). Disclose the observation, recommend the specialist, point to the planning
> counter, and decline the forecast.
>
> **On the late disclosure: raise it immediately.** Rights that run from delivery are not extinguished
> because the buyer acted earlier without the information — **late delivery may revive or extend his
> ability to respond.** Neither receipt before closing nor a closing extension addresses that right, and
> the proposal to say nothing unless he objects is itself the violation.
>
> **The rule covering all of it:** disclose what is material and known, attribute what is unverified,
> **decline what the law limits, and evaluate nothing.**

### Problem 8

Rank the defects in this closed file and state the response: (a) an agency disclosure dated three days
after the listing agreement; (b) a confirmation naming the brokerage as buyer's agent while the listing
file shows seller's agent, with no dual agency consent; (c) an email agreeing to move closing, no signed
amendment; (d) a contingency removal signed but undated; (e) a transfer disclosure with two blanks;
(f) a referral fee disclosure dated the day of closing for a warranty bought six weeks earlier;
(g) marketing copy reading "great family neighborhood." A colleague offers to "clean it up" before the
broker sees it.

**Rank, respond, and address the colleague.**

> **Solution.** **1. (b) The undisclosed dual agency** — different in kind, describing the brokerage
> acting on both sides without either party's informed written consent. A breach of duties actually
> owed.
>
> **2. (g) The fair housing copy** — also different in kind. It sorts people rather than describing the
> property, and **intent is not a defense.**
>
> **3. (d) The undated removal** — rights ran from it and the file cannot show when. The defect a
> dispute is most likely to turn on.
>
> **4. (a) Disclosure after the agreement** — the seller committed before being informed. The document
> exists; the protection does not.
>
> **5. (f) Late conflict disclosure** — delivered after the buyer acted, the one thing it must not be.
>
> **6. (c) Unsigned date change** — the operative contract still says the original date.
>
> **7. (e) The blanks** — ambiguous about whether the items were addressed.
>
> **The response:** record each finding, **escalate to the broker**, obtain what legitimately exists, and
> address the **processes** that produced them rather than only the instances.
>
> **The colleague is proposing falsification**, and it is the one thing here that is not survivable.
> Dating the removal now, completing the blanks now, producing an amendment with a matching date, or
> creating the consent that was never obtained converts a compliance problem into a question about
> integrity. **A documented gap a brokerage survives; a manufactured document ends careers.** Corrections
> are made **visibly**, with the original preserved and the change attributable — **a record that can be
> silently altered is not a record.**

## Chapter summary

**A lease conveys possession while the owner keeps the reversion**, and one exceeding a year must
generally be written. Assignment transfers the whole remaining interest and subletting less — and **in
both the original tenant generally remains liable.**

**Habitability is not waivable and runs continuously.** Entry requires purpose, notice, a reasonable
hour, and documentation, and **nothing substitutes for notice.**

**A defective notice defeats a good reason**, self-help is prohibited without exception, and retaliation
is answered only by a documented independent basis.

**A lease survives a sale.** Verify rather than accept assurances.

**Management is licensed activity, continuous, and performed for the broker.** Unsigned authority is
structural; emergency authority belongs in the agreement; selection runs on uniform written criteria;
requests are triaged; and reporting is regular, itemized, and verifiable.

**Trust funds require separation, records, and timeliness.** Commingling is complete on deposit, a
ledger does not cure it because a ledger does not shield money from the brokerage's creditors, shortages
are cured immediately and overages are never kept, and reconciliation compares **three** balances.

**Move-in documentation wins deposit disputes**, wear and tear is not deductible, and records belong to
the brokerage — including failed transactions.

**The brokerage is the party; supervision is a system; licensed activity is defined by the act, not the
title.** Most discipline categories require no intent to defraud, the recovery account is a last resort
with license consequences, and E&O responds only on prompt notice and never to intentional acts.

**Materiality is measured against a reasonable buyer**, and the seller's disclosure and the licensee's
visual inspection are **independent duties.** Record observations, never inferences; answer every item;
never adopt an omission.

**Attribute what is unverified, decline what the law limits, evaluate nothing** — and remember that
delivery, with its date, is half the obligation.

**A transaction is a chronology**, and every sequencing error is a decision made before its information
arrived.

**Audit for reconstructability, rank defects by kind rather than count, keep superseded versions, obtain
what exists, record what does not, and fix the process.**

**And when the situation is unfamiliar, four questions:** whose decision, whose duty, does this decision
have its information, and what will the file show.
