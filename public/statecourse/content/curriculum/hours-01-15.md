## Hour 01 — The Real Estate Profession and Its Economic Role

**Required subject mapping:** T01 — Economy and social impact of real estate

### Learning objectives

- Distinguish land, real estate, and real property.
- Identify major real estate industry functions and the salesperson’s supervised role.
- Explain why real estate activity affects households, communities, employment, and local tax bases.

### Instructional content

Land is the earth’s surface, the space above it, and the subsurface to the extent recognized by law. Real estate is land plus permanent human-made improvements. Real property adds the legal interests—the bundle of rights—that may be possessed, used, transferred, leased, encumbered, or excluded, subject to private rights and government powers.

Real estate is immobile, durable, heterogeneous, and expensive relative to most consumer goods. These characteristics create local markets, long development cycles, substantial financing needs, and heavy reliance on reliable information and professional coordination. Brokerage connects principals; lending supplies capital; appraisal develops supported value opinions; title and settlement services help transfer interests; property management operates assets; and public planning shapes permissible use.

A Virginia salesperson does not practice independently. The course will later connect licensed activity to the broker, firm, branch, and supervisory structure in Title 54.1, Chapter 21 and 18VAC135-20. At this stage, students should understand that licensure protects the public by setting minimum competence and conduct expectations; it does not guarantee investment results or replace legal, tax, engineering, inspection, or lending professionals.

### 50-minute instructional sequence

- **0–5:** Retrieval prompt, learning objectives, and vocabulary activation.
- **5–20:** Instructor-guided explanation of the first concept set with annotated examples.
- **20–35:** Second concept set, misconceptions, Virginia/national distinction, and source navigation where applicable.
- **35–45:** Applied exercise — Students classify ten tasks—showing property, underwriting a loan, estimating value for a lender, drafting a zoning map, collecting rent, and similar examples—by industry function. They then identify which tasks may create a need for licensed brokerage activity.
- **45–50:** Scored engagement check, feedback, and completion gate.

### Scored engagement check

**Question:** Why does the immobility of land make real estate markets local?

**Expected answer and rationale:** Because the property cannot be moved to meet demand elsewhere; location-specific employment, infrastructure, regulation, schools, amenities, and supply directly affect demand and value.

## Hour 02 — Supply, Demand, and Real Estate Cycles

**Required subject mapping:** T01, T02 — Economy/social impact; market and analysis

### Learning objectives

- Describe demand and supply drivers in a local property market.
- Explain why new real estate supply responds slowly.
- Recognize cycle indicators without treating them as guaranteed forecasts.

### Instructional content

Demand is the quantity of a property type that market participants are willing and financially able to obtain at various prices. Population, household formation, employment, income, credit availability, interest rates, preferences, and investor expectations can shift demand. A change in price moves quantity demanded along a curve; a change in an underlying driver shifts the demand relationship.

Supply includes existing inventory and property that can be produced or converted. Physical scarcity, zoning, entitlement time, infrastructure, construction capacity, materials, labor, financing, and developer expectations influence supply. Because development takes time, short-run supply may be relatively inelastic. A sudden demand increase can therefore raise prices and reduce vacancy before new supply arrives.

Cycles are simplified descriptions, not clocks. Expansion may bring falling vacancy, rising rents/prices, and construction. Overbuilding, reduced demand, or tighter credit can produce excess inventory and contraction. Students must distinguish evidence from narrative: one indicator does not prove a cycle stage, and a national trend may not describe a Virginia submarket.

### 50-minute instructional sequence

- **0–5:** Retrieval prompt, learning objectives, and vocabulary activation.
- **5–20:** Instructor-guided explanation of the first concept set with annotated examples.
- **20–35:** Second concept set, misconceptions, Virginia/national distinction, and source navigation where applicable.
- **35–45:** Applied exercise — Given a market with stable inventory, rising jobs, falling mortgage rates, and decreasing days on market, students predict likely directional pressures and name two facts still needed before reaching a conclusion.
- **45–50:** Scored engagement check, feedback, and completion gate.

### Scored engagement check

**Question:** If demand rises while short-run supply is fixed, what directional change is most likely?

**Expected answer and rationale:** Upward pressure on price or rent and downward pressure on available inventory/vacancy, all else equal.

## Hour 03 — Market Data and Comparative Analysis

**Required subject mapping:** T02 — Real estate market and analysis

### Learning objectives

- Define a relevant market area and property segment.
- Distinguish listing, pending, closed, expired, and withdrawn data.
- Calculate inventory and absorption and explain their limits.

### Instructional content

A market analysis begins with the decision the analysis must support. The relevant market is not automatically a ZIP code. It may be a school boundary, condominium project, neighborhood, property subtype, price band, or competing rental area. The analyst states the geographic area, property characteristics, time period, and intended use before selecting data.

Closed sales show transactions that actually occurred but are historical. Active listings describe current competition and seller expectations, not proven value. Pending sales may indicate direction but often lack final terms. Expired and withdrawn listings reveal resistance or changed intent. Data must be verified, normalized, and screened for non-arm’s-length transactions, concessions, condition differences, or reporting errors.

Months of inventory can be estimated as active listings divided by average monthly closed sales. An absorption rate can be expressed as units sold per period or as a percentage of available inventory; the chosen definition must be stated. Median reduces sensitivity to extremes; average uses all values but may be distorted by outliers. A CMA is a brokerage analysis, not an appraisal, and must not be represented as one.

### 50-minute instructional sequence

- **0–5:** Retrieval prompt, learning objectives, and vocabulary activation.
- **5–20:** Instructor-guided explanation of the first concept set with annotated examples.
- **20–35:** Second concept set, misconceptions, Virginia/national distinction, and source navigation where applicable.
- **35–45:** Applied exercise — Students select three comparables from six candidates, calculate three months of inventory, and write a one-sentence limitation statement.
- **45–50:** Scored engagement check, feedback, and completion gate.

### Scored engagement check

**Question:** Why can an active listing support competition analysis but not prove market value?

**Expected answer and rationale:** It shows what a seller is asking and what buyers can choose, but it does not prove that a willing buyer accepted that price in a completed arm’s-length transaction.

## Hour 04 — Real Property, Personal Property, and Fixtures

**Required subject mapping:** T03 — Property rights

### Learning objectives

- Differentiate real and personal property.
- Apply annexation, adaptation, intention, and agreement factors to fixtures.
- Explain why disputed items should be addressed in the contract.

### Instructional content

Real property consists of the legal interests in land and permanent improvements. Personal property, or personalty, is movable and is generally transferred by a bill of sale or other personal-property instrument rather than by deed. Money, furniture, freestanding appliances, and trade equipment may be personal property unless facts or agreement indicate otherwise.

A fixture began as personal property but became part of the real estate. No single physical test resolves every dispute. Students examine how firmly the item is annexed; whether it is specially adapted to the property; the objective intention shown by installation and circumstances; the relationship of the parties; and, most importantly, any written agreement. Trade fixtures installed by a commercial tenant for business use are usually treated differently and must be timely removed subject to lease terms and repair duties.

Licensees reduce disputes by using precise inclusion/exclusion language and by identifying ambiguous items before contract formation. The salesperson should not promise a legal result from a shorthand fixture test. A chandelier, wall-mounted television bracket, solar equipment, leased security system, smart-home device, or built-in refrigerator may require document review and explicit allocation.

- **35–45:** Applied exercise — Students apply the fixture factors to a wall-mounted EV charger, leased solar panels, a freestanding wine refrigerator, and custom shelving.
- **45–50:** Scored engagement check, feedback, and completion gate.

### Scored engagement check

**Expected answer and rationale:** A clear written agreement in the transaction documents identifying the item as included or excluded.

## Hour 05 — Estates in Land and Future Interests

**Required subject mapping:** T03 — Property rights

### Learning objectives

- Distinguish freehold from leasehold estates.
- Identify fee simple, defeasible, and life-estate interests.
- Recognize reversion and remainder interests.

### Instructional content

An estate describes the degree, quantity, nature, and duration of a person’s interest in real property. Freehold estates are ownership interests of uncertain duration. Fee simple absolute is the broadest private estate, subject to government powers and private encumbrances. A defeasible fee may end upon a stated event; the precise deed language matters.

A life estate lasts for the life of a named person. The life tenant has present possession but must respect the interests of the future holder. If possession returns to the grantor or the grantor’s successors, the future interest is a reversion. If possession passes to a named third party, it is a remainder. A life tenant’s transfer cannot ordinarily give the transferee a longer interest than the measuring life permits.

Leasehold estates confer possession without ownership. A tenancy for years has a fixed term; a periodic tenancy repeats until properly terminated; a tenancy at will has no fixed period; and a tenancy at sufferance describes possession after a lawful tenancy ends without consent. Virginia landlord-tenant law and the lease control actual rights and notices.

### 50-minute instructional sequence

- **0–5:** Retrieval prompt, learning objectives, and vocabulary activation.
- **5–20:** Instructor-guided explanation of the first concept set with annotated examples.
- **20–35:** Second concept set, misconceptions, Virginia/national distinction, and source navigation where applicable.
- **35–45:** Applied exercise — Students diagram ownership and possession when Owner grants a life estate to A, remainder to B, and A later leases the property to C.
- **45–50:** Scored engagement check, feedback, and completion gate.

### Scored engagement check

**Question:** What happens to a lease granted by a life tenant when the measuring life ends, absent another binding right?

**Expected answer and rationale:** The life tenant could not convey a longer estate than held, so the lease generally cannot continue against the future-interest holder solely on the life tenant’s authority.

## Hour 06 — Forms of Ownership and Home Ownership

**Required subject mapping:** T03, T10 — Property rights; home ownership

### Learning objectives

- Compare ownership in severalty, tenancy in common, joint tenancy, and tenancy by the entirety.
- Explain survivorship and transfer consequences at a principles level.
- Identify documents and professional advice needed before recommending ownership form.

### Instructional content

Ownership in severalty means one legal owner; the owner may be an individual or an entity. Tenants in common hold undivided interests that may be equal or unequal, with no automatic survivorship merely from that form. Each co-tenant generally has a right to possess the whole, subject to the equal rights of others, and may transfer that owner’s interest.

Joint tenancy traditionally includes a right of survivorship when properly created. Tenancy by the entirety is a form associated with married spouses and carries survivorship and other legal consequences. State law and the creating instrument control. Trusts, limited liability companies, partnerships, and corporations may hold title, but ownership through an entity changes governance, authority, financing, liability, and tax questions.

A salesperson can explain vocabulary and transaction logistics but should not select an ownership form for a client. The choice can affect probate, creditor rights, divorce, control, taxes, financing, and succession. Students learn to request the vesting instruction early and refer the client to the closing agent and qualified legal/tax professionals.

### 50-minute instructional sequence

- **0–5:** Retrieval prompt, learning objectives, and vocabulary activation.
- **5–20:** Instructor-guided explanation of the first concept set with annotated examples.
- **20–35:** Second concept set, misconceptions, Virginia/national distinction, and source navigation where applicable.
- **35–45:** Applied exercise — Students compare death, transfer, and management outcomes in four co-ownership scenarios, then list questions that require attorney or tax-adviser input.
- **45–50:** Scored engagement check, feedback, and completion gate.

### Scored engagement check

**Question:** Does tenancy in common automatically give the surviving co-owner the deceased owner’s share?

**Expected answer and rationale:** No. Tenancy in common does not create automatic survivorship merely by that form; the deceased owner’s interest passes under applicable estate arrangements and law.

## Hour 07 — Private Rights, Easements, and Encumbrances

**Required subject mapping:** T03, T09 — Property rights; liens

### Learning objectives

- Distinguish easements, licenses, encroachments, and restrictive covenants.
- Identify dominant and servient estates for an appurtenant easement.
- Explain how encumbrances can affect use, title, and value.

### Instructional content

An easement is a nonpossessory right to use another’s land for a stated purpose. An easement appurtenant benefits a parcel (dominant estate) and burdens another (servient estate); it typically follows the benefited land. An easement in gross benefits a person or organization rather than a dominant parcel, as with many utility rights. Easements may arise by express grant/reservation, implication, necessity, prescription, or other law.

A license is personal permission to use land and is generally revocable, although reliance and contract facts may matter. An encroachment is an unauthorized physical intrusion, such as a fence or building across a boundary. Restrictive covenants privately limit use and may be found in deeds, declarations, or association documents. They are distinct from zoning, which is governmental.

Encumbrance is a broad term for a right or claim that affects title, use, or value; it does not always prevent transfer. A licensee should obtain surveys, title materials, association documents, and specialist advice when access, boundary, utility, or restriction questions are material.

### 50-minute instructional sequence

- **0–5:** Retrieval prompt, learning objectives, and vocabulary activation.
- **5–20:** Instructor-guided explanation of the first concept set with annotated examples.
- **20–35:** Second concept set, misconceptions, Virginia/national distinction, and source navigation where applicable.
- **35–45:** Applied exercise — Students label dominant/servient parcels in an access easement and choose the appropriate investigation for a fence encroachment, shared driveway, and utility line.
- **45–50:** Scored engagement check, feedback, and completion gate.

### Scored engagement check

**Question:** What is the central distinction between an easement appurtenant and an easement in gross?

**Expected answer and rationale:** An appurtenant easement benefits a particular parcel, while an easement in gross benefits a person or organization without requiring a dominant parcel.

## Hour 08 — Public Controls, Land Use, and Development

**Required subject mapping:** T03, T21, T22 — Property rights; subdivision/development; regulatory statutes

### Learning objectives

- Identify police power, eminent domain, taxation, and escheat as government powers affecting property.
- Distinguish zoning, building codes, and subdivision controls.
- Recognize variance, special-use, and nonconforming-use concepts.

### Instructional content

Private property rights exist within public controls. Police power supports laws protecting health, safety, morals, and general welfare, including zoning, building codes, environmental requirements, and subdivision regulation. Eminent domain is the power to take private property for a public use subject to legal process and just compensation. Taxation funds public purposes and creates enforceable claims. Escheat addresses property when an owner dies without heirs or a valid disposition.

Zoning separates or conditions uses, intensity, setbacks, height, parking, and related development features. A variance is relief from specific requirements based on legally defined hardship criteria; it is not simply permission to ignore zoning. A special or conditional use may be allowed if stated standards and approvals are met. A lawful nonconforming use existed before a restriction and may continue subject to limits.

Subdivision controls govern division of land, plats, access, utilities, drainage, public improvements, and recording. A licensee verifies current public records and avoids promising that a use, expansion, accessory unit, or subdivision will be approved. Local rules differ across Virginia.

- **45–50:** Scored engagement check, feedback, and completion gate.

### Scored engagement check

**Question:** Why is a variance not the same as rezoning?

**Expected answer and rationale:** A variance grants limited relief from an existing requirement under defined standards; rezoning changes the zoning classification or applicable legislative rules.

## Hour 09 — Legal Descriptions, Plats, and Surveys

**Required subject mapping:** T03, T18 — Property rights; title search/examination/registration

### Learning objectives

- Recognize metes-and-bounds and lot-and-block descriptions.
- Explain the role of monuments, courses, distances, and recorded plats.
- Identify when a survey or legal professional is needed.

### Instructional content

A street address is useful for location but may be insufficient as the legal description that identifies the estate conveyed. Metes and bounds traces a boundary using directions, distances, and monuments, beginning and ending at a point of beginning. Physical monuments can be natural or artificial; conflicts among calls require professional interpretation.

Lot-and-block descriptions refer to a recorded subdivision plat by lot, block, plat book, page, jurisdiction, or equivalent record information. A recorded plat shows the subdivision geometry and may show easements, rights-of-way, common areas, and notes. Government rectangular survey terminology is important nationally even though Virginia transactions often use other descriptions.

A survey locates boundaries and visible improvements under professional standards. It can reveal encroachments, gaps, overlaps, or discrepancies, but the title examiner determines legal record issues and an attorney resolves legal disputes. Licensees compare the contract, deed, title commitment, tax record, and survey for consistency and escalate discrepancies before closing.

- **45–50:** Scored engagement check, feedback, and completion gate.

### Scored engagement check

## Hour 10 — Deeds and Voluntary Transfer

**Required subject mapping:** T05, T18 — Deeds; title recording

### Learning objectives

- Identify core deed components and transfer requirements.
- Compare general warranty, special warranty, and quitclaim deed functions.
- Explain delivery, acceptance, acknowledgment, and recordation.

### Instructional content

A deed is a written instrument used to transfer an interest in real property. Core elements generally include competent grantor, identifiable grantee, words showing present intent to convey, adequate legal description, the required grantor execution, and delivery and acceptance. Consideration may be recited, but the exact legal sufficiency and form are governed by law.

A general warranty deed provides broad covenants defined by its language and law. A special warranty deed typically limits covenants to claims arising through the grantor. A quitclaim deed conveys whatever interest, if any, the grantor possesses without the same title warranties. The deed label alone is not a substitute for reading the instrument.

Acknowledgment supports recordation and authenticity procedures; recordation gives public notice and helps establish priority, but recording is not the same event as delivery. Deeds used for fiduciary, tax, foreclosure, gift, entity, or estate transfers may have specialized forms and consequences. Licensees coordinate with the settlement/closing professional and do not select or draft deeds as legal advice.

## Hour 11 — Title Search, Chain of Title, and Notice

**Required subject mapping:** T18 — Search, examination, and registration of title

### Learning objectives

- Explain chain of title and clouds on title.
- Distinguish actual and constructive notice.
- Describe the licensee’s role versus the title examiner’s role.

### Instructional content

Title is the legal evidence of ownership, not a physical document. A title search reviews public records affecting the described property and relevant parties. A chain of title links successive transfers. Missing conveyances, inconsistent names, unreleased liens, probate issues, defective descriptions, or competing interests can create a cloud requiring curative action.

Actual notice is direct knowledge. Constructive notice is knowledge the law attributes because an instrument was properly recorded or because facts required further inquiry. Possession by someone other than the apparent record owner can create inquiry issues. Priority depends on applicable recording and lien law; students must not rely on a universal ‘first in time’ slogan when statutes create exceptions.

Licensees should obtain and track title work, identify discrepancies, communicate deadlines, and refer title opinions and curative work to qualified professionals. They should not state that title is clear merely because a database search found no problem.

- **35–45:** Applied exercise — Students build a four-deed chain, locate a missing link and an unreleased deed of trust, and specify who must resolve each issue.
- **45–50:** Scored engagement check, feedback, and completion gate.

### Scored engagement check

**Question:** What is constructive notice?

**Expected answer and rationale:** Notice the law treats a person as having because information was available through proper public recording or other legally recognized circumstances, even without actual knowledge.

## Hour 12 — Title Defects and Title Insurance

**Required subject mapping:** T11, T18 — Title insurance; title examination

### Learning objectives

- Differentiate a title search from title insurance.
- Compare owner and lender title policies.
- Read requirements and exceptions in a sample commitment.

### Instructional content

A title search and examination investigate the public record and produce a title conclusion or commitment conditions. Title insurance is a contract that allocates specified covered risks subject to exclusions, exceptions, conditions, policy amount, and date. It does not guarantee that no defect exists and does not insure every survey, zoning, environmental, or post-policy event.

A lender’s policy protects the insured lender’s mortgage or deed-of-trust interest up to policy terms; it does not protect the buyer’s equity merely because the buyer paid the premium. An owner’s policy protects the named owner subject to its terms. Coverage, endorsements, and local practice should be explained by the licensed title/settlement professional.

A commitment commonly lists the proposed insured parties and estate, requirements to satisfy before policy issuance, and exceptions that will remain outside coverage. Students learn to separate a requirement—such as release of a lien—from an exception—such as a recorded easement. A salesperson tracks resolution but does not issue a title opinion.

### Scored engagement check

**Question:** Why does a lender’s title policy not replace an owner’s policy?

**Expected answer and rationale:** The lender’s policy insures the lender’s secured interest, not the buyer’s ownership equity and risks as owner.

## Hour 13 — Liens, Priority, and Release

**Required subject mapping:** T09, T18 — Liens; title examination

### Learning objectives

- Distinguish voluntary/involuntary and general/specific liens.
- Explain why lien priority requires the governing law and facts.
- Identify satisfaction, release, subordination, and foreclosure concepts.

### Instructional content

A lien is a claim or charge against property as security for an obligation. A mortgage or deed-of-trust lien is voluntary. Tax, judgment, mechanics’, association, and other statutory liens may arise involuntarily. A general lien can reach multiple assets of a debtor; a specific lien attaches to identified property. Classification helps analysis but does not determine every enforcement rule.

Priority affects which interests are paid or extinguished first. Recording time is important, yet tax statutes, mechanics’ lien law, purchase-money rules, subordination agreements, bankruptcy, and other law can change the result. A salesperson gathers recording information and sends the issue to title/legal professionals rather than making a payoff promise.

Payment should lead to the legally required satisfaction or release in the public record. A subordination agreement changes priority by consent. Foreclosure enforces a secured interest through the applicable process; sale proceeds follow legal priority and costs. The underlying debt, lien, and release document must not be confused.

## Hour 14 — Taxes in Real Estate

**Required subject mapping:** T13 — Taxes in real estate

### Learning objectives

- Calculate ad valorem tax from assessed value and rate.
- Distinguish recurring property tax from special assessment and transaction-tax concepts.
- Recognize when tax advice must be referred.

### Instructional content

Ad valorem real estate tax is based on value. The jurisdiction determines taxable assessment and rate. A basic estimate multiplies the assessed value by the rate after converting the rate to the correct decimal or per-$100 basis. Local assessment practices, exemptions, relief programs, supplemental bills, and tax-year conventions must be verified.

A special assessment funds a specific public improvement or service under applicable law. Recordation and grantor/transferee tax concepts may apply to instruments or transfers, but current Virginia rates, exemptions, and allocation should be obtained from official and settlement sources at the time of the transaction. Property taxes can become liens and affect closing.

Federal and state income-tax topics—basis, adjusted basis, gain, depreciation, exclusions, and exchange rules—are introduced only as concepts. A licensee may identify a potential issue and collect documents but should not calculate a client’s tax liability or recommend a tax structure unless separately qualified.

## Hour 15 — Contract Foundations

**Required subject mapping:** T04 — Contracts

### Learning objectives

- Identify the core elements of an enforceable real estate contract.
- Classify valid, void, voidable, unenforceable, executed, and executory agreements.
- Explain the function of the Statute of Frauds without giving legal advice.

### Instructional content

A contract is a legally enforceable promise or set of promises. Formation analysis asks whether competent parties reached mutual assent through offer and acceptance, exchanged legally sufficient consideration, and pursued a lawful objective. Real estate agreements also require sufficiently certain terms, and many interests in land must be evidenced by a signed writing under the applicable Statute of Frauds.

A void agreement has no legal effect from inception. A voidable contract is valid unless the protected party elects to avoid it. An unenforceable agreement may be valid in substance but cannot be enforced because of a defense such as a writing requirement. An executory contract still has material performance due; an executed contract has been fully performed. Students should state which meaning of ‘executed’ they use because the word can also mean signed.

Licensees use approved forms and broker procedures, fill business terms within their authority, and refer legal interpretation or custom drafting to an attorney. They do not declare a contract cancelled, extend deadlines unilaterally, or advise a party to breach.

- **45–50:** Scored engagement check, feedback, and completion gate.

### Scored engagement check

**Question:** What is the difference between void and voidable?

**Expected answer and rationale:** A void agreement has no legal effect; a voidable contract remains effective unless the party protected by law chooses to avoid it.
