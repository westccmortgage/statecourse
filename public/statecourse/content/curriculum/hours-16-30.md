## Hour 16 — Offer, Acceptance, and Contract Formation

**Required subject mapping:** T04 — Contracts

### Learning objectives

- Trace an offer through acceptance, rejection, revocation, lapse, or counteroffer.
- Distinguish an amendment, addendum, assignment, and novation.
- Recognize electronic formation and communication issues.

### Instructional content

An offer gives the offeree the power to accept according to its terms while it remains open. It may terminate by rejection, counteroffer, valid revocation, lapse of stated or reasonable time, death/incapacity in some circumstances, destruction of the subject matter, or illegality. A counteroffer rejects the original offer unless the parties preserve it through clear language or another doctrine applies.

Acceptance must correspond to the offer and be communicated in the required manner. A signature by itself may not complete formation if the offer requires delivery or notice. Transaction platforms record timestamps, but the licensee still follows the contract’s notice provisions and broker procedure. Silence is not ordinarily acceptance merely because one party says it will be.

An addendum adds terms at formation; an amendment changes an existing agreement; an assignment transfers contractual rights subject to limitations; and a novation substitutes a party or obligation with required consent and releases the replaced obligation. Students must not use these labels interchangeably.

### 50-minute instructional sequence

- **0–5:** Retrieval prompt, objectives, and linkage to prior hours.
- **5–20:** Direct instruction segment A with annotated documents or source navigation.
- **20–35:** Direct instruction segment B, Virginia/national distinction, misconceptions, and examples.
- **35–45:** Applied exercise — Students place two offers, a counteroffer, withdrawal, electronic signature, and delivery events on a timeline and determine the formation point.
- **45–50:** Scored engagement check, immediate feedback, and progression gate.

### Scored engagement check

**Question:** A seller signs a buyer’s offer after it expired and sends it back unchanged. What is the safest contract classification?

**Expected answer and rationale:** The expired offer could no longer be accepted as written; the seller’s signed response is generally treated as a new offer requiring the buyer’s acceptance, subject to applicable law and documents.

## Hour 17 — Sales Contract Structure and Risk Allocation

**Required subject mapping:** T04 — Contracts

### Learning objectives

- Identify the operational clauses in a real estate sales contract.
- Explain how contingencies allocate risk and create deadlines.
- Build a transaction checklist from contract terms.

### Instructional content

A sales contract should identify the parties and estate, adequately describe the property, state price and financing structure, address deposit, settlement, possession, fixtures/personal property, title, inspections, casualty, association matters, disclosures, default, notices, and signatures. The contract and addenda must be read as a single integrated set.

A contingency makes performance or termination rights depend on stated conditions. Financing, appraisal, inspection, title, association-document, sale-of-property, and insurance contingencies differ in trigger, deadline, notice, documentation, waiver, and remedy. A licensee must track the actual language, not rely on a generic belief that a contingency automatically ‘protects the buyer.’

Risk control comes from precise facts, clear allocations, realistic dates, proof of notice, and escalation before a deadline. Approved forms reduce drafting risk but do not decide the business terms. Custom legal clauses should be referred to the broker and attorney.

### 50-minute instructional sequence

- **0–5:** Retrieval prompt, objectives, and linkage to prior hours.

- **35–45:** Applied exercise — Students convert a teaching contract into a deadline/owner/evidence matrix and flag three terms requiring clarification before signature.
- **45–50:** Scored engagement check, immediate feedback, and progression gate.

### Scored engagement check

**Question:** Why is the phrase ‘subject to inspection’ insufficient as a complete contingency analysis?

**Expected answer and rationale:** It does not identify the inspection scope, deadline, notice procedure, available responses, waiver rule, or remedy; the actual contract language must do so.

## Hour 18 — Performance, Breach, Remedies, and Contingencies

**Required subject mapping:** T04, T19 — Contracts; title closing

### Learning objectives

- Distinguish performance, waiver, default, and termination.
- Recognize common contract remedies and limits on licensee advice.
- Document contingency actions and closing changes.

### Instructional content

Performance is measured against the contract’s promises, conditions, dates, and standards. A party may satisfy, waive, or fail a contingency according to the contract. Waiver can remove a protection and should be informed and documented. Extending a deadline or changing a term ordinarily requires the required written agreement; a licensee should not imply that informal conversations changed a signed contract.

A material breach may support damages, rescission, termination rights, specific performance, liquidated-damages treatment, or other remedies depending on the agreement and law. The correct remedy is a legal question. The licensee identifies the event, preserves communications and evidence, informs the broker, and encourages timely legal advice.

Closing preparation exposes unresolved issues: title exceptions, financing conditions, casualty, repair obligations, possession, or missing documents. A strong transaction log identifies the obligation, responsible party, due date, current status, notice method, and evidence. It distinguishes a commercial accommodation from a legal waiver.

**Expected answer and rationale:** No. Cancellation and remedies depend on the contract and law; the salesperson should document facts, notify the broker, and refer legal interpretation.

## Hour 19 — Electronic Transactions and Property Disclosures

**Required subject mapping:** T04, T22, T23 — Contracts; regulatory statutes; housing legislation

### Learning objectives

- Explain consent, attribution, integrity, and delivery issues in electronic transactions.
- Navigate the Virginia Residential Property Disclosure Act and current Board materials.
- Identify federal lead-based-paint disclosure triggers at a principles level.

### Instructional content

Electronic signatures and records can satisfy writing/signature requirements when applicable law and party consent permit. Sound practice verifies identity and authority, preserves the complete document and audit trail, controls versions, and proves delivery. Sending a link, receiving a signature, and obtaining effective notice may be separate events under the contract.

Virginia property disclosure duties must be taught from the current Residential Property Disclosure Act, Board forms, and the property/transaction facts. Students learn to identify the governing source, deliver the current required statement or disclosure on time, preserve proof, and avoid replacing statutory language with memory. Known material facts and licensee duties are analyzed separately from a seller’s statutory disclosure framework.

Federal lead-based-paint requirements apply to many pre-1978 residential transactions and include disclosure, records/reports, a warning statement, and a buyer opportunity for inspection or risk assessment, subject to exemptions and current federal rules. Licensees use current forms and refer environmental or legal questions.

## Hour 20 — Brokerage Agreements and Agency Fundamentals

**Required subject mapping:** T15 — Brokerage agreements and agency contract responsibilities

### Learning objectives

- Distinguish client, customer, and unrepresented party.
- Compare standard agency and independent-contractor representation vocabulary.
- Explain how a written brokerage agreement defines scope, compensation, and duration.

### Instructional content

A brokerage relationship arises from the applicable agreement and law, not merely from who pays compensation. A client has engaged the licensee/firm for brokerage services; a customer or unrepresented party may receive ministerial or factual assistance without becoming a client. The licensee must not create confusion about whom the firm represents.

Agency vocabulary includes principal/client, agent, subagent where permitted, dual agent, and designated agent. Virginia law also recognizes independent-contractor representation terminology. Compensation, cooperation, MLS participation, or showing a property does not by itself decide the relationship.

A brokerage agreement should define the client, property or search scope, services, duties, compensation, term, termination, and relationship type. It must work with statutory duties and required disclosures. Students separate legal duties from optional services and understand that a limited-service arrangement requires specific disclosure.

## Hour 21 — Virginia Duties to Sellers, Buyers, Landlords, and Tenants

**Required subject mapping:** T15, T22 — Brokerage agreements/agency; regulatory statutes

### Learning objectives

- Locate Virginia statutory duties for seller, buyer, landlord, and tenant clients.
- Apply ordinary care, accounting, confidentiality, disclosure, and lawful-instruction duties.
- Distinguish client advocacy from misleading another party.

### Instructional content

Virginia Title 54.1, Chapter 21 provides separate sections for licensees engaged by sellers, buyers, landlords, and tenants. Students use §§ 54.1-2131 through 54.1-2134 rather than relying on a generic mnemonic. Duties include performing under the brokerage agreement, promoting the client’s interests within legal limits, ordinary care, accounting, confidentiality, disclosure of required material facts, and timely presentation or communication as the applicable section provides.

Client advocacy never authorizes misrepresentation, concealment, fair-housing violations, or disregard of law. Confidentiality survives according to statute and agreement, while some facts must be disclosed. The licensee asks: What fact is known? From whom? Is it confidential? Is disclosure required? Has the broker or counsel been involved?

Accounting covers money and property received in the brokerage relationship, but handling deposits also invokes Board escrow regulations and firm procedures. The affiliated salesperson promptly transmits funds and documents as required by the broker’s system rather than independently holding or deciding them.

- **45–50:** Scored engagement check, immediate feedback, and progression gate.

### Scored engagement check

**Expected answer and rationale:** No. Client instructions are followed only within lawful limits; statutory and regulatory duties cannot be waived by an instruction to conceal.

## Hour 22 — Virginia Brokerage Disclosures, Dual, Designated, and Limited Service

**Required subject mapping:** T15, T22 — Brokerage agreements/agency; regulatory statutes

### Learning objectives

- Identify when Virginia requires a written brokerage agreement.
- Explain disclosed dual and designated relationships at a high level.
- Recognize limited-service disclosure requirements.

### Instructional content

Under § 54.1-2137, a licensee generally enters a brokerage agreement with a prospective client before providing brokerage services, subject to the statute’s limited exception for preparing property-specific materials intended to obtain the relationship. The written agreement addresses termination, fees, services, and agreed terms. If no definite termination date is stated, the current statute supplies a 90-day termination rule; students must verify the current text before release.

Residential dual agency or dual representation requires informed written consent after written disclosure of consequences and before dual activity begins. Current § 54.1-2139 specifies that the required residential disclosure is not made compliant merely by placing it in a purchase agreement, lease, or other transaction document. Designated relationships allow different affiliated licensees to represent different clients under statutory safeguards and broker designation.

A limited-service agreement identifies the services provided and the standard-agent duties not provided, using conspicuous disclosure. In every model, the licensee uses the current statutory language and broker-approved forms, obtains signatures at the required time, and preserves proof.

### Scored engagement check

**Expected answer and rationale:** No. Current § 54.1-2139 states that the required disclosure does not comply if given in the purchase agreement, lease, or other transaction document; use the required separate/conspicuous process.

## Hour 23 — Virginia Licensing Structure and Regulatory Authority

**Required subject mapping:** T22, T25 — Regulatory statutes; Real Estate Board regulations

### Learning objectives

- Explain the roles of the Real Estate Board and DPOR.
- Distinguish salesperson, broker, firm, branch, principal broker, and supervising broker.
- Identify licensed activity and active/inactive status issues.

### Instructional content

The Virginia Real Estate Board establishes and enforces licensing requirements under statutory authority; DPOR administers licensing and regulatory operations. The Board’s law and regulations protect the public and define who may perform brokerage activity, how firms and offices are structured, and what conduct may be disciplined.

A salesperson performs licensed activity under broker supervision. A broker may qualify for broader responsibility, but firm, branch, sole-proprietor, principal-broker, supervising-broker, and business-entity rules still matter. Active status connects the individual to the licensed brokerage structure; inactive status does not authorize brokerage practice.

Students analyze substance rather than job titles. A ‘consultant,’ ‘transaction coordinator,’ ‘team leader,’ property manager, referral source, or technology platform can cross into licensed activity depending on what is done, for whom, and for what compensation. When uncertain, the licensee escalates to the principal/supervising broker and current Board sources.

## Hour 24 — Virginia Standards of Conduct and Discipline

**Required subject mapping:** T22, T25 — Regulatory statutes; Real Estate Board regulations

### Learning objectives

- Recognize conduct that can trigger Board discipline.
- Distinguish mistake correction from concealment or misrepresentation.
- Use a broker escalation and documentation process.

### Instructional content

Discipline can arise from fraud, material misrepresentation, omission, incompetence, unworthiness, failure to account, improper handling of funds, advertising violations, record failures, lack of supervision, unlawful discrimination, or other statutory/regulatory grounds. Students navigate the current disciplinary sections rather than memorizing an incomplete list.

A misrepresentation can be affirmative or created by half-truths and misleading presentation. A licensee who discovers an error should stop further dissemination, preserve the original, notify the supervising broker, correct affected communications, and document recipients and timing. Deleting evidence or silently changing records compounds risk.

Board discipline is separate from civil liability, criminal exposure, contract remedies, MLS action, or professional-association ethics. One event can create several processes. Salespersons cooperate with investigations and broker procedures, protect confidentiality appropriately, and do not retaliate against complainants.

## Hour 25 — Virginia Escrow, Records, Advertising, and Supervision

**Required subject mapping:** T25 — Real Estate Board regulations

### Learning objectives

- Explain escrow-account and deposit-control principles.
- Identify required record and supervision functions.
- Audit advertising for firm/licensee identity and misleading content.

### Instructional content

Escrow money belongs to others and is controlled by the contract, law, regulation, and broker’s account procedures. Core controls include prompt transmission to the authorized custodian, separation from operating funds, accurate ledgers, reconciliation, restricted disbursement, documentation, and escalation of disputes. Exact deadlines and disbursement authority are taught from the current regulation and firm policy.

Transaction records should reconstruct what occurred: agreements, disclosures, offers/counteroffers, notices, deposits, advertisements, communications, settlement documents, and supervision. The course’s five-year education-record rule is separate from brokerage transaction-record rules; students must not merge retention periods.

Advertising includes digital and social media. It must be truthful, not misleading, compliant with fair housing, and identify the responsible licensed brokerage as current regulations require. Team and individual branding cannot obscure the firm or imply a separate unlicensed brokerage. Principal and supervising brokers establish written systems and review practices; affiliated salespersons follow them.

## Hour 26 — Federal and Virginia Fair Housing

**Required subject mapping:** T23, T24 — Housing legislation; fair housing statutes

### Learning objectives

- List federal and current Virginia protected classes.
- Recognize refusal, different terms, steering, blockbusting, redlining, and discriminatory advertising.
- Apply uniform, documented service criteria.

### Instructional content

Federal fair housing protections include race, color, religion, sex, national origin, familial status, and disability. Virginia’s current protected classes also include elderliness, source of funds, sexual orientation, gender identity, and military status. Local ordinances may add protections. The official Virginia Fair Housing Office source is checked before every release.

Prohibited conduct can include refusing or making housing unavailable, different terms or services, discriminatory statements/advertising, false availability, steering, blockbusting, discriminatory MLS access, and lending or insurance discrimination. Intent is not the only risk; policies applied inconsistently can create unlawful outcomes.

Objective criteria must be relevant, written, consistently applied, and documented. A licensee provides equal information and opportunity, avoids neighborhood demographic steering, does not accept a client’s discriminatory instruction, and reports/escalates concerns. ‘Buyer preference’ does not excuse discriminatory search or marketing.

## Hour 27 — Disability, Accessibility, Assistance Animals, and Housing Legislation

**Required subject mapping:** T23, T24 — Housing legislation; fair housing statutes

### Learning objectives

- Distinguish reasonable accommodation from reasonable modification.
- Explain that assistance-animal rights do not depend on purchased certification.
- Recognize accessibility and ADA/Fair Housing Act boundaries.

### Instructional content

A reasonable accommodation changes a rule, policy, practice, or service when necessary for a person with a disability to use and enjoy housing. A reasonable modification is a physical change, often at the requesting person’s expense subject to law, with special rules for federally assisted housing and restoration. Each request is evaluated through the legal process; blanket refusal is unsafe.

An assistance animal may be a service animal or another animal that provides disability-related assistance or emotional support under fair housing rules. Virginia DPOR warns that online registration or certification does not create disability status and housing providers cannot demand special training or certification as a universal condition. Permissible verification depends on whether disability and need are apparent and current guidance.

Fair-housing design and construction requirements apply to covered multifamily housing under statutory dates and criteria. The ADA applies to public accommodations and other settings but is not a substitute for housing-law analysis. Students identify the covered area and obtain qualified guidance.

## Hour 30 — Subdivisions, Condominiums, and Development Disclosures

**Required subject mapping:** T21, T23 — Subdivision/development/condominiums; housing legislation

### Learning objectives

- Describe subdivision approval and plat-recording concepts.
- Explain condominium unit/common-element structure.
- Recognize developer/public-offering and resale-document questions.

### Instructional content

A subdivision divides land into lots or parcels subject to local/state process. Approval may address access, utilities, drainage, easements, dedications, streets, bonds, environmental constraints, and plat recording. Recording a plat does not guarantee that a particular structure, use, well, septic system, or future division is permitted.

A condominium is created through recorded instruments defining units, common elements, limited common elements, allocations, governance, and obligations. Development rights, phasing, conversion, declarant control, warranties, and budgets require careful document review. A buyer’s rights may differ between developer sale and resale.

Virginia’s current Condominium Act, Property Owners’ Association Act, and Resale Disclosure Act are controlling. The salesperson identifies the transaction category, requests the correct disclosure package, tracks receipt and decision deadlines from current law and contract, and preserves proof. Legal interpretation belongs to counsel.
