# Missouri Real Estate Practice Course — Module MREP-05

## Hours 18–21 · Types of financing and estimating closing costs

**Commission topics covered:** 20 CSR 2250-6.060(2)(K) Types of Financing · (L) Estimating Closing Costs
**Clock hours:** 4. A Missouri clock hour is **60 minutes containing at least 50 minutes of actual
instruction** — 20 CSR 2250-7.070(2).

**Course version:** 1.0 submission draft
**Regulatory status:** Development. Not approved. No Missouri pre-license credit is available.

## Learning objectives

1. Describe each category of financing structurally, without recommending one.
2. Explain what a Missouri deed of trust is and what follows from a trustee's power of sale.
3. Distinguish pre-qualification, pre-approval and commitment, and explain the financing contingency.
4. Build a buyer's estimated cost sheet and a seller's estimated net sheet.
5. Prorate a shared expense correctly, in the right direction, on a stated basis.
6. State what makes every one of those figures an estimate and label it accordingly.
7. State the licensee's boundary on financing advice.

## Hour 18 — Types of financing — 20 CSR 2250-6.060(2)(K)

### 18.1 The categories

**Conventional.** Not insured or guaranteed by a government agency. Below a threshold down payment the
lender requires **private mortgage insurance**, which **protects the lender, not the borrower**, and
which can generally be removed once equity conditions are met. Buyers consistently believe PMI protects
them; it does not, and telling them so is a genuine service.

**FHA-insured.** Insured by the Federal Housing Administration. Lower down payment requirements, mortgage
insurance premiums on their own terms, and property condition standards applied through the appraisal —
which can affect whether a particular house qualifies at all.

**VA-guaranteed.** For eligible veterans and service members. Typically no down payment, a funding fee in
place of monthly mortgage insurance, and its own appraisal and property requirements.

**USDA rural development.** For eligible properties in eligible areas with eligible borrowers. Relevant
across much of Missouri.

**State and local assistance programmes** — down payment assistance, first-time buyer and bond
programmes. **This course states no eligibility criterion, income limit or purchase price limit for any
Missouri programme**, because none was read from primary source for this project. They are routed to the
administering agency and to the lender.

**Seller financing**, including the **land contract or contract for deed** structure, in which the buyer
takes possession and equitable title while the seller retains legal title until the payments are
complete. This is the arrangement in which a buyer has the **least** protection, and the consequences of
default are a legal question with real stakes. **A licensee explains the structure and routes the
decision to counsel.**

### 18.2 Rate and payment structures

**Fixed rate** — the rate does not change for the life of the loan.

**Adjustable rate** — a rate tied to an index plus a margin, adjusting on a schedule, with **caps** on
the periodic adjustment and on the lifetime rate. A buyer considering one needs the **worst case**, not
the teaser: the lifetime cap tells you the highest rate possible, and it is computable.

**Interest-only, balloon and graduated payment** structures each carry a risk profile the buyer must
understand before signing. A balloon is a date on which a large sum becomes due, and a buyer who has not
diarised it has a problem waiting.

### 18.3 The rule that governs this hour

**A licensee explains structures and routes pricing.**

Which programme is cheapest for a particular borrower depends on the rate, the term, the mortgage
insurance treatment, the fees, the borrower's tax position and how long they will hold the property. A
licensee controls none of those and knows some of them not at all.

Naming the categories is education. **Recommending one is advice the licensee is not licensed to give**,
and § 339.740.1(3)(d), RSMo — advising the client to obtain expert advice on material matters beyond the
licensee's expertise — is the statutory instruction to route it.

## Hour 19 — Missouri instruments, qualification, and the contingency

### 19.1 Note and deed of trust

The **promissory note** is the borrower's promise to repay — the debt. The **security instrument**
pledges the property as security for it — the lien.

**Missouri practice uses a deed of trust**, a three-party instrument: **trustor** (borrower),
**beneficiary** (lender), and a **trustee** who holds a **power of sale**. On default the trustee may
conduct a sale **without a lawsuit** — a non-judicial process, in contrast to the judicial foreclosure
used in mortgage states.

**This course states no Missouri foreclosure timetable, notice requirement, redemption right or
deficiency rule**, because Missouri's foreclosure chapter was not read from primary text for this
project. The structural fact — a trustee holding a power of sale, exercised without a court — is what a
licensee carries. Every question about timing, notice, redemption or deficiency is routed to counsel,
and a licensee working a distressed file routes the seller's tax and credit consequences to their
accountant.

### 19.2 The clauses that decide outcomes

**Acceleration** — on default the whole balance becomes due. **Alienation, or due-on-sale** — on
transfer the lender may call the balance, which is why "taking subject to" is not a workaround a
licensee arranges and why **assumption requires lender consent**. **Prepayment** — whether early
repayment is permitted and on what terms. **Escrow or impound** — the lender collects taxes and
insurance monthly, which is why the payment is **PITI** and why an escrow analysis can change a payment
after closing without the rate having moved. Warn buyers about that last one; it arrives as a shock
otherwise.

**Assumption with a written release** takes the seller off the obligation. **Assumption without one**
does not — the seller remains liable for a loan on a house they no longer own.

### 19.3 Qualification, in one page

**The four Cs:** capacity (income against obligations), credit (the record of repayment), capital (funds
for down payment, costs and reserves — and their **sourcing and seasoning**, which surprises buyers
gifted money at the last minute), and collateral (the property, through the appraisal).

**The ratios:** the **housing ratio** is the proposed **PITI** plus mortgage insurance and association
dues, divided by gross monthly income. The **total debt ratio** adds every other recurring obligation.

*Worked.* Gross monthly income $7,100. PITI $1,704. Car $395, student loan $260, card minimums $110.
Housing ratio 1,704 ÷ 7,100 = **24.0%**. Total obligations $2,469 ÷ 7,100 = **34.8%**.

**The thresholds are the lender's and the programme's, not the licensee's**, they differ by programme,
they move, and they yield to compensating factors and automated underwriting decisions no licensee sees.
A licensee who quotes a qualifying ratio as a rule has stated an underwriting standard as law.

### 19.4 Pre-qualification, pre-approval, commitment — and the contingency

**Pre-qualification** is an estimate from stated information. **Pre-approval** follows verification and
underwriting. **Neither is a loan commitment.**

**That gap is the entire reason the financing contingency exists.** A buyer who believes a pre-approval
is a promise will not understand why the contingency matters, will resent the seller's anxiety about it,
and will be blindsided if underwriting turns. Say it at the beginning, plainly.

**The contingency itself** has four parts and all four must be read: the **condition** — a loan of a
stated type and amount on stated terms; the **deadline**; the **method of notice**; and **the effect of
silence** if the deadline passes with no notice. That last one differs by form, and assuming is how
transactions die.

**The licensee's actual job here is calendar work.** On the day of acceptance, write every financing
deadline in the file and in the calendar. Work backwards to the date the application must be complete.
Confirm each step in writing. Raise a slipping date with the client **before** it passes.

## Hour 20 — Estimating closing costs: the buyer's side — 20 CSR 2250-6.060(2)(L)

### 20.1 What a buyer pays, by category

**Loan costs.** Origination and underwriting fees; discount points if any; appraisal; credit report;
flood determination; any lender-required inspections.

**Title and settlement.** Lender's title policy and the owner's policy if the buyer is paying for it;
settlement or closing fee; recording fees; and a survey if obtained.

**Prepaid items and escrow deposits.** Prepaid interest from closing to the end of the month; the first
year's hazard insurance premium; and the initial deposit into the tax and insurance escrow.

**Prorations.** The buyer's share of items paid in advance by the seller; and a **credit** to the buyer
for items paid in arrears that the buyer will pay later.

### 20.2 A worked buyer's estimate

*Purchase price $318,000. Loan $286,200 (90 percent). Earnest money $4,000 already deposited. Closing 14
October. Taxes paid in arrears, annual $3,720, 365-day basis, closing day to the buyer.*

| Item | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Purchase price | $318,000.00 |
| Loan origination, 0.75% of loan | 2,146.50 |
| Appraisal | 675.00 |
| Credit report and flood determination | 145.00 |
| Lender's title policy | 495.00 |
| Owner's title policy | 1,190.00 |
| Settlement fee | 550.00 |
| Recording | 118.00 |
| Prepaid interest, 17 days at $47.70 | 810.90 |
| Hazard insurance, first year | 1,620.00 |
| Escrow deposit, taxes and insurance | 1,445.00 |
| **Subtotal** | **$327,195.40** |
| Less loan amount | (286,200.00) |
| Less earnest money | (4,000.00) |
| Less tax proration credit to buyer | (2,914.85) |
| **Estimated cash to close** | **$34,080.55** |

**The tax proration, worked.** $3,720 ÷ 365 = **$10.1918** a day. The seller's days run 1 January
through 13 October, because the closing day goes to the buyer: 31+28+31+30+31+30+31+31+30+13 = **286
days**. 286 × 10.1918 = **$2,914.85**.

**Count the days rather than estimating them.** "About nine and a half months" produces a figure that
looks right and is wrong by a hundred dollars or more, and it will be wrong on the settlement statement
too if nobody recomputes it. Add the month lengths, remember February, and check whether the year is a
leap year before dividing by 365.

It is a **credit to the buyer** because the buyer will pay the whole tax bill when it issues, including
the part covering the seller's period of ownership.

### 20.3 Who quotes what

The figures above come from **the lender** (loan costs, prepaid interest, escrow) and **the closing agent
or title company** (title, settlement, recording, prorations). **They do not come from the licensee.**

A licensee assembles the estimate from those sources and labels it as what it is.

## Hour 21 — Prorations and the seller's net

### 21.1 Proration, properly

Three questions before any arithmetic:

1. **What is the annual amount?**
2. **What basis** — 365-day year, or 360-day banker's year with 30-day months?
3. **Who gets the day of closing**, and is the item paid **in advance** or **in arrears**?

**The convention comes from the contract, not from habit.** Getting the basis or the closing-day
convention wrong produces a number close enough to look right and wrong enough to matter.

**Direction.** Ask: **who has had the use, and who has paid?** Whoever paid for time they will not have
gets a **credit**.

*Paid in arrears — taxes.* The seller has had the use and has not paid. The buyer will pay the whole
bill. **Credit the buyer, debit the seller.**

*Paid in advance — annual association dues.* The seller has paid for time the buyer will have. **Credit
the seller, debit the buyer.**

*Worked, in advance.* Association dues $540 paid annually on 1 January. Closing 14 October, 365-day
basis, closing day to the buyer. Daily 540 ÷ 365 = $1.4795. Days from 14 October through 31 December:
18+30+31 = **79**. 79 × 1.4795 = **$116.88**, a **credit to the seller and a debit to the buyer.**

### 21.2 The seller's estimated net

*Sale price $318,000. First deed of trust payoff $214,600. Second lien $18,200. Commission 6 percent.
Seller's title and settlement costs $1,860. Agreed repair credit to buyer $1,500. Tax proration debit to
seller $2,914.85. Association dues credit to seller $116.88.*

| Item | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Sale price | $318,000.00 |
| Less first deed of trust payoff | (214,600.00) |
| Less second lien payoff | (18,200.00) |
| Less commission, 6% | (19,080.00) |
| Less seller's title and settlement costs | (1,860.00) |
| Less repair credit to buyer | (1,500.00) |
| Less tax proration | (2,914.85) |
| Plus association dues proration | 116.88 |
| **Estimated seller's net** | **$59,962.03** |

### 21.3 What makes every one of these an estimate — and why the label matters

**Payoff figures include per-diem interest and are quoted only to a stated date.** A closing that slips
three days changes the payoff.

**Closing costs are the closing agent's and the lender's figures**, not the licensee's, and they move.

**Prorations depend on a tax bill that may not yet have issued**, in which case the parties are
estimating from the prior year — and the contract should say what happens if the actual bill differs.

**Repair credits, seller concessions and late changes all move the total**, and under **TRID** a change
close to closing can trigger a redisclosure and a new three-business-day waiting period before
consummation. Getting changes decided **early** and telling the lender promptly is a real service.

**So label it.** A net sheet is an **estimate**; it says so on its face; it shows its assumptions and its
sources; and the figures come from the closing agent, the lender and the payoff statement.

**A licensee who hands a seller a confident net figure that turns out to be $3,000 optimistic has
created a problem no apology fixes** — and, depending on how it was presented, a misrepresentation
question under § 339.730.1(2) and Module MREP-06's topic (O).
