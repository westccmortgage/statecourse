# Missouri Real Estate Practice Course — Module MREP-06

## Hours 22–24 · Contracts, earnest money, and avoiding misrepresentation

**Commission topics covered:** 20 CSR 2250-6.060(2)(M) Contracts · (N) Earnest Money · (O) Avoiding
Misrepresentation
**Clock hours:** 3. A Missouri clock hour is **60 minutes containing at least 50 minutes of actual
instruction** — 20 CSR 2250-7.070(2).
**Hour allocation:** a StateCourse proposal for Commission review; 20 CSR 2250-6.060(2) allocates no
hours among its sixteen topics.
**Course version:** 1.0 submission draft
**Regulatory status:** Development. Not approved. No Missouri pre-license credit is available.

## Learning objectives

1. Take a contract from offer through acceptance and identify the date every deadline runs from.
2. Distinguish an amendment from a notice and use each correctly.
3. State what earnest money is, what it is not, and the three rules that govern a licensee's handling.
4. State who decides the disposition of disputed earnest money.
5. Distinguish innocent, negligent and fraudulent misrepresentation, and puffing from a statement of
   fact.
6. Apply the § 339.710(1) adverse material fact definition and the two disclosure standards.
7. Describe the working method that prevents misrepresentation before it happens.
8. State what a Missouri salesperson carries out of the 24-hour practice course.

## Hour 22 — Contracts — 20 CSR 2250-6.060(2)(M)

### 22.1 From offer to contract

An **offer** is a definite proposal, communicated to the offeree, showing an intention to be bound on
acceptance. It ends by **acceptance**, **rejection**, **counter-offer**, **revocation** before
acceptance is communicated, or **lapse**.

**A counter-offer rejects the original offer and substitutes a new one.** The original is dead and can
no longer simply be accepted. That is why a seller with three offers cannot counter all three without
destroying all three, and why a multiple counter-proposal instrument exists where the firm's forms
supply one.

**Acceptance** must be unqualified and **communicated in the manner the offer requires.** Signature and
delivery are different events and may fall on different days.

### 22.2 The date everything runs from

**Every deadline in the contract runs from the moment the contract is formed** — and on most forms that
is the date acceptance is delivered, not the date the last party signed.

**The discipline:** on the day acceptance is delivered, **write the date in the place the contract
provides, and compute every deadline from it that same day**, into the file and into your calendar.

A deadline miscounted from the wrong anchor shifts every subsequent date, and it is among the most
common and most avoidable failures in residential practice.

### 22.3 The § 339.780.7 obligation, applied

Recall the statutory minimum for an **exclusive** brokerage agreement: the broker will provide, at a
minimum, **accepting delivery of and presenting** offers and counteroffers; **assisting** in developing,
communicating, negotiating and presenting offers, counteroffers and notices **until a lease or purchase
agreement is signed and all contingencies are satisfied or waived**; and **answering** the client's or
customer's questions about them.

**"Until all contingencies are satisfied or waived" is the operative phrase.** The statutory floor runs
past contract signature and through the contingency period. A firm that treats its work as done at
signature has not met it.

### 22.4 Presenting offers

Section 339.730.1(3)(b) — and § 339.740.1(3)(b) — requires **presenting all written offers to and from
the client in a timely manner regardless of whether the property is subject to a contract** for sale or
lease.

**All of them. In writing. Promptly. Even after the property is under contract.** What the seller then
does with a late-arriving offer is a legal question about their existing contract, and it goes to
counsel.

Section 339.755(2)(a) imposes the same presentation duty on a transaction broker, unless the agreement
with the party provides otherwise.

### 22.5 Three instruments, distinguished

- An **offer** or **counter-offer** proposes terms **before** there is a contract.
- An **amendment** changes a contract that **already exists** and requires the agreement of all parties.
- A **notice** exercises a right the contract **already gives** — removing a contingency, giving notice
  of a defect, giving notice of an inability to obtain financing. A notice is **unilateral**: it does not
  need the other side's agreement, but it must be given in the manner and by the deadline the contract
  specifies.

**Using the wrong one is a common and expensive error.** A buyer who "amends" when they should have given
notice has asked permission for something they were entitled to do — and has handed the other side a
negotiation they did not have to have.

### 22.6 The drafting boundary

**Inside the licence:** completing the forms your firm supplies by filling in the facts of the
transaction; selecting among the provisions the forms offer; explaining what a provision says in its
ordinary sense; and advising the client to obtain expert advice, as § 339.730.1(3)(d) requires.

**Outside the licence:** composing new legal language to solve a client's specific legal problem;
advising which form of ownership to take title in; opining on the legal effect of a provision; drafting
an addendum to address something the forms do not cover.

**When the forms do not fit, counsel drafts it and it is attached.** A licensee who composes a clause has
practised law — and has usually produced a clause that does not do what the client wanted.

## Hour 23 — Earnest money — 20 CSR 2250-6.060(2)(N)

### 23.1 What it is, and what it is not

**Earnest money is a deposit evidencing the buyer's good faith**, held in trust, and applied to the
purchase price at closing.

**It is not the consideration for the contract** — the consideration is the mutual promises. **It is not
a fee.** **It is not the licensee's or the firm's money at any point.** And a contract without earnest
money is not automatically invalid; earnest money is a commercial feature, not an element of formation.

Its practical functions are two: it signals seriousness to the seller, and it constitutes a fund from
which the seller may be made whole if the buyer defaults — which is why many forms designate it as
**liquidated damages** for a buyer's default, and why the amount matters.

### 23.2 The three rules

**One: it is trust money from the moment it comes into your hands.**

It goes to the firm for handling under the firm's trust account procedures. It never touches an operating
account. Section 339.730.1(4), RSMo, states the statutory duty: **to account in a timely manner for all
money and property received.**

**Two: you do not hold it.**

Not uncashed in a drawer as a favour to a buyer who asked you to wait until their contingency clears.
Not over a weekend because the broker is away. A licensee who is asked to hold a cheque says no and
explains why, and if the buyer wants a later deposit that is a **term of the contract** to be negotiated
in writing — not an informal arrangement with the licensee.

**Three: you do not release it on request.**

Not to a seller as a show of good faith. Not to a buyer who has changed their mind. Not on the strength
of both parties saying it is fine, unless the release is documented in the way your firm's procedures
require.

### 23.3 Who decides a disputed disposition

**Not the licensee, and not the firm on its own judgment.** The two questions are **what the contract
provides** and **what the parties or a court determine.**

Where the parties disagree, the firm's options are those its procedures and counsel identify —
customarily holding the funds until a written mutual release or a court order, or interpleading them.
The licensee's role is to route it, not to resolve it.

**Releasing disputed funds on your own judgment is a licence problem regardless of who turns out to have
been right about the contract.**

### 23.4 The Missouri rules this course does not state

**This course states no Missouri deposit deadline, trust account requirement, threshold, exception or
retention period**, because **§ 339.105, RSMo, and 20 CSR 2250 chapters 1 through 5 and 8 were not read
from primary text for this project.**

That is a real gap and it is named rather than filled. **Read § 339.105 and the trust account chapter in
current text, and read your firm's written procedures, before you accept your first cheque.**

Two reasons it matters more than most gaps. First, **trust account failures are the fastest route out of
this profession**, and they are almost never theft — they are convenience, helpfulness and sloppiness,
which look identical on an audit. Second, the deposit deadline is a **specific number of days** that
differs between states, and a licensee working from another state's habit will breach Missouri's without
noticing.

### 23.5 A working routine

1. **Receive it and deliver it to the firm the same day**, following the firm's procedure exactly.
2. **Document what you received, from whom, in what form, and when.**
3. **Confirm the deposit to the buyer in writing.**
4. **Diarise the deadline** the contract sets for the deposit.
5. **On any dispute, escalate immediately** to the designated broker. Do nothing else.

## Hour 24 — Avoiding misrepresentation — 20 CSR 2250-6.060(2)(O)

### 24.1 The three kinds

**Innocent misrepresentation** — a false statement of material fact the speaker believed true and had
reasonable grounds to believe. **It can still support rescission**, and it is still a licence problem,
because the buyer relied on it either way.

**Negligent misrepresentation** — a false statement made without reasonable care as to its truth. This
is the one licensees commit most, and it is almost always by **repeating something without checking it**.

**Fraudulent misrepresentation** — a false statement made knowingly or recklessly, with intent to induce
reliance. This is the one with the largest consequences and it includes **active concealment** — painting
over a stain, running a dehumidifier before every showing.

**And the fourth, which is not a statement at all: failure to disclose an adverse material fact.**
Silence is actionable in Missouri where the statute requires speech, and §§ 339.730.1(3)(c), 339.730.3,
339.740.3 and 339.755(2)(d) require it.

### 24.2 Puffing, and where it stops

**Puffing** is obvious opinion nobody could reasonably rely on: *lovely home; wonderful natural light;
you'll love the kitchen.* It is not actionable because it is not a statement of fact.

**It stops the moment a statement is specific, factual and checkable:**

| Puffing | A statement of fact |
|---|---|
| "Great location" | "It's a fifteen-minute drive to the hospital" |
| "Beautiful lot" | "The lot is half an acre" |
| "Solid house" | "The roof was replaced in 2019" |
| "Plenty of room" | "It's 2,100 square feet" |
| "Won't last long" | "There are two other offers" |

Every entry in the right-hand column is a representation. **If you cannot show where the number came
from, do not say it.**

### 24.3 Where misrepresentation actually comes from

Not from lying. From **repeating**:

- **The square footage from a tax record.** An assessment record is not a measurement.
- **The acreage from a prior listing.** Somebody else's figure, which may have been wrong then.
- **The school assignment.** Boundaries change. Route it to the district.
- **The zoning.** Route it to the municipality.
- **"The seller says the basement is dry."** Attribute it, or verify it, and disclose what you know.
- **"The roof is about ten years old."** Where did "about" come from?
- **The flood zone.** An inventory map is not a determination. Route it.
- **The lender's estimate of the buyer's payment**, restated as their payment.
- **The net sheet**, presented as a promise rather than an estimate.

**The rule that prevents nearly all of it: attribute or verify. Never assert.** *"The tax record shows
1,940 square feet; if the exact figure matters to you, have it measured."* That sentence costs nothing
and is complete protection.

### 24.4 The statutory frame, restated

**Section 339.710(1)** defines an **adverse material fact** as *a fact related to the property not
reasonably ascertainable or known to a party which negatively affects the value of the property*, and
lists environmental hazards, physical condition adversely affecting value, material defects in the
property, **material defects in title**, and **material limitation of the party's ability to perform
under the terms of the contract.**

**The two standards:**

- A **limited agent** discloses adverse material facts **actually known or that should have been known**
  — to the **client** under § 339.730.1(3)(c), and to any **customer** under § 339.730.3 and § 339.740.3.
- A **transaction broker** discloses those **of which the licensee has actual notice or knowledge** —
  § 339.755(2)(d).

**"Should have been known" makes wilful ignorance unavailable.** A licensee who arranges not to learn
something has not escaped the duty.

**And confidentiality does not override it.** Sections 339.730.2 and 339.740.2 permit disclosure of
confidential information where **failure to disclose would constitute a misrepresentation** — and
provide that **no cause of action arises against a licensee for making any required or permitted
disclosure.** A seller who tells you in confidence that the basement floods has told you an adverse
material fact; they have not sealed your mouth.

**Say so at the time.** *"I have to tell you that this is something I will need to disclose. Let's talk
about how we handle it."* Sellers accept that far better than they accept discovering it afterwards.

### 24.5 The method

Six habits, and they prevent nearly every misrepresentation problem a licensee will ever have:

1. **Attribute or verify. Never assert.**
2. **Put it in writing.** Disclosures, material conversations, and every figure with its source.
3. **Disclose early.** A defect disclosed at listing is information. The same defect disclosed the week
   of closing is a crisis, and looks like concealment whether or not it was.
4. **Disclose to everyone entitled to it** — clients under § 339.730.1(3)(c), customers under
   § 339.730.3 and § 339.740.3.
5. **Route what is not yours.** Legal, tax, engineering, environmental, appraisal, zoning, boundary,
   flood, well, septic. Section 339.730.1(3)(d) requires it.
6. **When you get something wrong, correct it in writing immediately**, tell your designated broker, and
   document it. The alternative — quietly fixing it and hoping — converts an error into a concealment,
   and concealment is what turns a survivable problem into a licence problem.

### 24.6 What you carry out of the 24-hour practice course

**The sixteen topics 20 CSR 2250-6.060(2) requires**, and behind them one structure:

**Who is this person?** Client or customer, decided by a written agreement and by nothing else — not
rapport, not time, not who pays.

**What do I owe them?** To a client, the six duties of § 339.730.1 or § 339.740.1. To a customer,
adverse material fact disclosure. In dual agency, both sets of duties minus the five items of
§ 339.750.4. As a transaction broker, neutrality with the § 339.755.2 duties.

**What has to be in writing?** The broker disclosure form at first substantial contact under
§ 339.770.1. The brokerage agreement under § 339.780, with the contents that section specifies. Every
compensation arrangement. Every disclosure. Every material conversation.

**What is not mine?** Legal, tax, engineering, environmental and appraisal questions; the disposition of
disputed earnest money; what a municipality will permit; what a lender will approve; and what a price
will be next year.
