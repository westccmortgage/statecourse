## Hours 30–34 · Valuation, market analysis, leases, and property management

## Learning objectives

1. Distinguish value, price and cost, and explain why an owner's improvement cost is not value.
2. Name the principles of value and apply substitution, contribution, conformity and anticipation.
3. Apply the four tests of highest and best use in the correct order.
4. Build a comparison grid with adjustments in the correct direction and reconcile to a range.
5. Describe the cost and income approaches and say when each governs.
6. State the boundary between a licensee's market analysis and an appraisal.
7. Classify a leasehold estate and identify what notice, if any, ends it.
8. Describe a property manager's functions and compute net operating income.

## Hour 30 — Value, price, cost, and the principles

### 30.1 Three words

**Value** is the worth of a property to a typical buyer under stated conditions — most often **market
value**, the most probable price a property should bring in a competitive and open market under
conditions requisite to a fair sale, with buyer and seller each acting prudently and knowledgeably and
neither under undue duress.

**Price** is what was actually paid in a particular transaction. It is a historical fact and it may
have been influenced by a motivated seller, an uninformed buyer, an unusual concession or a
relationship between the parties.

**Cost** is what it took to produce the improvements.

**Cost does not become value.** An owner who spent $92,000 on a pool, a sunroom and a kitchen is
frequently astonished by the appraisal, and the explanation is Hour 30's most important principle:
the **principle of contribution** says an improvement adds what it adds to the value of the whole, not
what it cost.

### 30.2 The principles of value

**Supply and demand.** Value rises when demand exceeds supply and falls when it does not.

**Substitution.** A buyer will pay no more for a property than the cost of acquiring an equally
desirable substitute. This is the foundation of all three approaches to value.

**Contribution.** As above. Measured by the change in the value of the whole, not by cost.

**Conformity.** Value is maximised where a property conforms reasonably to those around it. The
corollaries are **progression** — a modest house gains from better neighbours — and **regression** —
an over-improved house loses to worse ones. The most over-built house on the street is the standard
case of regression, and it is also the standard listing where the owner cannot understand the price.

**Anticipation.** Value reflects the expectation of future benefits, which is why a rumoured highway
interchange moves prices before it is built.

**Change.** Nothing is static. Markets, neighbourhoods and buildings all move through phases.

**Increasing and diminishing returns.** Additional investment increases value up to a point, after
which it does not.

**Plottage** — assembling adjacent parcels under one ownership to produce a value greater than the
sum of the parts, the increment being **assemblage** value. **Externalities** — influences from
outside the property.

### 30.3 The four forces

**Social** — demographics, household composition, preferences. **Economic** — employment, income,
rates, credit. **Governmental** — zoning, codes, taxation, public investment. **Environmental** —
physical characteristics, topography, transport, location.

## Hour 31 — Highest and best use, and the sales comparison approach

### 31.1 Highest and best use

The use that is:

1. **Legally permissible**,
2. **Physically possible**,
3. **Financially feasible**, and
4. **Maximally productive**.

**All four, and in that order.** A use that is not legally permissible never reaches the profitability
question. This is also where a licensee stops: whether a use is permissible is a determination for the
municipality, not an opinion for a listing presentation.

### 31.2 The sales comparison approach

The most reliable approach for owner-occupied residential property, because that is how the market
actually behaves — buyers compare.

**The rule that governs every grid: adjust the COMPARABLE, never the subject.** The subject is what is
being valued and has no price yet; there is nothing on it to adjust.

- Comparable **superior** to the subject → adjust the comparable **DOWN**.
- Comparable **inferior** to the subject → adjust the comparable **UP**.

Getting the direction wrong moves the answer twice as far as leaving the adjustment out, and it is the
single most common grid error.

### 31.3 Selecting comparables

Recent closed sales, similar in physical characteristics, in the same market area, and arm's-length.
Not listings, which are asking prices. Not pending sales, whose prices are not yet known. Not
distressed sales unless the subject is itself distressed. The fewer and larger the adjustments, the
weaker the comparable.

### 31.4 A worked grid

*Subject: 1,720 sq ft ranch, 3 bed, 2 bath, attached 2-car garage, unfinished basement, 0.31 acre.*

| | Comp A | Comp B | Comp C |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Sale price | $268,000 | $242,500 | $291,000 |
| Sold | 2 months ago | 7 months ago | 1 month ago |
| Market conditions +0.3%/month | +$1,608 | +$5,093 | +$873 |
| Living area | 1,790 (+70) | 1,610 (−110) | 1,880 (+160) |
| Area adj. at $62/sq ft | −$4,340 | +$6,820 | −$9,920 |
| Bathrooms | 2 | 1.5 (−0.5) | 3 (+1) |
| Bath adj. at $6,500 each | $0 | +$3,250 | −$6,500 |
| Garage | attached 2-car | attached 1-car | attached 2-car |
| Garage adj. | $0 | +$8,000 | $0 |
| Basement | unfinished | unfinished | finished |
| Basement adj. | $0 | $0 | −$14,000 |
| **Net adjustment** | **−$2,732** | **+$23,163** | **−$29,547** |
| **Adjusted price** | **$265,268** | **$265,663** | **$261,453** |

**Check every direction.** Comp A is larger — superior — so the area adjustment is negative. Comp B is
inferior on area, baths and garage, so all three are positive. Comp C is superior on area, baths and
basement, so all three are negative.

### 31.5 Reconciling

The three adjusted figures are $265,268, $265,663 and $261,453. **Reconciliation is judgment, not an
average.** Comp A required the smallest adjustments and sold most recently after Comp C; it deserves
the most weight. Comp B required $23,163 of gross adjustment and deserves the least. A defensible
conclusion is a **range of roughly $262,000 to $266,000**, with the weight of the evidence in the
upper half.

Note that the arithmetic average — $264,128 — is inside that range and tells you nothing about which
evidence was strongest. That is why reconciliation is not averaging.

## Hour 32 — The cost approach and the income approach

### 32.1 The cost approach

**Land value + (reproduction or replacement cost of the improvements − accrued depreciation) = value.**

**Reproduction cost** is the cost to build an exact replica. **Replacement cost** is the cost to build
a structure of equal utility using current materials and methods, and it is the more common basis.

**Accrued depreciation** has three sources:

- **Physical deterioration** — wear, age, deferred maintenance. Curable or incurable.
- **Functional obsolescence** — a defect in the design itself. A bedroom reachable only through
  another bedroom; a single bathroom in a four-bedroom house; a 1970s floor plan the market has moved
  past. Curable or incurable.
- **External obsolescence** — caused by something **outside** the property boundaries. A new highway,
  a closed employer, a declining area. **Always incurable**, because the owner cannot fix it.

The cost approach governs **new construction** and **special-purpose property** where comparables are
thin — a church, a school, a fire station. It is weakest on older buildings, because estimating
accrued depreciation on a seventy-year-old house is largely guesswork.

### 32.2 The income approach

For income-producing property.

**Potential gross income** — the income at full occupancy.
**Less vacancy and collection loss** → **effective gross income**.
**Less operating expenses** → **net operating income**.
**Net operating income ÷ capitalisation rate = value.**

*Worked.* An eight-unit building. Potential gross income $96,000. Vacancy and collection loss 6
percent = $5,760. Effective gross income **$90,240**. Operating expenses $38,300. **NOI = $51,940.**
At a capitalisation rate of 7.5 percent: 51,940 ÷ 0.075 = **$692,533**.

**Debt service and depreciation are NOT operating expenses.** They sit below the NOI line. This is why
two identical buildings with different financing have the same NOI and different cash flow, and it is
why NOI is the figure a buyer compares.

**The gross rent multiplier** is a cruder tool: sale price ÷ gross rent. It is quick and it ignores
expenses entirely, so it is a screening device, not a valuation.

### 32.3 How an income pro forma is dressed

Three things to check on any pro forma handed to a buyer, because all three inflate value directly:

**Is there a reserve line?** The roof, the mechanicals and the parking will need replacing. A pro
forma with no replacement reserve understates expenses, overstates NOI, and therefore overstates value
— by the reserve amount divided by the cap rate, which is a large number.

**Is the vacancy assumption real?** A pro forma showing 2 percent vacancy in a market running at 8
percent is a wish.

**Are the rents actual or projected?** "Market rents after renovation" is not income; it is a plan.

## Hour 33 — The appraisal process and the licensee's boundary

### 33.1 The process

1. Define the problem — the property, the interest, the purpose, the date and the definition of value.
2. Determine the scope of work.
3. Collect and analyse data — general market data and specific property data.
4. Analyse highest and best use.
5. Estimate land value.
6. Apply the approaches that are applicable.
7. **Reconcile** into a final opinion.
8. Report.

### 33.2 The boundary, stated plainly

**An appraisal is an opinion of value produced by a licensed or certified appraiser under that
person's own licence and under their professional standards.** A salesperson is not an appraiser and
does not perform appraisals.

**A licensee's market analysis** is the licensee's presentation of market evidence supporting a
pricing decision. It uses the same comparison logic. It is not an appraisal, it must not be labelled
or described as one, and it must not be presented in a way that lets a client believe it is one.

The professional method:

- **Present it in writing**, with the comparables and the adjustments shown.
- **Present a range**, not a single figure.
- **Say plainly that the price is the seller's decision.**
- **Label it for what it is.**

### 33.3 Why this boundary is a Missouri problem and not just an etiquette problem

Two provisions. Section 339.730.1(2), RSMo, requires reasonable skill and care — and confirming a
valuation the licensee did not derive is not care. Section 339.730.1(3)(d) requires the licensee to
**advise the client to obtain expert advice** as to material matters beyond the licensee's expertise —
and a valuation question the licensee cannot support is exactly that.

And the school-side rule reinforces it: 20 CSR 2250-7.010(1)(L)3 forbids a school to permit anyone
associated with it **to provide any misleading information to the public**.

### 33.4 The three things a seller will show you, and what to say about each

**An automated online valuation.** It is a model's output from public and listing data. It has not
been inside the house and cannot see condition. It is evidence to weigh, not a valuation. Do not adopt
it and do not dismiss it — put your own analysis beside it.

**An old appraisal.** A 2019 appraisal is a 2019 opinion, produced for a purpose that may not be this
one, on a property that may have changed.

**A neighbour's number.** Ask what it is based on. Usually it is a list price, which is an asking
price and not evidence of anything.

## Hour 34 — Leaseholds and property management

### 34.1 The four leasehold estates

**Estate for years.** A definite beginning and a definite end. **It terminates on its own terms and
requires no notice.**

**Periodic tenancy, or estate from period to period.** Renews automatically — month to month, year to
year — until proper notice is given by either party.

**Tenancy at will.** Possession with the landlord's consent for an indefinite period, terminable by
either party.

**Tenancy at sufferance.** A holdover — the tenant remains after the right to possess has ended. It is
not a tenancy the landlord agreed to.

**This course states no Missouri notice period for any of these**, because Missouri's landlord-tenant
chapter has not been read from primary text for this project. Which estate exists is what a licensee
must be able to identify; what notice ends it is routed to counsel and to the lease.

### 34.2 A lease is two things at once

A **conveyance** of a possessory interest for a term, and a **contract** of covenants between the
parties. That is why a lease dispute can be framed either as a breach of contract or as an
interference with possession, and why the **covenant of quiet enjoyment** matters as much as any
written clause.

**Assignment** transfers the tenant's entire remaining interest. **Sublease** transfers less than all
of it, leaving the original tenant as a landlord to the subtenant. In both cases the original tenant
generally **remains liable** unless expressly released — which tenants routinely assume they are not.

Lease types by rent structure: **gross**, where the landlord pays operating expenses; **net**, in its
several degrees, where the tenant pays some or all of them; **percentage**, common in retail, where
rent includes a share of sales; and **ground**, a long-term lease of land on which the tenant builds.

### 34.3 Property management

The manager is the **owner's agent**, and every duty in Module MO-03 attaches — while every fair
housing obligation runs to applicants and tenants at the same time.

**Functions:** marketing and leasing; tenant screening applied identically to every applicant;
collections; maintenance, both routine and capital; budgeting and reporting; regulatory compliance;
and risk management.

**The management agreement** must settle the scope of authority, the compensation, the term, the
reporting and accounting obligations, the owner's approval thresholds for expenditure, and the
handling of trust funds. It is a written agreement, and an unwritten scope of authority is how a
manager ends up having spent an owner's money on something the owner did not authorise.

**Trust accounting is where property managers lose licences**, and almost never for theft — for
sloppiness that looks identical on an audit. Security deposits and prepaid rent are **other people's
money**. They never touch an operating account, whatever the owner suggests and whatever spreadsheet
sits behind the arrangement. **This course states no Missouri trust account requirement**, because
§ 339.105 and the relevant 20 CSR 2250 chapters have not been read from primary text for this project;
the rule is read directly before any manager touches a deposit.

*Worked.* Effective gross income $184,000, operating expenses $71,000. **NOI = $113,000.** Debt
service of $76,400 is not subtracted here — it is below the line, and $113,000 − $76,400 = $36,600 is
**before-tax cash flow**, a different figure answering a different question.
