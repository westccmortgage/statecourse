# Missouri Salesperson Pre-Examination Course — Module MO-02

## Hours 6–11 · Interests in real estate, forms of ownership, and legal descriptions

**Clock hours:** 6. A Missouri clock hour is **60 minutes containing at least 50 minutes of actual
instruction** and no more than 10 minutes of recess — 20 CSR 2250-7.070(2).
**Content basis:** a StateCourse organisation for Commission review. **20 CSR 2250-6.060(1) requires
the 48-hour course to be developed from the testing service's detailed content outline, which this
project has not read.**
**Course version:** 1.0 submission draft
**Regulatory status:** Development. Not approved. No Missouri pre-license credit is available.

## Learning objectives

1. Classify an estate in land by duration and by possession, and identify a defeasible fee from its
   granting language.
2. Distinguish a life estate from a fee, name the future interests that follow it, and explain waste.
3. Distinguish easement appurtenant, easement in gross, licence and encroachment, and say how each
   arises and how each ends.
4. Compare the forms of concurrent ownership and state what happens at a death under each.
5. Explain what a condominium owner owns and how it differs from a cooperative and a time-share.
6. Read a rectangular-survey description, compute the acreage it describes, and recognise a
   metes-and-bounds description and a lot-and-block description.
7. State why a licensee never supplies a legal description from memory, a tax record or a prior
   listing.

## Hour 6 — Freehold estates

### 6.1 The classification

An **estate** is the degree, quantity, nature and extent of a person's interest in land. Estates
divide first into **freehold** — of indefinite duration, involving ownership — and **leasehold** — of
determinable duration, involving possession without ownership. Leaseholds are Module MO-06's
business; freeholds are this hour's.

**Fee simple absolute** is the greatest interest known to the law: perpetual duration, freely
transferable during life and at death, subject only to the four government powers and to whatever
private encumbrances burden it. It is what most Missouri residential transactions convey, and it is
what a buyer assumes they are getting unless told otherwise.

### 6.2 Defeasible fees

A **defeasible fee** is a fee that may end on the happening of a stated event. Two forms, and the
difference is in what happens when the event occurs:

**Fee simple determinable.** Created by durational language — *so long as*, *while*, *during*,
*until*. The estate **ends automatically** when the condition fails, and the grantor holds a
**possibility of reverter**. "To the School District so long as the land is used for a school."

**Fee simple subject to a condition subsequent.** Created by conditional language — *provided that*,
*on condition that*, *but if*. The estate **does not end automatically**; the grantor holds a **right
of re-entry** and must act to reclaim. "To the School District, provided that if the land ceases to
be used for a school the grantor may re-enter."

The distinction is drafted by counsel and litigated by counsel. What a licensee must do is
**recognise the language in a title commitment and route it**. A defeasible fee is a genuine
limitation on what a buyer is acquiring and it will not announce itself in the listing.

### 6.3 What the examination tests, and what practice tests

The examination will give you granting language and ask which estate it creates. Practice asks
something narrower and more useful: **did you read the exceptions in the title commitment, and did
you ask about anything you did not understand?** Most licensees will never meet a defeasible fee.
The ones who do will meet it in Schedule B.

## Hour 7 — Life estates, future interests, and waste

### 7.1 The life estate

A **life estate** is a freehold estate measured by a lifetime. Its holder — the **life tenant** — has
the right to possess, use and take the profits of the property for the duration of the measuring life,
and no right at all after it.

Measured by whose life? Usually the life tenant's. Where it is measured by the life of some **other**
person it is an estate **pur autre vie** — "for the life of another." An estate pur autre vie can
outlive its holder, in which case it passes to the holder's estate until the measuring life ends.

### 7.2 What follows

When the measuring life ends the estate must go somewhere, and there are exactly two possibilities:

- back to the **grantor** or the grantor's successors, which is a **reversion**; or
- to a named **third party**, which is a **remainder**, and that party is the **remainderman**.

Both are **future interests** — presently existing property rights whose enjoyment is postponed. A
remainderman owns something today, which is why a life tenant cannot sell the fee.

### 7.3 Waste

A life tenant must not commit **waste** — must not damage or diminish the property to the injury of
the remainderman. The life tenant is generally responsible for ordinary maintenance, taxes and
interest on encumbrances; permanent structural changes and destruction are waste.

**The practical consequence for a licensee is severe and immediate.** *A life tenant cannot convey a
fee simple.* If the seller holds a life estate, a sale of the fee requires the remainderman to join
in the conveyance. A licensee who takes a listing without establishing who must sign has built a
transaction on a defect that will surface at the closing table, and § 339.730.1(2) — reasonable skill
and care — is what makes establishing it part of the job. The determination is the title company's
and counsel's; the noticing is the licensee's.

### 7.4 The bundle, revisited

Note what the life estate does to Hour 1's bundle. The life tenant holds possession, use and
exclusion. The remainderman holds a form of disposition. Neither holds the whole thing, and neither
can convey what the other has. This is the clearest illustration in the course of why the bundle
metaphor is not decoration.

## Hour 8 — Encumbrances I: easements, licences, encroachments

### 8.1 What an encumbrance is

An **encumbrance** is any claim, right or interest held by someone other than the owner that
diminishes the value or restricts the use of the property, without preventing the owner from
transferring it. Encumbrances divide into those that **affect use** — easements, deed restrictions,
encroachments — and those that **affect title as security for a debt** — liens, which are Hour 9.

### 8.2 Easements

An **easement** is a right to use another's land for a particular purpose. Two kinds, and the
difference decides whether the right survives a sale:

**Easement appurtenant.** Benefits a **parcel** rather than a person. It has a **dominant tenement**
— the parcel benefited — and a **servient tenement** — the parcel burdened. It **runs with the land**,
passing automatically to each new owner of the dominant tenement. A driveway easement across a
neighbour's lot is the standard example. This is the kind that matters most in residential practice
because it transfers whether or not anyone mentions it.

**Easement in gross.** Benefits a **person or entity** rather than a parcel. There is a servient
tenement but no dominant one. Utility easements are the everyday case, and commercial easements in
gross are generally assignable.

**How easements are created:** by express grant or reservation in a deed; by implication, where prior
use and necessity are evident at severance; by **necessity**, most commonly for a landlocked parcel;
and by **prescription**, through long open use in the manner of adverse possession.

**How they end:** by merger — the two tenements coming into one ownership; by release from the
dominant owner; by abandonment shown by conduct rather than mere non-use; by expiration of a stated
term; or by the end of the necessity that created an easement by necessity.

**This course states no Missouri prescriptive period**, because the relevant Missouri statute has not
been read from primary text for this project. What a licensee carries is the recognition: a
long-standing use of someone else's land — a drive, a path, a mowed strip, a dock — is routed to
counsel and a surveyor, not resolved on the tailgate.

### 8.3 Licence

A **licence** is mere permission to enter land for a particular purpose. It is personal, **revocable
at will**, and does not run with the land. Permission to fish in a pond is a licence; a recorded
right of access is an easement. The two look identical on the ground and are entirely different in
law, which is why the question is answered from the records rather than from what the neighbours say.

### 8.4 Encroachment

An **encroachment** is an unauthorised physical intrusion of an improvement onto adjoining land — a
fence, a shed, an eave, a driveway. It is not a permission of any kind; it is a trespass that has been
built.

Encroachments are discovered by **survey**, and this is the reason a survey matters. A title
commitment will typically **except** survey matters and encroachments unless a survey is furnished
and the exception removed. A buyer who declines a survey has accepted that risk, and should be told
so in those words. Left long enough, an encroachment can mature into a prescriptive easement or an
adverse possession claim, which converts a fixable problem into a legal one.

## Hour 9 — Encumbrances II: liens, restrictions, and priority

### 9.1 Liens

A **lien** is a charge against property that secures a debt or obligation. It affects title; it does
not by itself dispossess the owner. Every lien is classified twice:

- **Voluntary** — the owner created it, as with a mortgage — or **involuntary** — the law or a court
  imposed it, as with a tax lien or a judgment.
- **General** — attaching to the debtor's property generally, as a judgment lien does — or
  **specific** — attaching to one identified parcel, as a mortgage, mechanic's lien or property tax
  lien does.

A judgment lien is the one that surprises people, because it can surface against a parcel nobody
associated with the debt.

### 9.2 Priority

The general rule is **first in time, first in right**, measured by recording. The standard exception
is the **real estate tax lien**, which customarily takes priority over earlier recorded liens
regardless of order. Priority may also be rearranged by contract through a **subordination
agreement**, which is why the **title commitment** rather than a list of recording dates is the
operative document in any real transaction.

### 9.3 Mechanic's and materialman's liens

A lien claimed by someone who furnished labour or materials for improvement of the property. Its
danger is structural: in many states the lien's priority may **relate back to a date earlier than the
filing** — the commencement of work or the first furnishing of materials — so a claim not yet in the
records can outrank an interest that is.

**This course states no Missouri filing period, notice requirement or priority date**, because
Missouri's mechanic's lien chapter has not been read from primary text for this project. What a
licensee carries is the **warning sign and the routing**: recent or ongoing construction, a
renovation finished in the past several months, an unpaid or disputed contractor. Raise it at
listing, route it to the title company and counsel, and do not let it be discovered a week before
closing.

### 9.4 Deed restrictions and covenants

Private limits recorded against the land, most commonly as a declaration of covenants, conditions and
restrictions governing a subdivision. They **run with the land** and are enforceable by the
association or by benefited owners.

They are **entirely separate from zoning**, and the **more restrictive controls**. A city may permit
a use the covenants forbid, and the covenants win. A buyer who intends to run a business, park a
recreational vehicle, keep animals, build an outbuilding or install a fence needs the covenants read
**before** the offer is written.

Note also that a restriction that discriminates on a protected basis is unenforceable and void
regardless of what the record says — a subject Module MO-08 takes up in full.

## Hour 10 — Concurrent ownership and common-interest ownership

### 10.1 Forms of concurrent ownership

**Tenancy in common.** Two or more owners hold **undivided** interests, which may be **unequal**.
Each interest is freely transferable during life and **passes by will or intestacy at death**. This is
the default in most circumstances where a deed does not say otherwise.

**Joint tenancy.** Equal undivided interests with the **right of survivorship** — the great
distinction. On a joint tenant's death their interest passes to the surviving joint tenants and does
not go through the estate. It traditionally requires four unities: time, title, interest and
possession.

**Tenancy by the entirety.** A form available to married couples in Missouri, carrying survivorship
and creditor characteristics that differ from joint tenancy.

**How a licensee handles this.** Which form a deed creates, and which form a particular buyer should
take, is a **legal and tax question for counsel and the buyer's tax adviser**. A licensee who advises
a buying couple on how to take title has practised law. The licensee's contribution is to raise the
question early enough that it is decided deliberately rather than by whatever the title company's
default happened to be.

**Missouri marital property.** Missouri's marital-property regime and the rules governing which
spouse must join in a conveyance were **not read from primary text for this project, and this course
states no rule from them.** The practice consequence is the one that matters: **do not assume from
the name on the deed who must sign.** Route it to counsel and the closing agent, and raise it at
listing rather than at the closing table.

### 10.2 Partition

Any co-owner may generally seek **partition** — a court division of the property in kind, or a sale
and division of proceeds. It is the remedy when co-owners cannot agree, and it is a lawsuit.

### 10.3 Condominium, cooperative, time-share

**Condominium.** The owner holds the **unit in fee**, plus an **undivided interest in the common
elements**. Governance is by a declaration, bylaws and an owners' association, funded by assessments.
Each unit is separately assessed, separately taxed, separately financed and separately transferred.

**Cooperative.** The owner holds **shares in a corporation** and a **proprietary lease** of a unit.
The corporation owns the real estate; the resident owns personal property. This changes financing —
share loans, not mortgages — changes transfer, because the board usually must approve a purchaser,
and changes the risk, because the corporation's blanket mortgage and the other shareholders'
delinquencies affect everyone.

**Time-share.** An interval interest, in one of several legal forms, carrying its own consumer
protection rules and a resale market that bears no resemblance to the sale market.

**The questions a licensee must ask about any common-interest property**, and get answered in
writing before the buyer's contingencies expire: what is the assessment and what does it cover; what
reserves does the association hold; is there a special assessment pending or a deferred maintenance
problem that implies one; is there litigation; what do the rules restrict — pets, rentals, vehicles,
occupancy; and will the buyer's intended lender lend on this project at all, which for condominiums
is a live question and not a formality.

## Hour 11 — Legal descriptions and land measurement

### 11.1 Three methods, and one non-method

**Metes and bounds.** Describes a boundary by courses and distances from a **point of beginning**,
running around the perimeter through calls that reference bearings, distances and monuments, and
**closing back to the point of beginning**. A description that does not close does not describe a
parcel. This is the method for irregular parcels and for older land.

**Rectangular survey, or government survey.** A grid built from **principal meridians** running north
and south and **base lines** running east and west. **Townships** are laid out in horizontal tiers and
vertical **ranges**; each township is six miles square and is divided into **36 sections**, each one
mile square and containing **640 acres**. Sections are numbered 1 through 36, beginning in the
north-east corner and running **boustrophedonically** — right to left, then left to right, back and
forth — so that section 36 lands in the south-east corner.

**Lot and block, or recorded plat.** References a lot and block on a subdivision plat recorded in the
county records. This is the everyday description for platted urban and suburban land, and it is the
one most Missouri residential licensees see.

**And the non-method: a street address is never a legal description.** Neither is a tax parcel
identification number. Both are conveniences for finding a property, not descriptions of it.

### 11.2 Reading a rectangular description

Read it **backwards**, dividing as you go, because the description narrows from the largest unit to
the smallest.

*The NE 1/4 of the SW 1/4 of Section 14.*

Start with the section: **640 acres**. The SW 1/4 of it: 640 ÷ 4 = **160 acres**. The NE 1/4 of that:
160 ÷ 4 = **40 acres**.

*The S 1/2 of the NW 1/4 of the SE 1/4.* 640 ÷ 4 = 160; ÷ 4 = 40; ÷ 2 = **20 acres**.

Each division by a quarter divides by four. Each division by a half divides by two. Work from the
right-hand end of the description toward the left and the arithmetic is trivial; work from the left
and it is confusing.

### 11.3 The measurements worth memorising

| Unit | Equivalent |
|---|---|
| 1 acre | **43,560 square feet** |
| 1 section | 1 square mile, **640 acres** |
| 1 township | 6 miles square, **36 sections**, 23,040 acres |
| 1 mile | 5,280 feet |
| 1 square mile | 640 acres |

**A worked problem.** A rectangular lot is 150 feet by 290.4 feet. Its area is 150 × 290.4 = 43,560
square feet — **exactly one acre**. Recognising 43,560 on sight saves time on the examination and in
practice.

**Another.** A parcel described as the W 1/2 of the NE 1/4 of Section 8 is 640 ÷ 4 ÷ 2 = **80 acres**.
At $6,200 an acre it is worth 80 × 6,200 = **$496,000**.

### 11.4 The licensee's rule about descriptions

**Never supply a legal description from memory, from a tax record, from a prior listing or from the
seller's recollection.** The legal description comes from the **deed** or from the **title company**,
and it is copied exactly, character for character.

The same discipline covers acreage. The assessment roll is an assessment record, not a survey.
Repeating its figure as fact in a listing is a representation about a measurement nobody verified,
and where acreage matters to the buyer's decision the source of the figure matters too. Route
acreage, boundaries and anything touching a water line to a **surveyor**.
