# Missouri Salesperson Pre-Examination Course — Module MO-05

## Hours 24–29 · Real estate finance and real estate mathematics

**Clock hours:** 6. A Missouri clock hour is **60 minutes containing at least 50 minutes of actual
instruction** and no more than 10 minutes of recess — 20 CSR 2250-7.070(2).
**Content basis:** a StateCourse organisation for Commission review. **20 CSR 2250-6.060(1) requires
the 48-hour course to be developed from the testing service's detailed content outline, which this
project has not read.**
**Course version:** 1.0 submission draft
**Regulatory status:** Development. Not approved. No Missouri pre-license credit is available.

## Learning objectives

1. Describe what underwriting examines and compute a housing ratio and a total debt ratio.
2. Distinguish the note from the security instrument and name the clauses that decide outcomes.
3. Describe the categories of loan programme without recommending one, and route pricing to the lender.
4. Explain the secondary market and what a borrower actually feels from it.
5. Compute loan-to-value, down payment, points, simple interest, commission, area and proration.
6. Read an amortisation schedule and explain why early payments are mostly interest.
7. Compute a buyer's cash to close and a seller's net, and state what makes each an estimate.
8. State the boundary between explaining a structure and quoting a payment.

## Hour 24 — Buyer qualification

### 24.1 What underwriting examines

Four things, traditionally the four Cs:

**Capacity** — can the borrower repay from income? Measured by the ratios below.
**Credit** — has the borrower repaid before? Measured by score and by the record behind it.
**Capital** — what does the borrower have for down payment, closing costs and reserves, and where did
it come from? Sourcing and seasoning of funds are real underwriting steps and they surprise buyers who
were gifted money at the last minute.
**Collateral** — is the property worth enough and is it acceptable security? This is where the
appraisal enters.

### 24.2 The ratios

**Housing ratio** — the proposed monthly housing payment divided by gross monthly income. The housing
payment is not the loan payment: it is **PITI** — principal, interest, taxes and insurance — plus
mortgage insurance and any association dues.

**Total debt ratio** — housing payment plus all other recurring monthly obligations, divided by gross
monthly income. Car payments, student loans, minimum credit card payments, court-ordered support.

*Worked.* Gross monthly income $6,400. Proposed PITI $1,536. Car payment $410, student loan $220,
credit card minimums $95.

Housing ratio: 1,536 ÷ 6,400 = **24%**.
Total obligations: 1,536 + 410 + 220 + 95 = 2,261. Total debt ratio: 2,261 ÷ 6,400 = **35.3%**.

**The thresholds are the lender's and the loan programme's, not the licensee's.** They differ by
programme, they move, and they are subject to compensating factors and to automated underwriting
decisions no licensee sees. A licensee who states a qualifying ratio as though it were a rule has
stated a lender's underwriting standard as law.

### 24.3 Pre-qualification, pre-approval, and commitment

**Pre-qualification** is an estimate from information the borrower stated. **Pre-approval** follows
verification and underwriting. **Neither is a loan commitment.**

That gap is the reason the financing contingency exists, and a buyer who believes a pre-approval is a
promise will not understand why the contingency matters or why anyone is anxious in week three. Say it
plainly at the beginning: *this is what the lender expects today, based on what has been verified; the
commitment comes later, and the contingency protects you until it does.*

### 24.4 What a licensee says about financing, and what they must not

**Do:** explain what the categories are and how they differ structurally; explain what the ratios
measure; explain the difference between pre-qualification, pre-approval and commitment; insist the
buyer speak to a lender early; explain what a rate lock is and that it expires.

**Do not:** recommend a programme as cheapest; compute a payment and give it to the buyer as their
payment; quote a rate; predict what rates will do; or characterise what a lender will approve.

Two Missouri provisions bear directly. Section 339.740.1(3)(d), RSMo, requires the buyer's agent to
**advise the client to obtain expert advice** on material matters beyond the licensee's expertise —
loan pricing is squarely that. And § 339.740.1(2) — reasonable skill and care — is what makes getting
the buyer to a lender **early** part of the job rather than a nicety.

## Hour 25 — The note, the security instrument, and the clauses

### 25.1 Two documents, two jobs

The **promissory note** is the borrower's promise to repay. It is the debt. It states the amount, the
rate, the term, the payment and the consequences of default.

The **security instrument** pledges the property as security for that promise. It creates the lien.
Without it the lender has a promise and no property; without the note the lender has a lien securing
nothing.

Two forms are used across the United States. A **mortgage** is a two-party instrument — mortgagor
(borrower) and mortgagee (lender) — typically enforced by **judicial** foreclosure. A **deed of
trust** is a three-party instrument — trustor (borrower), beneficiary (lender), and a **trustee** who
holds power of sale — typically enforced **non-judicially**, without a lawsuit.

**Missouri practice uses the deed of trust**, and Missouri foreclosure is predominantly a **trustee's
sale under the power of sale** rather than a court proceeding. **This course states no Missouri
foreclosure timetable, notice requirement or redemption right**, because Missouri's foreclosure
chapter has not been read from primary text for this project. The structural fact — a trustee holding
a power of sale, exercised without a lawsuit — is what a licensee carries, and every question about
timing, notice, deficiency or redemption is routed to counsel.

### 25.2 The clauses that decide outcomes

**Acceleration.** On default, the lender may declare the entire balance immediately due. Without it,
default would mean suing for each missed payment.

**Alienation, or due-on-sale.** On transfer of the property, the lender may call the balance due. This
is why "taking subject to" an existing loan is not a workaround a licensee should be arranging, and
why **assumption** requires the lender's consent.

**Prepayment.** Whether the borrower may repay early and on what terms.

**Escrow, or impound.** The lender collects taxes and insurance monthly and pays them when due. This
is why the housing payment is PITI rather than principal and interest, and why an escrow analysis can
change a payment after closing without the rate having moved — which buyers experience as a surprise
and should be warned about.

**Subordination.** An agreement that a lien will take a lower priority than it otherwise would.

**Release, or partial release.** Releasing part of the security as principal is repaid, used in
subdivision and land development lending.

### 25.3 Assumption, subject to, release

**Assumption** — the buyer takes over the existing loan. With the lender's consent and a written
release, the seller is off the obligation. **Without** a release, the seller remains liable even
though they no longer own the property.

**Taking subject to** — the buyer makes the payments but does not assume the obligation. The seller
remains fully liable, and the alienation clause is generally triggered by the transfer. This is a
structure a licensee explains and routes to counsel; it is not one a licensee arranges.

### 25.4 Alternatives to foreclosure

**Reinstatement** — curing the default and restoring the loan. **Forbearance or modification** —
agreements with the lender to change the terms. **Deed in lieu of foreclosure** — conveying the
property to the lender voluntarily; it does not automatically eliminate junior liens. **Short sale** —
the lender agrees to accept less than the balance, which is a lender approval process with a timetable
of its own and a set of tax and credit consequences the seller must take to their accountant and
counsel.

A licensee working a distressed file identifies the questions and routes them. The seller's tax
position, deficiency exposure and credit consequences are all outside the licence.

## Hour 26 — Programme categories, and what a licensee may say

### 26.1 The categories

**Conventional** — not insured or guaranteed by a government agency. Where the down payment is below
a threshold the lender requires **private mortgage insurance**, which protects the lender rather than
the borrower and which can generally be removed once equity conditions are met.

**FHA-insured** — insured by the Federal Housing Administration, with lower down payment requirements
and mortgage insurance premiums that follow their own rules, and property condition standards the
appraisal applies.

**VA-guaranteed** — guaranteed by the Department of Veterans Affairs for eligible veterans and service
members, with no down payment in the ordinary case and a funding fee in place of monthly mortgage
insurance.

**USDA rural development** — for eligible properties in eligible areas and eligible borrowers.

**State and local programmes** — down payment assistance, first-time buyer programmes, bond
programmes. Missouri has such programmes; **this course states no eligibility criterion, income limit
or purchase price limit for any of them**, because none was read from primary source for this project.
They are routed to the administering agency and to the lender.

**Seller financing** — including the land contract structure discussed in Module MO-04.

### 26.2 Fixed, adjustable, and the shapes in between

**Fixed rate** — the rate does not change for the life of the loan.
**Adjustable rate** — the rate adjusts on a schedule, tied to an index plus a margin, with caps on the
periodic adjustment and on the lifetime rate. A buyer considering one needs to understand the **worst
case**, not the initial rate, and the worst case is computable from the caps.
**Interest-only, balloon, and graduated payment** structures exist and each carries a risk profile a
buyer must understand before signing.

### 26.3 The rule that governs this hour

A licensee **explains structures** and **routes pricing.** The comparison of two programmes for a
particular borrower depends on the rate, the term, the mortgage insurance treatment, the fees, the
tax position and how long the borrower will hold the property — and the licensee controls none of
those and knows some of them not at all. Naming the categories is education. Recommending one is
advice the licensee is not licensed to give.

## Hour 27 — The secondary market and servicing

### 27.1 Primary and secondary

The **primary market** is where loans are made — the lender and the borrower. The **secondary market**
is where loans are bought and sold after origination.

It exists because it returns capital to originators, who can then lend again. Without it, a lender's
capacity would be limited to its deposits and its own balance sheet, and thirty-year fixed-rate
lending at scale would not exist.

### 27.2 What it causes

**Standardisation.** Because originators write loans intending to sell them, they write to the
purchaser's guidelines. This is why underwriting looks broadly similar across lenders, why the
documentation requirements feel identical, and why an unusual property or an unusual borrower is
harder to finance than the numbers alone suggest.

**Servicing transfer.** The right to collect payments is itself an asset and is bought and sold. A
borrower may make their first payment to one company and their second to another they never chose.
Tell buyers this will probably happen and that it does not change their loan terms, because otherwise
the notice arrives looking like a scam.

**Rate transmission.** Mortgage rates track the yields investors demand on mortgage-backed securities
more closely than they track any single policy rate — which is why mortgage rates sometimes move
opposite to the direction a news story implies. That is also why a licensee does not forecast them.

## Hour 28 — Mathematics I

### 28.1 The one method that solves most problems

Most real estate arithmetic is a single relationship:

> **Part = Whole × Rate**

Rearranged: Rate = Part ÷ Whole. Whole = Part ÷ Rate. Identify which two of the three you have, and
the problem is one operation. Nearly every commission, interest, appreciation, ratio and profit
problem on any examination is this relationship wearing a costume.

### 28.2 Loan-to-value and down payment

**LTV = loan amount ÷ the LESSER of price or appraised value.**

*Worked.* Price $284,000, appraised $284,000, 10 percent down.
Down payment: 284,000 × 0.10 = **$28,400**. Loan: **$255,600**. LTV: 255,600 ÷ 284,000 = **90%**.

*Worked, with a low appraisal.* Same price, appraisal comes in at $272,000. The lender lends 90
percent **of the lesser figure**: 272,000 × 0.90 = $244,800. The buyer now needs 284,000 − 244,800 =
**$39,200** in cash, not $28,400. The $12,000 appraisal gap became a $10,800 cash problem, and this is
exactly what an appraisal contingency exists to handle.

### 28.3 Points and interest

**A point is one percent of the LOAN AMOUNT**, not of the price. Get that wrong and every points
problem is wrong.

*Worked.* 1.5 points on a $255,600 loan: 255,600 × 0.015 = **$3,834**.

**Simple annual interest = principal × rate.** Monthly interest = that ÷ 12.

*Worked.* $255,600 at 6.25%: annual 255,600 × 0.0625 = $15,975. Monthly: 15,975 ÷ 12 = **$1,331.25**.

### 28.4 Commission

*Worked.* Sale price $284,000, total commission 6 percent, split evenly between two firms, and the
listing salesperson's agreement with their designated broker is 60/40 in the salesperson's favour.

Total: 284,000 × 0.06 = **$17,040**.
Listing firm: 17,040 ÷ 2 = **$8,520**.
Listing salesperson: 8,520 × 0.60 = **$5,112**. Firm retains $3,408.

**Commission rates are negotiable and are never set by any board, association or rule.** And note
where the money goes: from the closing to the **designated broker**, and from the broker to the
salesperson. A salesperson is not paid by a seller.

### 28.5 Area

Rectangle: length × width. Triangle: ½ × base × height. Irregular shapes: divide into rectangles and
triangles, compute each, add.

**1 acre = 43,560 square feet. 1 section = 640 acres. 1 mile = 5,280 feet.**

*Worked.* A lot 88 feet wide and 165 feet deep. 88 × 165 = 14,520 sq ft. As a fraction of an acre:
14,520 ÷ 43,560 = **1/3 acre**.

*Worked.* A house measures 46 ft by 28 ft with an attached 24 ft by 22 ft addition. 46 × 28 = 1,288;
24 × 22 = 528. Total **1,816 sq ft**. At a construction cost of $148 per square foot: 1,816 × 148 =
**$268,768**.

## Hour 29 — Mathematics II

### 29.1 Amortisation, and why early payments are mostly interest

An amortising payment is constant; its **composition** changes every month. Each month, interest is
computed on the **current** balance; whatever is left of the payment reduces principal; the balance
falls; so next month's interest is slightly smaller and next month's principal portion slightly
larger.

*Worked, first three months.* $255,600 at 6.25%, monthly payment $1,573.71.

| Month | Interest | Principal | Ending balance |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 255,600 × .0625 ÷ 12 = **1,331.25** | 242.46 | 255,357.54 |
| 2 | 255,357.54 × .0625 ÷ 12 = **1,329.99** | 243.72 | 255,113.82 |
| 3 | 255,113.82 × .0625 ÷ 12 = **1,328.72** | 244.99 | 254,868.83 |

Three payments of $1,573.71 — $4,721.13 — reduced the balance by **$731.17**. That is not a defect;
it is what amortisation is. A buyer who understands it will not be shocked by their first-year
statement, and a licensee who explains it has done something genuinely useful.

### 29.2 Proration

Proration divides a shared expense between seller and buyer at closing. Three questions must be
answered before any arithmetic:

1. **What is the annual amount?**
2. **What basis** — a 365-day year, or a 360-day banker's year with 30-day months?
3. **Who gets the day of closing**, and is the item **paid in advance** or **in arrears**?

**The convention comes from the contract, not from habit.** Getting the basis or the closing-day
convention wrong produces a number that is close enough to look right and is wrong.

*Worked, taxes paid in arrears, 365-day basis, closing day to the buyer.* Annual tax $3,284.00.
Closing 19 September.

Daily: 3,284.00 ÷ 365 = **$8.9973**.
Seller's days, 1 January through 18 September: 31+28+31+30+31+30+31+31+18 = **261 days**.
261 × 8.9973 = **$2,348.29**, a **credit to the buyer and a debit to the seller** — because the buyer
will pay the whole bill when it comes due.

*Worked, an item paid in advance.* Association dues of $360 paid annually on 1 January, closing 19
September, 365-day basis. The **seller** has already paid through 31 December. Days remaining to the
buyer, 19 September through 31 December: 12+31+30+31 = 104. 360 ÷ 365 = 0.9863 daily. 104 × 0.9863 =
**$102.56**, a **credit to the seller and a debit to the buyer.**

The direction is the part people get wrong. Ask: **who has had the use, and who has paid?** Whoever
paid for time they will not have gets a credit.

### 29.3 Cash to close

Purchase price, **plus** closing costs and prepaid items, **minus** the loan amount, **minus** the
earnest money already deposited, **plus or minus** the net of the prorations.

*Worked.* Price $284,000. Loan $255,600. Earnest money $3,000. Buyer's closing costs and prepaids
$6,850. Tax proration credit to buyer $2,348.29.

284,000 + 6,850 = 290,850.
290,850 − 255,600 − 3,000 − 2,348.29 = **$29,901.71**.

### 29.4 Seller's net

Sale price, **minus** the payoff of existing loans, **minus** commission, **minus** the seller's
closing costs, **minus** any credits to the buyer, **plus** any credits to the seller.

*Worked.* Price $284,000. Loan payoff $196,400. Commission 6 percent = $17,040. Seller's closing costs
$2,240. Tax proration debit to seller $2,348.29.

284,000 − 196,400 − 17,040 − 2,240 − 2,348.29 = **$65,971.71**.

### 29.5 What makes every one of these an estimate

Payoff figures include per-diem interest and are quoted to a date. Closing costs are the closing
agent's and the lender's figures, not the licensee's. Prorations depend on a tax bill that may not yet
have issued. Any of these can move between the estimate and the settlement statement.

**So label it.** A net sheet is an estimate, it says so on its face, it shows its assumptions, and the
figures come from the closing agent, the lender and the payoff statement. A licensee who hands a
seller a confident net figure that turns out to be $3,000 optimistic has created a problem that no
apology fixes.
