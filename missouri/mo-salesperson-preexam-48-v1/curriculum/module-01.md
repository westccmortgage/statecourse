# Missouri Salesperson Pre-Examination Course — Module MO-01

## Hours 1–5 · Real property, ownership, and the real estate business

**Clock hours:** 5. A Missouri clock hour is **60 minutes containing at least 50 minutes of actual
instruction** and no more than 10 minutes of recess — 20 CSR 2250-7.070(2).
**Content basis:** a StateCourse organisation for Commission review. **20 CSR 2250-6.060(1) requires
the 48-hour Salesperson Pre-Examination Course to be developed from the detailed content outline
supplied by the professional testing service contracted to administer the examination. This project
has not read that outline.** The module structure and hour allocation below are therefore
StateCourse's, and the course must be reconciled against the outline before submission.
**Course version:** 1.0 submission draft
**Regulatory status:** Development. Not approved. No Missouri pre-license credit is available.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module a student can:

1. Distinguish land, real estate and real property, and explain why the distinction decides what a
   licensee is actually marketing.
2. Apply the fixture test to a disputed item and state what a licensee does about it at listing.
3. Name the physical and economic characteristics of land and use each to explain a market behaviour.
4. Describe the four government powers over private property and identify which one is operating in
   a given fact pattern.
5. Describe the structure of the real estate business in Missouri — the licence categories, the
   designated broker, the affiliated licensee — and state where a salesperson's authority comes from.
6. Explain why supply responds slowly to demand in real estate and what that does to prices.
7. Identify the six-month education window in § 339.040.6, RSMo, and explain why it matters to the
   student personally.

## Hour 1 — Land, real estate, and real property

### 1.1 Three words that are not synonyms

Ordinary speech treats *land*, *real estate* and *real property* as one thing. The law does not, and
a licensee who cannot separate them will make errors in listing, in advertising and in contract.

**Land** is the surface of the earth, the space above it, and the subsurface beneath it — extending
in principle downward toward the centre of the earth and upward into the air, subject to everything
the law has since done to those extremes. Air rights are real and severable; a developer may buy the
right to build above a rail corridor without buying the corridor. Subsurface rights are likewise
severable, and mineral, oil and gas rights may belong to someone who has never seen the surface.

**Real estate** is land plus everything permanently attached to it, whether by nature or by human
effort — trees, water, buildings, fences, in-ground pools, the wiring inside the walls.

**Real property** is real estate plus **the bundle of legal rights** that attach to it. This is the
term that matters most, because it is the only one that describes what is actually being sold. When
a buyer purchases a house they are not purchasing bricks. They are purchasing a set of enforceable
rights against the rest of the world.

### 1.2 The bundle of rights

The traditional bundle contains five rights, and the metaphor is a bundle of sticks precisely because
sticks can be removed one at a time:

- **Possession** — the right to occupy and hold.
- **Use or enjoyment** — the right to use the property in any lawful manner.
- **Exclusion** — the right to keep others out.
- **Disposition** — the right to transfer the interest, by sale, gift, will or otherwise.
- **Encumbrance** — the right to use the property as security, or to grant lesser interests in it.

Every stick can be sold, leased, mortgaged, granted or reserved separately. A landowner may convey
the surface and reserve the minerals. A landowner may grant an easement — a slice of the use and
possession sticks — while keeping everything else. A tenant holds possession, use and exclusion for a
term while the landlord holds disposition. A mortgage lender holds a stick that becomes decisive only
if the borrower defaults.

**Why this is the first hour of the course.** Almost every listing dispute a new salesperson meets is
a dispute about which sticks were in the bundle. The seller thought the mineral rights came with the
farm. The buyer thought the boat slip was included. The neighbour thought the driveway easement had
lapsed. None of these is a question about the physical property; all of them are questions about the
bundle, and the licensee's job is to notice at listing that a stick is missing and get it written
down before anyone photographs the house.

### 1.3 Personal property, and the line between

**Personal property** — *chattels*, in the older vocabulary — is everything that is not real property.
It is movable. It transfers by **bill of sale**, not by deed. It is not conveyed by a contract for
real estate unless the contract says so.

The line matters commercially because it decides what a buyer gets. A refrigerator is personal
property; a built-in oven is usually real estate. A potted tree is personal property; a planted tree
is real estate. Standing timber is real estate; the moment it is cut it becomes personal property, a
transformation called **severance**. The reverse is **annexation** — personal property attached to
land so as to become part of it, which is where the next hour begins.

### 1.4 The licensee's discipline

The professional habit that follows from Hour 1 is simple and is worth adopting before anything else
in the course: **write down what conveys.** At the listing appointment, walk the property with the
seller and settle every item that could be argued about — appliances, window treatments, mounted
televisions, light fixtures, playsets, storage sheds, propane tanks, water treatment equipment. Put
the included and excluded lists in the contract. This costs twenty minutes at listing and saves a
transaction later.

## Hour 2 — Fixtures, trade fixtures, and the licensee's job

### 2.1 What a fixture is

A **fixture** is personal property that has been attached to real estate in such a way that it has
become part of it. Once an item is a fixture it conveys with the real estate unless it is expressly
excluded, and the buyer is entitled to find it there at closing.

Whether an item has become a fixture is decided by **the intention of the party who attached it,
judged objectively** — not by what that party privately meant, but by what the circumstances show.
Courts read intention through three kinds of evidence, and the standard mnemonic is **MARIA**, of
which the durable core is:

- **Method of annexation.** How firmly is it attached, and what damage would removal do? A chandelier
  wired into a ceiling box is more firmly annexed than a lamp plugged into a wall.
- **Adaptation to the real estate.** Was the item made or fitted for this particular property? Custom
  storm windows cut for these openings are adapted; a stack of generic screens in the garage is not.
- **Relationship of the parties.** A tenant is treated differently from an owner, and a seller
  differently from a buyer, because the expectations differ.
- **Intention**, read from the above and from any **agreement** the parties made — which is why a
  written agreement settles the question and the absence of one leaves it to be argued.

### 2.2 Trade fixtures

A **trade fixture** is an item a tenant attaches to leased premises for the conduct of their business:
a walk-in cooler in a restaurant, a hydraulic lift in a garage, shelving fitted to a shop floor.

Trade fixtures are the great exception. **They remain the tenant's personal property and may be
removed before the end of the term**, with the tenant responsible for repairing damage caused by
removal. But the right expires with the term. Property left behind after the lease ends generally
becomes the landlord's by **accession** — a departing business that abandons its equipment has
usually made a gift of it.

### 2.3 Where this goes wrong in practice

Three patterns account for most fixture disputes, and all three are preventable at listing:

**The item everyone assumes.** The seller's grandmother's dining room chandelier. It is wired in, it
is a fixture, and it conveys unless excluded. The correct handling is not to note it in the file and
hope: it is to have the seller **remove it and replace it with a fixture that will convey, before the
photographs are taken.** A buyer who fell in love with a photograph of a room has a reasonable
expectation, and a licensee who let that photograph be taken created the problem.

**The item that is not the seller's to convey.** Leased equipment — propane tanks, water softeners,
solar arrays, security systems, some HVAC. It is physically attached and looks like a fixture, and
the seller does not own it. A buyer cannot discover this by looking at it. The licensee's job is to
ask at listing whether any equipment on the property is leased, and to get the answer written down.

**The item installed by a tenant.** On a property with a business tenant, some of what is bolted down
belongs to the tenant and is going with them. That has to be established before a buyer bids on a
building partly furnished with equipment that will leave.

### 2.4 The Missouri frame

Missouri's brokerage relationships statute puts real weight behind this housekeeping. Under
§ 339.730.1(2), RSMo, a seller's agent owes the client the duty **to exercise reasonable skill and
care**. Under § 339.730.1(3)(c) the agent must disclose to the client **all adverse material facts
actually known or that should have been known**. And under § 339.730.3 the agent owes even a
**customer** — a party with no brokerage relationship — the duty to disclose all adverse material
facts actually known or that should have been known.

An **adverse material fact** is defined at § 339.710(1) as *a fact related to the property not
reasonably ascertainable or known to a party which negatively affects the value of the property*, and
the statute's own list includes **material defects in the title to the property**. A leased water
heater that the buyer will inherit a payment obligation for, or an item the seller intends to remove
that the buyer believes conveys, sits close to that line. The safe practice is not to litigate
whether a particular item crosses the statutory threshold. It is to write everything down.

## Hour 3 — The characteristics of land, and why markets behave as they do

### 3.1 The physical characteristics

Three, and each explains something about the business:

**Immobility.** A parcel cannot be moved. Demand in Kansas City cannot be satisfied by supply in
Springfield. This is the single most important fact about real estate markets and the reason they are
local: a national statistic about house prices describes no actual market anyone can buy in.
Immobility is also why *location* dominates value and why the same building is worth different
amounts on different streets.

**Indestructibility, or durability.** Land endures. Improvements depreciate, but the land beneath
them does not disappear. This is why land is treated as a long-term investment, why lenders will
write thirty-year loans against it, and why the overwhelming majority of the housing stock in any
Missouri market was built in the past rather than this year.

**Uniqueness, or heterogeneity, or non-homogeneity.** No two parcels are identical; even two
identical houses sit on different ground. This is the legal foundation of **specific performance** as
a remedy in real estate contracts — because damages cannot buy the injured party an equivalent
parcel, courts will order the actual transfer.

### 3.2 The economic characteristics

Four:

**Scarcity.** Land is finite. Not scarce everywhere — Missouri has a great deal of it — but scarce
where people want to be, which is what drives price.

**Improvements.** What is built on and around a parcel changes its value, and improvements to
neighbouring property affect it too. A new interchange, a new school, a new plant.

**Permanence of investment.** Capital sunk into land and improvements — grading, utilities,
foundations, streets — is fixed and long-lived. It cannot be recovered and redeployed elsewhere. This
is why development decisions are slow and why they are hard to reverse.

**Area preference, or *situs*.** People's preference for a particular area, for reasons that are
partly rational and partly not. Situs is the economic expression of immobility, and it is what an
appraiser is measuring when they adjust for location.

### 3.3 Supply, demand, and the lag

Real estate supply is **inelastic in the short run**. Bringing new housing to market takes years:
entitlement, financing, platting, infrastructure, construction. In Missouri the construction season
is further compressed by winter. So when demand rises — a large employer expands, a rate cut brings
buyers off the fence, a demographic wave reaches first-purchase age — supply cannot respond, and the
adjustment happens entirely in **price and in time on market**.

That lag is the structural reason real estate cycles. By the time the supply response arrives, the
demand condition that caused it may have passed, which produces the familiar overshoot in both
directions.

**Factors that move demand:** population and household formation, employment and income, interest
rates and credit availability, consumer confidence, and the price of the alternative — rent.

**Factors that move supply:** the availability of land, construction cost including labour and
materials, the cost and availability of construction finance, the entitlement and permitting
environment, and the existing stock coming to market.

**What a licensee may and may not say about this.** Naming these factors and citing recorded data is
providing information. Predicting where prices will go is not — nobody can, and a licensee who states
a forecast as fact has made a representation they cannot support. Under § 339.730.1(2) that is a
reasonable-skill-and-care problem, and under 20 CSR 2250-7.010(1)(L)3 the school itself is forbidden
to permit anyone associated with it **to provide any misleading information to the public**.

## Hour 4 — Government powers and the limits of private ownership

### 4.1 Four powers, and the mnemonic

Private ownership in the United States has always been ownership **subject to** government authority.
Four powers are traditionally named, and **PETE** holds them:

**Police power.** The power to regulate for public health, safety, morals and general welfare —
zoning, building codes, subdivision regulation, environmental controls, licensing. It is exercised
**without compensation**, which is the whole point: a landowner whose property is downzoned and loses
value is generally not entitled to be paid, because the government has regulated rather than taken.

**Eminent domain.** The power to take private property for public use, exercised through the process
called **condemnation**, and requiring **just compensation** under the Fifth Amendment and the
Missouri Constitution. A partial taking — a strip for a road widening — compensates for the strip and
for damage to the remainder.

**Taxation.** The power to raise revenue by charging against property. The real estate tax lien is
the ordinary case, and it customarily takes priority over other liens regardless of recording order.

**Escheat.** The power by which property reverts to the state when an owner dies leaving no heirs and
no will. It exists so that land never becomes ownerless.

### 4.2 Where the line between police power and taking runs

This is a constitutional question, not a licensee's question, and the correct professional response
is to name it and route it. But a licensee should be able to recognise the shape of the argument: a
regulation that goes too far may be a **regulatory taking** requiring compensation, and where that
line falls has occupied the courts for a century. A client who believes a zoning decision has taken
their property needs a lawyer, not a salesperson.

### 4.3 Private limits: the other half

Government is not the only limit. **Private land-use controls** — restrictive covenants, conditions
and restrictions recorded against a subdivision, enforced by the association or by the benefited
owners — operate alongside zoning and are entirely independent of it.

**The more restrictive controls.** A use the city permits may be forbidden by the deed. Buyers
routinely assume that a municipal approval settles the question, and it does not. The covenants are
read before an offer is written, not after a detached garage has been ordered.

### 4.4 What a licensee does with all of this

Identify the question, tell the client it must be answered by whoever answers it, and see that it is
answered before the client's contingencies expire. That last clause is where a licensee actually adds
value. Whether a home occupation is permitted is the municipality's determination; getting the
buyer's question in front of the municipality in week one rather than week four is the licensee's
contribution, and § 339.730.1(3)(d) — the duty to **advise the client to obtain expert advice as to
material matters** beyond the licensee's expertise — is the statutory expression of it.

## Hour 5 — The Missouri real estate business

### 5.1 The structure of a Missouri brokerage

Missouri's vocabulary is set by statute and it is worth learning exactly, because the examination
uses it and because the duties attach to the defined terms rather than to ordinary speech.

A **designated broker** is, under § 339.710(12), RSMo, an individual licensed as a broker operating
under the definition of real estate broker in § 339.010, or an individual licensed as a broker who is
appointed by a partnership, association, limited liability company, professional corporation or
corporation to be **responsible for the acts of** that entity. Every brokerage entity must appoint
one.

An **affiliated licensee** is, under § 339.710(2), **any broker or salesperson who works under the
supervision of a designated broker.** That is the salesperson's position: a salesperson does not
practise on their own account. Authority flows from the designated broker.

The practical consequence appears immediately in § 339.780.1: **all written agreements for brokerage
services are entered into by the designated broker** on behalf of the broker and the affiliated
licensees — unless the designated broker has authorised affiliated licensees **in writing** to enter
into them. A salesperson signing a listing agreement is doing so on the broker's behalf, under the
broker's written authority, and needs to know that authority exists.

**Every designated broker with affiliated licensees must adopt a written policy** identifying and
describing the relationships the broker and the affiliated licensees may engage in with any seller,
landlord, buyer or tenant — § 339.760. Read your firm's policy on your first day. It tells you which
brokerage relationships you are permitted to offer, and it is not the same at every firm.

### 5.2 Where a salesperson's income comes from

**Compensation is negotiable.** It is not set by any board, association, rule or custom, and any
suggestion that a rate is standard is a serious problem in itself.

Section 339.800, RSMo, governs how it moves, and three of its subsections matter to a new licensee:

- **.1** The designated broker's compensation may be paid by the seller, the landlord, the buyer, the
  tenant, or a third party, or shared between designated brokers.
- **.2** **Payment of compensation by itself shall not establish an agency relationship or transaction
  brokerage relationship** between the party who paid and the broker. This is the single most
  misunderstood sentence in Missouri brokerage law. Who pays does not decide whom you represent. The
  written agreement decides.
- **.5** A designated broker may be compensated by more than one party **with the knowledge of all
  the parties at or before the time of entering into a written contract** to buy, sell or lease.

And **.6**: nothing in the section relieves the licensee of the requirement to obtain a written
agreement for brokerage services or another written agreement addressing compensation.

Note also that a salesperson is compensated **by their designated broker**, not by the parties. Money
does not flow from a seller to a salesperson.

### 5.3 The specialties

The business is wider than residential sale. Residential brokerage; commercial brokerage — and note
that § 339.710(7) defines **commercial real estate** as real estate other than one-to-four
residential units or property classified agricultural and horticultural under § 137.016, with
single-family units sold unit-by-unit excluded; farm and land brokerage, which is substantial in
Missouri; property management; appraisal, which is a separately licensed profession and not something
a salesperson does; leasing; auction; development; and the mortgage, title and inspection businesses
that surround every transaction.

**Competence in the specialty is not optional.** 20 CSR 2250-7.060 makes a school responsible for
hiring only instructors with practical experience or formal training in the subject taught, and the
same principle governs a licensee: taking on a property type, a market or a transaction structure
you do not understand is a professional failure before it is a bad outcome. The statutory expression
is § 339.730.1(2), reasonable skill and care.

### 5.4 The path to a Missouri salesperson licence, and one date that catches people

The sequence is: complete the **48-hour Salesperson Pre-Examination Course**; complete the **24-hour
Missouri Real Estate Practice Course**; pass the examination administered by the Commission's
contracted testing service; and apply, with the required certificates, while associated with a
licensed broker.

**And then the fact almost nobody is told in time.** Section 339.040.6, RSMo, requires each
application for a salesperson licence to include a certificate from an accredited school that the
applicant **"has, within six months prior to the date of application, successfully completed the
prescribed salesperson curriculum."**

**Six months.** Finish the education, then let eight months pass before applying, and the education
no longer supports the application. Students lose entire courses to this. Plan the application around
it from the day you enrol. (The Commission may waive all or part of the educational requirement on
proof of other educational background or experience acceptable to it — but that is a discretionary
waiver to be applied for, not a fallback to be relied on.)

Applicants must be at least eighteen — § 339.040.3. And 20 CSR 2250-7.010(1)(D) requires this school
to advise every student, **before contractual obligation**, that **certain criminal convictions may
prevent a student from receiving or retaining a real estate licence**. That notice is given at
enrolment and repeated here. If it may apply to you, get the question answered before you pay for
education, not after you have finished it.

### 5.5 Two things this school may never say to you

Both are rules on the school rather than on the student, and knowing them helps you evaluate every
provider you will ever meet:

- **20 CSR 2250-7.010(1)(L)4** — no school shall permit anyone associated with it **to make any
  warranties or guarantees that a student will pass the real estate licence examination** by taking
  its courses. A provider advertising a guaranteed pass is telling you something about itself.
- **20 CSR 2250-7.010(1)(L)2** — no school shall advertise or imply that it is **recommended,
  endorsed or approved by the Commission**, though an accredited school may state which courses the
  Commission has approved it to teach. There is a real difference between "the Commission approved
  this course" and "the Commission endorses this school," and only the first can ever be true.

**And the status of this course today.** It is a version 1.0 submission draft. It is **not approved**,
no Missouri pre-license credit is available for it, and under 20 CSR 2250-7.010(1)(L)6 no course may
even be announced or advertised for credit until the Commission has approved it in writing.
