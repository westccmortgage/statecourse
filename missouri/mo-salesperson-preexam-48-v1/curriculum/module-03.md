   Missouri complaints.

5. Distinguish dual agent, transaction broker and designated agent, and name the five items neither a

6. State when a written agreement is required under § 339.780 and what each kind must contain.
7. Explain what survives termination under § 339.790.2 and what does not.
8. Identify a ministerial act and explain why the category exists.

## Hour 12 — Common-law agency and Missouri's statutory replacement

### 12.1 The common-law vocabulary

Agency is the relationship in which one person, the **agent**, acts on behalf of another, the
**principal**, in dealings with third parties. Common law traditionally described the agent's duties
to the principal with the mnemonic **OLD CAR**: Obedience to lawful instructions, Loyalty, Disclosure,
Confidentiality, Accounting, and Reasonable care.

Common law also classified agents by scope. A **universal agent** may act in all matters. A
**general agent** may act in a continuing series of transactions — a property manager is the standard
example. A **special agent** is authorised for one transaction with limited authority — which is what
a listing broker traditionally was.

And it classified authority as **express** (stated), **implied** (reasonably necessary to carry out
express authority), or **apparent** (created by the principal's conduct leading a third party
reasonably to believe authority exists, whether or not it does). Apparent authority is the dangerous
one, because it can bind a principal to something they never authorised.

### 12.2 Why Missouri's statute matters more than the common law

You should learn the common-law vocabulary because examinations test it and because it is the frame
the statute sits inside. But **in Missouri the operative law is the statute**, and it does not simply
codify the common law — it displaces it in specific and important ways.

Sections 339.710 through 339.860, RSMo — the Missouri brokerage relationships provisions — create a
category the common law did not have: the **limited agent**. Section 339.710(18) defines a limited
agent as *a licensee whose duties and obligations to a client are those set forth in sections 339.730
to 339.750.*

That definition is the whole point. A Missouri limited agent's duties are **the ones the statute
lists** — not the general fiduciary obligations a court might otherwise imply. The statute is a
closed list, and a licensee who knows the list knows their duties.

### 12.3 The two words the statute turns on

**Client** — § 339.710(6) — *a seller, landlord, buyer, or tenant who has entered into a brokerage
relationship with a licensee.*

**Customer** — § 339.710(10) — *an actual or potential seller, landlord, buyer, or tenant in a real
estate transaction in which a licensee is involved but who has not entered into a brokerage
relationship with the licensee.*

Everything in Missouri agency law follows from which of these two words describes the person in front
of you, and the difference is created by **a written agreement**, not by helpfulness, rapport, time
spent, or who is paying. Section 339.800.2 says so explicitly: *payment of compensation by itself
shall not establish an agency relationship or transaction brokerage relationship.*

### 12.4 Ministerial acts

Section 339.710(19) defines **ministerial acts** as *those acts that a licensee may perform for a
person or entity that are informative in nature and do not rise to the level which requires the
creation of a brokerage relationship.* The statute's own examples:

- responding to telephone inquiries from consumers about the availability and pricing of brokerage
  services;
- responding to telephone inquiries about the price or location of property;
- attending an open house and responding to questions about the property from a consumer;
- setting an appointment to view property;
- responding to questions of consumers walking into a licensee's office.

The category exists so that ordinary courtesy does not accidentally create a representation. You may
answer the phone. You may hold an open house. You may set a showing. None of that makes anyone your
client — and equally, none of it suspends the disclosure duty you owe every customer.

## Hour 13 — The limited agent's duties, and the customer sentence

### 13.1 Six duties, and they are the same on both sides

Section 339.730.1 sets out what a **seller's or landlord's agent** owes the client. Section 339.740.1
sets out what a **buyer's or tenant's agent** owes the client. They are the same six, mirrored:

1. **To perform the terms of the written agreement** made with the client.
2. **To exercise reasonable skill and care** for the client.
3. **To promote the interests of the client with the utmost good faith, loyalty, and fidelity**,
   including four specified things:
   - **(a)** seeking a price and terms acceptable to the client — with an express carve-out that the
     licensee is **not obligated to seek additional offers** while the property is already subject to
     a contract for sale, or a lease or letter of intent to lease;
   - **(b)** **presenting all written offers to and from the client in a timely manner regardless of
     whether the property is subject to a contract** for sale or lease;
   - **(c)** **disclosing to the client all adverse material facts actually known or that should have
     been known** by the licensee;
   - **(d)** **advising the client to obtain expert advice** as to material matters about which the
     licensee knows but the specifics of which are beyond the licensee's expertise.
4. **To account in a timely manner for all money and property received.**
5. **To comply** with §§ 339.710–339.860, with § 339.100.2, and with the rules under them.
6. **To comply with applicable federal, state and local laws, rules, regulations and ordinances,
   including fair housing and civil rights statutes and regulations.**

**Read (3)(a) and (3)(b) together, because they are a matched pair and they are commonly misread.**
Once a property is under contract you need not go looking for more offers — but if a written offer
arrives anyway, **you must still present it.** The obligation to present is not suspended by the
existence of a contract. Sellers ask about this constantly and licensees get it wrong constantly.

**And (3)(d) is the sentence that keeps a licensee inside their licence.** The duty is not to answer
the legal, tax, engineering or environmental question. It is to *know that the question exists and
tell the client to get it answered by someone qualified.* That is the whole of what is required, and
doing more than it is how licensees end up practising law.

### 13.2 What is owed to a customer

Section 339.730.3 — and § 339.740.3 in the mirror — is the most consequential sentence in Missouri
brokerage law:

> A licensee acting as a seller's or landlord's agent **owes no duty or obligation to a customer,
> except that a licensee shall disclose to any customer all adverse material facts actually known or
> that should have been known** by the licensee.

**No duty — except one, and it is a real one.** The same subsection continues: the agent **owes no
duty to conduct an independent inspection** or discover adverse material facts for the customer, and
**owes no duty to independently verify** the accuracy or completeness of any statement made by the
client or by any independent inspector.

Section 339.740.3 adds the buyer-side analogue: a buyer's agent **owes no duty to conduct an
independent investigation of the client's financial condition for the benefit of the customer.**

So the structure is: everything to the client, and to the customer honesty about adverse material
facts. That is not a small residue. A licensee cannot conceal a known defect from the other side and
then say the buyer was not their client. That defence does not exist in Missouri.

### 13.3 What an agent may do without breaching anything

Sections 339.730.4 and 339.740.4 protect ordinary practice explicitly:

- A **seller's or landlord's agent may show alternative properties** not owned by the client to
  prospective buyers, and **may list competing properties**, without breach.
- A **buyer's or tenant's agent may show properties the client is interested in to other prospective
  buyers**, and may assist competing buyers in attempting to purchase the same property, without
  breach.

These provisions exist because otherwise every listing would be a conflict with every other listing.

## Hour 14 — Adverse material facts and confidential information

### 14.1 The definition, read exactly

Section 339.710(1) defines an **adverse material fact** as:

> a fact related to the property not reasonably ascertainable or known to a party which negatively
> affects the value of the property.

Three elements, and each does work. It must be **related to the property**. It must be **not
reasonably ascertainable or known** to the party — a fact the buyer can see for themselves is not
concealed by not mentioning it. And it must **negatively affect value**.

The statute then lists what such facts *may* include:

- **(a)** environmental hazards affecting the property;
- **(b)** physical condition of the property which adversely affects value;
- **(c)** material defects in the property;
- **(d)** material defects in the **title** to the property;
- **(e)** **material limitation of the party's ability to perform under the terms of the contract.**

### 14.2 Item (e), which is not about the property at all

Read (e) again. *Material limitation of the party's ability to perform under the terms of the
contract.* That is not a defect in a house. It is a fact about a **party** — a buyer whose financing
has collapsed, a seller who cannot deliver clear title, a party who has told you they cannot close.

New licensees skip this item because the other four are about buildings. It belongs on the list
because a party's inability to perform destroys value in a transaction as surely as a cracked
foundation does, and because a licensee usually learns it before anyone else.

### 14.3 The two standards, and the difference between them

Missouri uses **two different disclosure standards** depending on the relationship, and the difference
is precise:

- A **limited agent** under §§ 339.730.1(3)(c), 339.730.3, 339.740 discloses adverse material facts
  **actually known or that should have been known.**
- A **transaction broker** under § 339.755(2)(d) discloses adverse material facts **of which the
  licensee has actual notice or knowledge.**

"Should have been known" is an objective standard and it makes wilful ignorance unavailable. A
licensee who arranges not to learn something has not escaped the duty. "Actual notice or knowledge"
is narrower.

The examination tests this distinction. Practice tests something simpler: **disclose it.** The
professional method is not to litigate which standard applies to a fact you already know. It is to
put it in writing, to everyone, and move on.

### 14.4 Confidential information

Section 339.710(9) defines **confidential information** as information obtained from the client and
designated confidential by the client; information made confidential by §§ 339.710–339.860 or any
other statute or regulation; or written instructions from the client — **unless** the information is
or becomes public by the client's own words or conduct, or from a source other than the licensee.

Section 339.730.2 and § 339.740.2 forbid disclosure of confidential information about the client
**unless** disclosure is required by statute, rule or regulation; **or failure to disclose would
constitute a misrepresentation**; or disclosure is necessary to defend the licensee against an action
of wrongful conduct in an administrative or judicial proceeding or before a professional committee.
And: **no cause of action arises against the licensee for making any required or permitted
disclosure.**

**That middle exception is where confidentiality and adverse-material-fact disclosure meet.** A
client may not make a fact confidential by asking. If withholding it would make what you have said a
misrepresentation, or if the law requires disclosure, it goes out. A seller who tells you in
confidence that the basement floods has not sealed your mouth; they have told you an adverse material
fact, and § 339.730.3 requires you to disclose it to any customer.

## Hour 15 — Dual agency and transaction brokerage

### 15.1 Dual agency

A **dual agent** represents both sides of the same transaction. Section 339.750.1: a licensee may act
as a dual agent **only with the consent of all parties**, and consent **is presumed by a written
agreement pursuant to § 339.780.**

Section 339.750.2: a dual agent is a **limited agent for both** the seller and buyer, or landlord and
tenant, with the duties of §§ 339.730 and 339.740 except as the section provides.

The structural problem is obvious and the statute does not pretend otherwise: two clients with
opposed interests cannot both receive undivided loyalty. So the statute narrows what a dual agent may
say.

### 15.2 The five items — memorise them

Section 339.750.4 lists five things a dual agent **shall not disclose without the consent of the
client to whom the information pertains**:

1. That a **buyer or tenant is willing to pay more** than the purchase price or lease rate offered.
2. That a **seller or landlord is willing to accept less** than the asking price or lease rate.
3. **What the motivating factors are** for any client buying, selling or leasing the property.
4. That a client **will agree to financing terms other than those offered**.
5. **The terms of any prior offers or counter offers** made by any party.

Section 339.750.3 supplies the other half: **outside those five items and outside confidential
information, a dual agent MAY disclose to one client information gained from the other if it is
material to the transaction.** Dual agency is not a vow of silence; it is a specific list of things
that stay in.

And § 339.750.6: **in a dual agency relationship there is no imputation of knowledge or information**
between the client and the dual agent, or among persons within an entity engaged as a dual agent.

### 15.3 Transaction brokerage

Section 339.755.1 permits a licensee to serve any party **without an agency or fiduciary relationship
to anyone**. Such a licensee is a **transaction broker**, and § 339.710's definition describes them
as **neutral, serving neither as an advocate nor advisor for either party.**

The § 339.755.2 duties:

- perform the terms of any **written or oral** agreement with any party — note that this is the one
  place the statute contemplates an oral agreement;
- exercise **reasonable skill, care and diligence**, including presenting all written offers and
  counteroffers timely; **informing the parties** regarding the transaction and **suggesting they
  obtain expert advice** on material matters beyond the broker's expertise; accounting timely for
  money and property; **disclosing to each party any adverse material facts of which the licensee has
  actual notice or knowledge**; and **assisting the parties in complying with the terms and
  conditions of any contract**.

Section 339.755.3 carries **the same five non-disclosure items** as dual agency, phrased as requiring
the **informed consent** of the party who disclosed them, plus confidential information about the
other party unless disclosure is required by law or failure to disclose would constitute fraud or
dishonest dealing.

Sections 339.755.4 and .5: a transaction broker **has no duty to conduct an independent inspection or
investigation for adverse material facts**, and **no duty to investigate the buyer's financial
condition.**

Section 339.755.7: **each party and the transaction broker possess only actual knowledge and
information. There is no imputation of knowledge or information by operation of law.**

Section 339.755.13: a licensee may **without liability withdraw** from representing a client who has
not consented to a conversion to transaction brokerage — and that withdrawal does not prejudice the
licensee's ability to continue representing the other client, or to represent the refusing client in
a different transaction.

### 15.4 Choosing between them

This is a **firm policy question before it is a transaction question.** Section 339.760 requires every
designated broker with affiliated licensees to adopt a written policy identifying and describing the
relationships the broker and the affiliated licensees may engage in. Some Missouri firms offer dual
agency; some route in-house situations to transaction brokerage; some appoint designated agents. **You
practise your firm's policy, not your preference**, and you read it before your first appointment.

## Hour 16 — Designated agency, subagency, and the firm

### 16.1 Designated agency

Section 339.820.1: a designated broker entering a limited agency agreement with a client — for a
listing or to represent a person in buying, selling, exchanging, renting or leasing — **may appoint in
writing affiliated licensees as designated agents to the exclusion of all other affiliated
licensees.** The same mechanism applies to designated transaction brokers under a transaction
brokerage agreement.

This solves the in-house problem. Without it, one firm listing a property and also working with the
buyer would be a dual agency for the entire firm. With it, one licensee is appointed to the seller and
another to the buyer, each with full limited-agent duties, and the firm is not thereby a dual agent.

Section 339.820.2 draws the boundary precisely: a designated broker **is not** a dual agent or
transaction broker **solely because** of making an appointment — **but any licensee who personally
represents both the seller and buyer in a particular transaction IS** a dual agent or transaction
broker and must comply accordingly.

So: the firm is protected by the appointment; the individual is not, if the individual is on both
sides. If both sides of your transaction are yours personally, you are a dual agent or a transaction
broker, and the appointment does not change that.

There is also a default rule worth knowing. Where an appointment has been made under § 339.820.1, an
affiliated licensee assisting a party **without a written agreement is presumed to be a transaction
broker** to the exclusion of all other affiliated licensees, unless a different status has been
disclosed to or established with that party.

Section 339.830.1: designated agents and designated transaction brokers **have the same duties and
responsibilities to the client and customer under §§ 339.730 to 339.755 as the designated broker**,
except as § 339.820 provides. Section 339.830.2: all licensees have **the same protections from
vicarious liability** as their designated broker.

### 16.2 Subagency

A **subagent** is a designated broker working for the client through the client's designated broker.
Section 339.730.5 permits a seller or landlord to agree in writing that other designated brokers may
be retained and compensated as subagents, and any such subagent is **a limited agent with the same
obligations** under §§ 339.730.1 to .4. Section 339.740.5 mirrors it for the buyer side.

Section 339.780.5 sets out how a subagency is entered: by written agreement with the client's
designated broker; or, where a designated broker has made a **unilateral offer of subagency**, by the
act of the second broker **disclosing to the customer that they are a subagent of the client**; or,
where a § 339.820 appointment has been made, by an excluded affiliated licensee disclosing the same.

Subagency has become uncommon in residential practice as buyer representation has become normal.
Know what it is, know that it makes the subagent the **seller's** agent while working with a buyer,
and know that the buyer must be told.

## Hour 17 — Written agreements, disclosure, termination

### 17.1 Section 339.780: what must be in writing, and when

Section 339.780.1: **all written agreements for brokerage services are entered into by the designated
broker** on behalf of the broker and the affiliated licensees — except that the designated broker may
**authorise affiliated licensees in writing** to enter into them.

Then, **before engaging in any of the activities enumerated in § 339.010**:

- **.2 Seller or landlord limited agency** — a written agency agreement including the § 339.730
  duties, **the terms of compensation**, and **whether an offer of subagency may be made** to any
  other designated broker.
- **.3 Single agency for a buyer or tenant** — a written agency agreement including the § 339.740
  duties and the terms of compensation. Note the carve-out: this applies **except for ministerial
  acts** as defined in § 339.710.
- **.4 Dual agency** — a written agreement **with both** seller and buyer (or landlord and tenant)
  permitting the broker to serve as dual agent, including the § 339.750 duties and terms of
  compensation.
- **.5 Subagency** — a written agreement with the client's designated broker, or the two disclosure
  routes described above.
- **.6 Transaction brokerage** — a designated broker who **expects to receive compensation from the
  party assisted** enters a written transaction brokerage agreement including the § 339.755 duties
  and terms of compensation.

**And § 339.780.7 — the minimum service standard for exclusive agreements.** Every exclusive brokerage
agreement must specify that the broker will provide at minimum:

1. **accepting delivery of and presenting** to the client or customer offers and counteroffers;
2. **assisting** the client or customer in developing, communicating, negotiating and presenting
   offers, counteroffers and notices **until a lease or purchase agreement is signed and all
   contingencies are satisfied or waived**;
3. **answering** the client's or customer's questions relating to offers, counteroffers, notices and
   contingencies.

That is a floor, not a ceiling — § 339.780.8 preserves the public's freedom to contract for more.

**Section 339.780 was amended in 2025**, effective 28 August 2025. It is read here in its current
text as of 2026-08-20 and is re-read before submission, because a recently amended section is the one
most likely to move again.

### 17.2 Section 339.770: the Broker Disclosure Form

In a **residential** real estate transaction, **at the earliest practicable opportunity during or
following the first substantial contact** with a seller, landlord, buyer or tenant **who has not
entered into a written agreement for services**, the licensee **shall provide that person with a
written copy of the current broker disclosure form prescribed by the Commission** — § 339.770.1.

Three conditions, all of which must be present: residential; first substantial contact; no existing
written agreement. Section 339.770.2 supplies the exception — where the person **has already** entered
into a written agreement with a designated broker, **no other licensee** is required to make the
disclosure.

And § 339.770.3: **disclosures made in accordance with §§ 339.710 to 339.860 are sufficient as a
matter of law** to disclose brokerage relationships to the public.

**The form itself is a Commission-prescribed form and has NOT been read for this project. This course
does not reproduce it, state its fields, or describe its layout.** What you carry out of this hour is
the statutory trigger and the routing: use the **current** form, obtained from the Commission, and
check that it is current rather than assuming the copy in your firm's drawer still is.

*What is "first substantial contact"?* The statute does not define it, and this course will not invent
a definition. The safe practice is the early one: give the form at the point the conversation stops
being ministerial and starts being about this person's transaction.

### 17.3 Termination, and what survives it

Section 339.790.1: the relationships **commence on the effective date of the broker's agreement** and
continue until **performance, completion, termination or expiration** of that agreement.

Section 339.790.2: after that point the broker and affiliated licensee **owe no further duty or
obligation, except**:

1. **accounting in a timely manner** for all money and property related to and received during the
   relationship; and
2. **treating as confidential** information provided by the client during the relationship **that may
   reasonably be expected to have a negative impact on the client's real estate activity** — unless
   the client gives written consent, disclosure is required by law, the information became public
   through the client or a source other than the brokerage, or disclosure is necessary to defend
   against an action of wrongful conduct.

**Confidentiality outlives the relationship. The rest of it does not.** A licensee who learned a
seller's divorce was forcing the sale does not become free to mention it when the listing expires.

### 17.4 A working method

Before every first appointment, four questions, answered in this order:

1. **What does my firm's § 339.760 written policy permit me to be here?**
2. **Is this residential, is this first substantial contact, and does this person already have a
   written agreement?** If the answer is residential / yes / no, the current Commission broker
   disclosure form goes in their hands.
3. **What relationship are we entering, and what written agreement does § 339.780 require for it?**
4. **What do I already know about this property or these parties that is an adverse material fact
   under § 339.710(1)?** Whatever it is, it goes in writing to everyone entitled to it.

Do those four things every time and most of Missouri agency law takes care of itself.
