## Learning objectives

1. Name the requirements of a valid deed and explain why recording is not one of them.
2. Distinguish the deed types by the covenants each gives and say when each is used.
3. Explain the three kinds of notice and why possession must be checked as well as the records.
4. Describe voluntary and involuntary alienation, including probate and adverse possession.
5. Read a title commitment's structure and say what requirements and exceptions each do.
6. State what an owner's policy covers and what it excepts.
7. Describe what happens at a closing, who does what, and what the licensee's role actually is.
8. Explain what RESPA and TRID require and what the licensee's part in them is.

## Hour 35 — Deeds

### 35.1 What makes a deed valid

A **deed** is the instrument that conveys an interest in real estate. To be valid it requires:

- a **grantor** with legal capacity;
- a **grantee** identified with certainty;
- **words of conveyance** — the granting clause;
- an **adequate legal description**;
- the **grantor's signature**, and in practice acknowledgement before a notary so that it may be
  recorded;
- **delivery** by the grantor and **acceptance** by the grantee.

**Title passes at delivery and acceptance.** Recording is not a requirement of validity. An unrecorded
but delivered deed is valid **between the parties** — and can still lose to a later interest taken and
recorded by someone without notice of it, which is the entire point of Hour 36.

Note also what is **not** required: the grantee's signature, and consideration in any particular
amount. Deeds routinely recite nominal consideration.

### 35.2 Deed types, by what the grantor promises

**General warranty deed.** The broadest covenants. The grantor warrants title against defects arising
at **any** time, including before the grantor owned the property. The traditional covenants are
seisin, right to convey, against encumbrances, quiet enjoyment, further assurance and warranty
forever. This is the ordinary residential sale deed.

**Special or limited warranty deed.** The grantor warrants only against defects arising **during the
grantor's own ownership.** Common where the grantor is an entity, a lender or a fiduciary that cannot
speak to what happened before it held title.

**Quit claim deed.** Conveys whatever interest the grantor has — **if any** — and warrants nothing. It
is a **clearing** instrument, used to remove a cloud, correct a name, release a possible interest or
transfer between related parties. It is not a sale instrument, and a buyer offered one in an
arm's-length purchase should ask why.

**Deeds by fiduciaries** — personal representative's deed, trustee's deed, sheriff's deed, guardian's
deed. Each carries the authority of the proceeding behind it and warrants little or nothing. **The
authority is the question**, and it is a legal one.

**Which deed is used in a particular transaction is a legal decision for counsel and the closing
agent.** A licensee who tells a client which deed to take or give has practised law.

## Hour 36 — Recording, notice, and the chain

### 36.1 What recording does

Recording an instrument in the county records gives **constructive notice to the world** of the
recorded interest, protecting its holder against later takers. It does **not** transfer title and it
does **not** validate an invalid instrument.

The practical rule is short: **record immediately.** The entire risk lives in the interval between
delivery and recording.

### 36.2 Three kinds of notice

**Actual notice** — the party in fact knows.

**Constructive notice from the records** — the party is charged with knowing what a proper search of
the public records would reveal, whether or not they searched.

**Inquiry notice, or notice from possession** — the party is charged with what a reasonable inspection
of the property would reveal, and with following up on what that inspection would make a reasonable
person ask about.

**The possession problem is the classic trap and it is a real one.** Someone living in the house, a
tenant with an unrecorded lease, a neighbour openly using a strip of the yard — each is a fact a
purchaser is expected to have noticed. Title is checked in the records **and** on the ground.

### 36.3 The chain of title, and clouds

The **chain of title** is the successive record of conveyances from an accepted starting point to the
present owner. A **cloud** is anything in it — a gap, an unreleased mortgage, a missing signature, a
name discrepancy, an estate that never went through probate — that raises a doubt about the title.

Clouds are resolved **before closing**, not explained away at it. The instruments used are typically a
quit claim deed, a corrective deed, an affidavit, a release, or in a hard case a **suit to quiet
title**.

**Evidence of title** is the proof of title's condition. In current Missouri practice that is
predominantly a **title insurance commitment and policy**; historically it was an abstract of title
with an attorney's opinion. Which form a transaction uses is settled in the purchase contract, which
is one more reason the contract is read before it is signed.

## Hour 37 — Involuntary alienation, probate, and estate sales

### 37.1 Voluntary and involuntary

**Voluntary alienation** is transfer by the owner's act — sale, gift, dedication, or transfer at death
by will.

**Involuntary alienation** happens without the owner's consent:

- **Escheat** — to the state where an owner dies with no heirs and no will.
- **Eminent domain** — through condemnation, with just compensation.
- **Foreclosure** — enforcement of a lien.
- **Tax sale** — enforcement of the tax lien.
- **Adverse possession** — a court's determination that long possession has ripened into title.
- **Natural processes** — accretion, erosion, avulsion and reliction, which move boundaries along
  watercourses.

### 37.2 Adverse possession

Possession that is **actual, open and notorious, hostile** to the true owner's interest, **exclusive**,
and **continuous for the statutory period** may ripen into title. **Tacking** allows successive
possessors in privity to add their periods together.

Adverse possession is **established by a court**, not asserted by a party. **This course states no
Missouri statutory period**, because the relevant Missouri statute has not been read from primary text
for this project.

What a licensee carries out of this is the recognition, and it is worth stating as a practice rule: a
**fence, drive, dock, garden or building in the wrong place, in place for a long time**, is routed to
counsel and a surveyor. It is never resolved by asking the neighbours what they think.

### 37.3 Transfer at death, and estate sales

**Testate** — the owner died leaving a valid will; real property passes by **devise** to a
**devisee**. **Intestate** — no valid will; property passes to heirs under the state's descent and
distribution statute.

**Probate** is the court proceeding that proves the will, appoints a personal representative, settles
claims and authorises distribution.

**What a licensee must know about an estate sale:**

- **Authority to convey comes from the probate proceeding and the personal representative's
  appointment.** It is a legal determination and it is established before a listing is taken, not
  discovered at closing.
- **Probate timing can control the closing date.** Building a closing date around a probate step
  nobody has confirmed is the avoidable error in estate work, and it is the one that happens most.
- The deed will typically be a fiduciary's deed warranting little, and the buyer should understand
  that.

**This course states no Missouri probate rule, procedure or timetable**, because Missouri's probate
chapter has not been read from primary text for this project.

## Hour 38 — Title evidence and title insurance

### 38.1 The commitment

A **title commitment** is the title company's conditional promise to insure. Read its structure:

**Schedule A** — the facts. The proposed insured, the amount, the estate or interest, the current
record owner, the legal description.

**Schedule B-I — Requirements.** What must be done before the policy issues: payoff and release of
existing liens, corrective instruments, evidence of authority, satisfaction of judgments, resolution
of probate.

**Schedule B-II — Exceptions.** What the policy will **not** cover. This is where the policy's actual
scope is decided and it is the part a buyer is least likely to read. The standard exceptions commonly
include **rights of parties in possession**, **survey matters and encroachments** absent a survey,
easements not shown by the records, mineral rights, and taxes not yet due and payable.

**A commitment is not a coverage summary.** A licensee who tells a buyer "the title policy will take
care of it" has characterised another party's contract they have not read and cannot bind.

### 38.2 What title insurance covers

An **owner's policy** insures against loss from covered title defects existing **at the date of
policy** — forged instruments, undisclosed heirs, incompetent grantors, recording errors, defective
deeds, undisclosed liens. Coverage is retrospective: it insures the past, not future events.

A **lender's policy** insures the lender's lien position in the amount of the loan and decreases as
the loan is repaid. **A lender's policy does not protect the buyer**, and buyers regularly believe
that it does. Say so.

**What is excepted:** everything in Schedule B-II, plus defects created, assumed or agreed to by the
insured, and defects known to the insured and not disclosed to the company.

**The survey exception is the practical one.** Encroachments, boundary discrepancies and matters a
survey would disclose are typically excepted **unless a survey is furnished and the exception
removed**. A buyer who declines a survey has accepted that risk and should be told so in exactly those
words.

### 38.3 The licensee's part

Get the commitment into the buyer's hands **early**. Tell them to read Schedules B-I and B-II. Route
every question to the title company and counsel. Watch the objection deadline in the contract, because
it will pass while everyone assumes the title work is "in progress."

## Hour 39 — The closing

### 39.1 What actually happens

Closing is the simultaneous exchange in which the buyer's funds and the seller's deed change hands and
the instruments are recorded. In Missouri practice it is customarily conducted by a **title company or
closing agent**, and it may be conducted with the parties present or in escrow.

**The steps, in order:**

1. Title examination and clearing of Schedule B-I requirements.
2. The lender's underwriting, appraisal, and clear-to-close.
3. Preparation of the settlement statement and the closing documents.
4. The buyer's **final walkthrough** — not an inspection, but a confirmation that the property is in
   the agreed condition, that agreed repairs were made, and that the included items are still there.
5. Signing, funding, disbursement.
6. **Recording** of the deed and the security instrument.

### 39.2 Who does what

**The closing agent** prepares the settlement statement, handles the funds, records the instruments
and issues the policy. **The lender** funds and sets its own conditions. **Counsel** advises on legal
questions and drafts anything that needs drafting. **The licensee** does none of those things.

**What the licensee actually does at closing** is narrower and useful: makes sure everyone has what
they need before the day; reviews the settlement statement **against the contract** for the items the
contract allocated — the prorations, the credits, the agreed repairs, the commission — and raises any
discrepancy **before** signing; attends; and does not answer questions that belong to the closing
agent, the lender or counsel.

### 39.3 Prorations and credits at settlement

A **credit** to a party is money in their favour; a **debit** is money against them. Items customarily
prorated include real estate taxes, association dues, rents and security deposits on a leased
property, and fuel on a tank.

The **contract** decides the basis and the closing-day convention, and Module MO-05 Hour 29 supplies
the arithmetic. What matters here is the check: does the settlement statement do what the contract
said?

### 39.4 RESPA and TRID

**RESPA** — the Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act — governs settlement services on most federally
related residential mortgage loans. Two prohibitions matter directly to a licensee:

- **Section 8 prohibits kickbacks and unearned fees** for the referral of settlement service business.
  A payment, a thing of value, or a fee for a service not actually performed, in exchange for
  referrals, is prohibited. This is not a technicality; it is the provision that ends careers.
- **Section 9 prohibits a seller from requiring the buyer to use a particular title company** as a
  condition of sale.

RESPA also requires disclosure of **affiliated business arrangements** — where the licensee or the
firm has an ownership interest in a settlement service provider they refer business to. The interest
is disclosed, in writing, and the consumer is told they are not required to use the affiliate.

**TRID** — the TILA-RESPA Integrated Disclosure rule — requires the **Loan Estimate**, delivered
within three business days of application, and the **Closing Disclosure**, which the consumer must
receive at least **three business days before consummation**. Certain changes restart that three-day
period.

**The licensee's part in TRID is to protect the timeline.** Late-breaking changes — a repair credit
agreed two days before closing, a change in the loan amount — can trigger a redisclosure and move the
closing date. Getting changes decided early, and telling the lender promptly, is a real service and
costs nothing.

**This course states no dollar threshold, fee tolerance or exemption under RESPA or TRID**, because
neither the statute nor Regulation X nor Regulation Z was read from primary text for this project. The
prohibitions and the three-day rule above are stated at the level of principle; every specific is
routed to the lender, the closing agent and counsel.
