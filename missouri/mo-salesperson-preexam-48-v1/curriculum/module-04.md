# Missouri Salesperson Pre-Examination Course — Module MO-04

## Hours 18–23 · Contracts

**Clock hours:** 6. A Missouri clock hour is **60 minutes containing at least 50 minutes of actual
instruction** and no more than 10 minutes of recess — 20 CSR 2250-7.070(2).
**Content basis:** a StateCourse organisation for Commission review. **20 CSR 2250-6.060(1) requires

## Learning objectives

1. Name the elements of a valid contract and identify which one is missing from a defective fact
   pattern.
2. Distinguish valid, void, voidable and unenforceable, and executory from executed.
3. Explain the statute of frauds as it applies to real estate and name what it requires.
4. Trace an offer through acceptance, counter-offer, revocation and lapse, and state the effect of
   each.
5. Distinguish a contingency from a general right to cancel, and explain how contingencies actually
   fail.
6. Distinguish assignment from novation and state who remains liable under each.
7. Name the remedies for breach and explain why specific performance is available in real estate.
8. State the boundary between completing a form and practising law, and route accordingly.

## Hour 18 — Elements and classifications

### 18.1 The elements of a valid contract

A contract is a legally enforceable agreement to do or refrain from doing a legal act. Five elements,
and for real estate a sixth:

1. **Offer and acceptance** — mutual assent, sometimes called a meeting of the minds. There must be a
   definite offer and an unqualified acceptance of it.
2. **Consideration** — something of legal value exchanged. In a purchase contract the consideration is
   the mutual promises: the buyer promises to pay, the seller promises to convey. Earnest money is not
   the consideration; it is evidence of good faith and a fund for damages.
3. **Legal capacity** — parties competent to contract. Minors, persons adjudicated incompetent, and
   persons acting under an inadequate power of attorney all raise capacity questions, and every one of
   them is a question for counsel and the title company rather than for a licensee.
4. **Lawful object** — a legal purpose. A contract to do something illegal is void.
5. **Genuine assent** — agreement not produced by fraud, misrepresentation, mistake, duress or undue
   influence.
6. **A writing** — for a conveyance of an interest in real estate, under the statute of frauds.

**Genuine assent is where a licensee most often supplies the defect.** A misrepresentation — even an
innocent one, even a repeated one, even one the licensee believed — can undermine the assent the
contract depends on. That is why Module MO-08's treatment of avoiding misrepresentation is not a
professional courtesy topic but a contract-formation topic.

### 18.2 Classifications

**By validity.** *Valid* — meets all elements and is enforceable. *Void* — never had legal effect at
all; there is nothing to enforce and nothing to disaffirm. *Voidable* — valid and enforceable **unless
and until** a party with the power to disaffirm elects to do so. *Unenforceable* — has the elements
but cannot be enforced in court, typically because it is not in writing or a limitation period has
run.

The examination tests void against voidable relentlessly. The distinction: a void contract is a
nullity; a voidable contract is real and binding on at least one party until the other one gets out
of it.

**By performance.** *Executory* — something remains to be done. A signed purchase contract before
closing is executory. *Executed* — fully performed by all parties. Note that "executed" in this sense
means *completed*, not *signed*, which is a genuine trap because the word is used both ways in
ordinary practice.

**By the shape of the promise.** *Bilateral* — a promise for a promise; both sides are bound. A
purchase contract is bilateral. *Unilateral* — a promise for an act; only the offeror is bound until
someone performs. An open listing is the classic example: the seller promises to pay whoever produces
a buyer, and nobody is obliged to try.

**By formation.** *Express* — stated in words. *Implied* — inferred from conduct.

## Hour 19 — The statute of frauds, and the life of an offer

### 19.1 The statute of frauds

The statute of frauds requires certain contracts to be **in writing and signed by the party to be
charged** in order to be enforceable. Contracts for the sale of an interest in real estate are within
it, as are leases beyond a stated duration.

Two consequences worth stating plainly. First, **an oral real estate contract is not void — it is
unenforceable**, which is a different thing and produces different arguments. Second, the writing must
contain the essential terms: the parties, the property described adequately, the price, and the
signature of the party against whom enforcement is sought.

**This course states no Missouri lease-duration threshold** for the writing requirement, because
Missouri's statute of frauds has not been read from primary text for this project. The practice rule
does not depend on the threshold: **get it in writing.**

### 19.2 Offer

An **offer** is a definite proposal, communicated to the offeree, showing an intention to be bound on
acceptance. An advertisement is generally not an offer; it is an invitation to make one — which is why
a listing at a price is not a promise to sell at that price.

An offer ends in one of five ways:

- **Acceptance** — it becomes a contract.
- **Rejection** by the offeree.
- **Counter-offer**, which is a rejection plus a new offer.
- **Revocation** by the offeror **before acceptance is communicated**.
- **Lapse** — the time stated in the offer expires, or a reasonable time passes where none is stated.

Death or incapacity of a party before acceptance also terminates an offer.

### 19.3 Acceptance

Acceptance must be **unqualified** — the mirror image of the offer — and it must be **communicated in
the manner the offer requires.** Signature and delivery are different events and may fall on different
days. **The formation date anchors provisions that expressly measure time from formation or the
effective date; it is not the trigger for every contractual deadline.** Other provisions may use
receipt of a document, delivery of a notice, a specified event, or a fixed calendar date.

The practical discipline: when acceptance is delivered, **record the date in the place the contract
provides and build a deadline calendar using each provision's own trigger.** Check calendar versus
business days, the counting convention, cut-off time, permitted delivery method, designated recipient,
and any amendment. Record the receipt evidence when a period begins on receipt. Do not replace a
clause-specific trigger with the signature or formation date. Ask the designated broker and, where
interpretation is required, counsel about ambiguous terms before a deadline passes.

### 19.4 Counter-offers

A **counter-offer rejects the original offer and substitutes a new one.** The original is dead and can
no longer simply be accepted.

This creates a real problem for a seller with several offers, because countering all of them destroys
all of them. The standard solution is a **multiple counter-proposal** instrument that reserves the
seller's right to accept only one — and whether such an instrument is available to you depends on the
forms your firm uses. What a licensee must never do is invent language to solve this. See Hour 23.

### 19.5 Amendments and notices

Distinguish three instruments that are routinely confused:

- An **offer** or **counter-offer** proposes terms before there is a contract.
- An **amendment** changes a contract that already exists, and requires the agreement of all parties.
- A **notice** exercises a right the contract already gives — removing a contingency, giving notice of
  a defect, giving notice of an inability to obtain financing. A notice is **unilateral**: it does not
  need the other party's agreement, but it does need to be given in the manner and by the deadline the
  contract specifies.

Using the wrong one is a common and expensive error. A buyer who "amends" when they should have given
notice has asked permission for something they were entitled to do.

## Hour 20 — The purchase contract, provision by provision

### 20.1 What the contract must settle

Whatever form your firm uses, a residential purchase contract has to answer the same questions, and a
licensee should be able to find each of them:

- **Parties and property.** Full names of every party who must sign; the legal description copied from
  the deed or supplied by the title company, not from a tax record.
- **Price and how it will be paid** — cash, financing, or a combination, and the terms of the
  financing the buyer will seek.
- **Earnest money** — the amount, who holds it, when it is deposited, and what happens to it in each
  of the ways the contract can end.
- **Included and excluded items** — the fixture discipline from Module MO-01, reduced to two lists.
- **Contingencies** — each with its own condition, deadline, method of notice, and stated effect if
  the condition fails.
- **Title evidence** — what will be furnished, by whom, by when, and what happens if the buyer objects.
- **Closing date and possession.** These are not the same date and the contract must say so if they
  differ. Possession before closing, or after, needs its own written agreement.
- **Prorations** — taxes, assessments, rents, fuel, association dues, and the basis on which each is
  divided.
- **Risk of loss** before closing.
- **Default and remedies** — what each party may do if the other fails to perform.
- **Signatures and the date of acceptance.**

### 20.2 Earnest money

Earnest money is **not** the consideration for the contract and it is **not** a fee. It is a deposit
evidencing the buyer's good faith, held in trust, and applied to the purchase price at closing.

Three things a licensee must know cold:

**It is trust money the moment it is received.** It goes to the firm for handling under the firm's
trust account procedures. It is never held uncashed in a drawer as a favour to a buyer, and it never
touches an operating account.

**Its disposition on a dispute is not the licensee's decision.** What happens to disputed earnest
money is decided by what the contract provides and by the parties or a court. A licensee who releases
disputed funds to keep a client happy has committed a licence problem regardless of who turns out to
have been right about the contract.

**This course states no Missouri deposit deadline or trust-account requirement**, because 20 CSR 2250
chapters 1 through 5 and 8, and § 339.105, RSMo, have not been read from primary text for this
project. The operative deadlines come from those chapters and from your firm's policy, and both are
read before you accept your first cheque.

### 20.3 Risk of loss, possession, and the gap between contract and closing

Between formation and closing the buyer holds **equitable title** — a recognised interest in the
property — while the seller retains legal title. The contract allocates the risk of casualty loss in
that interval, and it should be read rather than assumed.

Possession before closing is a bad idea executed badly more often than it is a bad idea. If it must
happen it needs a separate written agreement addressing rent, insurance, utilities, damage,
maintenance, and what happens if the sale does not close. A buyer already living in the house who
cannot close is a problem with no good solution.

## Hour 21 — Contingencies and how transactions actually fail

### 21.1 What a contingency is

A **contingency** is a condition that must be satisfied or waived before a party is obliged to
perform. It is not a general right to change one's mind. A buyer who has found a house they like
better has not triggered the inspection contingency.

Every contingency has four parts, and all four must be read:

1. **The condition** — what must happen.
2. **The deadline** — by when.
3. **The method of notice** — how the party tells the other side, in what form, delivered how.
4. **The effect of silence** — what happens if the deadline passes with no notice given. Sometimes the
   contingency is waived; sometimes the contract terminates. **It depends entirely on the form**, and
   assuming is how transactions die.

### 21.2 The common contingencies

**Financing.** The buyer's obligation is conditioned on obtaining a loan of a stated type and amount
on stated terms. A pre-approval is not a commitment — this is why the contingency exists, and a buyer
who has been allowed to think otherwise will not understand why anyone is worried.

**Inspection.** The buyer may inspect within a period and then, depending on the form, object,
request repairs, or terminate. Note what an inspection contingency is *not*: a warranty of condition,
a price renegotiation clause, or a way out for a buyer who has changed their mind — though in practice
it is used as the last of these constantly.

**Appraisal.** The property must appraise at or above a stated value for the lender's purposes. A low
appraisal does not lower the price by itself; it creates a gap someone must close.

**Sale of the buyer's existing home.** The most fragile contingency in residential practice, because
it makes this transaction depend on another one nobody in this room controls.

**Title.** The buyer may examine title evidence and object to defects within a period.

**Survey.** Where the buyer wants boundaries, encroachments and easements established, and it is the
only way to remove the survey exception from the title policy.

### 21.3 How contingencies actually fail

Not through the condition. Through **inattention.** The overwhelming majority of contingency failures
in residential practice are a deadline that passed while everyone assumed the contingency was still
open, or a notice given in the wrong form, or a notice given to the wrong person.

The method that prevents this is unglamorous and it works:

1. On the day of acceptance, **write every deadline in the file and in your calendar**, computed from
   the date of acceptance.
2. Work backwards from each deadline to the date the underlying work must **start** — an inspection
   deadline is useless if the inspector is booked out ten days.
3. **Confirm every notice in the form the contract requires**, and keep proof of delivery.
4. When a deadline is at risk, raise it with the client **before** it passes, in writing.

This is what § 339.730.1(2) — reasonable skill and care — actually looks like in daily practice. It is
almost entirely calendar work.

## Hour 22 — Assignment, discharge, breach, remedies

### 22.1 Assignment and novation

**Assignment** transfers rights under a contract to another party. Unless the contract prohibits it or
the duties are personal, contract rights are generally assignable — but **the original party
ordinarily remains liable** for performance.

**Novation** substitutes a new party for an original one **and releases the original**. It requires
the consent of all parties.

The practical difference is who can be sued when performance fails. A buyer who assigns a purchase
contract and believes they have walked away usually has not. Only a novation does that, and a novation
is a document the parties actually sign.

### 22.2 Discharge

A contract can be discharged by **performance** — the ordinary case; by **agreement** — mutual
rescission, novation, or accord and satisfaction; by **operation of law** — bankruptcy, expiration of
a limitation period, destruction of the subject matter, impossibility; and by **breach**.

### 22.3 Breach and remedies

A **breach** is a failure to perform without legal excuse. The remedies:

- **Compensatory damages** — money to put the injured party where performance would have.
- **Liquidated damages** — an amount the parties fixed in advance. In residential practice earnest
  money is frequently the agreed liquidated damages for a buyer's default, which is one reason the
  amount matters.
- **Specific performance** — a court order compelling the actual transfer. **Available in real estate
  because each parcel is unique**, so money may not make the injured party whole. This is Module
  MO-02's uniqueness characteristic doing legal work.
- **Rescission** — unwinding the contract and restoring the parties to their prior positions.
- **Reformation** — correcting a writing to reflect what the parties actually agreed.

**None of these is a licensee's to advise on.** A client asking what they can do about a breach is
asking a legal question, and § 339.730.1(3)(d) requires the licensee to tell them to get expert
advice. What a licensee contributes is an accurate factual record of what happened and when.

## Hour 23 — Other real estate contracts, and the drafting boundary

### 23.1 The other contracts a salesperson meets

**Listing agreements** — the contract between the seller and the firm. Exclusive right to sell: the
firm is owed a commission on a sale during the term regardless of who procures the buyer, including
the seller. Exclusive agency: the seller may sell themselves without owing a commission. Open listing:
only the procuring broker is paid, and the seller may give it to many firms. In Missouri each of these
is a written agreement required by § 339.780.2 and entered into by the designated broker.

**Buyer agency agreements** — § 339.780.3, required before any of the § 339.010 activities except
ministerial acts, and containing the § 339.740 duties and the terms of compensation.

**Transaction brokerage agreements** — § 339.780.6, required where the transaction broker expects
compensation from the party assisted.

**Options** — a unilateral contract in which the optionor gives the optionee the right, but not the
obligation, to buy on stated terms within a period, for consideration. The optionee is not bound.

**Land contracts, or contracts for deed** — the buyer takes possession and equitable title while the
seller retains legal title until the payments are complete. Structurally, this is the arrangement in
which the buyer has the least protection, and the consequences of default are a legal question with
real stakes. A licensee explains the structure and routes the decision to counsel. **This course
states no Missouri rule governing land contracts**, because the relevant chapter has not been read
from primary text for this project.

**Leases** — a conveyance of a possessory interest and a contract of covenants at once.

### 23.2 The boundary: completing a form is not drafting one

This is the most important professional line in the module, and it is not a technicality.

**Inside the licence:** completing forms the firm supplies by filling in the facts of the transaction;
selecting among the approved provisions the forms offer; explaining what a provision means in the
ordinary sense; and advising the client to obtain expert advice, as § 339.730.1(3)(d) requires.

**Outside the licence:** composing new legal language to solve a client's specific legal problem;
advising which form of ownership to take title in; opining on the legal effect of a provision;
drafting an addendum to address something the forms do not cover.

When the forms do not fit, the answer is not to write something. The answer is: **counsel drafts it
and it is attached.** A licensee who composes a clause has practised law, and — separately — has
usually produced a clause that does not do what the client wanted.

### 23.3 A working method for the contract stage

1. **Read the whole form before you use it the first time**, not the first time a client asks about a
   provision.
2. **Fill nothing in from memory.** Legal description from the deed or the title company. Names from
   identification. Figures from the documents.
3. **Date the acceptance correctly and calendar every deadline the same day.**
4. **Where a term does not fit, say so and route it** rather than improvising.
5. **Confirm in writing.** Every material conversation about the contract ends with an email that says
   what was decided.
