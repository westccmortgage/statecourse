## Forms of conveyances and recording of documents

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module the student will be able to:

- State the elements a deed needs to be valid in Alaska and identify which one fails most often.
- Distinguish the deed types used in Alaska and say what each warrants.
- Describe how Alaska's recording system is organised and what recording accomplishes.
- Explain how priority is decided between competing claims and what notice means.
- Read a title commitment's requirements and exceptions and act on them within a transaction's deadlines.
- Identify the involuntary transfers a licensee meets and route the legal questions each raises.

## 1. Deeds and valid conveyance

### 1.1 What a deed does

A deed is the written instrument transferring an interest in real property from a **grantor** to a **grantee**. It is the act of conveyance and the evidence of it — not the ownership itself.

### 1.2 Elements

In substance, an Alaska deed requires:

- A **competent grantor** — of legal age and sound mind.
- A **grantee** named with reasonable certainty.
- **Words of conveyance**.
- An adequate **legal description**.
- The grantor's **signature**, acknowledged so the instrument may be recorded.
- **Delivery** to and **acceptance** by the grantee.

**Delivery is the element that fails.** A deed signed and kept in a drawer conveys nothing, because delivery requires the grantor's present intent to convey. A deed handed over with instructions not to record until the grantor dies raises a delivery question that has produced a great deal of litigation everywhere it has been tried.

**Consideration** is recited but need not be substantial. A gift deed is valid.

### 1.3 The deed types

**Warranty deed** — the grantor warrants title against all defects, including those arising before the grantor owned. The ordinary instrument in an arm's-length Alaska sale.

**Special or limited warranty deed** — warrants only against defects arising during the grantor's own ownership. Common from institutional sellers, relocation companies, and estates.

**Quitclaim deed** — conveys whatever interest the grantor has, possibly nothing, with no warranty. Used between family members, to clear a cloud, in divorce, and between entities. A quitclaim is not defective; it simply promises nothing, and a buyer accepting one in an arm's-length purchase should be asking why.

**Personal representative's deed, trustee's deed, and deeds given in a representative capacity** — each carrying its own authority question from module 02.

### 1.4 What a deed does not do

A deed does not prove the grantor owned anything. A grantor can execute a warranty deed for property they do not own; the deed is valid on its face and the warranty is a promise enforceable against a person who may have nothing.

That is the entire reason title examination and title insurance exist, and it is why the answer to "do I really need title insurance?" is never a flat no.

#### Checkpoint 1.1

At closing a seller offers a quitclaim deed instead of the warranty deed the purchase agreement specifies, saying it is simpler. What does the licensee do?

*Answer:* Stop the substitution. The deed to be delivered is a term of the contract, and changing it is a contract amendment requiring both parties' agreement. Whether the buyer should accept it is expert advice under AS 08.88.620(3) — the licensee routes it to counsel and the title company. What must not happen is a quiet swap at the closing table.

## 2. Recording, and priority

### 2.1 Alaska's recording system

Alaska records instruments through the **recording districts** administered by the Department of Natural Resources, rather than through county offices — Alaska has boroughs and an unorganised borough rather than counties, and the recording districts are their own geography.

A licensee's practical takeaway is that the correct **recording district** for a parcel is a fact to confirm, not to assume from the town's name.

### 2.2 What recording does

Recording does not make a deed valid — delivery does. Recording gives **constructive notice** to the world of the interest, and constructive notice is what protects the holder against later claims.

An unrecorded deed is good between the parties and dangerous against everyone else.

### 2.3 Notice, in three forms

**Actual notice** — you were told, or you read it.
**Constructive notice** — it was recorded, so the law charges you with knowledge whether or not you looked.
**Inquiry notice** — something visible would have prompted a reasonable person to investigate. A tenant in possession is the standard example: a buyer who never asks who is living there is charged with what asking would have revealed.

Inquiry notice is why a licensee walks the property and asks about occupants. It is the buyer's protection, not curiosity.

### 2.4 Priority

The ordinary rule is first in time, first in right, measured by recording, and modified by the recording statute's protection of a good-faith purchaser for value without notice.

Exceptions matter more than the rule in practice. Property tax liens and certain statutory liens can outrank earlier recorded interests, and construction liens may relate back to a date earlier than their recording. **A licensee does not resolve a priority question**; a licensee notices that one exists and gets it to the title company.

### 2.5 What gets recorded

Deeds, deeds of trust and mortgages, releases and reconveyances, leases and memoranda of lease, easements, liens, judgments, plats, and restrictive covenants. Anything affecting title, recorded, becomes constructive notice.

#### Checkpoint 2.1

A buyer's walkthrough reveals someone living in a house represented as vacant. Beyond the practical problem, what is the legal significance?

*Answer:* Possession is inquiry notice, so a buyer who proceeds without asking the occupant about their rights may be charged with whatever an enquiry would have revealed — including an unrecorded lease or an unrecorded purchase interest. The licensee stops, documents what was found, and gets it to the title company and to counsel before closing.

## 3. Title evidence and title insurance

### 3.1 The commitment

Before closing the title company issues a **commitment**: what it will insure and on what conditions. Two schedules carry the work.

**Requirements** — what must happen before the policy issues: payoffs, releases, signatures, authority documents.

**Exceptions** — what the policy will **not** cover: easements, restrictions, reservations including mineral reservations, matters an accurate survey would show, rights of parties in possession.

**The exceptions are the point.** A buyer who reads only the front page learns they are insured; a buyer who reads the exceptions learns what they are buying.

### 3.2 The two policies

An **owner's policy** protects the buyer, generally for the purchase price. A **lender's policy** protects the lender for the loan amount and protects the buyer not at all. Explaining that difference is a factual statement a licensee may make; advising whether to buy one is the client's decision with the title company available.

### 3.3 What title insurance does not cover

Defects the buyer created or knew about and did not disclose; governmental regulation including zoning; eminent domain; and — unless a survey exception is removed — matters an accurate survey would show. It is not an inspection, not a survey, and not a guarantee of value.

### 3.4 The routine that prevents surprises

Obtain the commitment when it issues. Read the requirements and the exceptions. Compare the legal description against the deed and the listing. Identify anything that would surprise the client. Send it to the client with a plain-language note saying what it is, that the licensee cannot interpret it, and that questions go to the title company or counsel — **before the contingency date**.

Twenty minutes, and it prevents most of the title surprises that become complaints.

#### Checkpoint 3.1

An exception reads "reservations contained in United States Patent, including but not limited to minerals." The buyer asks what that means for the gravel on the property.

*Answer:* The licensee delivers the patent and the exception, states plainly that what the reservation covers and permits is beyond their expertise, and advises the buyer to obtain expert advice — a duty under AS 08.88.620(3) for a represented buyer. Whether gravel is within a mineral reservation is a legal question with real money attached, and it is answered before the contingency expires, not afterwards.

## 4. Involuntary transfer and transfer on death

### 4.1 Foreclosure

Alaska deeds of trust ordinarily permit **non-judicial foreclosure** by the trustee through a power of sale, and judicial foreclosure is also available. The two routes differ in procedure and in what follows the sale, including the availability of a deficiency and any right of redemption.

**A licensee must not state redemption or deficiency rules from memory.** They decide what a buyer would be acquiring and when possession is available, and they come from the title company and counsel in writing.

### 4.2 Tax foreclosure

Boroughs and municipalities that levy property tax foreclose for delinquency on a statutory schedule. A property far enough along the process may not be saleable in the ordinary way, and delinquency on a tax record is something a licensee raises with the title company at listing rather than at closing.

### 4.3 Adverse possession and boundary claims

Occupation that is actual, open, notorious, exclusive, hostile, and continuous for the statutory period can ripen into title. Whether it has is a question only a court answers. A licensee who tells a client that a long-used strip "is theirs now" has given a legal opinion they are not permitted to give and, in Alaska, has also breached the duty to advise the client to obtain expert advice.

### 4.4 Death, probate, and non-probate transfer

Property passes by **will** or by **intestate succession**, and may also pass outside probate — by survivorship, by trust, or by a transfer-on-death deed where the law provides for one.

The licensee's operational question is always the same: **who is authorised to sign, and what document proves it?** Letters for a personal representative, a certificate of trust for a trustee, a death certificate for a survivorship claim. Gather; do not interpret.

### 4.5 Condemnation and escheat

**Eminent domain** takes private property for public use with just compensation, and appears in Alaska around road, utility, and public-project corridors. **Escheat** passes property to the state when an owner dies with no heirs and no will.

#### Checkpoint 4.1

A buyer wants to offer on a property that went through a trustee's sale six weeks ago. What must the licensee establish first, and from whom?

*Answer:* What rights, if any, remain in the former owner, whether any redemption period applies, and what the purchaser at the sale actually holds — from the title company and counsel, in writing. Until that is answered the buyer does not know what they would be buying or when they could occupy. Stating the answer from memory is exactly the error this module exists to prevent.

## Key terms

**Constructive notice** — knowledge the law charges a person with because an instrument is recorded.

**Delivery** — the grantor's act, with present intent to convey, that makes a deed effective.

**Inquiry notice** — knowledge charged because visible circumstances would have prompted investigation.

**Recording district** — Alaska's recording geography, administered by the Department of Natural Resources.

**Special warranty deed** — warrants only against defects arising during the grantor's own ownership.

## Practice problems

**Problem 1.** A grantor signs a deed to her son in 2020, tells him about it, and keeps it in her safe until her death in 2026.

*Working:* Delivery requires present intent to convey, and a deed retained by the grantor generally has not been delivered — but this is a legal question with a large consequence and it belongs with a probate lawyer before anyone lists the house. The licensee's role is to recognise it and route it.

**Problem 2.** A title commitment requires a reconveyance of a deed of trust the seller says was paid in 2012.

*Working:* The reconveyance has to be recorded. A paid loan that was never reconveyed still appears in the chain, and the title company will not insure over it. Recollection is not a document, and obtaining a reconveyance from a lender that has since been acquired takes time the transaction may not have. Raise it at listing.

**Problem 3.** A buyer intends to record their deed "when they get around to it."

*Working:* An unrecorded deed is good between the parties and exposed to a later good-faith purchaser who records first. The licensee explains why recording happens at closing and lets the title company handle it — and does not advise on the legal consequences beyond describing what recording does.

**Problem 4.** The legal description on the purchase agreement does not match the seller's deed.

*Working:* A finding, not a typo, until proven otherwise. It goes to the title company immediately. Marketing and contracting on a description that does not match the record risks a contract for something nobody can identify.

## Module summary

**A deed needs a competent grantor, a named grantee, words of conveyance, a description, a signature, and delivery** — and delivery is what fails.

**Warranty, special warranty, and quitclaim differ in what is promised**, not in what is conveyed.

**Alaska records through recording districts** administered by the Department of Natural Resources; confirm the district rather than assuming it.

**Recording gives notice; delivery gives effect.** Notice comes in three forms, and inquiry notice is why you ask who is living in the house.

**The exceptions schedule is the document that matters.** Read it when it arrives and send it to the client before the contingency date.

**Foreclosure, redemption, deficiency, adverse possession, and probate authority are legal questions.** Recognise, route, and record — and remember that AS 08.88.620(3) makes advising a represented client to get expert advice a duty rather than a courtesy.
