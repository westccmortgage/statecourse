# Alaska Real Estate Salesperson Prelicense - Module 02

## Forms of ownership

**Topic:** 12 AAC 64.063(c)(2)
**Contact hours:** 4
**Course version:** 1.0 submission draft

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module the student will be able to:

- Distinguish the estates in land and state what each gives its holder.
- Distinguish Alaska's forms of concurrent ownership and say what happens to each on the death of an owner.
- Explain what a common interest community is under AS 34.08 and what the resale certificate requires.
- Identify the ownership structures a licensee meets in Alaska practice, including Native corporation land and land held under a federal or state patent, and know when to stop and route the question.
- Identify who is authorised to sign for an entity, a trust, or an estate, and what document proves it.

## 1. Estates in land

### 1.1 Freehold estates

A **fee simple absolute** is the largest interest a person can hold: unlimited in duration, inheritable, and freely transferable. Most Alaska residential transactions convey a fee simple.

A **defeasible fee** ends if a stated event occurs. These appear in older conveyances and in charitable and governmental grants, and their effect is a legal question a licensee routes rather than answers.

A **life estate** lasts for the life of a named person. The life tenant may possess and use the property, must not commit waste, and must maintain it. When the measuring life ends the property passes to a **remainderman**, or reverts to the grantor's estate as a **reversion**.

### 1.2 Leasehold estates

An **estate for years** has a fixed beginning and end and terminates without notice. A **periodic tenancy** runs period to period until notice. A **tenancy at will** exists with permission and no fixed term. A **tenancy at sufferance** is what remains when a tenant stays after the right to possession ends.

Alaska's Uniform Residential Landlord and Tenant Act, AS 34.03, governs residential tenancies and is covered in module 07. What matters here is the ownership consequence: a buyer of tenanted property takes subject to the tenancy unless the lease or the law provides otherwise, so the leases are documents to obtain before an offer, not after.

### 1.3 The bundle of rights

Ownership is a collection of rights — to possess, to use, to exclude, to enjoy, and to dispose. Everything in modules 03 and 04 is about carving pieces off that bundle: a lease takes possession for a term, an easement takes a use, a mortgage or deed of trust takes a security interest, a covenant takes some of the right to use.

### 1.4 The four powers above ownership

Police power, eminent domain, taxation, and escheat sit above every private title. In Alaska the taxation limb is unusual and worth stating now because it surprises people from other states: **there is no statewide property tax on ordinary real property and no state income tax.** Property taxation is a function of boroughs and municipalities, and large parts of Alaska sit in the unorganised borough with no borough property tax at all. Module 04 returns to this.

#### Checkpoint 1.1

A buyer intends to occupy a duplex on closing day. The upper unit has a written lease with five months to run. What does the licensee do?

*Answer:* Obtain the lease before the offer is written and tell the buyer plainly that a tenancy exists and that whether possession can be obtained is a legal question. Under AS 08.88.620(3) a licensee representing the buyer is required to advise them to obtain expert advice on a matter beyond the licensee's expertise, and this is one. Assuring the buyer the tenant "will have to leave" is both wrong and a breach.

## 2. Concurrent ownership in Alaska

### 2.1 Tenancy in common

Two or more owners hold undivided fractional interests, which may be unequal. Each may convey or encumber their own share. On death a share passes by will or intestacy — **not** to the co-owners. This is the ordinary result in Alaska when a conveyance to two or more people does not say otherwise.

### 2.2 Joint tenancy with right of survivorship

Owners hold equal undivided interests and the survivor takes the whole on the death of one. Creating it requires the instrument to say so; survivorship is not presumed from joint purchase.

### 2.3 Tenancy by the entirety

Available to a married couple. Alaska recognises it, and a licensee's operational rules are the same wherever it exists: both spouses sign anything that conveys or encumbers, and a deed signed by one conveys nothing.

Whether a particular Alaska property is held this way, and what that means for a creditor of one spouse, are questions answered by the recorded instrument and by counsel — not by assumption from another state's law.

### 2.4 The licensee's actual job

Read the vesting on the deed, at the listing appointment, and match it to who is going to sign. Three questions answer almost everything:

**Who is on title, and in what capacity?** "John Smith and Mary Smith, husband and wife" is a different problem from "John Smith, a single man" or "The Smith Family Trust".

**Is anyone on title unavailable?** A spouse working on the Slope, an heir in another state, a co-owner who has stopped speaking to the others. Unavailability is a problem to solve before listing, not at closing.

**Does anyone disagree?** Co-owners who cannot agree may end in a partition action, which is a legal proceeding rather than a listing.

#### Checkpoint 2.1

Two unmarried people take a deed reading only "to A and B." A dies. What happens to A's half, and what should the licensee have said at closing?

*Answer:* Absent language creating survivorship the result is a tenancy in common, so A's share passes under A's will or by intestacy rather than to B. What the licensee should have said, before closing, is that the choice exists and that they should get advice on which form they want — AS 08.88.620(3) makes advising them to obtain expert advice a duty, not a courtesy.

## 3. Common interest communities

### 3.1 What AS 34.08 covers

Alaska's Uniform Common Interest Ownership Act, AS 34.08, governs condominiums, planned communities, and cooperatives. A unit owner owns their unit together with an interest in the common elements, and an association administers under a declaration and bylaws.

### 3.2 The resale certificate

**AS 34.08.590** requires, on a resale by a unit owner, that the seller furnish the buyer a **resale certificate** from the association. It is the buyer's window into what they are actually joining, and it covers matters including the assessment position, any unsatisfied judgments or pending suits against the association, the reserve position, insurance, and any known unsatisfied violations.

**For a licensee this is a deadline, not a document.** The certificate comes from the association, associations take time, and a transaction that discovers on day 25 that nobody ordered it is a transaction in trouble. Order it at listing.

### 3.3 What to read in it

Assessments and whether any are delinquent. Special assessments levied, pending, or under discussion. Reserves against the age of the building — a roof at the end of its life with no reserve is a future assessment. Insurance, and what the association covers against what the owner must. Rules that conflict with the buyer's stated purpose — pets, rentals, vehicles, home business.

### 3.4 What a licensee may and may not say

A licensee obtains the documents, delivers them, and identifies the provisions likely to matter given what the client has said they intend to do. Whether a provision is enforceable, and what it means for this buyer, is expert advice under AS 08.88.620(3).

#### Checkpoint 3.1

A buyer wants to rent the condominium out for part of the year, and the declaration contains a rental restriction. What does the licensee do?

*Answer:* Deliver the declaration and the resale certificate, point to the provision, state plainly that whether and how it applies is beyond the licensee's expertise, and advise the buyer to obtain expert advice — which AS 08.88.620(3) requires for a represented buyer. Then make the answer a contingency if the buyer's purpose depends on it, and record that the advice was given.

## 4. Entities, trusts, estates, and Alaska-specific holdings

### 4.1 Entities

Property is often held by a limited liability company, a corporation, a partnership, or a trust. Two questions, every time:

**Who may sign?** An operating agreement, corporate resolution, partnership agreement, or certificate of trust answers it. The licensee obtains the document; counsel and the title company read it.

**Does the entity exist and is it in good standing?** Alaska entity records are searchable through the Division of Corporations, Business and Professional Licensing, and a lapsed registration discovered at closing is an avoidable delay.

### 4.2 Estates and trusts

A property whose owner has died is owned by the estate or by the trust, and the authority to sell comes from letters, a court order, or the trust instrument. A licensee gathers those documents and does not read them for the client. The pattern is the same as everywhere: identify the authority question, route it, record it.

### 4.3 Alaska Native corporation land

Land conveyed under the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act to regional and village corporations is a substantial part of Alaska's land, and it is not ordinary fee land for transactional purposes. Restrictions, corporate authority, and federal law can all bear on whether and how a parcel may be conveyed.

**The licensee's rule here is a hard one: recognise and refer.** A licensee who treats ANCSA land as ordinary fee simple because the deed looks familiar is out of their depth, and AS 08.88.620(3) makes advising the client to obtain expert advice an affirmative duty in exactly this situation.

### 4.4 Federal and state patents, and remote parcels

Much Alaska land traces to a federal or state patent, and patents can carry reservations — minerals, access, easements for public use. Remote and off-road parcels raise access questions that look like title questions and are often neither: legal access, physical access, and seasonal access are three different things, and a buyer who assumes a road exists because a line appears on a map has assumed something a licensee should not confirm.

The professional habit is to ask the three questions separately, get the answers in writing from the sources that hold them, and disclose exactly what was found.

#### Checkpoint 4.1

A buyer is interested in a remote parcel advertised as having "road access." What should the licensee establish?

*Answer:* Whether there is a **legal** right of access — a recorded easement or a public right of way — separately from whether there is a **physical** road, and separately again from whether it is passable year-round. Advertising "road access" without knowing which of the three is true is a representation the licensee cannot support, and the disclosure duty under AS 08.88.615(a)(4) reaches known material information about the physical condition of the real estate.

## Key terms

**Common interest community** — a development under AS 34.08 in which a unit owner holds an interest in common elements and is subject to an association.

**Defeasible fee** — a fee estate that ends on the occurrence of a stated event.

**Remainderman** — the person who takes when a life estate ends.

**Resale certificate** — the association document AS 34.08.590 requires a selling unit owner to furnish a buyer.

**Tenancy in common** — concurrent ownership in undivided shares that pass by will or intestacy rather than to co-owners.

## Practice problems

**Problem 1.** A deed vests title in "The Nome Family Trust, dated 12 March 2014." Who signs the listing?

*Working:* Whoever the trust instrument authorises — normally the trustee, in that capacity. The licensee obtains a certificate of trust or the relevant excerpt and gives it to the title company. Signing as an individual rather than as trustee is a defect that surfaces at closing.

**Problem 2.** A seller says the condominium has "no assessments coming." The reserve study shows a roof at the end of its service life.

*Working:* The seller's statement and the document conflict. The licensee delivers the resale certificate and the reserve information to the buyer and does not repeat the seller's assurance. Whether an assessment is coming is a factual enquiry the licensee can make of the association; what the documents permit the association to do is expert advice.

**Problem 3.** Co-owning siblings disagree about selling. One asks the licensee to list his half.

*Working:* A tenant in common may convey their undivided interest, so something is technically saleable, but a fractional interest with an unwilling co-owner has almost no market and invites a partition action. The honest response is to explain that the remedy is likely a legal one, advise expert advice under AS 08.88.620(3), and decline to market a fractional interest as though it were a house.

**Problem 4.** A buyer asks whether a parcel adjoining Native corporation land can be reached across it.

*Working:* Crossing another owner's land requires a right to do so, and land held by a Native corporation raises questions of corporate authority and federal law on top of ordinary easement analysis. Recognise, refer, and do not describe access the licensee has not verified.

## Module summary

**Fee simple, defeasible fee, and life estate**, with the four leaseholds beneath them; and four government powers above all of it, of which Alaska's taxation limb is borough and municipal rather than statewide.

**Alaska's concurrent forms differ on death.** Tenancy in common passes by will or intestacy; joint tenancy and tenancy by the entirety pass by survivorship; and the deed's vesting language is what decides.

**Read the vesting at the listing appointment** and match it to who will sign.

**AS 34.08 governs common interest communities**, and the AS 34.08.590 resale certificate is a deadline as much as a document. Order it at listing.

**Entities, trusts, and estates all reduce to one question**: who may sign, and what document proves it.

**ANCSA land, patents, and remote access are Alaska's own hazards.** Recognise and refer — and remember that AS 08.88.620(3) makes advising a represented client to obtain expert advice a duty.
