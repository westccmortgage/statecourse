# Alaska Real Estate Salesperson Prelicense - Module 03

## Property law: public and private rights and limitations

**Topic:** 12 AAC 64.063(c)(3)
**Contact hours:** 4
**Course version:** 1.0 submission draft
**Regulatory status:** Not certified. No Alaska prelicense credit is available for this course.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module the student will be able to:

- Distinguish real from personal property and apply the fixture tests to a disputed item.
- Identify the private limitations on title — easements, covenants, licences, encroachments — and how each is created and ended.
- Identify the public limitations — zoning and platting, building regulation, and environmental and coastal controls — and say which body answers which question.
- Explain Alaska's water, access, and subsurface issues well enough to know what to verify before advertising.
- Recognise a limitation that has become a legal question and route it under AS 08.88.620(3).

## 1. Real and personal property

### 1.1 The categories

Real property is land, everything permanently attached to it, and the rights that go with it. Everything else is personal property. The distinction decides how a thing transfers, how it is financed, and how it is taxed.

### 1.2 Fixtures

A fixture is personal property that has become part of the real property, judged by three things together:

**Annexation** — how firmly it is attached, and what removal would damage.
**Adaptation** — whether it was made for or fitted to this property.
**Intention** — inferred from the other two and from the relationship of the parties. This is the controlling factor.

**Trade fixtures** installed by a tenant for their business remain the tenant's and may be removed before the term ends, with the tenant repairing removal damage.

### 1.3 The Alaska items that generate disputes

The general list — window treatments, mounted televisions, appliances, chandeliers — plus several that are specifically Alaskan and that a licensee should raise by name:

| Item | Usual analysis | Why it is disputed |
|---|---|---|
| Fuel oil in the tank | Personal property, ordinarily prorated or purchased at closing | Nobody addresses it until the day |
| Standby generator | Fixture if wired in, personal if portable | The wiring is the question |
| Heat tape and arctic entries | Fixtures | Rarely mentioned, always assumed |
| Wood stove | Depends on installation and flue connection | Sentimental and expensive |
| Snow machine shed or cache | Depends on anchoring and permanence | Skid-mounted is not a fixture |
| Water tank, holding tank, or hauled-water system | Fixture if plumbed | Buyers assume it conveys |
| Shipping container used as storage | Usually personal property | Until it is set on a foundation |

**Fuel is the one to build a habit around.** In much of Alaska the tank is full or nearly empty at closing and it is worth real money. The agreement should say whether the fuel is measured and purchased, prorated, or included, and a licensee who addresses it in every transaction removes an argument that otherwise happens at the walkthrough.

### 1.4 The licensee's job

Walk the property with the seller. Ask what is not staying. List it as excluded in writing. List anything ambiguous that is staying as expressly included. Repeat with the buyer so both lists agree. That is the whole discipline, and it prevents most disputes.

#### Checkpoint 1.1

A seller's oil tank is three-quarters full at closing and the agreement says nothing. What should have happened?

*Answer:* The agreement should have addressed the fuel — measured and purchased at a stated basis, prorated, or expressly included. Fuel is ordinarily personal property and a full tank in an Alaska winter is a significant sum. Silence produces a walkthrough negotiation with no contractual basis, which is the situation a competent licensee prevents at the listing appointment.

## 2. Private limitations

### 2.1 Easements

An easement is a right to use another's land for a specific purpose, and when recorded it binds subsequent owners.

An **easement appurtenant** benefits a parcel — the dominant estate — and burdens another — the servient estate. It runs with the land. An **easement in gross** benefits a person or entity, and utility easements are the everyday example.

**Created** by express grant or reservation, by implication from prior use, by necessity where a division leaves a parcel landlocked, or by prescription from long adverse use. **Ended** by expiry, by merger, by written release, by abandonment, or by the purpose becoming impossible.

Whether a use has ripened into a prescriptive easement is a question only a court answers, and a licensee must not tell a client that it has or has not.

### 2.2 Section-line easements

Alaska carries a limitation that surprises licensees from other states. Historic federal and state law reserved **section-line easements** across many parcels — a strip along section lines dedicated to public access. Their width, their status, and whether they have been vacated vary, and they can sit across land a buyer intends to build on or fence.

**A licensee's obligation is to notice the possibility and route it**, not to state whether one exists or what it permits. The title company, the borough or municipality, and the state are where the answer lives.

### 2.3 Covenants and associations

Recorded covenants limit what owners may do — setbacks, materials, outbuildings, vehicles, rentals, business use. They are private agreements enforced by other owners or by an association, not by government.

A restrictive covenant purporting to limit ownership or occupancy by race, colour, religion, national origin, sex, familial status, or disability is **unlawful and unenforceable**, and a licensee must never treat it as operative, repeat it, or use it in marketing. Module 08 returns to this.

### 2.4 Licences and encroachments

A **licence** is revocable permission and is not an interest in land. An **encroachment** is a physical intrusion over a boundary, found by survey. Both are common, and both are facts the licensee discloses and legal questions the licensee refers.

#### Checkpoint 2.1

A seller says a neighbour "has always driven across the corner, but it's just neighbourly." What does the licensee do?

*Answer:* Record what the seller said, disclose it to the buyer in writing, and route the question of whether the use has become a prescriptive easement — that is beyond the licensee's expertise and AS 08.88.620(3) makes advising a represented client to obtain expert advice a duty. What the licensee must not do is repeat the seller's confidence as though it were the legal position.

## 3. Public limitations

### 3.1 Who regulates land use in Alaska

This is where Alaska differs most from other states, and a licensee who assumes otherwise will be wrong regularly.

**Municipalities and boroughs** regulate land use where they exist and have adopted it. The Municipality of Anchorage, the Fairbanks North Star Borough, the Matanuska-Susitna Borough, the City and Borough of Juneau and others each have their own codes, and they differ.

**The unorganised borough** covers a very large part of Alaska and has **no borough government**, which means no borough zoning and no borough platting authority. In much of the state the answer to "what is this zoned?" is that it is not zoned at all.

**The State of Alaska** regulates in areas the boroughs do not reach, including state land disposal and certain platting functions.

**The practical rule:** find out which jurisdiction the parcel sits in before answering any land-use question, and then ask that jurisdiction. A licensee who applies Anchorage's rules to a Mat-Su parcel, or any municipality's rules to unorganised-borough land, is confidently wrong.

### 3.2 Platting and subdivision

Where a borough or municipality has platting authority, dividing land requires platting approval, and a parcel created without it can be unsaleable or unbuildable. Where there is no platting authority, other rules apply.

A licensee asked to sell "part of" a parcel is looking at a subdivision question that belongs with the platting authority and with counsel before anything is marketed.

### 3.3 Building regulation, permits, and unpermitted work

Building codes and permit requirements are municipal or borough functions, and they are not universal. In some areas there is no building department at all.

Unpermitted work is common and is a disclosure issue: a finished basement, an addition, or a deck built without a permit may need to be brought into compliance where a permitting authority exists. If a licensee learns work was unpermitted, that is known material information about the physical condition and AS 08.88.615(a)(4) reaches it.

### 3.4 Environmental and site controls

**Wetlands** are federally regulated and are widespread in Alaska; a determination can prevent a buyer building where they intended.

**Coastal and shoreline** issues arise on tidewater parcels.

**On-site water and wastewater.** Much Alaska property is on a well and septic, or on a holding tank with hauled water. Requirements and approvals are borough, municipal, or state functions depending on location, and evaluation at sale is a live issue in some jurisdictions and not others.

**Contaminated sites, tanks, and mining history.** Above-ground and underground fuel tanks are widespread and are a genuine environmental exposure. Alaska maintains contaminated-site records, and a licensee who learns of a tank, a spill, or a mining history discloses it.

**Permafrost and ground conditions.** Settlement, frost heave, and thaw are structural facts in much of the state, and a buyer who does not have the ground assessed is taking a risk a licensee should be naming rather than minimising.

#### Checkpoint 3.1

A buyer asks whether they can put a second dwelling on a five-acre parcel outside any borough. What is the answer?

*Answer:* That the licensee does not know and will not guess, and that the first question is which jurisdiction the parcel is in. Outside an organised borough there may be no zoning at all, which is not the same as "anything is permitted" — state law, covenants, plat notes, and access and wastewater approvals can all bear on it. The licensee identifies the jurisdiction, sends the buyer to the bodies that answer, and makes it a contingency if the purchase depends on it.

## 4. Water, access, and what lies beneath

### 4.1 Water rights

Alaska allocates water by **appropriation** administered by the state, not by riparian entitlement, and a right to use water may require a state water right. A parcel bordering a stream is not thereby entitled to take from it in unlimited quantity.

Navigable and public waters carry public rights, and the boundary between private upland and public tideland or submerged land is its own question on coastal and river parcels.

**A licensee should never state that a property "has water rights"** without seeing the instrument or the state record that creates them.

### 4.2 Access

Access is three separate questions, and running them together is how buyers are misled:

**Legal access** — is there a recorded easement, a dedicated right of way, a section-line easement, or a public road?
**Physical access** — does a usable road actually exist on the ground?
**Seasonal access** — is it passable year-round, or only in winter over frozen ground, or only by air or water?

Each is verified from a different source, and all three go into the disclosure. A parcel with legal access and no road is a different product from one with a maintained road, and the buyer's financing may turn on it.

### 4.3 Minerals and the subsurface

Mineral rights are frequently reserved to the state or to a prior owner in Alaska, and a buyer of surface may not be acquiring what lies beneath. Reservations appear in the chain and in patents, and the title company is where they surface.

The licensee's obligation is to notice the possibility, ask, and disclose what is found — never to explain what the mineral owner may do on the surface.

### 4.4 Bringing it together

Nearly every problem in this module resolves the same way. The licensee **verifies before advertising**, **discloses what was found**, **states the source of every figure and claim**, and **routes the legal question**. Alaska adds more things to verify than most states — access, water, permafrost, tanks, section lines, jurisdiction — and the discipline is identical.

#### Checkpoint 4.1

A listing says "year-round access, water rights included." The seller has a driveway and takes water from a creek. What must the licensee establish before that advertisement runs?

*Answer:* Whether there is legal access and by what instrument; whether the road is maintained and by whom; whether it is genuinely passable year-round; and whether a state water right exists or the seller is simply taking water. Until each is verified from its own source, neither claim may be advertised. Repeating the seller's description without verification is a representation the licensee cannot support, and AS 08.88.615(a)(2) requires honest and good faith dealing with everyone assisted.

## Key terms

**Easement appurtenant** — a right benefiting one parcel and burdening another, running with the land.

**Section-line easement** — a public access reservation along section lines affecting many Alaska parcels; existence, width and vacation status must be verified.

**Trade fixture** — an item installed by a tenant for their business, removable before the term ends.

**Unorganised borough** — the part of Alaska with no borough government, and therefore no borough zoning or platting authority.

**Water right** — a state-administered right to appropriate water; not an incident of owning land beside a stream.

## Practice problems

**Problem 1.** A seller wants to take a wood stove that is connected to a masonry flue.

*Working:* Strong annexation and adaptation to this flue point to a fixture, so it conveys unless excluded. The answer is not to argue at the walkthrough but to exclude it expressly in the listing and the purchase agreement, and to say so before photographs are taken.

**Problem 2.** A buyer intends to fence a boundary that a section-line easement may cross.

*Working:* Recognise the possibility, route it to the title company and the platting or municipal authority, and disclose the finding. Do not state whether the easement exists or what it permits. If the fence is central to the purchase, make it a contingency.

**Problem 3.** A parcel is described in an advertisement as "buildable."

*Working:* That is a representation about zoning, platting, wastewater approval, access, and ground conditions all at once, in a state where several of those may have no governing authority at all. A licensee should not make it. Describe what has been verified, name the source, and let the buyer's own enquiries answer the rest.

**Problem 4.** A seller mentions that a fuel tank was removed from the yard "years ago."

*Working:* That is known material information about the physical condition, and AS 08.88.615(a)(4) reaches it. Disclose it in writing, ask what the seller knows about how it was removed and whether the ground was tested, and route the environmental question. Silence here is the kind of omission that becomes a claim.

## Module summary

**Fixtures turn on annexation, adaptation, and intention**, and in Alaska the list of disputed items includes fuel in the tank, generators, heat tape, water systems, and containers.

**Private limitations run with the land** — easements, covenants, licences, encroachments — and Alaska's section-line easements are a limitation licensees from other states do not expect.

**Public land-use authority is not uniform.** Municipalities and boroughs regulate where they exist; the unorganised borough has no borough government, so "unzoned" is a real answer. Identify the jurisdiction before answering anything.

**Water is appropriated, not riparian**, and a water right is an instrument, not an assumption.

**Access is three questions** — legal, physical, and seasonal — and they are verified separately.

**Minerals are frequently reserved**, and the reservation is in the chain.

**Verify, disclose, source, and route.** Alaska adds more to verify; the discipline is the same.
