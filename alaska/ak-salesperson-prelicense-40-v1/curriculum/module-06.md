## 1. The note and the security instrument

### 1.1 Two documents, two jobs

Financed real estate always involves at least two instruments, and students who blur them get the foreclosure questions wrong for the rest of their careers.

**The promissory note** is the debt. It is a personal promise to repay: amount, rate, term, payment schedule, late charges, default terms, prepayment terms. Sue on the note and you are suing a person.

**The security instrument** pledges the property as collateral for that debt. Foreclose the security instrument and you are proceeding against land.

You can have a note with no security (an unsecured loan). You cannot sensibly have a security instrument with no underlying obligation, because there would be nothing to secure.

### 1.2 Mortgage and deed of trust

A **mortgage** has two parties: the **mortgagor** (borrower, who gives the mortgage) and the **mortgagee** (lender, who receives it). Alaska is a **lien theory** jurisdiction in effect — the borrower keeps title and the lender holds a lien. A mortgage in Alaska is foreclosed **judicially**, through AS 09.45.170.

A **deed of trust** has three parties: the **trustor** (borrower), the **beneficiary** (lender), and a **trustee** who holds a power of sale. If the deed of trust says the trustee may sell for condition broken, **AS 34.20.070** lets the trustee execute the trust by sale **without first securing a decree of foreclosure and order of sale from the court**.

**The deed of trust is the ordinary residential security instrument in Alaska**, for the reason section 4 makes clear: it is faster, and it comes with a trade the lender is usually willing to make.

### 1.3 What AS 34.20 says about the paperwork

**AS 34.20.010** — recording an **assignment** of a mortgage is not, by itself, notice of the assignment to the mortgagor, so as to invalidate a payment the mortgagor makes to the original mortgagee. A borrower who keeps paying the original lender because nobody told him the loan was sold is protected.

**AS 34.20.030 and .040** — a mortgage is **discharged of record** when the recorder receives a certificate executed by the mortgagee or its personal representatives or assigns, acknowledged and certified in the manner AS 34.15 prescribes for conveyances, specifying that the mortgage is paid or otherwise satisfied. The certificate is recorded at full length and referenced in the discharge entry.

**AS 34.20.050** provides a penalty for failing to discharge a satisfied mortgage or execute the certificate.

Connect this to module 04: the paid-but-never-reconveyed loan that still sits in the chain of title is exactly this failure, and it is the title commitment requirement that most often delays an Alaska closing.

### 1.4 Clauses inside the instruments

**Acceleration** — on default, the whole balance becomes due at once. Without it, a lender could only sue for missed payments as they fell due.

**Alienation, or due-on-sale** — the balance becomes due if the property is transferred. This is why "just take over my payments" is not a plan; the Garn-St Germain Depository Institutions Act (12 U.S.C. 1701j-3) enforces due-on-sale clauses generally while exempting certain family and estate transfers.

**Prepayment** — whether, and at what cost, the borrower may pay early.

**Subordination** — an agreement moving a lien behind one that would otherwise be junior. It is the mechanism by which a construction lender or a seller carryback steps aside for a permanent first loan.

**Release, partial release, and reconveyance** — how the lien comes off, wholly or as to part of the land. A subdivision developer needs partial releases parcel by parcel.

**Assumption** — a buyer taking on the existing loan. Without a **novation** releasing the seller, the seller stays liable, which sellers regularly assume is not true.

### 1.5 Seller financing

Alaska sees more seller financing than most states, because remote and unconventional property is often difficult to finance conventionally: no comparable sales, no road access, off-grid utilities, a cabin on a lot that no secondary-market underwriter will touch.

Two common structures:

**Purchase money deed of trust** — the buyer takes title and gives the seller a note secured by a deed of trust. Title passes; the seller holds a lien.

**Installment land sale contract (contract for deed)** — the seller keeps legal title and the buyer takes possession, with title passing after payments are complete. **AS 34.70.200(4)** expressly counts an installment land sale contract as a "transfer" for the residential disclosure chapter, so the module 05 disclosure obligation applies.

Both are documents "related to real property," so **AS 08.88.405** permits a licensee to prepare them in the course of licensed work. Both also carry consequences — what happens on default under a contract for deed is a genuinely hard question — so **AS 08.88.620(3)**'s duty to advise a represented person to seek expert advice is squarely engaged. Prepare the instrument; recommend counsel; note it in the file.

**Checkpoint 1.** A buyer says he will "assume the seller's loan and take over payments." Name the clause that stands in his way and the doctrine that would release the seller.

## 2. Loan products, AHFC, and who may originate

### 2.1 The conventional market

A **conventional** loan is one not insured or guaranteed by a federal agency. Conforming loans meet Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac limits and guidelines; non-conforming loans exceed them (jumbo) or fail a guideline for another reason.

**Private mortgage insurance** is customarily required above 80 percent loan-to-value on a conventional loan. The federal Homeowners Protection Act (12 U.S.C. 4901 et seq.) governs PMI cancellation and automatic termination on qualifying residential mortgages.

### 2.2 The government programmes

**FHA** — insured by the Federal Housing Administration under 24 C.F.R. parts 200 and 203. Lower down payment, more permissive credit, with an upfront and an annual mortgage insurance premium. Property condition standards apply, which matters in Alaska where a great deal of housing is older, off-grid, or unconventional.

**VA** — guaranteed under 38 U.S.C. chapter 37 and 38 C.F.R. part 36. Available to eligible veterans and service members; no down payment in many cases; a funding fee with statutory exemptions.

**USDA Rural Development** — 7 C.F.R. part 3555. Income-limited, area-eligible. Much of Alaska is eligible territory, so this programme is more relevant here than in a mostly urban state.

**Alaska state veterans' loans** — Alaska runs its own veterans' housing loan programme under **AS 26.15**, and **AS 18.56.010(d)** records the legislature's finding that the programme's continuation is essential to the state's housing supply, with the Alaska Housing Finance Corporation participating in purchasing and insuring those loans.

### 2.3 The Alaska Housing Finance Corporation

**AS 18.56.020:** "The Alaska Housing Finance Corporation is a public corporation and government instrumentality within the Department of Revenue, but having a legal existence independent of and separate from the state."

**AS 18.56.010** states why it exists: the legislature found a serious shortage of decent, safe, and sanitary housing at prices lower- and moderate-income Alaskans can afford, and found that private enterprise had not met it without assistance — including in "remote, underdeveloped, or blighted areas" where housing is necessary to economic growth. AHFC was created to assist in acquiring and developing land and in the construction, rehabilitation, financing, management, maintenance, sale, and rental of dwelling units for those persons and places, and to purchase insured and uninsured mortgage loans.

For a licensee, the working point is that **AHFC is a major purchaser and programme sponsor in the Alaska mortgage market**, with programmes aimed at first-time buyers, rural and remote borrowers, veterans, energy efficiency, and interest-rate reduction.

**A caution this course states plainly:** AHFC's specific programme names, income limits, purchase price limits, rate structures, and eligibility rules change. This course teaches that AHFC exists, what its statutory purpose is, and that it matters to Alaska buyers. **It does not state current programme terms, because a course that did would be teaching last year's numbers.** Check AHFC's current published programme materials for any live transaction, and never quote a rate or a limit from memory to a client.

### 2.4 Who may originate a loan

Recommending financing generally is part of a licensee's work. **Originating a mortgage loan is a separately licensed activity.**

**AS 06.60.010(a):** unless exempt under AS 06.60.015, a person may not operate in Alaska as a **mortgage lender or mortgage broker** with respect to a dwelling located in the state unless licensed under AS 06.60, and must register with the nationwide registry and maintain a **unique identifier**.

**AS 06.60.012(a):** unless exempt, an individual may not operate as a **mortgage loan originator** with respect to a dwelling in the state unless licensed as one. Such an individual must register with the registry, hold a unique identifier, work under an exclusive contract for or as an employee of a mortgage licensee or a registered depository institution, and be **sponsored in the registry**.

**AS 06.60.013(a):** an **independent contractor** loan processor or underwriter must be licensed as a mortgage loan originator. A processor or underwriter who is not an independent contractor is exempt **only if** the individual does not represent to the public — by advertising, business cards, stationery, brochures, signs, rate lists, promotional items, or the Internet — that the individual can or will perform mortgage loan originator activities.

So: explain loan types, refer to lenders, and help a client assemble what a lender asks for. **Do not take a loan application, do not negotiate loan terms, and do not offer or negotiate rates**, unless you hold the separate licence. Referral compensation is also constrained — RESPA section 8 in module 08, and **AS 08.88.401(b)** with **12 AAC 64.130(7)** on your own side of the line.

## 3. Loan arithmetic

A licensee is not an underwriter. A licensee who cannot check an underwriter's arithmetic is, however, useless to a client at the moment it matters.

### 3.1 Simple interest

Interest for a period = **principal × annual rate × time in years**.

*Example.* $310,000 at 6.5 percent for one month:
$310,000 × 0.065 = $20,150 per year ÷ 12 = **$1,679.17** interest in the first month.

### 3.2 Amortisation

In a fully amortising loan, each payment covers the interest accrued and applies the remainder to principal. Early payments are mostly interest; late payments are mostly principal.

*Example, continuing.* Payment of principal and interest is $1,959.65.

| | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Payment | $1,959.65 |
| Interest, month 1 | $1,679.17 |
| Principal, month 1 | **$280.48** |
| Balance after month 1 | **$309,719.52** |

Month 2 interest: $309,719.52 × 0.065 ÷ 12 = **$1,677.65**, so principal rises to $282.00. The shift is slow, and showing a client this table is the honest answer to "how much will I owe in five years."

### 3.3 Loan-to-value

**LTV = loan ÷ the lesser of price or appraised value.**

*Example.* Price $385,000, appraisal $372,000, loan $330,000.
LTV = 330,000 ÷ 372,000 = **88.7 percent** — computed on the appraisal, because it is lower. The buyer needs $55,000 down against a $385,000 price, not $38,500, and the deal now depends on whether the contract said what happens on a low appraisal. That is module 05's appraisal contingency, and this is why it has to have a date and a consequence.

### 3.4 Points and buydowns

One **point** is one percent of the **loan amount** — never of the price.

*Example.* $330,000 loan, 1.5 points = $4,950.

Discount points buy a lower rate. Whether they are worth it is a break-even calculation: divide the cost by the monthly saving.

*Example.* $4,950 buys a payment reduction of $92 a month. 4,950 ÷ 92 = **53.8 months**, about four and a half years. A buyer planning to sell in three years is paying for a benefit they will not collect.

### 3.5 Qualifying ratios

**Front-end (housing) ratio** = PITI ÷ gross monthly income, where PITI is principal, interest, taxes, and insurance — plus association dues where they apply.

**Back-end (total debt) ratio** = (PITI + other recurring monthly debt) ÷ gross monthly income.

*Example.* Gross income $7,400/month. PITI $2,110. Car $460, student loan $215, card minimums $95.
Front: 2,110 ÷ 7,400 = **28.5 percent**.
Back: (2,110 + 770) ÷ 7,400 = 2,880 ÷ 7,400 = **38.9 percent**.

Programme guidelines vary and change. Use ratios to tell a client where they stand and what would move them; **do not tell a client they are approved.** Only the lender approves.

### 3.6 Interest rate, APR, and disclosure

The **note rate** prices the loan. The **annual percentage rate** expresses the cost including certain finance charges, and it is a disclosure concept under the federal Truth in Lending Act (15 U.S.C. 1601 et seq.) and Regulation Z (12 C.F.R. part 1026).

Advertising a rate can trigger Regulation Z's trigger-term rules: state a trigger term — such as the down payment amount, the payment amount, the number of payments, the term, or the finance charge — and the required additional disclosures must accompany it. **The practical rule for a licensee is not to advertise financing terms at all**, because the disclosure obligation belongs with a lender who is set up to meet it. Layer on **12 AAC 64.130(8)**, which requires the broker's registered business name in every advertisement, and the safe habit is clear.

**Checkpoint 3.** Price $420,000, appraisal $409,000, loan $360,000. Compute the LTV and say which figure you used and why.

## 4. Default and Alaska's two foreclosure routes

This is where Alaska law differs sharply from the general picture, and the difference is worth memorising.

### 4.1 The trustee's sale — AS 34.20.070

If a deed of trust conveys Alaska real property to a trustee as security and provides that the trustee may sell for condition broken, the trustee may execute the trust **by sale, without first obtaining a court decree**, provided the notice requirements of AS 34.20.070(b) are met.

**The notice timetable.** Not less than **30 days after the default**, and not less than **90 days before the sale**, the trustee records a **notice of default** in the recording district where the property lies. AS 34.20.070(b) lists what it must contain: the trustor's name; the book and page or serial number of the trust deed; a description of the property including its street address if it has one; a statement that a breach has occurred; the nature of the breach; the sum owing; the trustee's election to sell; the **date, time, and place of sale**; and the statement described in (e) explaining how the default may be cured. An inaccuracy in the street address may not be used to set aside the sale if the legal description is correct.

**Who gets mailed notice.** Within **10 days** after recording the notice of default, the trustee mails a copy by **certified mail** to the last known address of: the trustor; the trustor's successor in interest whose interest appears of record or of which the trustee or beneficiary has actual notice or who is in actual physical possession; any other person actually in physical possession; and any person holding a lien or interest subsequent to the trustee's, where it appears of record or the trustee or beneficiary has actual notice — AS 34.20.070(c). Personal delivery may be used instead of mail.

**Cure.** At any time before the stated sale date, or the date to which the sale is postponed, a default arising from missed payments may be cured and the sale terminated by paying the **sum then in default** — not the principal that would not yet be due but for the default — plus the attorney and other foreclosure fees and costs actually incurred. Alaska attaches a limit: if notice of default has been recorded **two or more times previously** under the same trust deed and the default was cured each time, the trustee **may elect to refuse payment and continue the sale**.

**The sale.** AS 34.20.080(a): at **public auction at the front door of a superior court courthouse in the judicial district** where the property is located, unless the deed of trust specifies a different place — and the trustee may also accept bids by telephone, Internet, and email if reasonable steps ensure the methods are fair, accessible, and produce immediately available money. Sale is to the highest and best bidder, and the beneficiary may bid. Proceeds go into a **trust account** until disbursed. A sale may be postponed, each postponement announced publicly, but **not more than 12 months** without new notice.

**Distribution.** AS 34.20.080(f), in order: to the foreclosing beneficiary until paid in full; then to holders of recorded interests (excluding easements) subordinate to the foreclosed deed of trust, in order of priority; then to the trustor, or to the trustor's successor in interest of record if the trustor no longer owns at the time of sale.

### 4.2 The two consequences that define Alaska practice

**No redemption after a trustee's sale.** **AS 34.20.090(a):** the sale transfers all title and interest the trustor had, and the trustor "and the heirs or assigns of that party have **no right or privilege to redeem** the property, **unless the deed of trust so declares**." The default is no redemption. Read the instrument before saying otherwise.

**No deficiency after a trustee's sale.** **AS 34.20.100:** when a sale is made by a trustee under a deed of trust as authorised by AS 34.20.070 – 34.20.130, "no other or further action or proceeding may be taken nor judgment entered against the maker or the surety or guarantor of the maker, on the obligation secured by the deed of trust **for a deficiency**."

So the trade is explicit. The lender gets a fast sale with no court decree and no redemption period clouding the buyer's title. The borrower gets a hard stop: the debt ends at the sale, whatever the property brought.

**And the election is binding.** AS 34.20.070(a) closes with: "If the deed of trust is foreclosed judicially or the note secured by the deed of trust is sued on and a judgment is obtained by the beneficiary, **the beneficiary may not exercise the nonjudicial remedies described in this section.**" A lender cannot sue on the note, take a judgment, and then run a trustee's sale as well.

### 4.3 The judicial route — AS 09.45.170

**AS 09.45.170:** a person holding a lien on real property other than a judgment, whether created by mortgage or otherwise, may bring an action to foreclose. The court may direct sale and application of proceeds to costs, expenses of sale, and the amount due — and "the judgment shall also **determine the personal liability of a defendant** for the payment of the debt secured by the lien and be entered accordingly."

**AS 09.45.180:** the sale is conducted in the same manner as a sale of real property on execution, and **"a deficiency between the amount of the judgment and the sale price may be enforced by execution."**

**AS 09.45.190:** property sold on a judgment of foreclosure **may be redeemed** in the manner and with the effect of real property sold on execution.

**AS 09.35.250** supplies that period: the judgment debtor or a successor may redeem before confirmation of sale on paying the purchase money with interest at **eight percent a year** from the date of sale, plus taxes and certain expenses the purchaser or redemptioner paid after purchase — and if the debtor does not redeem before confirmation, the debtor may redeem **only within 12 months from the order of confirmation**.

**AS 09.45.200** adds a sequencing rule: during or after an action to recover the debt, a foreclosure action cannot be maintained unless judgment is given in that action that the plaintiff recover the debt or part of it **and** an execution issued against the defendant's property is returned unsatisfied in whole or in part.

### 4.4 The comparison, in one table

| | Trustee's sale (deed of trust) | Judicial foreclosure (mortgage or lien) |
|---|---|---|
| Authority | AS 34.20.070 – .130 | AS 09.45.170 – .220 |
| Court decree needed | No | Yes |
| Notice | Notice of default recorded ≥30 days after default and ≥90 days before sale; certified mail within 10 days | Court process; execution-sale notice under AS 09.35.140 |
| Sale | Public auction at superior court front door, AS 34.20.080(a) | As on execution |
| Redemption | **None, unless the deed of trust declares it** — AS 34.20.090(a) | Before confirmation, or **within 12 months of confirmation** — AS 09.35.250 |
| Deficiency | **Prohibited** — AS 34.20.100 | **Enforceable by execution** — AS 09.45.180 |

### 4.5 What this means at the listing table

A seller in default has a dated timetable, a cure right, and a choice worth understanding. A licensee's role is to know that the timetable exists, to know that recording the notice of default starts a 90-day minimum clock, and to get the seller in front of a lawyer and a lender quickly — because the difference between a short sale negotiated in month one and a trustee's sale in month four is measured in the seller's whole equity.

Alternatives a licensee should be able to name: **reinstatement** (curing under AS 34.20.070(b)), **loan modification**, **forbearance**, **short sale** (lender accepts less than the balance to release the lien), and **deed in lieu of foreclosure** (which does not extinguish junior liens and therefore is not always available).

**Never** tell a distressed seller what their deficiency exposure will be. The answer depends on which instrument secures the loan, whether the lender proceeds under AS 34.20 or AS 09.45, whether there is a second lien, and federal tax consequences. That is AS 08.88.620(3) in its purest form.

**Checkpoint 4.** A lender holding a deed of trust sues on the note and obtains a judgment. May it then conduct a trustee's sale? Cite the provision.

## 5. The settlement statement and prorations

### 5.1 Debits and credits

A settlement statement is a two-column account for each party. A **debit** is a charge to that party; a **credit** is an amount in that party's favour. The buyer's debits less credits is cash to close; the seller's credits less debits is proceeds.

Standard placements:

| Item | Buyer | Seller |
|---|---|---|
| Purchase price | Debit | Credit |
| Earnest money already deposited | Credit | — |
| New loan proceeds | Credit | — |
| Payoff of seller's existing loan | — | Debit |
| Brokerage compensation as agreed | Per contract | Per contract |
| Prepaid taxes covering time after closing | Debit | Credit |
| Unpaid taxes covering time before closing | Credit | Debit |
| Fuel remaining in the tank | Debit | Credit |

**Fuel is on that list because Alaska is Alaska.** A 250-gallon heating oil tank two-thirds full at closing is a real number, it is measured and priced at closing, and a contract that says nothing about it produces an argument on closing day. Say in the contract how it is measured and how it is priced.

### 5.2 Federal disclosure

For most closed-end consumer credit secured by real property, the **Loan Estimate** and **Closing Disclosure** required by Regulation Z (12 C.F.R. 1026.19(e) and (f)) and Regulation X (12 C.F.R. part 1024) replace the older HUD-1 form. **The Closing Disclosure must be received by the consumer at least three business days before consummation**, and certain changes restart that period. Building the three-day rule into your closing calendar is not optional; it is how closings actually get delayed.

**12 AAC 64.130(4)(C)** makes failing to disclose information as required in 12 AAC 64.940 grounds for revocation or suspension in connection with **the settlement statement**, alongside the listing contract. The settlement statement is a licence-law document in Alaska, not merely a lender document.

### 5.3 Prorations

To prorate is to divide a shared expense at the closing date. Three decisions must be settled before you compute anything:

1. **Which items** are prorated — taxes, association assessments, rents, fuel, water and sewer, prepaid insurance where assigned.
2. **Who owns the day of closing.** State it; different regions assume differently.
3. **What year convention** applies — a 360-day banker's year of twelve 30-day months, or actual days in an actual year. **Use what the contract says**, and if it says nothing, use what the closing agent uses and confirm it with both parties in advance.

*Worked example — property tax, 365-day method.* Annual tax $4,380, paid in arrears by the buyer after closing. Closing 12 September. Seller owns the day of closing.

Daily rate = 4,380 ÷ 365 = **$12.00**.
Days 1 January through 12 September = 31+28+31+30+31+30+31+31+12 = **255 days**.
Seller's share = 255 × 12.00 = **$3,060.00**.
Because the buyer will pay the full year's bill, the seller is **debited $3,060.00** and the buyer **credited $3,060.00**.

*Worked example — the same on a 360-day year.* Daily rate = 4,380 ÷ 360 = $12.1667. Months January–August = 8 × 30 = 240, plus 12 = **252 days**. Seller's share = 252 × 12.1667 = **$3,066.00**. A $6 difference here, larger on bigger numbers — which is why the convention is a term to agree, not a detail to assume.

*Worked example — rent received in advance.* Monthly rent $1,850 collected by the seller on 1 October. Closing 18 October; buyer owns from 19 October.
Days remaining, 19–31 October = **13 days**. Daily = 1,850 ÷ 31 = $59.677. Buyer's share = 13 × 59.677 = **$775.81**, credited to the buyer and debited to the seller. Any **security deposit** transfers to the buyer in full — it was never the seller's money, and module 07 covers the landlord-tenant rules that govern it.

*Worked example — association assessment.* Quarterly assessment $690 paid in advance on 1 July, closing 5 August, 92 days in the quarter.
Days remaining, 6 August through 30 September = 26 + 30 = **56 days**. Daily = 690 ÷ 92 = $7.50. Buyer's share = 56 × 7.50 = **$420.00**, credited to the seller, debited to the buyer, because the seller prepaid the buyer's period. Cross-check this figure against the AS 34.08.590 resale certificate from module 05 — the certificate states the assessment and any unpaid amounts, and the two should agree.

*Worked example — heating fuel.* Tank gauged at 176 gallons at closing, agreed price $4.42 per gallon.
176 × 4.42 = **$777.92**, credited to the seller and debited to the buyer.

### 5.4 Checking the statement

A licensee should verify, before closing: the price and earnest money match the contract as amended; every proration uses the agreed convention and the agreed closing-day ownership; the brokerage compensation matches the written agreement; the payoff matches the lender's demand; and no charge appears that no document supports. Find the error the day before closing, not on it.

**Checkpoint 5.** Annual tax $5,110, closing 22 April, taxes paid in arrears by the buyer, seller owns the day of closing, 365-day convention. Compute the seller's share and say who is debited.

## 6. Trust accounting arithmetic

Module 05 covered the trust account rules. This section covers doing the arithmetic they require. The two are deliberately separate: the rules are law, and the arithmetic is a skill that has to be practised.

### 6.1 The three-way reconciliation

**12 AAC 64.220(f)** requires a broker to reconcile trust accounts **monthly** by:

1. reconciling the **bank statement** with the **check register**;
2. reconciling the monthly deposits and expenditures with the **independent transaction ledger for each transaction**; and
3. **cross-checking the bank balance with the sum of the transaction ledgers**.

Step 3 is the one that catches theft and error. Steps 1 and 2 can both balance while money has been moved between transactions; only the cross-check against the sum of the ledgers exposes it.

*Worked example.*

| | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Bank statement ending balance | $84,310.00 |
| Less deposits in transit | ($6,000.00) |
| Plus outstanding checks | $1,190.00 |
| **Adjusted bank balance** | **$79,500.00** |

Sum of transaction ledgers:

| Transaction code | Held |
|---|---:|
| 26-0311 | $12,000.00 |
| 26-0318 | $25,000.00 |
| 26-0322 | $7,500.00 |
| 26-0329 | $15,000.00 |
| 26-0335 | $19,900.00 |
| Broker's maintenance deposit, 12 AAC 64.180(d) | $100.00 |
| **Total** | **$79,500.00** |

The three agree. Note the $100: it is the **only** broker money permitted in the account, it is capped at $100 on deposit, and it must have its own ledger under **12 AAC 64.220(c)**.

Suppose instead the ledgers summed to $81,000 against an adjusted bank balance of $79,500. There is a **$1,500 shortage** — money the ledgers say is held that the bank does not have. That is a shortage in a trust account, and under **12 AAC 64.260** failing to comply with the trust account rules "is considered fraudulent and dishonest conduct" within the meaning of AS 08.88.071(a)(3)(A)(iv). It is found, traced, and cured immediately.

### 6.2 The deposit clock

**12 AAC 64.200:** money must be deposited in or mailed to the trust account **within five days following receipt**, unless geographical location, weather conditions, or transportation facilities make it impossible or unreasonable.

*Worked example.* Earnest money received Thursday 4 June in a community off the road system. Weather closes the airstrip Friday through Monday. Mailed Tuesday 9 June, the first day mail moved. Five days has passed, and the exception applies on its terms — **and the file records the dates and the reason**. An exception you cannot evidence is an exception you did not have.

### 6.3 Commission arithmetic

*Worked example.* Sale price $412,000. Total compensation per the written agreements, 5.5 percent, split evenly between the listing and selling brokerages. The listing salesperson's agreement with her broker is 60/40 in her favour.

Total = 412,000 × 0.055 = **$22,660.00**.
Listing brokerage = **$11,330.00**.
Salesperson = 11,330 × 0.60 = **$6,798.00**; broker retains **$4,532.00**.

Now the rules that constrain the payment:

**12 AAC 64.250(1):** the broker may **not pay a salesperson's commission directly from the trust account**. The brokerage's share moves to the operating account first, and the salesperson is paid from there.

**12 AAC 64.240(a):** each withdrawal of a broker's commission from a trust account must be **separate and identified as to the specific transaction** — not a lump sweep of the month's earnings.

**12 AAC 64.240(b):** the broker must withdraw the commission **within 15 days** after the transaction closes or is otherwise settled.

**AS 08.88.331:** the salesperson never held the money in the first place; anything collected in trust goes immediately to the broker.

**12 AAC 64.940(a):** in a sales transaction, the broker must **disclose in writing to the broker's principal** the dollar amount or percentage of any rebate, compensation, or fee **paid to another broker** in connection with the transaction — though not a franchise fee, internal office operating cost, or compensation to a licensee within the broker's own office, per 12 AAC 64.940(b).

### 6.4 Cooperating brokers and out-of-state licensees

**12 AAC 64.240(d):** an Alaska broker **may** pay a commission to a person not licensed under AS 08.88 who **is licensed to perform real estate activities in another jurisdiction**, for assistance in performing an act requiring an Alaska licence — but the Alaska broker must obtain and **retain for three years** (1) a **written request** from that person for payment, and (2) a copy of that person's **active, current real estate licence** from their jurisdiction.

**12 AAC 64.240(e):** subject to AS 08.88.401(b), an Alaska broker may pay a fee or commission directly to an associate broker or salesperson licensed in Alaska, or to such a person doing business as a legal entity organised and validly existing under Alaska law, **provided the entity is owned by an associate broker or salesperson employed by that broker**.

**12 AAC 64.130(7):** paying referral fees, sharing commissions, or otherwise compensating a person **prohibited from receiving compensation under AS 08.88.401(b)** is grounds for revocation or suspension.

### 6.5 Property management funds

**12 AAC 64.220(b)** requires, for each property management contract, a transaction code identified with a specific landlord or property and an accompanying ledger tracking funds received and disbursed for the landlord — **and each tenant's funds tracked through a related but separate transaction code and ledger.**

**AS 08.88.351(b)(4) and (5)** govern community association funds: prompt deposit into a reserve or operating account, transfer of operating-account money exceeding three months' budgeted expenditures to reserves as soon as practicable, and **no commingling** of one association's funds with another's or with the licensee's.

**12 AAC 64.240(c):** the property management fee is disbursed from the trust account on a **regular monthly basis**, after collection of that month's receipts and disbursement of that month's expenses are complete.

**Checkpoint 6.** Your broker's adjusted bank balance is $61,400 and the transaction ledgers sum to $61,850. State what has happened, what it is called, and which regulation characterises the failure.

## Key terms

**Adjusted bank balance** — the bank statement balance corrected for deposits in transit and outstanding checks; step one of the three-way reconciliation.

**Deficiency** — the shortfall between the debt and the sale price. Prohibited after an Alaska trustee's sale (AS 34.20.100); enforceable by execution after judicial foreclosure (AS 09.45.180).

**Notice of default** — the instrument recorded under AS 34.20.070(b), not less than 30 days after default and not less than 90 days before sale.

**Point** — one percent of the loan amount.

**Redemption** — the right to reclaim after sale. None after an Alaska trustee's sale unless the deed of trust declares it; twelve months from confirmation after judicial foreclosure.

**Three-way reconciliation** — bank statement to check register, deposits and expenditures to each transaction ledger, and bank balance to the sum of the ledgers.

## Practice problems

**Problem 1.** Loan $268,000 at 7.125 percent, monthly principal and interest $1,805.42. Compute month one's split and the balance after the first payment.

*Working:* Interest = 268,000 × 0.07125 ÷ 12 = **$1,591.25**. Principal = 1,805.42 − 1,591.25 = **$214.17**. Balance = 268,000 − 214.17 = **$267,785.83**.

**Problem 2.** A seller in default asks whether the lender can come after her for the shortfall. The loan is secured by a deed of trust and the trustee has recorded a notice of default.

*Working:* If the sale proceeds as a trustee's sale under AS 34.20.070, AS 34.20.100 bars any further action or judgment against the maker, surety, or guarantor for a deficiency. But whether the lender proceeds that way, whether a second lien exists, and what the tax consequences are, are legal and tax questions. Tell her the trustee's-sale timetable exists and that she has a cure right under AS 34.20.070(b) until the sale date. Then apply AS 08.88.620(3) and get her to a lawyer — do not answer the deficiency question yourself.

**Problem 3.** Closing 3 March. Annual tax $6,205, paid in arrears by the buyer. Seller owns the day of closing. 365-day convention.

*Working:* Daily = 6,205 ÷ 365 = **$17.00**. Days 1 January–3 March = 31 + 28 + 3 = **62**. Seller's share = 62 × 17.00 = **$1,054.00**, **debited to the seller and credited to the buyer**, because the buyer will pay the whole bill.

**Problem 4.** A buyer wants to buy down the rate with 2 points on a $295,000 loan, saving $118 per month. He expects to sell in four years.

*Working:* Cost = 295,000 × 0.02 = **$5,900**. Break-even = 5,900 ÷ 118 = **50 months**, about 4 years 2 months. He would sell just before recovering the cost. Present the arithmetic; the decision is his and the loan terms are the lender's to set.

**Problem 5.** A broker sweeps $9,400 of earned commissions from the trust account in one withdrawal covering four closed transactions, and pays two salespeople from the same account the same day.

*Working:* Two violations at least. **12 AAC 64.240(a)** requires each commission withdrawal to be separate and identified to a specific transaction — a lump sweep is not. **12 AAC 64.250(1)** prohibits paying a salesperson's commission directly from the trust account. Under 12 AAC 64.260, failure to comply with 12 AAC 64.180 – 64.271 is treated as fraudulent and dishonest conduct within AS 08.88.071(a)(3)(A)(iv).

**Problem 6.** A deed of trust is silent on redemption. The property sells at a trustee's sale for $40,000 less than the debt. What are the trustor's redemption right and the lender's deficiency right?

*Working:* **No redemption** — AS 34.20.090(a) says the trustor has no right or privilege to redeem unless the deed of trust so declares, and this one is silent. **No deficiency** — AS 34.20.100 bars any further action or judgment on the secured obligation for a deficiency after a trustee's sale. The lender absorbs the $40,000.

## Module summary

**The note is the debt; the security instrument is the collateral.** Alaska residential lending runs mostly on the deed of trust.

**AHFC exists under AS 18.56 and matters to Alaska buyers** — and its current programme terms must be read from AHFC's current materials, never quoted from a course.

**Originating a mortgage loan is separately licensed** under AS 06.60. Explain and refer; do not take applications or negotiate terms.

**A licensee checks the lender's arithmetic and never announces approval.**

**Alaska's two foreclosure routes trade off exactly.** Trustee's sale: fast, no court, **no redemption unless the deed of trust says so**, **no deficiency**. Judicial: slower, **deficiency by execution**, **redemption within 12 months of confirmation**. And a beneficiary who sues on the note and takes judgment cannot then use the nonjudicial remedy.

**Prorations depend on three agreed decisions** — which items, who owns the closing day, and which year convention — before any arithmetic starts. In Alaska, add the fuel in the tank.

**The three-way reconciliation is monthly and non-negotiable**, and the cross-check against the sum of the ledgers is the step that finds what the other two miss.
