## Overview

The interests a person may hold in Oklahoma land, and the three ways those interests are described in
writing. This module covers freehold estates and their defeasible forms, life estates and the future
interests that follow them, encumbrances of both kinds, the forms of concurrent and common-interest
ownership, and the rectangular survey system — which matters more in Oklahoma than in most states,
because most Oklahoma land outside the platted towns is described by it.

## Learning objectives

1. Classify an estate in land by duration and identify a defeasible fee from its granting language.
2. Distinguish a life estate from a fee, name the future interests that follow it, and explain waste.
3. State why a life tenant cannot convey a fee and what a licensee does about it at listing.
4. Distinguish easement appurtenant, easement in gross, licence and encroachment.
5. Name the four government powers and identify which is operating in a fact pattern.
6. Compare the forms of concurrent ownership and state what happens at a death under each.
7. Explain what a condominium owner owns and how that differs from a cooperative.
8. Read a rectangular-survey description and compute the acreage it describes.
9. Recognise a metes-and-bounds description and a lot-and-block description.
10. State why a licensee never supplies a legal description from memory or a tax record.
11. Identify a severed mineral estate as a title question and route it.
12. Compute area problems in square feet and acres.

## Hour 6 — Government powers and freehold estates

### 6.1 Four powers — PETE

**Police power.** Regulation for public health, safety, morals and general welfare — zoning, building
codes, subdivision regulation, environmental controls, licensing. Exercised **without compensation**,
which is the point: an owner whose property is downzoned and loses value is generally not entitled to
be paid, because the government has regulated rather than taken.

**Eminent domain.** The power to take private property for public use, exercised through
**condemnation** and requiring **just compensation**. A partial taking compensates for the part taken
and for damage to the remainder.

**Taxation.** The power to charge against property. The real estate tax lien customarily takes priority
over other liens regardless of recording order.

**Escheat.** Reversion to the state when an owner dies leaving no heirs and no will, so that land never
becomes ownerless.

### 6.2 Estates

An **estate** is the degree, quantity, nature and extent of a person's interest in land. **Freehold**
estates are of indefinite duration and involve ownership; **leasehold** estates are of determinable
duration and involve possession without ownership — Module OK-16's business.

**Fee simple absolute** is the greatest interest the law knows: perpetual, freely transferable in life
and at death, subject only to the four powers and to whatever encumbrances burden it.

### 6.3 Defeasible fees

**Fee simple determinable.** Created by durational language — *so long as, while, during, until*. The
estate **ends automatically** on the stated event and the grantor holds a **possibility of reverter**.

**Fee simple subject to a condition subsequent.** Created by conditional language — *provided that, on
condition that, but if*. The estate **does not end automatically**; the grantor holds a **right of
re-entry** and must act.

These are drafted and litigated by counsel. **A licensee's job is to recognise the language in a title
commitment and route it**, because a defeasible fee is a genuine limitation on what a buyer is
acquiring and it will not announce itself in the listing.

## Hour 7 — Life estates, future interests, waste

### 7.1 The life estate

A freehold estate measured by a lifetime. The **life tenant** may possess, use and take the profits for
the measuring life, and has no right at all after it.

Usually measured by the life tenant's own life. Where measured by another's it is an estate **pur autre
vie** — "for the life of another" — and it can outlive its holder, passing to their estate until the
measuring life ends.

### 7.2 What follows

- Back to the **grantor** or the grantor's successors: a **reversion**.
- To a named **third party**: a **remainder**, and that party is the **remainderman**.

Both are **future interests** — presently existing property rights whose enjoyment is postponed. **A
remainderman owns something today**, which is precisely why a life tenant cannot sell the fee.

### 7.3 Waste

A life tenant must not commit **waste** — must not damage or diminish the property to the injury of the
remainderman. The life tenant is generally responsible for ordinary maintenance, taxes and interest on
encumbrances; permanent structural changes and destruction are waste.

**The practical consequence is immediate and severe: a life tenant cannot convey a fee simple.** A sale
of the fee requires the remainderman to join. A licensee who takes a listing without establishing who
must sign has built a transaction on a defect that surfaces at the closing table.

The determination is the title company's and counsel's. **The noticing is the licensee's**, and it
happens at the listing appointment, in the form of one question: *"Who else has to sign?"*

## Hour 8 — Encumbrances I: easements, licences, encroachments

### 8.1 Easements

An **easement** is a right to use another's land for a particular purpose.

**Easement appurtenant.** Benefits a **parcel**. It has a **dominant tenement** — benefited — and a
**servient tenement** — burdened — and it **runs with the land**, passing automatically to each new
owner of the dominant tenement. This is the kind that matters most in residential practice, because it
transfers whether or not anyone mentions it.

**Easement in gross.** Benefits a **person or entity** rather than a parcel. There is a servient
tenement but no dominant one. Utility easements are the standard case, and commercial easements in
gross are generally assignable.

**In Oklahoma the utility and pipeline easement deserves particular attention.** Gathering lines,
transmission lines and access easements cross a great deal of Oklahoma land, and they carry rights of
access, of surface use and sometimes of future construction that a buyer will not expect from looking
at the ground. Every one of them is a title-work question routed to the title company and counsel.

**Creation:** by express grant or reservation in a deed; by implication from prior use and necessity at
severance; by **necessity**, most commonly for a landlocked parcel; and by **prescription**, through
long open use.

**Termination:** merger, release, abandonment shown by conduct rather than mere non-use, expiration of
a stated term, or the end of the necessity that created an easement by necessity.

**This course states no Oklahoma prescriptive period**, because Oklahoma's limitations provisions were
not read from primary text for this project. A long-standing use of someone else's land — a drive, a
path, a mowed strip, a gate — is routed to counsel and a surveyor.

### 8.2 Licence and encroachment

A **licence** is mere permission to enter for a purpose. It is personal, **revocable at will**, and does
not run with the land. Permission to hunt on a section is a licence; a recorded right of access is an
easement. They look identical on the ground and are entirely different in law, which is why the
question is answered from the records.

An **encroachment** is an unauthorised physical intrusion of an improvement onto adjoining land — a
fence, a shed, an eave, a drive. It is not permission of any kind; it is a trespass that has been
built.

**Encroachments are discovered by survey.** A title commitment will typically **except survey matters
and encroachments unless a survey is furnished and the exception removed.** A buyer who declines a
survey has accepted that risk and should be told so in those words. Left long enough, an encroachment
can mature into a prescriptive easement or an adverse possession claim, which converts a fixable
problem into a lawsuit.

## Hour 9 — Encumbrances II: liens, restrictions, priority

### 9.1 Liens

A **lien** is a charge against property securing a debt or obligation. It affects title; it does not by
itself dispossess. Every lien is classified twice:

- **Voluntary** (the owner created it — a mortgage) or **involuntary** (the law or a court imposed it —
  a tax lien, a judgment).
- **General** (attaching to the debtor's property generally — a judgment) or **specific** (attaching to
  one identified parcel — a mortgage, a mechanic's lien, a property tax lien).

The judgment lien is the one that surprises people, because it can surface against a parcel nobody
associated with the debt.

### 9.2 Priority

**First in time, first in right**, measured by recording — with the **real estate tax lien** as the
standard exception, customarily taking priority regardless of order. Priority may also be rearranged by
contract through a **subordination agreement**, which is why the **title commitment** rather than a list
of recording dates is the operative document.

### 9.3 Mechanic's and materialman's liens

Claimed by someone who furnished labour or materials for improvement of the property. Its danger is
structural: in many states a mechanic's lien's priority may **relate back to a date earlier than the
filing** — commencement of work, or first furnishing of materials — so a claim not yet in the records
can outrank one that is.

**This course states no Oklahoma filing period, notice requirement or priority date**, because
Oklahoma's mechanic's lien provisions were not read from primary text for this project. What a licensee
carries is the **warning sign and the routing**: recent or ongoing construction, a renovation finished
in the past several months, an unpaid or disputed contractor. Raise it at listing; route it to the
title company and counsel; do not let it be found a week before closing.

### 9.4 Deed restrictions and covenants

Private limits recorded against the land, commonly as a declaration of covenants, conditions and
restrictions governing a subdivision. They **run with the land** and are enforceable by the association
or by benefited owners.

**They are separate from zoning and the more restrictive controls.** A city may permit a use the
covenants forbid, and the covenants win. Buyers routinely assume municipal approval settles it. It does
not, and the covenants are read **before** the offer is written.

A restriction discriminating on a protected basis is **void and unenforceable** whatever the record
says — Module OK-13.

## Hour 10 — Concurrent and common-interest ownership

### 10.1 Concurrent ownership

**Tenancy in common.** Undivided interests, which may be **unequal**, each freely transferable in life
and **passing by will or intestacy at death**.

**Joint tenancy.** Equal undivided interests with the **right of survivorship** — on a joint tenant's
death the interest passes to the survivors and does not go through the estate. Traditionally requires
four unities: time, title, interest and possession.

**Tenancy by the entirety**, where a jurisdiction recognises it for married couples, carries
survivorship and its own creditor characteristics.

**Which form a deed creates, and which a particular buyer should take, is a legal and tax question for
counsel and the buyer's tax adviser.** A licensee who advises a buying couple on how to take title has
practised law. **This course states no Oklahoma rule of concurrent ownership or marital property**,
because those provisions were not read from primary text for this project. The licensee's contribution
is to raise the question early enough that it is decided deliberately rather than by whatever default
the closing agent used.

**Partition.** Any co-owner may generally seek a court division in kind, or a sale and division of
proceeds. It is the remedy when co-owners cannot agree, and it is a lawsuit.

### 10.2 Common-interest ownership

**Condominium.** The owner holds the **unit in fee** plus an **undivided interest in the common
elements**, governed by a declaration, bylaws and an association funded by assessments. Each unit is
separately assessed, taxed, financed and transferred.

**Cooperative.** The owner holds **shares in a corporation** and a **proprietary lease**. The
corporation owns the real estate; the resident owns personal property. That changes financing — share
loans, not mortgages — changes transfer, because a board usually approves purchasers, and changes the
risk, because the corporation's blanket mortgage and other shareholders' delinquencies affect everyone.

**Time-share.** An interval interest with its own consumer rules and a resale market bearing no
resemblance to the sale market.

**Questions to get answered in writing before a buyer's contingencies expire:** the assessment and what
it covers; the reserves; any pending or implied special assessment; litigation; restrictions on pets,
rentals, vehicles and occupancy; and whether the buyer's lender will lend on this project at all —
which for condominiums is a live question and not a formality.

## Hour 11 — Legal descriptions and land measurement — 605:10-3-1(a)(1)(D)

### 11.1 Three methods, and one non-method

**Metes and bounds.** Courses and distances from a **point of beginning**, running the perimeter and
**closing back to it**. A description that does not close does not describe a parcel.

**Rectangular survey, or government survey.** A grid built from **principal meridians** running north
and south and **base lines** running east and west. **Most of Oklahoma uses the Indian Meridian and
Indian Base Line; the Panhandle uses the Cimarron Meridian and Cimarron Base Line.** Identify the
applicable survey system from the actual description rather than assuming one statewide origin. **Townships** run
in horizontal tiers and vertical **ranges**; each township is six miles square and holds **36
sections**, each one mile square and **640 acres**. Sections are numbered 1 through 36 beginning in the
north-east corner and running back and forth, so section 36 lands in the south-east corner.

**Lot and block, or recorded plat.** References a lot and block on a recorded subdivision plat. The
everyday description for platted urban and suburban land.

**And the non-method: a street address is never a legal description.** Neither is a tax parcel number.
Both find a property; neither describes one.

### 11.2 Reading a rectangular description

Read it **backwards**, dividing as you go, because the description narrows from largest to smallest.

*The NE 1/4 of the SW 1/4 of Section 14.* Section = **640**; SW quarter = 640 ÷ 4 = **160**; NE quarter
of that = 160 ÷ 4 = **40 acres**.

*The S 1/2 of the NW 1/4 of the SE 1/4.* 640 ÷ 4 = 160; ÷ 4 = 40; ÷ 2 = **20 acres**.

*The W 1/2 of the NE 1/4 of Section 8.* 640 ÷ 4 ÷ 2 = **80 acres**. At $4,900 an acre: 80 × 4,900 =
**$392,000**.

Each quarter divides by four; each half divides by two. Work right to left and the arithmetic is
trivial.

### 11.3 The measurements

| Unit | Equivalent |
|---|---|
| 1 acre | **43,560 square feet** |
| 1 section | 1 square mile, **640 acres** |
| 1 township | 6 miles square, **36 sections**, 23,040 acres |
| 1 mile | 5,280 feet |

*Worked.* A lot 150 feet by 290.4 feet is 150 × 290.4 = 43,560 square feet — **exactly one acre**.
Recognising 43,560 on sight saves time on the examination and in practice.

*Worked.* A lot 88 feet by 165 feet is 14,520 square feet; 14,520 ÷ 43,560 = **one third of an acre**.

### 11.4 The rule about descriptions

**Never supply a legal description from memory, from a tax record, from a prior listing or from the
seller's recollection.** It comes from the **deed** or from the **title company**, and it is copied
exactly, character for character.

Distinguish a legal description from an attributed size figure. Under **59 O.S. 858-515.1**, a licensee
is not required to provide an area figure. If the licensee supplies one from a permitted third-party
source, including tax-assessor records, the source must be identified. The statute does not impose an
independent duty to investigate or verify that figure. An assessor figure is not thereby a boundary
survey. Recommend a qualified surveyor where exact acreage or boundaries matter; do not claim that
source attribution guarantees accuracy or immunizes unrelated misrepresentations.

**And in Oklahoma, add one more:** a legal description of the surface says nothing about who owns the
minerals. That is a separate title question and it is routed with the rest of the title work.
