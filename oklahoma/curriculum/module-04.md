## Overview

How title is proved, what can be wrong with it, and what the public and private controls on land use
do to what a buyer may actually do with a property. This module takes the title commitment apart
schedule by schedule, because that document is where most title problems are either caught or missed,
and then covers zoning, subdivision regulation and private covenants — with the licensee's boundary
stated at every point.

## Learning objectives

1. Explain what a title search establishes and what a chain of title is.
2. Name the three kinds of notice and explain why possession is checked as well as the records.
3. Read a title commitment's Schedules A, B-I and B-II and say what each does.
4. State what an owner's policy covers and what it excepts, and distinguish it from a lender's policy.
5. Explain the survey exception and what removes it.
6. Identify a cloud on title and name the instruments that clear one.
7. Distinguish the four government powers and identify which operates in a fact pattern.
8. Distinguish variance, conditional use, rezoning and legal nonconforming use.
9. Explain why private covenants are separate from zoning and which controls.
10. Describe subdivision regulation and plat approval at the level of principle.
11. State the licensee's boundary on every land-use question and route accordingly.
12. Recognise a severed mineral estate and a pipeline easement as title questions.

## Hour 19 — The title search, the chain, and notice

### 19.1 What a title search establishes

A **title search** is an examination of the public records to determine the condition of title: who owns
the property, what interests burden it, and whether anything in the record casts doubt on the owner's
ability to convey what the contract says they will convey.

**Where the records live.** Real property records are maintained at the **county** level — in Oklahoma,
the **county clerk** records instruments affecting title. Judgments, tax liens, probate proceedings and
court actions may sit in other indices in the same county or elsewhere, which is why a competent search
is not one look at one book.

### 19.2 The chain of title

The **chain of title** is the successive record of conveyances from an accepted starting point to the
present owner. Each link should connect: the grantee of one deed is the grantor of the next.

A **gap** in the chain — a missing conveyance, an estate that never went through probate, a name that
does not match, a signature that is absent — is a **cloud**, and clouds are resolved before closing
rather than explained away at it.

An **abstract of title** is a compiled history of the recorded instruments affecting a parcel. Oklahoma
practice retains a real role for abstracts, and an abstract that is brought current and examined by an
attorney produces an **attorney's title opinion**. Title insurance is the other route, and the two
coexist. **Which form of title evidence a transaction uses is settled in the contract**, which is one
more reason the contract is read before it is signed.

### 19.3 Three kinds of notice

**Actual notice** — the party in fact knows.

**Constructive notice from the records** — the party is charged with knowing what a proper search of the
public records would reveal, **whether or not they searched.** This is what recording accomplishes and
it is why recording matters.

**Inquiry notice, or notice from possession** — the party is charged with what a reasonable **inspection
of the property** would reveal, and with following up on what that inspection would make a reasonable
person ask about.

**The possession problem is the classic trap and a real one.** Someone living in the house. A tenant
with an unrecorded lease. A neighbour openly using a strip of the yard. A gate, a drive or a fence in a
place the plat does not explain. Each is a fact a purchaser is expected to have noticed.

**Title is checked in the records AND on the ground.**

### 19.4 What recording does and does not do

Recording gives **constructive notice to the world**, protecting the recorded interest against later
takers. It does **not** transfer title — delivery and acceptance do that — and it does **not** validate
an otherwise invalid instrument.

The practical rule is short: **record immediately.** The entire risk lives in the interval between
delivery and recording.

### 19.5 Two Oklahoma questions that belong in every title search

**Minerals.** Surface and mineral estates are frequently severed in Oklahoma. **A licensee never states
what mineral interest a property carries.** Raise it at listing, get it into the title work early, and
route the answer to counsel.

**Pipeline, gathering line and utility easements.** They cross a great deal of Oklahoma land and they
carry rights of access, surface use and sometimes future construction that a buyer will not infer from
looking at the ground.

**This course states no Oklahoma rule of mineral title or of easement scope**, because those provisions
were not read from primary text for this project. The recognition and the routing are what a licensee
carries.

## Hour 20 — The title commitment, schedule by schedule

### 20.1 What a commitment is

A **title commitment** is the title company's **conditional promise to insure**. It is not a title
opinion, not a coverage summary, and not a guarantee that anything is clear. It says: *on these facts,
if these requirements are satisfied, we will issue a policy subject to these exceptions.*

### 20.2 Schedule A — the facts

The proposed insured; the amount of insurance; the estate or interest to be insured; the current record
owner; and the **legal description**.

**Read Schedule A against the contract.** Is the legal description the property everyone thinks is being
sold? Is the record owner the person who signed the listing? Is the amount right? Discrepancies at
Schedule A are cheap to fix and expensive to discover later.

### 20.3 Schedule B-I — Requirements

**What must be done before the policy will issue.** Typically:

- Payoff and **release** of existing mortgages and liens.
- **Corrective instruments** for defects in the chain.
- **Evidence of authority** — a corporate resolution, a trustee's certificate, letters testamentary.
- **Satisfaction of judgments**, or evidence that a judgment debtor with a similar name is not this
  seller. A name match is not an identification, and resolving it is the title company's work, not the
  licensee's.
- Resolution of **probate** where the seller is an estate.

**Every item on Schedule B-I is a task with an owner and a deadline.** The licensee's job is to make
sure each one has both, early enough that the closing date survives.

### 20.4 Schedule B-II — Exceptions

**What the policy will NOT cover.** This is where the policy's actual scope is decided and it is the
part a buyer is least likely to read.

Standard exceptions commonly include:

- **Rights of parties in possession** — the possession problem, again.
- **Survey matters and encroachments** absent a survey.
- **Easements** not shown by the public records.
- **Mineral rights** and prior reservations or severances — which in Oklahoma is frequently the most
  consequential exception on the page.
- **Taxes and assessments** not yet due and payable.

### 20.5 What a licensee does with a commitment

**Get it into the buyer's hands early.** Tell them to read Schedules B-I and B-II. Route every question
to the title company and counsel. **Watch the objection deadline in the contract**, because it will pass
while everyone assumes the title work is "in progress."

**And never say "the title policy will take care of it."** That characterises another party's contract
that the licensee has not read and cannot bind, and under § 858-353(A)(1) — honesty and reasonable skill
and care to all parties — it is exactly the kind of casual reassurance that becomes a complaint.

## Hour 21 — Title insurance

### 21.1 Owner's and lender's policies

An **owner's policy** insures the owner against loss from covered title defects **existing at the date
of policy** — forged instruments, undisclosed heirs, incompetent grantors, recording errors, defective
deeds, undisclosed liens. Coverage is **retrospective**: it insures the past, not future events.

A **lender's policy** insures the lender's **lien position** in the amount of the loan and decreases as
the loan is repaid. **A lender's policy does not protect the buyer**, and buyers regularly believe that
it does. Say so, plainly, every time.

### 21.2 What is excepted

Everything in Schedule B-II, plus:

- defects **created, assumed or agreed to by the insured**;
- defects **known to the insured and not disclosed** to the company;
- matters arising **after** the date of policy.

That second item is worth a licensee's attention. A buyer who learns of a title problem and closes
anyway without telling the title company may have excluded it from their own coverage.

### 21.3 The survey exception

The practically important one. **Encroachments, boundary discrepancies, and anything a survey would
disclose are typically excepted unless a survey is furnished and the exception removed.**

A buyer who declines a survey has accepted that risk, and should be told so in exactly those words —
**not** "you probably don't need one." Whether a survey is needed is the buyer's decision on advice; the
licensee's job is to make sure the decision is informed and made before the deadline.

### 21.4 Endorsements

Coverage can be extended by **endorsement** — for access, for zoning, for restrictions, for survey
matters where a survey is provided. Which endorsements are available and appropriate is a question for
the title company and counsel. **A licensee neither recommends nor characterises endorsements.**

## Hour 22 — Clouds on title and how they clear

### 22.1 Common clouds

- An **unreleased mortgage** long since paid. Payment alone does not clear the record.
- A **missing signature** — a spouse, a co-owner, a person whose interest nobody noticed.
- A **name discrepancy** — the grantee took as one name and conveyed as another.
- An **estate that never went through probate**, so the heirs' interests were never established.
- A **judgment** against someone with the seller's name.
- A **defective legal description** in an earlier deed.
- An **unreleased oil and gas lease** or a **severed mineral interest** whose owner cannot be located.

### 22.2 The instruments that clear them

- A **release** or **satisfaction**, recorded.
- A **corrective deed**, to fix a description or a name.
- A **quit claim deed**, to convey away a possible interest.
- An **affidavit** — of identity, of heirship, of scrivener's error — where the title company will
  accept one.
- A **probate proceeding**, where an estate must be administered.
- A **suit to quiet title**, where nothing else will do. It is a lawsuit, it takes months, and a closing
  date built around one that nobody has filed is a closing date that will move.

**Every one of these is prepared and decided by counsel and the title company.** A licensee identifies
the problem early, routes it, and manages the timetable.

### 22.3 The timetable is the licensee's real contribution

The licensee is not the person who clears title. The licensee is the person who **notices in week one
that title work has not been ordered**, who **reads Schedule B-I the day it arrives**, and who **tells
the client in writing that the closing date depends on an item nobody has started.**

That is § 858-353(A)(1) — reasonable skill and care — and § 858-353(B)(2) — keeping the party informed —
doing their actual work.

## Hour 23 — Public land use control

### 23.1 The four powers, applied

Module OK-02 named them; here they operate. **Police power** is the one that produces zoning, building
codes, subdivision regulation and environmental controls, and it operates **without compensation**.
**Eminent domain** takes, through condemnation, with just compensation. **Taxation** charges. **Escheat**
reverts.

### 23.2 The comprehensive plan and zoning

The **comprehensive plan** is a municipality's long-range policy document. **Zoning** is meant to
implement it, dividing a jurisdiction into districts and regulating **use** and **dimension** — setbacks,
height, lot coverage, density, parking.

The instruments a licensee will hear named:

**Variance.** Permission to depart from a **dimensional** requirement because of a hardship arising from
the property itself. Granted by a board of adjustment on its own standards. **A licensee never tells a
buyer a variance will be granted.**

**Conditional or special use permit.** A use the ordinance contemplates subject to review and
conditions.

**Rezoning.** A legislative change to the district or the map. It is a political process as much as a
legal one and its outcome cannot be predicted.

**Legal nonconforming use.** A use that lawfully predates the current ordinance and is allowed to
continue **subject to limits** on expansion, on resumption after abandonment, and on rebuilding after
destruction. **Those limits are the point.** A buyer who assumes the use is permanent and unrestricted
may find they cannot rebuild after a fire or resume after a vacancy.

**Spot zoning** — rezoning a single parcel inconsistently with the surrounding area — is disfavoured and
vulnerable to challenge.

### 23.3 Building codes

Minimum construction standards enforced through **permits and inspections**. Separate from zoning: zoning
governs use and dimension, codes govern construction.

Two things a licensee must know:

- **A code-compliant building is not a defect-free building.** Codes are minimums.
- **A permit history records what was applied for, not what was built.** Unpermitted work is a routine
  finding on older houses, and it is routed to the municipality — not characterised by the licensee as
  "fine" or "no problem."

### 23.4 The licensee's boundary, stated once for the hour

**Identify the question, tell the client which authority answers it, and see that it is answered before
their contingencies expire.**

**Never** state what a municipality will permit. **Never** assume a current use is permitted merely
because it exists — it may be nonconforming, unpermitted, or about to become impossible to continue.

Timing is where the licensee actually adds value: getting the buyer's question in front of the
municipality in week one rather than week four.

## Hour 24 — Subdivision regulation and private controls

### 24.1 Subdivision regulation

Governs the **division of land into lots** and the standards for the plat, the streets, the utilities,
the drainage and the dedications. It is a municipal and county process, and the **recorded plat** it
produces becomes the legal description for every lot in it.

A licensee working raw land, or a lot in a new development, routes plat questions to the municipality,
the county and a surveyor **before anything is marketed.**

**This course states no Oklahoma platting requirement, dedication standard or approval timetable**,
because Oklahoma's subdivision provisions were not read from primary text for this project.

### 24.2 Private controls

**Restrictive covenants** — conditions and restrictions recorded against the land, usually as a
declaration governing a subdivision, enforced by an owners' association or by benefited owners. They
**run with the land**.

**Two facts a buyer needs and usually does not have:**

1. **Covenants are separate from zoning, and the more restrictive controls.** A city may permit a use the
   covenants forbid, and the covenants win.
2. **Covenants are enforced privately.** No municipal official will tell a buyer they are about to breach
   one. The association or a neighbour will — after the garage is built.

**A buyer who intends to run a business from home, park a recreational vehicle or boat, keep animals,
build an outbuilding, install a fence, or rent the property short-term needs the covenants read BEFORE
the offer is written.**

### 24.3 Homeowners' associations

Where a declaration creates one, it typically has power to levy **assessments**, to enforce the
covenants, to maintain common areas and to adopt rules. What a buyer must get in writing before their
contingencies expire: the assessment and what it covers; reserves; any pending or implied special
assessment; litigation; the rules on pets, rentals, vehicles and occupancy; and whether their lender
will lend on this project.

### 24.4 Discriminatory covenants

Racially restrictive covenants appear in the older records of essentially every American city,
Oklahoma's included. **They are void and unenforceable**, and they have no legal effect whatever the
record says.

A licensee who encounters one does not repeat it, does not describe a neighbourhood by reference to it,
and routes the question of removing it from the record to counsel. Module OK-13 takes fair housing up in
full; the point here is that a recorded restriction is not automatically an enforceable one.
