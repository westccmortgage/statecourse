# Can we actually operate here? — screening states by delivery rule

**Opened:** August 19, 2026. **Reviewer:** StateCourse build.
**Source policy:** the state's statute, administrative rules, and current regulator forms control. A search-engine summary, a competitor's page, or a trade article may point at a source but can never establish a requirement. Every row below was read in the primary document named beside it.

## Why this register exists, and why it comes before any course is written

StateCourse is a fully asynchronous online school. That is not a preference; it is the whole delivery model. A jurisdiction that requires classroom hours, live instruction, or a proctored physical location for part of its pre-license requirement is a jurisdiction where the model cannot deliver the licence on its own — no matter how attractive its hour count or fee looks in a comparison table.

So the first question asked of every state is not "how many hours" but **"how many of those hours may be self-paced and online, and who says so"**. Getting that wrong is expensive in exactly the way that matters: it is discovered after the course is written.

Two findings on this page were the opposite of what a summary said, which is the argument for the rule at the top:

- **Iowa** reads, in the commission's own rules, as an online-friendly state — the 60-hour course may be "live instruction or distance/online learning". The same rule then requires the applicant to supply, in addition, **36 hours of *live* instruction** in three named courses. A search summary reported only the first half.
- **New Hampshire** describes 40 hours of accredited pre-licensing education. The rule caps the self-paced share at **8 of those 40**.

A third case cut the other way and is just as instructive. A summary reported that Mississippi requires ARELLO certification for distance courses. The Commission's Rules and Regulations, read in full, mention ARELLO once and only for reciprocity of continuing-education approval — so the claim looked wrong. It was not wrong; it was in a different document. The requirement is in the *statute*, Miss. Code 73-35-14.3(2), and reading only the rules would have missed a statutory gate. Checking a summary against one primary document is not the same as checking it against the right one.

## What the viable states require of the engine

These are the concrete build requirements the primary sources impose, over and above what California already made us build.

**Michigan** — under current R 339.22157(2)–(5), a distance course either holds ARELLO certification or documents modules, at least one learning objective per module, a structured learning method for each objective, assessment during each module, **remediation that repeats the module until the student understands the material**, and the remedy for hardware/software failures. R 339.22141 separately requires the topics-and-hours summary, certificate specimen, asynchronous attestation, makeup policy, and an enrollment application that discloses the previous calendar year's completion percentage. The 2023 form still prints the former Rule 618/626 numbers, but they are not used here as current authority.

**Kansas** — K.A.R. 86-1-10(c)(1) requires an asynchronous course to submit the means of access, evidence of sufficient IT support, documentation of **active participation and substantial interaction** between the student and the instructor, other students, or a computer program, and **evidence that the testing system will scramble questions and items to ensure a random presentation**. ARELLO certification creates a **presumption** that (c)(1) is met — 86-1-10(c)(2) — rather than being required. A prelicense course must require the student to answer **at least 50** quiz or examination questions — (c)(4) — and **the school issues the certificate of completion to a student who answered at least 90 percent of them correctly** — (c)(5).

Three consequences for the engine, and one for every document:

- **90 percent is a Kansas requirement, not a provider standard.** It is the first screened state that prescribes the passing figure, so Kansas documents must state it as the regulator's and must not carry the StateCourse 80 percent language used in California, Michigan and Alaska.
- **Kansas asks for both questions and items to be scrambled.** Our engine permutes choices deterministically per item id and delivers items in a fixed order. Choice-order permutation is half of what (c)(1)(D) describes; item-order presentation is the other half. It is a contained engine change, and it must not disturb the property that a recorded `selected_choice` keeps its meaning permanently — which means any item-order change has to be recorded per attempt rather than inferred from position.
- **At least 50 questions per prelicense course** is a floor on the bank, not on a single assessment.

**Montana** — attendance is measured, not assumed: the applicant must attend 90 percent of the first hour and 100 percent of each additional hour of approved course time to receive credit, and course design may allow no more than ten minutes of break per sixty minutes. Our engine records active study time per assignment already, which is the machinery this rule needs; what it does not yet do is express credit as an attendance percentage of a scheduled hour.

**Wyoming** — every instructor and institution delivering education must keep attendance records for **at least five years** and submit the attendance record to the Commission **within ten days after each course is delivered**. A ten-day reporting clock is tighter than Virginia's five business days per completion in one sense and looser in another: it is per course rather than per student, but it is unconditional.

**Alaska** — a correspondence course must include "an unanswered examination, or a project activity, to be turned in to the instructor for grading upon completion of the course", and that instrument goes to the commission for prior approval. This is the first state that requires a **human** to grade the instrument: our engine scores server-side and automatically, which satisfies nobody's rule here. Alaska also prescribes certificate contents including the **course approval number** — the same obligation California imposes through §3004(b) — and requires a student course-and-instructor evaluation before the certificate issues, which is a variation on the notice Virginia and California require.

## ARELLO turned out to be the cross-state finding

The question started as a registry field to fill and became the thing that decides the order we build in. Five states, five different answers:

| State | What ARELLO means there | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Virginia | **Nothing.** The term appears nowhere in 18VAC135-20 Part VI or in the current course-approval form | 18VAC135-20 sections 360, 361, 362, 390; Form A490-0214PRE-v9 |
| Michigan | **One of two routes.** A distance course supplies proof of ARELLO certification, or documents an equivalent system directly under R 339.22157(2)–(4) | Current R 339.22157; form rev. 3/23 still carries superseded numbering |
| Kansas | **A safe harbour.** An ARELLO-certified asynchronous course is *presumed* to meet the commission's requirements; a non-certified course must document them | Kansas Register vol. 43 no. 50 |
| Idaho | **Required, with an escape.** Distance course design and delivery must be certified by ARELLO "or by another institution whose certification standards are deemed equivalent by the commission" | Idaho Real Estate License Law and Rules, eff. 1 July 2025 |
| Mississippi | **Required by statute.** If ARELLO operates a certification programme, a pre-license course and a distance learning course must each be certified before the commission may approve it | Miss. Code 73-35-14.3(2), 73-35-14.4(2) |
| Montana | **Required, and the approval dies with it.** A distance course may be approved only if ARELLO-certified with documentation that the certification is in effect, and "approval will cease immediately should ARELLO certification be discontinued for any reason" | ARM 24.210, prelicensing course and instructor requirements |
| Alaska | **Not referenced.** Certification is by the commission or its designee | 12 AAC 64 Article 7 |
| South Dakota | **A price signal.** Course approval costs $150 for a distance education course and $50 for an ARELLO-certified one, and the rules carry a separate provision for ARELLO-certified courses | ARSD 20:69:11:08.04, 20:69:11:10 |
| Wyoming | **Adjacent.** Instructor certification runs through IDECC, ARELLO's certification subsidiary | Wyoming Real Estate Commission rules |

The consequence for sequencing is concrete. ARELLO Distance Education Certification is not a nice-to-have badge to acquire once there is spare time; in four of these ten states it is a precondition to approval, in a third it is the cheap path, and in a fourth it converts a documentation burden into a presumption. It is a program-level investment with a lead time, and it unlocks several states at once. It should be started in parallel with the California submission rather than after it — and California itself does not require it, which is exactly why it would otherwise be forgotten until it is on the critical path.

**Also worth recording:** where a state gates on ARELLO, the gate applies to the *course*, not the school. Certification of one course does not carry to another, which means the certification cost scales with the number of courses, not the number of states.

And Montana adds a maintenance obligation the others do not spell out: approval there is not merely granted on the strength of a certification, it is **contingent on the certification remaining in effect**, and lapses the moment the certification does. A certification that expires quietly does not degrade the offering; it ends the approval.

## What building Michigan taught that screening did not

Michigan was screened first and built first, and the build surfaced three things the screening pass had missed. That is itself the finding: a delivery-rule screen tells you whether a state is possible, not what it will cost.

**The rules had been renumbered.** Everything in the old R 339.22301–R 339.22667 range now reads "Rescinded", effective 4 June 2025, and LARA's own current course-approval form still cites the superseded numbers. A screen that reads the form and stops would cite rescinded rules throughout. Reading the rules in full is not the same as reading the form that references them.

**There is a second regulator, and its applicability is resolved.** R 339.22143(2) binds a proprietary real estate school to the Proprietary Schools Act, and LEO now states expressly that a non-degree proprietary or trade school with no Michigan physical presence that provides distance education to Michigan residents must be licensed. Real-estate prelicensing then adds BPL approval of the programme/course and each instructor. **Every future screen must ask who else licenses schools in the state**, not only who approves the course.

**Approval expires in one year.** R 339.22143(1), with renewal filed 60 days ahead. Against Virginia's three years and California's course life, Michigan turns approval maintenance into an annual routine, and the cost of a state is not only the cost of entering it.

## Method, so this is repeatable

1. Find the controlling text — statute, administrative rules, and the regulator's current course-approval form. Check whether the rules have been renumbered, and whether the form predates the renumbering. Prefer the legislature or secretary-of-state publication of the rules; regulator sites often refuse an automated request, and the rule text is what governs anyway.
2. Answer the delivery question first, from the rule, in full sentences quoted or closely paraphrased into the register.
3. Answer the operational questions second: who approves the school, **whether a separate private-school or proprietary-school agency also licenses it**, how long the approval lasts, who approves the instructor, how long records are kept, what the completion report must contain and when it is due, and what the certificate must carry.
4. Record the date read and the document revision. Rules change; a fact without a date is not a fact for long.
5. Fill the public registry only from what was read, and leave anything unread empty rather than plausible.

## Correction record

**2026-08-19 — Nebraska, entry hours.** This register recorded Nebraska as **60 hours in two courses of not less than 30 class hours each**. That is wrong. **Neb. Rev. Stat. 81-885.13(2)**, read from the Nebraska Legislature's statute service, requires a salesperson applicant to furnish evidence of **three courses in real estate subjects, approved by the commission, composed of not less than ninety class hours of study in total** — or, in lieu thereof, courses delivered in a distance education format approved by the commission. The 60-hour figure appears to have been carried from a secondary description of the requirement rather than from the statute.

The row is corrected above, and Nebraska drops from the cheapest tier to the 90-hour tier alongside Idaho and New Mexico.

**This is the fourth time in this project a secondary reading has diverged from the primary text** — after Iowa's hour count, New Hampshire's asynchronous cap, and Mississippi's ARELLO requirement. The rule that produced all four corrections is the rule that stands: **no row in this register may be relied on until the statute or regulation behind it has been read directly.** Rows whose source column names a commission web page rather than a statute or regulation are, on the evidence of this record, the rows most likely to be wrong.

**2026-08-19 — Kansas, second regulator, confirmed.** The screening pass noted that Kansas needs a Kansas Board of Regents certificate before commission approval is worth anything. That is now confirmed at the statute: **K.S.A. 58-3046a(g)** limits approved courses to those offered by an institution accredited by a USDE-recognised agency; a technical college under K.S.A. 74-32,407; **a private or out-of-state postsecondary educational institution issued a certificate of approval under the Kansas private and out-of-state postsecondary educational institution act**; an agency of the State of Kansas; a similar commission-approved institution in another state; or **an entity approved by the commission to provide continuing education**.

The last of those, (g)(6), is a route that on its face does not require the postsecondary certificate. **Whether the commission will accept a (g)(6) entity for prelicense courses is the question to put to KREC before any Kansas curriculum is written**, and it is a call rather than a build. Kansas also confirms the 50-minute hour at **K.S.A. 58-3046a(i)**, and course registration **expires 31 January each year** under K.A.R. 86-1-10(g), with a late renewal requiring a fresh application.

## Two states screened again on 2026-08-19, and what changed

Both were re-read because Kansas was about to be built and Vermont was its cheapest alternative. Both readings changed the row.

### Kansas — the regulator writes the outline and does not publish it

**K.A.R. 86-1-11(a):** a school offering the course under K.S.A. 58-3046a(a) "shall **use a course outline provided by the commission**" and shall use the title "principles of real estate". **86-1-11(c)** says the same for the "Kansas practice course".

This is a category of blocker the screening method had not yet met. Alaska prescribes its curriculum **in the regulation**, which is a gift: the outline is public, and the build simply fills it. Kansas prescribes its curriculum **in a document the Commission hands you**, which is a wall until the document arrives. The Commission's site returned HTTP 403 to every request from this environment.

The full Kansas reading is in `kansas/regulatory/official-sources.md`. Everything else about Kansas is favourable — asynchronous delivery is a defined and separately approvable type, ARELLO is a presumption rather than a gate, the fee is $75, and the rules say exactly what to file. It is blocked on an email, not on effort.

**Kansas also changed the platform.** K.A.R. 86-1-10(c)(1)(D) requires the testing system to scramble **questions and items**, where the engine only permuted choices; and 86-1-10(c)(5) sets **90 percent** as the certificate condition, the first regulator-prescribed pass mark in this project. `config/assessment-presentation.mjs` answers the first and is written to be reusable.

### Vermont — the opposite failure mode

Vermont's Part 5 is the whole of its initial-education regime:

> **5.1 Initial Salesperson and Broker Education.** All applicants for a salesperson or broker license must complete the salesperson or broker prelicensing course, which will consist of at least forty hours of instruction approved by the Commission.

There is no topic list, no hour allocation, no delivery rule, no course-approval application, no fee, no school records requirement, and no provider standard anywhere in the rules. The definitions add only that a prelicensing course is "a course of instruction to become a salesperson, at least 40 hours long, which has been approved by the Commission."

So Vermont cannot be built **wrong** — there is no prescribed structure to miss. It also cannot be built **safely**: whether an asynchronous course is acceptable is entirely a matter of the Commission's approval practice, and the rules give no signal either way. That is a different risk from Kansas's, not a smaller one.

**And one check could not be made.** Massachusetts is blocked in this register because its statute says "40 **classroom** hours" while its regulations were unreachable. Vermont's *rules* say "forty hours of instruction" with no such word — but **26 V.S.A. chapter 41 could not be retrieved from this session**, so the possibility that the Vermont statute carries classroom wording the rules do not repeat has not been excluded. It must be excluded before Vermont is scheduled.

**Useful for the eventual Vermont curriculum**, recorded now so the reading is not repeated: rule 4.14 requires a brokerage firm to maintain **all records of brokerage services for at least seven years** at its usual place of business, available to the Commission during regular business hours — the longest retention period met in this project so far. Rule 4.15 requires a licensee to respond to a Commission inquiry **as soon as reasonably practicable or within 30 days, whichever is sooner**. Rule 4.13 governs referral fees, which must be in writing and create no agency relationship, and permits cross-side compensation without creating agency and without client consent.

### What this pair teaches the method

The register's "Verdict" column has been asking one question — *may an asynchronous course be approved?* — and that turns out to be two questions:

1. **May it be delivered this way?**
2. **Is the content mine to write?**

Kansas answers yes to the first and no to the second. Vermont answers "unstated" to the first and yes to the second. Alaska and Michigan answer yes to both, which is why they were buildable. **Every remaining row should be re-read for question 2 before it is scheduled**, because a state that hands you the outline is a state that cannot be started on a weekend.
