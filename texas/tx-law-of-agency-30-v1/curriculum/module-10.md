# Texas Law of Agency — Module 10

## TREC Topic: Agency, Ethics and the Law

**Prescribed time: 155 minutes.** This module carries the five units the Commission's LOA-1 course
approval form places under *Agency, Ethics and the Law*, whose scope note is *"An agent's duties,
including fiduciary duties."*

**Learning objectives.** After completing this module you will be able to: separate a legal duty
enforceable by the state from an ethical duty enforceable by a licensing agency or a private
association and from a moral conviction enforceable by nobody, and explain why a Texas license
holder can lose a license over conduct that is neither a crime nor a tort; state the protected
characteristics exactly as the federal Fair Housing Act, the Texas Fair Housing Act, and 22 TAC
§ 531.19 each state them, apply the reasonable-accommodation and reasonable-modification provisions
with their qualifying language intact, and identify which fair housing exemptions are destroyed the
moment a license holder touches the transaction; read the Canons of Professional Ethics and Client
Relations at 22 TAC §§ 531.1–531.20 as the ethical code that actually binds you, locate a given act
within the correct subsection of Occupations Code § 1101.652, and trace a consumer complaint from
filing through investigation under 22 TAC § 535.141 to a sanction authorized by § 1101.702 and
priced by 22 TAC § 535.191; describe what a trade association code of ethics is, how 22 TAC § 535.1
defines a Trade Association, why such a code binds by contract rather than by statute, and how a
private ethics complaint differs in forum, remedy and consequence from a complaint to the
Commission; and state the minimum ethical standard a Texas license holder must meet, drawing every
element of it back to a canon or a rule you can cite.

**Every legal statement in this module comes from a source that was read for this course.** Where a
statement is practice rather than law, the module says so and cites nothing for it.

## Unit: Distinctions between Law, Ethics, and Morals

The distinction this unit draws is not philosophical. It is jurisdictional. Three different
enforcement systems can reach your conduct in a Texas real estate transaction, each with its own
decision-maker, its own procedure, its own sanction, and its own vocabulary. If you cannot sort a
complaint into the right box, you will either panic over something that carries no consequence or
shrug at something that will cost you your license.

**Law is the set of rules the state itself enforces.** A criminal statute is enforced by a
prosecutor, in a court, against a defendant, with the state's coercive power at the end of it. A
civil statute or a common-law duty is enforced by a private party who sues, in a court, and collects
a judgment. Both are law. Their common feature is that the enforcer, ultimately, is a government
body with the power to take money, property or liberty. Texas fair housing law supplies an example
of a statute that reaches all three of those levels at once: Property Code § 301.171 makes
intimidating or interfering with a protected person by force or threat of force an offense, and
provides that "An offense under this section is a Class A misdemeanor"; § 301.151(a) lets an
aggrieved person file a civil action in district court; and § 301.112 lets a state agency assess
civil penalties administratively. One underlying prohibition, three enforcement machines.

**Ethics, in a licensed profession, is the set of rules a licensing body or a membership
organization enforces against people who have accepted its authority.** The word carries a soft
connotation in ordinary speech — a matter of conscience, or of taste. In Texas real estate it is
nothing of the kind. The Commission's Canons of Professional Ethics and Client Relations are
administrative rules published at 22 TAC Chapter 531, and they are enforceable. Occupations Code § 1101.656(a) provides that "In addition to any other
authority under this chapter, the commission may suspend or revoke a license, place on probation a
person whose license has been suspended, or reprimand a license holder if the license holder
violates this chapter or a commission rule." Section 1101.701(a) adds the money: "The commission may
impose an administrative penalty on a person who violates this chapter or a rule adopted or order
issued by the commission under this chapter." Read those two sentences together and the point is
unmistakable. The phrase "or a commission rule" and the phrase "or a rule adopted" are what convert
an ethical canon into an enforceable obligation with a license and a bank balance behind it. An
ethical breach in this field is not a matter of taste. It is a rule violation, and the state agency
that issued your license is the enforcer.

The second sort of ethics enforcement is private, and Unit 4 deals with it in detail. Hold on to the
structural difference for now: the Commission enforces against everyone who holds a license, whether
they like it or not; a trade association enforces only against its own members, and only because
they agreed to be bound when they joined and paid dues.

**Morals are the convictions a person holds about right conduct, enforced by nobody.** No procedure,
no sanction, no forum. That is not a criticism of morals — a license holder's private sense of
decency is usually the thing that keeps the other two systems from ever having to be invoked — but
it is a real limitation, and the limitation runs both directions. A moral conviction does not by
itself create a legal duty. It also does not excuse you from one.

Texas gives a sharp example of morals running against law. Suppose a buyer's agent in San Antonio
learns that a previous occupant of a listed house died of an AIDS-related illness. Many people's
moral intuition is that a buyer would want to know, and that concealing it is a species of lying.
The law says otherwise, in two places, and both were written deliberately. Occupations Code § 1101.802 provides that
"Notwithstanding Section 1101.801, a person is not civilly or criminally liable because the person
failed to inquire about, make a disclosure relating to, or release information relating to whether a
previous or current occupant of real property had, may have had, has, or may have AIDS, an
HIV-related illness, or HIV infection as defined by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention
of the United States Public Health Service." And 22 TAC § 531.19, the Commission's discriminatory
practices canon, closes with the sentence: "For the purpose of this section, disability includes
AIDS, HIV-related illnesses, or HIV infection as defined by the Centers for Disease Control of the
United States Public Health Service." Put those together and the agent who volunteers the
information has not merely done something the law did not require. The agent has made a disclosure
about a previous occupant's disability, which is exactly what § 531.19 forbids. The moral intuition,
followed uncritically, walks you into a rule violation.

**Why a license holder can be disciplined for conduct that breaks no criminal law.** Discipline does
not require wrongdoing of the sort a jury would recognize — theft, fraud, a falsified document.
Title 22 TAC § 535.157 requires a broker or sales agent to respond
to a principal, to a license holder representing another party, or to an unrepresented party within
two calendar days; that rule is taught in Module 02. Failing to return a cooperating agent's call
for four days is not a crime, and very likely not a tort — the cooperating agent has no contract
with you and probably no damages. But it is a violation of a Commission rule, § 1101.656(a)
authorizes discipline for violating a commission rule, and 22 TAC § 535.191 places "22 TAC
§535.157" in the band for which "An administrative penalty range of $100 - $1,500 per violation per
day may be assessed." Per violation. Per day. That is the whole distance between ethics as a feeling
and ethics as an enforceable standard.

The three systems also run in parallel rather than in sequence, and TRELA says so expressly. Section
1101.801 provides: "Disciplinary action taken against a person under Section 1101.652 does not
relieve the person from civil or criminal liability." A single act — say, spending a buyer's earnest
money out of the operating account — can produce a criminal charge brought by a district attorney, a
civil suit brought by the buyer, and a Commission proceeding, and none of the three waits for the
others. Nor does winning one win the others. An acquittal in a criminal case does not bar the
Commission, whose burden and whose rules are different. A Commission order that assesses a penalty
does not compensate the buyer; § 1101.659(b) limits what a refund order may do, providing that the
amount "may not exceed the amount the consumer paid to the person for a service or accommodation
regulated by this commission" and that "The commission may not require payment of other damages or
estimate harm in a refund order." The Commission is a regulator, not a small-claims court. If the
consumer wants damages, the consumer sues.

It is worth noticing what forum the ethics system actually uses, because it is neither a court nor a
private tribunal. Under § 1101.657(a), "If the commission proposes to deny, suspend, or revoke a
person's license or certificate of registration, the person is entitled to a hearing conducted by
the State Office of Administrative Hearings," and subsection (e) provides that "A hearing under this
section is governed by the contested case procedures under Chapter 2001, Government Code." From
there, § 1101.658(a) gives "A person aggrieved by a ruling, order, or decision under this
subchapter" the right "to appeal to a district court in the county in which the administrative
hearing was held." So the ethics system does eventually touch the courts — but on review, and on an
administrative record, not as a fresh lawsuit. The record the Commission builds during its
investigation is the record you will argue from later.

A last distinction that matters in daily practice. Law tends to be specific and bounded; ethics
rules in this field are frequently open-textured, and that is intentional. Compare Occupations Code
§ 1101.652(b)(30), which tells you precisely where escrow money goes — "in trust with a title
company authorized to do business in this state" or "in a custodial, trust, or escrow account
maintained for that purpose in a banking institution authorized to do business in this state" — with
22 TAC § 531.3, which requires a license holder to exercise integrity "including employment of
prudence and caution so as to avoid misrepresentation, in any way, by acts of commission or
omission." The first is a rule you can comply with by following an instruction. The second is a
standard you comply with by exercising judgment, and it is written broadly precisely so that it can
reach conduct nobody anticipated. Your protection against an open-textured standard is not to hunt
for the loophole. It is to build habits that keep you well inside the line.

### Applied exercise: Sorting three complaints in Lubbock

Marisol is a sales agent in Lubbock sponsored by a broker whose office is on 19th Street. In the
same week, three things happen.

First, a cooperating agent in Amarillo emails Marisol an offer on Tuesday morning and calls twice.
Marisol is at a family funeral and does not respond until the following Monday. The offer is still
open and her seller accepts it; nobody loses money.

Second, Marisol tells a buyer that the seller *will definitely take $10,000 less* because she wants
the deal to close. The seller had told her no such thing. The buyer offers less, the seller is
offended, and the deal dies. The seller relists with another brokerage.

Third, Marisol privately believes that a landlord client is a dishonest man who overcharges his
tenants, though every lease he signs complies with its terms and no law restricts what he may
charge. She continues to represent him and says nothing to anyone.

1. For each of the three, identify which enforcement system — law, Commission-enforced ethics, or
   morals — can reach the conduct, and name the specific section that supports your answer for each
   system you say applies.
2. For the second incident, § 1101.652(b) contains more than one subdivision that could be charged.
   Identify at least two, quote the language you are relying on, and say which you think fits better
   and why.
3. The seller in the second incident wants money. Explain, using § 1101.659(b) and § 1101.801, what
   the Commission can and cannot do for that seller and what the seller must do to be made whole.
4. The first incident caused no harm to anyone. Explain why that fact does not dispose of it, citing
   the rule Marisol broke and the penalty band 22 TAC § 535.191 assigns to it.
5. Marisol's broker was never named in any complaint. Read 22 TAC § 535.141 and explain whether the
   broker is nonetheless drawn into the matter, and for what limited purposes.

## Unit: Federal and State Law Relating to Conduct

Fair housing is the body of law most likely to end a Texas real estate career, and it is the body of
law students most often learn wrong: a memorized list of *protected classes* that matches no
statute, held on the assumption that the federal Act, the Texas Act and the Commission's rule all
say the same thing. They do not, and the differences are the parts that matter. In this unit you
learn each list exactly as its own source states it.

**The federal Fair Housing Act.** The operative prohibitions live at 42 U.S.C. § 3604. Its opening
words matter: "As made applicable by section 3603 of this title and except as exempted by sections
3603(b) and 3607 of this title, it shall be unlawful—". Everything that follows is subject to those
two cross-references, which is why the exemptions discussed below are part of the statute rather
than an afterthought.

Subsection (a) makes it unlawful "To refuse to sell or rent after the making of a bona fide offer,
or to refuse to negotiate for the sale or rental of, or otherwise make unavailable or deny, a
dwelling to any person because of race, color, religion, sex, familial status, or national origin."
Subsection (b) makes it unlawful "To discriminate against any person in the terms, conditions, or
privileges of sale or rental of a dwelling, or in the provision of services or facilities in
connection therewith, because of race, color, religion, sex, familial status, or national origin."
Subsection (c) makes it unlawful "To make, print, or publish, or cause to be made, printed, or
published any notice, statement, or advertisement, with respect to the sale or rental of a dwelling
that indicates any preference, limitation, or discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex,
handicap, familial status, or national origin, or an intention to make any such preference,
limitation, or discrimination."

Read those three lists again, slowly. Subsections (a) and (b) name six characteristics: race, color,
religion, sex, familial status, national origin. Subsection (c) names seven: the same six plus
handicap. That is not a drafting slip. Disability protection was added to the federal Act later than
the original 1968 list, and Congress put the substantive disability prohibitions in their own
subsection, § 3604(f), rather than editing (a) and (b). So under federal law, refusing to rent
because of a disability is unlawful — but the provision you cite is § 3604(f)(1), not § 3604(a). Subsections (d) and (e), which deal with falsely
representing that a dwelling is unavailable for inspection, sale or rental and with inducing sales
for profit by representations about who is moving into a neighborhood, both include handicap in
their lists.

Section 3604(f)(1) makes it unlawful "To discriminate in the sale or rental, or to otherwise make
unavailable or deny, a dwelling to any buyer or renter because of a handicap of—" the buyer or
renter, a person residing in or intending to reside in that dwelling after it is so sold, rented, or
made available, or "any person associated with that buyer or renter." That last phrase is the
associational provision, and it catches a real and common error: refusing an applicant because the
applicant's adult child who will visit uses a wheelchair is a violation even though the applicant
has no disability.

Paragraph (f)(3) then defines what counts as discrimination in this context, and two of its three
branches are the provisions you will use most. Subparagraph (A) covers modifications: "a refusal to
permit, at the expense of the handicapped person, reasonable modifications of existing premises
occupied or to be occupied by such person if such modifications may be necessary to afford such
person full enjoyment of the premises except that, in the case of a rental, the landlord may where
it is reasonable to do so condition permission for a modification on the renter agreeing to restore
the interior of the premises to the condition that existed before the modification, reasonable wear
and tear excepted." Subparagraph (B) covers accommodations: "a refusal to make reasonable
accommodations in rules, policies, practices, or services, when such accommodations may be necessary
to afford such person equal opportunity to use and enjoy a dwelling".

Notice four things in those two sentences that students routinely drop. First, a *modification* is a
physical change to the premises and it is made "at the expense of the handicapped person" — the
tenant pays. An *accommodation* is a change in "rules, policies, practices, or services," and the
statute does not put its cost on the tenant. Second, both are qualified by "may be necessary." The
statute does not say a modification or accommodation must be proven indispensable; it says it is
discrimination to refuse one that *may be necessary*. Truncating that qualifier — and it gets
truncated constantly, in brochures, in office training, and in courses — converts a broad protection
into a narrow one and gets people sued. Third, the modification provision has a landlord-side
qualifier of its own: on a rental, the landlord may, where reasonable, condition permission on the
renter agreeing to restore the interior, "reasonable wear and tear excepted." Fourth, the standards
differ: a modification must "may be necessary to afford such person full enjoyment of the premises,"
while an accommodation must "may be necessary to afford such person equal opportunity to use and
enjoy a dwelling." Full enjoyment; equal opportunity. Different words, deliberately.

Section 3604(f)(9) supplies the limit: "Nothing in this subsection requires that a dwelling be made
available to an individual whose tenancy would constitute a direct threat to the health or safety of
other individuals or whose tenancy would result in substantial physical damage to the property of
others." That is a narrow safety valve about a specific individual's tenancy, not a general licence
to decide that a category of applicants is risky.

The federal definitions are at 42 U.S.C. § 3602. "Handicap" means, with respect to a person, "a
physical or mental impairment which substantially limits one or more of such person's major life
activities," "a record of having such an impairment, or" "being regarded as having such an
impairment," and the section adds that the term "does not include current, illegal use of or
addiction to a controlled substance". "Familial status" means "one or more individuals (who have not
attained the age of 18 years) being domiciled with" a parent or another person having legal custody,
or the designee of such a parent or person with written permission, and the definition adds that
"The protections afforded against discrimination on the basis of familial status shall apply to any
person who is pregnant or is in the process of securing legal custody of any individual who has not
attained the age of 18 years."

Two more federal sections bear directly on brokerage. Section 3605(a) provides that "It shall be
unlawful for any person or other entity whose business includes engaging in residential real
estate-related transactions to discriminate against any person in making available such a
transaction, or in the terms or conditions of such a transaction, because of race, color, religion,
sex, handicap, familial status, or national origin," and § 3605(b)(2) defines the covered
transactions to include "The selling, brokering, or appraising of residential real property." You
are inside that definition every working day. Section 3606 makes it unlawful, "After December 31,
1968," "to deny any person access to or membership or participation in any multiple-listing service,
real estate brokers' organization or other service, organization, or facility relating to the
business of selling or renting dwellings, or to discriminate against him in the terms or conditions
of such access, membership, or participation, on account of race, color, religion, sex, handicap,
familial status, or national origin." And § 3617 makes it unlawful "to coerce, intimidate, threaten,
or interfere with any person in the exercise or enjoyment of, or on account of his having exercised
or enjoyed, or on account of his having aided or encouraged any other person in the exercise or
enjoyment of, any right granted or protected by section 3603, 3604, 3605, or 3606 of this title".
Section 3617 is the provision that reaches retaliation against the neighbor who testified, or
against the agent who refused to go along.

**The federal exemptions, and why they almost never help you.** Section 3603(b) exempts from § 3604
— "other than subsection (c)" — the sale or rental of a single-family house by an owner who does not
own more than three such houses at any one time, and rooms or units in a dwelling of no more than
four families where the owner occupies one of the units. The single-family exemption is loaded with
conditions, and one of them is decisive for you: the sale or rental is excepted "only if such house
is sold or rented (A) without the use in any manner of the sales or rental facilities or the sales
or rental services of any real estate broker, agent, or salesman". The moment a licensed broker or
agent is used, the exemption is gone. Note also that the exemption never covers § 3604(c) — the
advertising prohibition applies to everyone, exempt owner or not. Section 3607 adds the religious
organization and private club exemptions and the housing-for-older-persons exemption, which relieves
only the familial status provisions and requires, for the 55-and-older category, that "at least 80
percent of the occupied units are occupied by at least one person who is 55 years of age or older"
plus published policies and compliance with the Secretary's verification rules.

**The Texas Fair Housing Act.** Property Code Chapter 301 "may be cited as the Texas Fair Housing
Act" (§ 301.001). Its purposes, at § 301.002, include to "provide for fair housing practices in this
state," to "create a procedure for investigating and settling complaints of discriminatory housing
practices," and — the clause that explains the chapter's shape — to "provide rights and remedies
substantially equivalent to those granted under federal law." Section 301.062 reinforces that: the
agency may adopt rules, "but substantive rules adopted by the commission shall impose obligations,
rights, and remedies that are the same as are provided in federal fair housing regulations." Texas
is deliberately tracking Washington. Where the two texts differ, they usually differ in wording
rather than in substance — but the wording is what you must state accurately, so learn the Texas
words too.

Section 301.021(a): "A person may not refuse to sell or rent, after the making of a bona fide offer,
refuse to negotiate for the sale or rental of, or in any other manner make unavailable or deny a
dwelling to another because of race, color, religion, sex, familial status, or national origin." Six
characteristics, as in the federal § 3604(a). Subsection (b) uses the same six for terms,
conditions, privileges, services and facilities. Subsection (c) contains a limit worth knowing:
"This section does not prohibit discrimination against a person because the person has been
convicted under federal law or the law of any state of the illegal manufacture or distribution of a
controlled substance."

Then the seven-characteristic sections. Section 301.022 forbids making, printing or publishing a
notice, statement or advertisement about the sale or rental of a dwelling "that indicates any
preference, limitation, or discrimination or the intention to make a preference, limitation, or
discrimination because of race, color, religion, sex, disability, familial status, or national
origin." Section 301.023 forbids representing "because of race, color, religion, sex, disability,
familial status, or national origin that a dwelling is not available for inspection for sale or
rental when the dwelling is available for inspection." Section 301.024 forbids, "for profit,"
inducing or attempting to induce another to sell or rent "by representations regarding the entry or
prospective entry into a neighborhood of a person of a particular race, color, religion, sex,
disability, familial status, or national origin" — the Texas blockbusting provision. Section
301.026(a) covers residential real estate related transactions with the same seven, and
§ 301.026(b)(2) defines those transactions to include "the selling, brokering, or appraising of
residential real property." Section 301.027 forbids denying "another access to, or membership or
participation in, a multiple-listing service, real estate brokers' organization, or other service,
organization, or facility relating to the business of selling or renting dwellings" on the same
seven grounds.

Texas states the disability provisions in its own section. Section 301.025(a) provides that "A
person may not discriminate in the sale or rental of, or make unavailable or deny, a dwelling to any
buyer or renter because of a disability of" the buyer or renter, a person residing in or intending
to reside in the dwelling after it is sold, rented or made available, or "any person associated with
the buyer or renter." Subsection (c) defines discrimination to include "a refusal to permit, at the
expense of the person having a disability, a reasonable modification of existing premises occupied
or to be occupied by the person if the modification may be necessary to afford the person full
enjoyment of the premises" and "a refusal to make a reasonable accommodation in rules, policies,
practices, or services if the accommodation may be necessary to afford the person equal opportunity
to use and enjoy a dwelling". That qualifier — "if the accommodation may be necessary to afford the
person equal opportunity to use and enjoy a dwelling" — is the whole test, and it is the clause most
often clipped off when the rule is repeated in an office meeting. Do not clip it. Subsection (f)
carries the Texas version of the direct threat limit: "This section does not require a dwelling to
be made available to an individual whose tenancy would constitute a direct threat to the health or
safety of other individuals or whose tenancy would result in substantial physical damage to the
property of others."

Texas defines its terms at § 301.003. "Disability" means "a mental or physical impairment that
substantially limits at least one major life activity, a record of the impairment, or being regarded
as having the impairment. The term does not include current illegal use or addiction to any drug or
illegal or federally controlled substance and does not apply to an individual because of an
individual's sexual orientation or because that individual is a transvestite." That is the statute's
own text, quoted whole because a partial quotation would misstate it. Section 301.004 supplies
familial status: a discriminatory act is committed because of familial status if it is committed
because the subject is "pregnant," "domiciled with an individual younger than 18 years of age" in
regard to whom the person is the parent or legal custodian or has the parent's or custodian's
written permission for domicile, or "in the process of obtaining legal custody of an individual
younger than 18 years of age." Section 301.0045 is a recent addition worth knowing: it defines a
protective hairstyle to include "braids, locks, and twists," and provides that a reference in the
chapter to discrimination because of race, other than in Subchapter I, "includes discrimination
because of or on the basis of a person's hair texture or protective hairstyle commonly or
historically associated with race."

The Texas exemptions are at §§ 301.041 through 301.044. Section 301.041(a) exempts from §§ 301.021,
301.023, 301.024 and 301.025 the sale or rental of a single-family house by an owner who does not
own more than three single-family houses at any one time, but only if "the house is sold or rented
without" the "use of the sales or rental facilities or services of a broker, agent, or salesperson
licensed under Chapter 1101, Occupations Code" and without prohibited advertising, and § 301.041(b)
limits that exemption to "one sale in a 24-month period if the owner was not the most recent
resident of the house at the time of the sale." Section 301.041(a)(2) exempts owner-occupied
dwellings of no more than four families. Section 301.042 carries the religious organization, private
club and appraisal exemptions; § 301.043 exempts qualifying elderly housing from the familial status
provisions; and § 301.044(b) provides that "This chapter does not affect a requirement of
nondiscrimination in any other state or federal law." Note the same architecture as the federal Act,
and the same practical conclusion: the owner-seller exemptions evaporate the instant a license
holder is engaged. The seller who says *it's my house, I can sell to whoever I want* is describing
an exemption that your listing agreement destroys. A section numbered § 301.045 was not held for
this course and is not taught here.

**Who enforces Texas fair housing.** The chapter's powers were transferred: § 301.0015 provides that
the powers and duties exercised by the Commission on Human Rights under the chapter are transferred
to the Texas Workforce Commission, and that a reference in the chapter to the commission "means the
Texas Workforce Commission." So the fair housing commission in Chapter 301 is not TREC, and a
discriminatory housing practice complaint is filed with that agency rather than with the Commission
that issued your license.

Now the provision that connects this unit to the rest of the module. Section 301.114 provides that
if the commission issues an order about a discriminatory housing practice "that occurs in the course
of a business subject to a licensing or regulation by a governmental agency," then not later than
the 30th day after the order it must send copies of the findings and order to that agency and
"recommend to the governmental agency appropriate disciplinary action." A fair housing order against
a Texas license holder does not stay in the fair housing system. It is delivered to TREC with a
recommendation attached.

**And the Commission's own rule is broader than either statute.** Title 22 TAC § 531.19 provides:
"No license holder shall inquire about, respond to or facilitate inquiries about, or make a
disclosure of an owner, previous or current occupant, potential purchaser, lessor, or potential
lessee of real property which indicates or is intended to indicate any preference, limitation, or
discrimination based on the following:" and then lists, in this order, "race;" "color;" "religion;"
"sex;" "national origin;" "ancestry;" "familial status;" or "disability." Eight characteristics —
the statutory seven plus ancestry. And the verbs are the point. The statutes prohibit refusing,
discriminating, representing, inducing, publishing. The rule prohibits *inquiring about*,
*responding to or facilitating inquiries about*, and *making a disclosure*. Under § 531.19 you can
violate the rule without refusing anyone anything. A buyer asks you on the phone whether a Katy
neighborhood is *mostly families like us*. If you answer, you have responded to an inquiry
indicating a preference. If you forward the question to the listing agent, you have facilitated it.
If you volunteer that the seller is an immigrant family, you have made a disclosure. None of that is
a refusal to sell, and all of it is a rule violation. The correct response is to say that you do not
answer questions of that kind, to explain that fair housing law prohibits it, and to redirect the
client to objective sources of neighborhood data they can evaluate themselves — that redirection is
practice, not law, and nothing is cited for it.

Finally, TRELA itself makes discrimination a ground for discipline in its own right. Section
1101.652(b)(32) reaches a license holder who, while engaged in real estate brokerage, "discriminates
against an owner, potential buyer, landlord, or potential tenant on the basis of race, color,
religion, sex, disability, familial status, national origin, or ancestry, including directing a
prospective buyer or tenant interested in equivalent properties to a different area based on the
race, color, religion, sex, disability, familial status, national origin, or ancestry of the
potential owner or tenant." Eight characteristics again, including ancestry, and the "including"
clause names steering explicitly. So one act of steering a Nigerian-born buyer away from a Sugar
Land subdivision can be a federal violation under 42 U.S.C. § 3604(a), a Texas statutory violation
under Property Code § 301.021(a), a Commission rule violation under 22 TAC § 531.19, and a TRELA
ground for revocation under § 1101.652(b)(32) — four separate exposures from one sentence spoken in
a car.

### Statute reading exercise: Reading § 301.025 and § 301.041 for what they do not say

Open Property Code § 301.025 and § 301.041 and read both in full before answering.

1. Section 301.025(c)(1) and (c)(2) both describe refusals that count as discrimination. Quote the
   qualifying clause at the end of each and explain, in your own words, how the two standards differ
   and who bears the cost in each case.
2. Section 301.025(c)(3) imposes design and construction duties. Read § 301.025(e) and § 301.025(g)
   and state precisely which buildings those duties reach and which they do not.
3. Section 301.041(a) exempts certain sales and rentals. List the sections of the chapter from which
   the exemption operates, and identify the sections it does *not* touch. What is the significance
   of a section being left off that list?
4. Section 301.041(a)(1)(B)(i) states a condition on the exemption. Explain what happens to the
   exemption when a seller in Waco signs a listing agreement with your brokerage, and identify who,
   after that point, is exposed under the chapter.
5. Section 301.041(d) defines when a person is "in the business of selling or renting a dwelling."
   Apply each of its three tests to a Fort Worth investor who owns six rent houses and self-manages
   them, and state your conclusion with the subdivision you relied on.

## Unit: TRELA and Rules of the Commission (Ethics)

The previous unit was law of general application, binding landlords, sellers and lenders as much as
license holders. This unit is the code that binds *you specifically because you hold a Texas
license*, and it has two layers. The first is Chapter 1101 of the Occupations Code — the Texas
Real Estate License Act, which the Commission's own rules abbreviate as "the Act," since 22 TAC
§ 535.1 defines "Act–Chapter 1101, Texas Occupations Code." The second is the Commission's rules in
Title 22 of the Texas Administrative Code, and in particular Chapter 531, titled Canons of
Professional Ethics and Client Relations. Those canons are short. There are seven sections held for
this course, and together they run to about a page. They are also, as a practical matter, the Texas
code of ethics — the one enforceable against every license holder in the state without regard to
what organizations they have joined.

**Who the canons apply to.** Section 531.1 defines the two terms that carry the chapter:
"Commission–The Texas Real Estate Commission" and "License Holder–A real estate broker or sales
agent licensed under Chapter 1101, Texas Occupations Code." Notice that the canons speak of the
"license holder," not of the broker alone. A sales agent cannot say that fidelity or competency is
the broker's problem. Each canon attaches to each individual license.

**Fidelity — 22 TAC § 531.2.** This is the fiduciary canon, and it is the single most important text
in this course. It opens: "A license holder, while acting as an agent for another, is a fiduciary.
Special obligations are imposed when such fiduciary relationships are created. They demand:" and
then states three demands. The first: "that the primary duty of the license holder is to represent
the interests of the client, and the license holder's position, in this respect, should be clear to
all parties concerned in a real estate transaction; that, however, the license holder, in performing
duties to the client, shall treat other parties to a transaction fairly". The second: "that the
license holder be faithful and observant to trust placed in the license holder, and be scrupulous
and meticulous in performing the license holder's functions". The third: "that the license holder
place no personal interest above that of the client."

Take those apart. The first demand does two jobs in one sentence. It establishes the primacy of the
client's interests, and it requires that your position be clear "to all parties concerned" — which
is why representation disclosure is not a formality but a canon obligation. Then it turns, with
"that, however," and requires fair treatment of the other side: a hierarchy with a floor, loyalty
owed to one and fairness owed to all. The second demand catches sloppiness rather than dishonesty —
"scrupulous and meticulous" is a standard of care, and an agent who is honest but careless with
deadlines and documents fails it. The third is the conflict-of-interest rule at its widest: not
merely no self-dealing, but "no personal interest above that of the client." Your commission and
your desire to close before month end are personal interests; the canon does not forbid having them,
it forbids placing them above the client's.

**Integrity — 22 TAC § 531.3.** In a single sentence: "A license holder has a special obligation to
exercise integrity in the discharge of the license holder's responsibilities, including employment
of prudence and caution so as to avoid misrepresentation, in any way, by acts of commission or
omission." The operative phrase is "by acts of commission or omission." Saying something false is a
misrepresentation. So is staying silent where speaking was required. And note the standard:
"prudence and caution." The canon does not require merely that you avoid lying; it requires you to
take care that you do not mislead by accident. An agent who repeats a seller's square footage claim
without checking it, and without saying where it came from, has not lied. Under § 531.3 that is not
a defence.

**Competency — 22 TAC § 531.4.** "It is the obligation of a license holder to be knowledgeable and
competent as a real estate brokerage practitioner. The license holder must: be informed on local
market issues and conditions affecting real estate in the geographic area where a license holder
provides services to a client; be informed on national, state, and local issues and developments in
the real estate industry; exercise judgment and skill in the performance of brokerage activities;
and be educated in the characteristics involved in the specific type of real estate being brokered
for others." Four duties, and the first and fourth are the ones that discipline agents. Geographic
competence: an agent based in Plano who takes a listing in Marfa because a friend asked is exposed
under the first clause unless she does the work to become informed on that market. Product-type
competence: an agent who has sold thirty suburban houses and accepts a listing on a working ranch,
an industrial building or a condominium regime he does not understand is exposed under the fourth.
TRELA backs the same idea from the statutory side: § 1101.652(a)(6) makes it a ground for discipline
if the license holder "fails to consider market conditions for the specific geographic area in which
the license holder is providing a service," and § 1101.652(b)(1) reaches a license holder who, while
engaged in real estate brokerage, "acts negligently or incompetently."

**Consumer Information — 22 TAC § 531.18.** The Commission "adopts by reference the Consumer
Protection Notice, TREC No. CN 1-5," and requires each license holder to provide it by "displaying
it in a readily noticeable location in each place of business the broker maintains" and by
"providing a link to it in a readily noticeable place on the homepage of each business website,"
labeled either "Texas Real Estate Commission Consumer Protection Notice", in at least 10 point font;
or "TREC Consumer Protection Notice", in at least 12 point font. The rule defines the business
websites this reaches, and where the link goes on a social media account; Module 08 teaches the
notice as a document.

**Information About Brokerage Services — 22 TAC § 531.20.** The Commission adopts the IABS Notice by
reference and requires each license holder to link it on the homepage of each business website and
to deliver the completed notice at first substantive communication, as Occupations Code § 1101.558
requires. The IABS rule and its statute are taught in full in Module 02; what belongs here is that
§ 531.20 is a canon, that failing to comply with it is therefore a rule violation reachable under
§ 1101.656(a), and that 22 TAC § 535.191 prices it in the middle band.

**Discriminatory Practices — 22 TAC § 531.19.** Set out in full in the previous unit, and it sits
here as well: it is the Commission's statement that trading in discriminatory preference, even as a
conduit, is professional misconduct.

**Two rules that operationalize fidelity.** Title 22 TAC § 535.156 is the Commission's expansion of
what dishonesty, bad faith and untrustworthiness mean: "A license holder's relationship with the
license holder's principal is that of a fiduciary. A license holder shall convey to the principal
all known information which would affect the principal's decision on whether or not to make, accept
or reject offers; however, if the principal has agreed in writing that offers are not to be
submitted after the principal has entered into a contract to buy, sell, rent, or lease a property,
the license holder shall have no duty to submit offers to the principal after the principal has
accepted an offer." That "however" clause is a qualifier students drop: the duty to submit offers
can be switched off, but only by the principal, only in writing, and only after a contract. The rule
continues: "The license holder must put the interest of the license holder's principal above the
license holder's own interest. A license holder must deal honestly and fairly with all parties;
however, the license holder represents only the principal and owes a duty of fidelity to such
principal." Then two affirmative duties: "A license holder has an affirmative duty to keep the
principal informed at all times of significant information applicable to the transaction or
transactions in which the license holder is acting as agent for the principal," and "A license
holder has a duty to convey accurate information to members of the public with whom the license
holder deals."

**The compensation rules.** Section 1101.651(b), quoted in full, provides: "A sales agent may not
accept compensation for a real estate transaction from a person other than the broker that is
sponsoring the sales agent or was sponsoring the sales agent when the sales agent earned the
compensation." The "or was sponsoring" clause matters: an agent who changes brokerages is still paid
by the old broker for transactions earned there. Subsection (a) forbids a licensed broker from
paying a commission to or otherwise compensating a person for performing an act of a broker unless
the person is "a license holder" or "a real estate broker licensed in another state who does not
conduct in this state any of the negotiations for which the commission or other compensation is
paid." Subsection (c) provides that "A sales agent may not pay a commission to a person except
through the broker that is sponsoring the sales agent at that time." Subsection (d), which governs the intermediary and its appointed license
holders, ends with two subdivisions that are pure ethics: such a broker or appointee may not "treat
a party to a transaction dishonestly" or "violate this chapter."

**Section 1101.652 and its architecture.** This is the disciplinary heart of TRELA, and you must be
precise about its internal structure, because the subsection you are in changes what the Commission
must prove.

Subsection (a) lists eight grounds that apply to a license holder generally, without regard to
whether the conduct occurred in brokerage. They are: entering a plea of guilty or nolo contendere to
or being convicted of a felony or a criminal offense involving fraud, with the appeal time elapsed
or the conviction affirmed; procuring or attempting to procure a license by fraud,
misrepresentation, deceit, or material misstatement in an application; failing to honor within a
reasonable time a payment issued to the commission after a certified-mail request; failing to
provide within a reasonable time information requested by the commission relating to a formal or
informal complaint; failing to surrender to the owner, without just cause, a document or instrument
the owner requests and the license holder holds; failing "to consider market conditions for the
specific geographic area in which the license holder is providing a service"; failing to notify the
commission "not later than the 30th day after the date of a final conviction or the entry of a plea
of guilty or nolo contendere" of a felony or a fraud offense; and disregarding or violating the
chapter or Chapter 1102.

Subsection (a-1) contains three grounds concerning the license holder's own transactions and
records: engaging "in misrepresentation, dishonesty, or fraud when selling, buying, trading, or
leasing real property in the name of" the license holder, the license holder's spouse, or "a person
related to the license holder within the first degree by consanguinity"; failing or refusing to
produce on request, within a reasonable time, a document, book or record in the license holder's
possession relating to a real estate transaction the license holder conducted; and failing "to use a
contract form required by the commission under Section 1101.155."

Subsection (b) contains thirty-four grounds, and it is gated by six words: the Commission may act
"if the license holder, while engaged in real estate brokerage" does any of them. That gate is why
subsection (a-1) exists separately — conduct in your own deals is not brokerage, so the Legislature
gave it its own home. Within subsection (b) you should be able to place the recurring ones from
memory: negligence or incompetence at (b)(1); dishonesty, bad faith or untrustworthiness at (b)(2);
material misrepresentation to a potential buyer about a known significant defect at (b)(3) and
failure to disclose such a defect at (b)(4); false promise at (b)(5); failure "to make clear to all
parties to a real estate transaction the party for whom the license holder is acting" at (b)(7);
receiving compensation from more than one party without full knowledge and consent of all at (b)(8);
failing within a reasonable time to account for or remit another's money at (b)(9) and commingling
at (b)(10); failure to specify a definite termination date at (b)(12); lottery at (b)(14) and
deceptive practice at (b)(15); negotiating around an exclusive agency at (b)(22); misleading
advertising at (b)(23); the escrow deposit and disbursement rules at (b)(30) and (b)(31);
discrimination at (b)(32); disregarding or violating the chapter at (b)(33); and failure "to enter
into a written agreement with a prospective buyer as required by Section 1101.563" at (b)(34).

**Where the Commission fills in the statute by rule.** Two examples show the pattern. Section
535.145 tells you what "false promise" means for § 1101.652(b)(5): it "includes both oral and
written promises," and the fact that a written agreement does not recite the promise, or that nobody
detrimentally relied on it, "will not prevent the Commission from determining that a false promise
was made." The rule goes further: "neither a written contractual provision disclaiming oral
representations nor the Texas Rules of Evidence Rule 1004, the parol evidence rule, shall prevent
the Commission from considering oral promises made by a license holder." Read that twice. The merger
clause in your contract does not protect you in a Commission proceeding. What you said at the
kitchen table is evidence. Section 535.149 does the same work for § 1101.652(b)(14) and (b)(15):
it defines the elements of a lottery, states that "The giving of gifts as an inducement for
prospective clients does not violate this section or §1101.652(b)(14) of the Act, but license
holders when procuring prospects must otherwise comply with the provisions of §535.20 of this
title," and provides that deceptive practices "include, but are not limited to the acts described
in the Texas Business and Commerce Code §17.46, done in a manner defined in that section."

**The unauthorized practice of law.** Section 1101.654(a) is one of the few places TRELA says the
Commission "shall" — not "may" — act. It provides that the Commission "shall suspend or revoke the
license or certificate of registration of a license or certificate holder who is not a licensed
attorney in this state and who, for consideration, a reward, or a pecuniary benefit, present or
anticipated, direct or indirect, or in connection with the person's employment, agency, or fiduciary
relationship as a license or certificate holder" either "drafts an instrument, other than a form
described by Section 1101.155, that transfers or otherwise affects an interest in real property" or
"advises a person regarding the validity or legal sufficiency of an instrument or the validity of
title to real property." Subsection (b) supplies the safe harbor: a license holder who completes a
contract form for a sale, exchange, option or lease "incidental to acting as a broker is not engaged
in the unauthorized or illegal practice of law in this state if the form was" adopted by the
commission for that type of transaction, prepared by a Texas-licensed attorney and approved by that
attorney for that type of transaction, or "prepared by the property owner or by an attorney and
required by the property owner."

Rule 537.11 draws the same line in operational terms and is taught in Module 04. What belongs here
is the price: 22 TAC § 535.191 puts a § 1101.654(a) violation in the middle band.

**How a complaint becomes an investigation.** Section 1101.204(b) is the trigger: the commission
"shall investigate the actions and records of a license holder if" "a person submits a signed,
written complaint" and "the complaint and any evidence presented with the complaint provide
reasonable cause for an investigation." Two limits follow immediately. Subsection (c): the
commission "may not conduct an investigation of a person licensed under this chapter or Chapter 1102
in connection with a complaint submitted later than the fourth anniversary of the date of the
incident that is the subject of the complaint." Subsection (f): "An investigation or other action
against a person licensed under this chapter or Chapter 1102 may not be initiated on the basis of an
anonymous complaint." So: signed, and within four years of the incident. Subsection (d) requires
prompt written notice to the license holder under investigation "unless after deliberation the
commission decides against notification," and subsection (e) forbids an undercover or covert
investigation "unless the commission expressly authorizes the investigation after considering the
circumstances and determining that the investigation is necessary to implement this chapter."
Subsection (h) requires priority for consumer complaints and a risk-based approach to the rest, and
subsection (i) requires dismissal if the commission determines "at any time that an allegation made
or formal complaint submitted by a person is inappropriate or without merit."

Rule 535.141 is where a sales agent's complaint becomes a broker's problem. Its first sentence: "A
complaint which names a licensed real estate sales agent as the subject of the complaint but does
not specifically name the sales agent's sponsoring broker, is a complaint against the broker
sponsoring the sales agent at the time of any alleged violation for the limited purposes of
determining the broker's involvement in any alleged violation and whether the broker fulfilled the
broker's professional responsibilities provided the complaint concerns the conduct of the sales
agent as an agent for the broker." The rule does the same for designated brokers of business
entities, stating that "The designated broker is responsible for all real estate brokerage
activities performed by, on behalf of, or through a business entity." A copy of the complaint notice
goes to the broker who sponsors the respondent if the respondent is a sales agent.

Rule 535.141 also publishes the Commission's triage. Using the criteria in § 1101.204, complaints
are prioritized as Level 1 — "Fraud or misrepresentation that involves loss of $10,000 or more,"
"Continuing threat to public welfare," "Unlicensed activity"; Level 2 — "Fraud or misrepresentation
that involves loss of less than $10,000," "Negligence," and specified inspector violations; and
Level 3 — "Technical violations" and the remaining categories. The rule adds that if information
obtained during an investigation "reveals reasonable cause to believe the respondents to the
complaint may have committed other violations of the Act or rules, no additional authorization shall
be required to investigate and take action based upon the information." An investigation that starts
as a late-response complaint can end as a trust-account case.

The rule also governs life under a suspension: "A person whose license has been suspended may not
during the period of any suspension perform, attempt to perform, or advertise to perform any act for
which a license is required by the Act or Commission rules," and it requires written notice not
later than the third day before the date of the suspension to the sponsoring broker, to sponsored
agents, to other parties to contracts for licensed services, and to principals and other brokers in
transactions in which the agent is acting, plus remittance of trust money "in accordance with the
instructions of the principals." Rule 535.191 prices practising during suspension in the top band,
listing "22 TAC §535.141(g)".

Investigation files are confidential. Section 1101.2051(a) makes information or material "including
an investigation file" confidential and not subject to disclosure where it is prepared or compiled
by the commission in connection with a complaint, investigation or audit, with disclosure permitted
to a listed set of recipients that includes the respondent. Subsection (d-1) requires the commission
to "protect the identity of a complainant to the extent possible by excluding the complainant's
identifying information from a complaint notice sent to a respondent," and subsection (e) opens the
file to disclosure "on the dismissal or final resolution of a complaint, investigation, or audit."

**What sanctions are authorized, and what they cost.** Section 1101.656 supplies the non-monetary
sanctions: suspension, revocation, probation of a suspended license, or reprimand for violating the
chapter or a commission rule; the power to probate a suspension, revocation or cancellation "under
reasonable terms determined by the commission"; and the power to require a license holder on
probation to "report regularly to the commission on matters that are the basis of the probation," to
"limit practice to an area prescribed by the commission," or to "continue to renew professional
education until the license holder attains a degree of skill satisfactory to the commission in the
area that is the basis of the probation."

Section 1101.702(a) sets the ceiling: "The amount of an administrative penalty may not exceed $5,000
for each violation. Each day a violation continues or occurs may be considered a separate violation
for purposes of imposing a penalty." Subsection (b) lists the five factors the executive director
must weigh: "the seriousness of the violation, including the nature, circumstances, extent, and
gravity of the prohibited acts"; "the history of previous violations"; "the amount necessary to
deter a future violation"; "efforts to correct the violation"; and "any other matter that justice
may require." Subsection (c) directs the Commission to adopt a schedule by rule, and provides that
the rules "must provide authority for the commission to suspend or revoke a license in addition to
or instead of imposing an administrative penalty."

That schedule is 22 TAC § 535.191, and it opens by confirming the point: "The Commission may suspend
or revoke a license or take other disciplinary action authorized by the Act in addition to or
instead of assessing the administrative penalties set forth in this section." It then sets three
bands, each expressed per violation per day: $100 to $1,500, $500 to $3,000, and $1,000 to $5,000.
The Commission may add up to twice the amount assessed under those bands where a person has a
history of previous violations, subject to the § 1101.702(a) ceiling. The complete band-by-band
table is reproduced in the optional reference section at the end of this module; the bands that
matter for the violations this module teaches are given as each violation is taught.

Study the bands and you learn the Commission's priorities better than from any lecture. Failing to
maintain a current mailing address is in the bottom band, and so is failing to enter into the
written buyer representation agreement § 1101.563 requires. Failing to give the IABS notice —
§ 531.20 — is in the middle band, alongside acting negligently or incompetently and failing to give
a definite termination date. Discrimination, commingling, failing to account for another's money,
breaching the fiduciary rule of § 535.156, and practising during a suspension are all in the top
band. The schedule tells you which mistakes the state treats as bookkeeping and which it treats as
betrayal.

**Process, once the executive director decides.** Section 1101.703 lets the executive director issue
a notice of violation stating a brief summary of the alleged violation, the recommendation on
penalty or other sanction, and "that the respondent has the right to a hearing to contest the
alleged violation, the recommended penalty, or both." Section 1101.704(a) gives the license holder
twenty days from receipt to accept the determination or "request in writing a hearing on the
occurrence of the violation, the amount of the penalty, or both," and subsection (b) provides that
if the person accepts, "or fails to respond in a timely manner to the notice," the commission by
order approves the determination and orders payment or imposes the sanction. Missing the twentieth
day is not a neutral act; it is an acceptance. If a hearing is requested, an administrative law
judge of SOAH conducts it, makes findings of fact and conclusions of law, and issues a proposal for
decision (§ 1101.705). Section 1101.707(a) then gives thirty days after the order becomes final to
pay or to "file a petition for judicial review contesting the occurrence of the violation, the
amount of the penalty, or both," with enforcement stayed by paying into escrow, by supersedeas bond,
or by an affidavit of financial inability that the executive director may contest within five days.

**Who else is on the hook.** Section 1101.803 is short and severe: "A licensed broker is liable to
the commission, the public, and the broker's clients for any conduct engaged in under this chapter
by the broker or by a sales agent associated with or acting for the broker." The compliance program
that follows from that liability — the sponsoring broker's written policies, the notice of
authorized activities, the coaching requirement, and the four-year record retention — is 22 TAC
§ 535.2, taught in Module 09. Note only the number here: four years for records under § 535.2, four
years for the complaint window under § 1101.204(c). Those two numbers are designed to line up, and a
broker who purges at three years has destroyed the file the Commission is entitled to ask for.

Finally, TRELA allocates misrepresentation liability between license holders and their principals in
§ 1101.805, which Module 03 teaches with both of its conditions. What belongs here is its limit:
subsection (c) provides that the section "does not diminish a broker's responsibility for the acts
or omissions of a sales agent associated with or acting for the broker." Nothing in it moves the
broker's exposure under § 1101.803.

## Unit: Professional Codes of Ethics

Every ethical obligation in this module so far has come from the state — a statute or an
administrative rule. This unit is about a different animal: the code of ethics adopted by a private
trade association, which is what most people mean when they say *the Code of Ethics* in a real
estate office. Agents confuse the two systems constantly, and the confusion is expensive in both
directions: ignoring a Commission complaint in the belief that it is an association matter, or
panicking at an association grievance as though the license were at stake.

**Start with a caution about sources.** No trade association's code of ethics was held as a source
for this course. This module therefore does not reproduce, quote, paraphrase, summarize article by
article, or number the provisions of any association's code. Those documents are copyrighted works
owned by the associations that publish them, and the honest thing to say is that if you join an
association you will receive its code, be required to take periodic training on it, and be bound by
the version in force at the time. What this unit teaches instead is the structure such codes share,
the legal character of the obligation they create, and the Texas law that governs the associations
themselves — all of which comes from sources that were held and read.

**What Texas law says a trade association is.** Title 22 TAC § 535.1 provides: "Trade Association–A nonprofit voluntary member association or
organization: whose membership consists primarily of persons who are licensed as real estate license
holders and pay membership dues to the association or organization; that is governed by a board of
directors elected by the members; and that subscribes to a written code of professional conduct or
ethics."

Read the three elements and you have the whole legal character of these bodies. First, "nonprofit
voluntary member association" whose members "pay membership dues" — you are not conscripted, you buy
in. Second, "governed by a board of directors elected by the members," which is self-governance by
the regulated, the opposite of the arrangement TRELA makes for the Commission. Third, a body is not
a Trade Association in the Commission's sense unless it "subscribes to a written code of
professional conduct or ethics." The definition treats a written ethics code as *constitutive* of
what a trade association is.

TRELA supplies the mirror image. Section 1101.053(a) defines a "Texas trade association" as "a
cooperative and voluntarily joined statewide association of business or professional competitors in
this state designed to assist its members and its industry or profession in dealing with mutual
business or professional problems and in promoting their common interest." Note the frankness of
that definition: an association of *competitors*, organized to promote *their common interest*. Then
subsection (b) does something you should not skip past. A person may not be a member of the
Commission, and may not be a Commission employee in a bona fide executive, administrative or
professional capacity, if "the person is an officer, employee, or paid consultant of a Texas trade
association in the real estate industry" or if "the person's spouse is an officer, manager, or paid
consultant of a Texas trade association in the real estate industry." The Legislature deliberately
built a wall between the regulator and the trade body. That wall is the clearest possible statement
that these are two different systems with two different purposes: the association exists to advance
its members' common interest; the Commission exists to protect the public. A code of ethics can
serve both, but you should never assume they point the same direction in every case.

**What such a code typically does, structurally.** Described at the level of structure rather than
text, a trade association code of ethics in this industry generally contains: a statement of
aspirational principle, usually in a preamble, that is not itself enforced; a body of enforceable
provisions organized around duties to clients and customers, duties to the public, and duties to
other members of the association; a mechanism for interpreting those provisions over time, usually
through published case interpretations decided by a committee; and a disciplinary procedure with a
hearing panel drawn from the membership. Many such codes also provide a separate arbitration
procedure for money disputes between members — most commonly, who is entitled to a cooperating
brokerage fee. That track is not disciplinary at all; it is a private substitute for suing each
other.

**The legal character of the obligation.** An association code binds by contract, not by statute.
When you join and pay dues you agree to abide by the association's governing documents, and those
documents include the code and the enforcement machinery. Every practical difference follows from
that.

Because the obligation is contractual, the association can only reach members. It has no
jurisdiction over a Texas license holder who never joined, no matter how badly that person behaved.
The Commission, by contrast, reaches every license holder in the state: 22 TAC § 531.1 defines
License Holder as "A real estate broker or sales agent licensed under Chapter 1101, Texas
Occupations Code," and the canons apply on that basis alone.

Because the obligation is contractual, the sanctions are those a private body can impose on its own
members: fines payable to the association, required education, letters of reprimand, suspension of
membership and of the privileges it carries, and expulsion. The privileges are what sting, because
they typically include participation in a multiple listing service and the data and lockbox systems
that come with it. But an association cannot suspend or revoke your Texas license; only the
Commission can, under § 1101.656(a) and § 1101.652. Conversely, the Commission cannot expel you from
a private association or take away your MLS access. Two systems, two sets of tools.

Because the obligation is contractual, the procedure is the association's own, not Chapter 2001 of
the Government Code. There is no administrative law judge, no state agency record, and no appeal to
a district court "in the county in which the administrative hearing was held" as § 1101.658(a)
provides for Commission matters. Whatever review exists is internal, defined by the association's
bylaws.

Because the obligation is contractual, the code can be — and generally is — *stricter* than the law.
That is the point of a private code: to commit members to standards above the statutory floor, and
to advertise that commitment to the public. Nothing prevents an association from requiring conduct
that TRELA does not require. But the reverse does not follow, and this is the error to avoid: an
association code cannot license conduct that Texas law forbids. If a code's disciplinary body
declines to act on something, that is not a finding that the conduct was lawful, and it has no
effect at all on the Commission's authority. And if you were expelled from an association, that is
not a finding that you violated TRELA.

**Where the law does reach associations directly.** Three places, all held and read for this course.
First, membership discrimination. Under 42 U.S.C. § 3606 it is unlawful "to deny any person access
to or membership or participation in any multiple-listing service, real estate brokers' organization
or other service, organization, or facility relating to the business of selling or renting
dwellings, or to discriminate against him in the terms or conditions of such access, membership, or
participation, on account of race, color, religion, sex, handicap, familial status, or national
origin," and Property Code § 301.027 states the same prohibition in Texas terms. An association's
private character does not immunize its admission decisions.

Second, information sharing. Occupations Code § 1101.804 provides that "A license holder or
nonprofit real estate board or association that provides information about real property sales
prices or the terms of a sale for the purpose of facilitating the listing, selling, leasing,
financing, or appraisal of real property is not liable to another person for providing that
information unless the disclosure of that information is specifically prohibited by statute." That
is a deliberate shelter for the MLS function, and note the qualifier at the end: the shelter fails
where a statute specifically prohibits the disclosure.

Third, licensure and forms. Rule 535.5 provides that "Trade associations or other organizations that
provide an electronic listing service for their members, but do not receive compensation when the
real estate is sold, are not required to be licensed under the Act" — so running an MLS is not, by
itself, brokerage. And Rule 537.11 — taught in Module 04 — permits a license holder, where the
Commission has approved no mandatory form, to use a form "prepared by an attorney licensed by this
state, or a trade association in consultation with an attorney licensed by this state," subject to
the disclosures the rule lists. A trade association may publish forms for its members, but only
through a Texas attorney and only with those disclosures on the form.

**The practical difference between a private ethics complaint and a Commission complaint.** Most of
it follows from the contractual character just described: the association applies its own code, by
its own procedure, to its own members, on its own deadlines, and what is at stake is membership and
MLS privileges rather than the license. Three things do not follow from it. First, who may start the
matter. An association's grievance process runs on the terms its bylaws set, and those bylaws were
not held for this course and are not described here; a Commission complaint must be signed and
written under § 1101.204(b), may not be anonymous under § 1101.204(f), and may also be brought by
"The commission or commission staff" on the Commission's own initiative under § 1101.204(a). Second,
the Commission's clock is the four-year window of § 1101.204(c). Third, the Commission's
investigation file is confidential under § 1101.2051 and rule 535.141 draws in your sponsoring
broker, both on the terms set out in the previous unit.

The two proceedings are not alternatives, and neither is a prerequisite to the other. A consumer
harmed by your conduct may file with the association, file with the Commission, and sue you, in any
order or all at once; § 1101.801, quoted in Unit 1, is the basis for that, and nothing in TRELA
makes an association's decision binding on the Commission or the reverse.

A last word on how to hold a private code if you join one. A code adopted by an association of
competitors is, by the definition in § 1101.053(a) itself, an instrument that serves "their common
interest" as well as the public's. Where it raises the standard above the statutory floor, follow it
— you agreed to. Where the question is what the *law* requires, the answer is never in the code. It
is in Chapter 1101, in the Commission's rules, and in the fair housing statutes.

### Applied exercise: Two forums in Denton

A Denton sales agent, Ruben, belongs to a local trade association and holds an active Texas license.
He lists a house and, over three weeks, does the following. He tells the seller he has *a buyer
ready at $395,000* when he has no such buyer, in order to get the listing. He then advertises the
property in a way that omits his broker's name entirely. When a cooperating agent from a rival
brokerage submits an offer, Ruben does not present it to the seller for nine days because he wants
his own buyer to have first chance; the seller had signed nothing about withholding offers. The
cooperating agent files a grievance with the association. Two months later the seller, having
learned all of this, files a signed written complaint with the Commission.

1. Identify each of Ruben's three acts and, for each, cite the TRELA subdivision or Commission rule
   it violates. Quote the operative language for at least two of them.
2. The association's hearing panel finds Ruben violated its code and fines him. Explain, using
   § 1101.656(a), § 1101.702(a) and § 1101.801, whether that outcome resolves, reduces or affects
   the Commission's proceeding in any way.
3. Ruben's defence to the *buyer ready at $395,000* statement is that the listing agreement contains
   a clause disclaiming all oral representations. Read 22 TAC § 535.145 and explain, quoting it, why
   that defence fails before the Commission.
4. The seller's complaint names Ruben only. Using 22 TAC § 535.141, state who else is now a
   respondent and for what limited purposes, and identify from § 535.2 two of the written policies
   the Commission will expect that person to produce.
5. Using 22 TAC § 535.191, assign a penalty band to each of Ruben's three violations and explain
   what the ranking of those bands tells you about how the Commission weighs advertising failures
   against failures of fidelity.

## Unit: Minimum Ethical Standards

The last four units gave you law, rules, canons, forums and penalty schedules. This unit answers the
question a working license holder actually asks: what, concretely, is the minimum I must do? The
minimum is not a number of hours or a list of forms. It is a set of behaviours, each tracing back to
a canon or a rule you can now cite. Do all of it, every time, and you will be inside the line the
State of Texas has drawn.

**1. Make your position clear to everyone in the transaction.** Canon § 531.2, quoted in Unit 3,
requires the license holder's position to be clear to all parties concerned in the transaction.
Section 1101.652(b)(7) makes it a ground for discipline to fail "to make clear to all parties to a
real estate transaction the party for whom the license holder is acting." Section 1101.558(b)
requires disclosure of representation, "orally or in writing," at the time of first contact with
another party or another party's license holder. The minimum standard is therefore not *hand over a
form when you remember*. It is: at first contact, say who you represent; at first substantive
communication about a specific property, deliver the IABS notice by one of the delivery methods
22 TAC § 531.20 allows, which Module 02 sets out in full.

**2. Put the client first and say so in writing where the statute requires.** Canon § 531.2 and
22 TAC § 535.156, both quoted in Unit 3, forbid placing a personal interest above the client's. With
a buyer, the statute now requires paper before you begin: § 1101.563 requires a written agreement
with a prospective buyer of residential real property before you show property or present an offer,
and prescribes the terms it must contain; Module 05 teaches it. What is yours here is the
consequence: failing to do it is § 1101.652(b)(34), in the $100 - $1,500 band of 22 TAC § 535.191.
Note also § 1101.652(b)(12): every agreement in which you agree to perform services requiring a
license, other than a property management contract, must "specify a definite termination date that
is not subject to prior notice."

**3. Tell the client everything that bears on the decision, promptly.** Rule 535.156, quoted in
Unit 3, imposes two affirmative duties: convey all known information that would affect the
principal's decision on offers — subject only to the written post-contract opt-out the rule itself
describes — and keep the principal informed at all times of significant information applicable to
the transaction. Section 1101.557(b)(2) requires a broker who
represents a party to "inform the party if the broker receives material information related to a
transaction to list, buy, sell, or lease the party's real estate, including the receipt of an offer
by the broker," and § 1101.557(b)(3) requires the broker to "at a minimum, answer the party's
questions and present any offer to or from the party." And 22 TAC § 535.157 fixes the clock: two
calendar days, to your principal, to a license holder representing another party, or to an
unrepresented party.

**4. Be accurate with everyone, including the people you do not represent.** Canon § 531.2 requires
fair treatment of the other parties, rule 535.156 requires honest and fair dealing with all parties
and accurate information to the public, and canon § 531.3 requires prudence and caution against
misrepresentation by act or omission — all three quoted in Unit 3. Section 1101.652(b)(3) and (b)(4)
make it a ground for discipline to misrepresent to a potential buyer, or to fail to disclose to a
potential buyer, a significant defect known to the license holder — including "a latent structural
defect" — "that would be a significant factor to a reasonable and prudent buyer in making a decision
to purchase real property." The minimum standard: do not repeat a fact you have not verified without
saying where it came from, and never conceal a defect you know about.

**5. Do not promise what you cannot deliver.** Section 1101.652(b)(5) reaches a false promise "that
is likely to influence a person to enter into an agreement when the license holder is unable or does
not intend to keep the promise," and § 1101.652(b)(17) reaches guaranteeing, or authorizing or
permitting a person to guarantee, "that future profits will result from a resale of real property."
Rule 535.145, set out in Unit 3, closes the escape routes: the oral promise counts, and neither the
merger clause nor the parol evidence rule keeps the Commission from considering what you said.

**6. Handle other people's money as though it were held in trust, because it is.** Section
1101.652(b)(9) reaches failure "within a reasonable time to properly account for or remit money that
is received by the license holder and that belongs to another person"; (b)(10) reaches commingling
"money that belongs to another person with the license holder's own money"; (b)(30) requires deposit
of escrow or trust funds, within a reasonable time, with a title company or in a custodial, trust or
escrow account, in the terms quoted in Unit 1; and (b)(31) forbids disbursing that money "before the
completion or termination of the real estate transaction." Rule 535.2, taught in Module 09, makes
trust-fund handling a named broker responsibility. Under 22 TAC § 535.191,
subdivisions (b)(9) and (b)(10) sit in the top penalty band and (b)(30) and (b)(31) in the middle
band.

**7. Take only compensation you are entitled to, from the right person, with disclosure.** Section
1101.651(b) and (c), quoted in Unit 3, confine a sales agent to compensation from the sponsoring
broker and to paying a commission only through that broker. Section 1101.652(b)(8) reaches
receiving
compensation from more than one party "without the full knowledge and consent of all parties to the
transaction"; (b)(13) reaches accepting, receiving or charging "an undisclosed commission, rebate,
or direct profit on an expenditure made for a principal"; and (b)(11) reaches paying or dividing a
fee with a person who is not a license holder or a broker or sales agent licensed in another state.
Two rules fill in the detail and are taught elsewhere: 22 TAC § 535.148 conditions a fee from anyone
other than the person you represent on prior disclosure to and consent from your client, and governs
settlement-service referral fees (Module 05); 22 TAC § 535.147 governs sharing compensation with
unlicensed persons and rebates to a party (Module 09).

**8. Disclose when you are on the other side of the deal.** Rule 535.144, taught clause by clause in
Module 12, extends § 1101.652(a-1)(1) beyond your own name to deals done for close family, for
entities you substantially own and for trusts you are tied to, and requires written disclosure of
your license-holder status before any contract of sale or rental agreement. Section 1101.652(b)(16)
separately reaches acting "in a dual capacity as broker and undisclosed principal in a real estate
transaction."

**9. Be competent for the market and the property type, and say no when you are not.** Canon
§ 531.4, quoted in Unit 3, requires geographic and product-type competence; § 1101.652(a)(6) makes
failure to consider market conditions for the specific geographic area a ground for discipline;
§ 1101.652(b)(1) reaches negligence and incompetence in brokerage; and 22 TAC § 535.2, taught in
Module 09, puts the same obligation on the sponsoring broker's written policies. The practical edge
students resist: declining a listing you are not equipped to handle, or bringing in a competent
co-broker, is compliance, not a failure of ambition.

**10. Stay out of the practice of law.** Section 1101.654(a), quoted in Unit 3, commands suspension
or revocation for a non-attorney license holder who drafts an instrument affecting an interest in
real property other than a form described by § 1101.155, or who advises on the validity or legal
sufficiency of an instrument or the validity of title. Rule 537.11, taught in Module 04, states the operational line: no legal advice, no drafting or recommending contract
language, no discouraging a principal from employing an attorney, and an affirmative duty to send
principals to an attorney where a transaction involves unusual matters or an instrument that must be
acknowledged and filed of record.

**11. Never trade in preference or prejudice, in either direction.** Canon § 531.19, set out in
Unit 2, forbids inquiring about, responding to or facilitating inquiries about, or disclosing any of
its eight listed characteristics in a way indicating a preference, limitation or discrimination.
Section 1101.652(b)(32) makes discrimination a ground for revocation and names steering expressly.
The minimum standard is behavioural, not attitudinal: you decline the question, you say why, and you
never pass it along.

**12. Give the required notices and keep the required records.** Rule 531.18 requires the Consumer
Protection Notice displayed and linked as Unit 3 sets out. Rule 531.20 requires the IABS link and
the completed notice at first substantive communication (Module 02). Rule 535.17
requires a prescribed written statement with any broker price opinion, comparative market analysis
or estimate of worth or sale price (Module 12). Rule 535.2 sets the broker's four-year record
retention (Module 09), which is the same four years as the complaint window in § 1101.204(c).

**13. Answer the Commission.** Section 1101.652(a)(4) makes it a ground for discipline to fail "to
provide, within a reasonable time, information requested by the commission that relates to a formal
or informal complaint to the commission that would indicate a violation of this chapter or Chapter
1102," and § 1101.652(a-1)(2) reaches failure or refusal to produce, on request and within a
reasonable time, a document, book or record in your possession relating to a transaction you
conducted. Both are self-inflicted: the underlying complaint may be meritless, and a failure to
respond is a fresh, independent violation that survives the dismissal of the original one.

**What this adds up to.** Every one of the thirteen has a citation behind it in this module, and
together they are the standard: loyal to the client, fair to everyone else, accurate in what you say
and in what you leave unsaid, competent for the work you accept, exact with other people's money,
and in writing about your role, your interest and your compensation.

Here is the test to apply when you are unsure, and it is a practice test, not a legal one, so
nothing is cited for it: assume that the entire file — every email, every text, every note of every
phone call — will be read aloud, in order, by a lawyer for the other side, in front of an
administrative law judge, four years from now. Would the record show a professional who put the
client first and treated everyone else fairly? If yes, you are at or above the minimum. If you find
yourself hoping a particular message will not surface, you already know the answer, and you should
fix the underlying conduct rather than the message.

### Module recap

This module began by insisting that law, ethics and morals are not three grades of seriousness but
three enforcement systems. Law is enforced by the state through courts and, in fair housing, through
administrative penalties as well: Property Code § 301.171 makes intimidation by force a Class A
misdemeanor, § 301.151(a) lets an aggrieved person file a civil action in district court, and
§ 301.112 lets a state agency assess civil penalties administratively. Ethics, for a Texas license holder, is enforced by the Commission under
§ 1101.656(a), which authorizes suspension, revocation, probation or reprimand for violating "this
chapter or a commission rule," and under § 1101.701(a), which authorizes an administrative penalty
for violating the chapter, a rule or an order. Morals are enforced by nobody — and § 1101.802, which
removes civil and criminal liability for not disclosing whether an occupant had AIDS, an HIV-related
illness or HIV infection, is the standing reminder that a moral impulse followed uncritically can
walk you straight into a violation of 22 TAC § 531.19.

The second unit set out the law that constrains your conduct, and the discipline it demands is
precision about lists. Federal § 3604(a) and (b) name race, color, religion, sex, familial status
and national origin; § 3604(c) adds handicap; the substantive disability prohibitions live at
§ 3604(f), where subparagraph (3)(A) covers modifications made "at the expense of the handicapped
person" that "may be necessary to afford such person full enjoyment of the premises" and
subparagraph (3)(B) covers accommodations in "rules, policies, practices, or services, when such
accommodations may be necessary to afford such person equal opportunity to use and enjoy a
dwelling." Texas mirrors this in Property Code § 301.021 (six characteristics), §§ 301.022–301.027
(seven, including disability), and § 301.025, whose accommodation clause carries the same
indispensable qualifier: "if the accommodation may be necessary to afford the person equal
opportunity to use and enjoy a dwelling." The Texas definitions are at § 301.003(6) for disability,
§ 301.004 for familial status and § 301.0045 for hair texture and protective hairstyle. The
owner-seller exemptions at §§ 301.041–301.044 and at 42 U.S.C. § 3603(b) evaporate the moment a
licensed broker or agent is used, and § 301.114 sends a fair housing order against a licensee to the
licensing agency with a recommendation of discipline attached. Above all of it sits 22 TAC § 531.19,
which is broader than either statute: eight characteristics including ancestry, and verbs — inquire
about, respond to or facilitate inquiries about, make a disclosure — that catch conduct no statute's
refusal-to-sell language would reach.

The third unit is the spine. The Commission's Canons of Professional Ethics and Client Relations are
the Texas code of ethics: § 531.1 defines who is bound; § 531.2 imposes fidelity, demanding that the
primary duty is to represent the client's interests, that the position be clear to all parties, that
other parties nonetheless be treated fairly, that the license holder be "scrupulous and meticulous,"
and that the license holder "place no personal interest above that of the client"; § 531.3 imposes
integrity and reaches misrepresentation "by acts of commission or omission"; § 531.4 imposes
competency in the local market, in industry developments, in judgment and skill, and in the specific
property type; § 531.18 and § 531.20 impose the Consumer Protection Notice and the IABS Notice, the
latter taught in Module 02; and § 531.19 forbids trading in preference. On the statutory side,
§ 1101.652 is organized
into subsection (a) with eight general grounds, subsection (a-1) with three grounds about the
license holder's own transactions and records, and subsection (b) with thirty-four grounds that
apply "while engaged in real estate brokerage," discrimination being (b)(32) and the § 1101.563
written-agreement failure being (b)(34).

Enforcement runs on numbers you must retain. A complaint must be signed and written and supported by
reasonable cause (§ 1101.204(b)); it cannot be anonymous (§ 1101.204(f)); and no investigation may
proceed on a complaint submitted later than the fourth anniversary of the incident (§ 1101.204(c)).
Rule 535.141 makes a complaint against a sales agent a complaint against the sponsoring broker for
the limited purposes of determining involvement and whether the broker met professional
responsibilities, and sets the triage levels, with Level 1 reserved for fraud or misrepresentation
involving loss of $10,000 or more, continuing threats to public welfare, and unlicensed activity.
Contested matters go to the State Office of Administrative Hearings under § 1101.657 and on to a
district court in the county of the hearing under § 1101.658. Section 1101.702(a) caps an
administrative penalty at $5,000 for each violation, with each day potentially a separate violation,
and 22 TAC § 535.191 sets the three bands — $100 to $1,500, $500 to $3,000, and $1,000 to $5,000 per
violation per day — with up to double for a history of previous violations. Section 1101.659(b) caps
a refund at what the consumer paid and forbids the Commission from awarding other damages, and
§ 1101.801 confirms that discipline "does not relieve the person from civil or criminal liability."

The fourth unit drew the line between that machinery and a private trade association code. Rule
535.1 defines a Trade Association as a nonprofit voluntary member association whose members pay
dues, which is governed by a board elected by the members, and "that subscribes to a written code of
professional conduct or ethics" — so a code is part of what makes such a body what it is. Section
1101.053(a) defines a Texas trade association as "a cooperative and voluntarily joined statewide
association of business or professional competitors" promoting "their common interest," and
subsection (b) bars officers, employees and paid consultants of such associations, and their spouses
in specified roles, from serving on or working in a professional capacity for the Commission. That
wall is the whole point: association codes bind by contract, reach only members, and are enforced by
member panels with fines, education, suspension and expulsion, including loss of MLS privileges; the
Commission binds by statute, reaches every license holder, and can take the license itself. Neither
decides the other. No association's code of ethics was held as a source for this course, and none is
reproduced or paraphrased here. What the law does say about associations was taught from held
sources: 42 U.S.C. § 3606 and Property Code § 301.027 forbid discrimination in MLS and
brokers'-organization membership; § 1101.804 shelters a license holder or nonprofit real estate
board or association that provides sales price information "unless the disclosure of that
information is specifically prohibited by statute"; 22 TAC § 535.5 exempts a dues-based electronic
listing service from licensure; and 22 TAC § 537.11 permits use of a trade-association form prepared
in consultation with a Texas attorney, with the disclosures the rule specifies.

The fifth unit turned all of it into a standard you can carry into an appointment: disclose your
role at first contact and deliver the IABS at first substantive communication; paper the buyer
relationship before you show anything, on the terms § 1101.563 requires; answer within two calendar
days under
§ 535.157; convey everything material under § 535.156 and § 1101.557(b); never conceal a known
significant defect under § 1101.652(b)(3) and (b)(4); keep trust money out of your own account under
(b)(9), (b)(10), (b)(30) and (b)(31); take compensation only as § 1101.651 and § 535.148 allow;
disclose your own interest under § 535.144; accept only work you are competent to do under § 531.4;
stay out of legal drafting under § 1101.654 and § 537.11; keep the file for four years under
§ 535.2; answer the Commission under § 1101.652(a)(4); and refuse, always, to inquire about, respond
to, facilitate or disclose the characteristics listed in § 531.19.

Two closing cautions. First, this course is not TREC-approved and nothing in this module should be
read as a Commission publication; every proposition here is drawn from the statutes and rules named
beside it, and you should read those texts yourself. Second, the numbers in this module are the ones
that will be quoted back at you: $5,000 per violation per day under § 1101.702(a); the $100–$1,500,
$500–$3,000 and $1,000–$5,000 bands of § 535.191; four years for records under § 535.2 and four
years for complaints under § 1101.204(c); two calendar days under § 535.157; twenty days to accept
or contest under § 1101.704(a) and thirty days to pay or appeal under § 1101.707(a); and, in
§ 1101.652, eight grounds in subsection (a), three in (a-1), and thirty-four
in (b), with discrimination at (b)(32). Learn them as numbers attached to sections, not as numbers
floating free, because a number without its section is a number you cannot defend.

§ 535.154, 22 TAC § 535.155 and 22 TAC § 535.157. The middle band is "An administrative penalty
range of $500 - $3,000 per violation per day," covering among others §§ 1101.652(a)(4) - (7),
§ 1101.652(b)(1), §§ 1101.652(b)(7) - (8), § 1101.652(b)(12), § 1101.652(b)(14), § 1101.652(b)(22),
§ 1101.654(a), 22 TAC § 531.18, 22 TAC § 531.20, 22 TAC § 535.2, 22 TAC § 535.16 and 22 TAC
§ 535.17. The top band is "An administrative penalty range of $1,000 - $5,000 per violation per

the multiplier: "The Commission may assess an additional administrative penalty of up to two times

#### Optional practice: Pricing a Galveston enforcement file

Optional. This is arithmetic practice against the schedule above — no required exercise in this
module asks you to compute an exposure — and no assessment is drawn from it.

An agent sponsored by a Galveston brokerage takes a listing on a beach house. Over the course of the
file the Commission ultimately finds four things. (i) On eleven consecutive days, the agent failed
to respond to the buyer's agent, in violation of 22 TAC § 535.157. (ii) The agent never provided the
IABS notice to the unrepresented buyer at first substantive communication, in violation of 22 TAC
§ 531.20. (iii) The agent deposited the buyer's $9,000 earnest money into her personal checking
account for six days before moving it to the title company, implicating § 1101.652(b)(10). (iv) The
agent told the buyer the seller would *definitely* replace the seawall before closing, when the
seller had refused, implicating § 1101.652(b)(5). The agent has one prior Commission order against
her from three years ago.

1. Using 22 TAC § 535.191, state the penalty band that applies to each of the four findings, and
   quote the band language for each.
2. Compute the minimum and maximum administrative penalty for finding (i) alone, showing the
   arithmetic and stating the per-violation-per-day rule you relied on.
3. Compute the maximum administrative penalty for findings (ii), (iii) and (iv) treating each as a
   single violation on a single day, then apply the history-of-previous-violations provision in
   § 535.191 and recompute. State the ceiling that § 1101.702(a) places on your result and whether
   your figure hits it.
