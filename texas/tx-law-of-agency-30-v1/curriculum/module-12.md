# Texas Law of Agency — Module 12

## TREC Topic: Implementation and Presentation

**Prescribed time: 140 minutes.** This module carries the five units the Commission's LOA-1 course
approval form places under *Implementation and Presentation*.

**Learning objectives.** After completing this module you will be able to: run a Texas seller-side
engagement from the listing appointment to closing, and a buyer-side engagement from first contact
to closing, in the order the statutes require each piece of paper to exist, naming the rule that
compels each step; prepare a price opinion that carries the language 22 TAC § 535.17 requires
verbatim and goes out in the broker's name; advertise inside 22 TAC §§ 535.154 and 535.155; stay
inside the contract-form and practice-of-law boundary of 22 TAC § 537.11; give the written advisory
§ 1101.555 requires when an offer is signed; handle a transaction for your own account under 22 TAC
§ 535.144; keep the office, addresses and contact information § 1101.552 and 22 TAC § 535.21
require; and identify the fact patterns that generate Texas complaints, know where in your working
week each one is created, and act correctly on the day you realise the mistake is yours.

**Every legal statement in this module comes from a source that was read for this course.** Where a
statement is practice rather than law, the module says so and cites nothing for it.

**How this module cites.** This is the capstone, and its subject is sequence and judgment rather
than doctrine. Where a rule is taught in full in an earlier module, this one states it in a
sentence, cites it, names the module that teaches it, and then spends its words on where that rule
falls in the working week, what you actually do at that moment, and what goes wrong if you do not.

## Unit: The Broker Working for/with the Seller

Everything you have studied so far in this course has been law in the abstract: what agency is, whom
you owe duties to, what the Commission may do to you. This module is the same law arranged
differently. It is arranged as a week of work. You will walk a seller from the moment the phone
rings to the moment the funding wire lands, and at every step you will be asked the same question a
Commission investigator asks: *what required you to do that, and where is the paper that proves you
did it?*

Start with a concrete engagement so the sequence has something to attach to. A homeowner in
Pflugerville emails your brokerage's website form: three-bedroom, two-bath, built in 2004, they
think it is worth about 385,000 dollars, they want to know what you would charge. You reply and set
an appointment for Thursday evening. That single email exchange has already put two obligations in
motion, and most new license holders miss both.

**Step one, before the appointment: the IABS.** Occupations Code § 1101.558(b-1) requires written
notice — the Information About Brokerage Services form, which 22 TAC § 531.20 adopts by reference —
at the license holder's "first substantive communication with a party relating to a proposed
transaction regarding specific real property"; § 1101.558(b) separately requires you to disclose
whom you represent at the time of first contact with another party or another party's license
holder. Module 02 teaches both provisions, the four permitted delivery methods, and the three
situations § 1101.558(c) excepts.

What is yours in this module is *when in the appointment the notice is delivered*, and the answer is
that it is delivered before the appointment. The statute does not define "first substantive
communication", and this course holds no source that defines it, so the safe habit has to do the
work the definition would: the Pflugerville email named a specific property and discussed a proposed
transaction, so treat your reply as the trigger and attach the completed IABS to it. Two field
habits lose that bet — handing over a blank form on arrival at the appointment, and relying on a
permanent IABS link in an email signature block, a shortcut § 531.20 forecloses by name.

The second thing you owe from the outset is the Consumer Protection Notice: 22 TAC § 531.18 requires
it displayed in each place of business and linked on the homepage of each business website, and
§ 531.20 imposes the parallel homepage-link duty for the IABS; Module 08 teaches both as documents.
In the sequence, the point is that the links go up on the day your page goes live, not on the day
someone asks.

**Step two, at the appointment: the price opinion.** You will be asked what the house is worth.
Answering that question is regulated, it is regulated in two directions at once, and 22 TAC § 535.17
is this module's own rule — the one to know cold.

The outer boundary: "A real estate license holder may not perform an appraisal of, or provide an
opinion of value for, real property unless the license holder is licensed or certified under Texas
Occupations Code, Chapter 1103." You are not an appraiser. What you may prepare instead is
brokerage — Occ. Code § 1101.002(1)(A)(xi) brings within the definition of a broker the provision of
"a written analysis, opinion, or conclusion relating to the estimated price of real property" that
is not referred to as an appraisal, is provided in the ordinary course of the person's business, and
relates to the actual or potential management, acquisition, disposition or encumbrance of an
interest in real property. A comparative market analysis is brokerage. An appraisal is not.

Because the two products look alike to a consumer, § 535.17 requires you to say so in writing. If
you provide "a broker price opinion, comparative market analysis, or estimated worth or sale price
under the Act," you "shall also provide the person for whom the opinion, analysis, or estimate is
prepared with a written statement containing the following language: 'This represents an estimated
sale price for this property. It is not the same as the opinion of value in an appraisal developed
by a licensed appraiser under the Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice.'" And the
rule tells you how the sentence must appear: it "must be made part of any written opinion, analysis,
or estimate of worth or sale price and must be reproduced verbatim in at least 12-point font."

Three practical consequences follow. First, "verbatim" means verbatim — you may not tidy the
sentence, shorten it, or paraphrase it into your own listing-presentation voice. Second, "made part
of" means it belongs inside the CMA document, not in a separate email. Third, 12-point is a floor,
and a CMA laid out in 9-point body text with the disclaimer matched to the body text violates the
rule even though the words are right.

One more clause of § 535.17 matters enormously to a sales agent: "A sales agent may prepare, sign,
and present a broker price opinion, comparative market analysis, or estimate of worth or sale price
for the sales agent's sponsoring broker, but the sales agent must submit the broker price opinion,
comparative market analysis, or estimate of worth or sale price in the broker's name and the broker
is responsible for it." You may build it and you may present it. It goes out in the broker's name —
the same principle that puts listings themselves in the broker's name under 22 TAC § 535.2, taught
in Module 09.

The CMA is not a courtesy you may skip. 22 TAC § 535.16, taught in Module 04, obliges a license
holder to provide a BPO or CMA "when negotiating a listing or offering to purchase the property for
the license holder's own account as a result of contact made while acting as a real estate agent."
For Thursday evening, the consequence is that the Pflugerville CMA is compulsory and must carry the
§ 535.17 statement inside it. The second half of that rule — the anti-self-dealing half — is taken
up in Unit 4.

**Step three: the listing agreement.** Four rules taught elsewhere in the course meet on this one
page, and the sequence is what makes them a single act rather than four.

It is solicited and accepted in the broker's name: 22 TAC § 535.2, Module 09. It must specify a
definite termination date that is not subject to prior notice, because § 1101.652(b)(12) makes the
absence of one a ground for discipline (Module 10) — an open-ended listing "until sold" is a
disciplinary problem, not a marketing preference. It is ordinarily where the owner's written consent
to place a sign lives, § 1101.652(b)(18) making a sign without that written consent a ground for
discipline; if you are marketing under an arrangement that is not a signed listing, get the sign
consent in writing separately, at the same appointment. And a listing contract form adopted by the
Commission must under § 1101.155(c) inform the parties that commissions are negotiable and explain
the availability of Texas coastal natural hazards information where that is appropriate.

Net listings are constrained by 22 TAC § 535.16, taught in Module 04, and are available only on the
conditions that rule sets. In everyday residential practice the honest advice is to decline, and to
say why — the arrangement pays you more the worse you do for your client.

Nothing about the writing is a formality. Section 1101.806(c) bars any action to recover a
commission unless the agreement, or a memorandum of it, is in writing and signed by the party
against whom the action is brought, and § 1101.806(b) requires you to allege and prove you held a
licence "at the time the act was commenced"; Module 04 teaches both, with the parallel requirement
Business & Commerce Code § 26.01 puts on the sale contract. In the sequence, the consequence is
blunt. A seller who shook your hand, watched you spend 4,000 dollars on staging and then closed with
a neighbour owes you nothing you can sue for, and a licence that lapses mid-listing is a
compensation catastrophe rather than a renewal problem.

**Step four, the same evening: the seller's disclosure notice.** Property Code § 5.008 requires a
seller of residential real property comprising not more than one dwelling unit to give the purchaser
the prescribed written notice or one substantially similar to it. Module 03 teaches the notice, its
content and its exemptions. What belongs here is which step of the listing workflow it is.

Get it completed before the photographs and before the sign. Section 5.008(f) requires delivery "on
or before the effective date of an executory contract binding the purchaser to purchase the
property," and provides that where a contract is entered without it, "the purchaser may terminate
the contract for any reason within seven days after receiving the notice." A listing agent who
leaves the notice until after an offer is accepted has handed the buyer a free option on the deal.

Two things you do at the kitchen table. It is the seller's notice: you obtain it, you deliver it,
and you never fill it in for them. And when the seller stalls over a question — the age of the roof,
the date of the last leak — tell them what § 5.008(d) says, that where the information is unknown to
the seller, the seller "shall indicate that fact on the notice, and by that act is in compliance
with this section." "Unknown" is a lawful answer. A guess is not, and a guess that turns out wrong
becomes the buyer's misrepresentation claim and, where you knew better, your § 1101.652(b)(3) and
(b)(4) problem as well.

**Step five: marketing the listing.** The moment you publish anything about the Pflugerville house,
22 TAC § 535.155 applies. Its scope is deliberately enormous. An "Advertisement" is "any form of
communication by or on behalf of a license holder designed to attract the public to use real estate
brokerage services and includes, but is not limited to, all publications, brochures, radio or
television broadcasts, all electronic media including email, text messages, social media, the
Internet, business stationery, business cards, displays, signs and billboards." The rule carves out
only two things: "a communication from a license holder to the license holder's current client; and
a directional sign that may also contain only the broker's name or logo."

Every advertisement must include, "in a readily noticeable location in the advertisement", two
items: "the name of the license holder or team placing the advertisement" and "the broker's name in
at least half the size of the largest contact information for any sales agent, associated broker, or
team name contained in the advertisement." That is a size ratio, not a mere presence requirement,
and "Contact Information" is broadly defined as "any information that can be used to contact a
license holder featured in the advertisement, including a name, phone number, email address, website
address, social media handle, scan code or other similar information." If your Instagram tile
carries your handle in 60-point type, the broker's name must be at least 30-point. For social media
and text, the rule allows the required information to sit "on a separate page or on the account user
profile page of the license holder," but only if that page is "readily accessible by a direct link
from the social media or text" and "readily noticeable on the separate page or in the account user
profile."

The rule then lists, "For purposes of this section and §1101.652(b)(23) of the Act," the
advertisements that mislead or are likely to deceive. Several of them are ordinary marketing habits.
An advertisement violates the rule if it is "inaccurate in any material fact or representation"; if
it "identifies a sales agent as a broker"; if it "uses a title, such as owner, president, CEO, COO,
or other similar title, email or website address that implies a sales agent is responsible for the
operations of a brokerage"; if it "contains the name of a sales agent that is not the name as shown
on the sales agent's license issued by the Commission or an alternate name registered with the
Commission"; if it "creates confusion regarding the permitted use of a property"; if it is "about
the value of a property, unless it is based on an appraisal that is disclosed and readily available
upon request by a party or it is given in compliance with §535.17"; or if it "implies the person
making the advertisement was involved in a transaction regarding a property when the person had no
such role".

Three of the listed items are pure timing rules, and they catch careful people. An advertisement
violates the rule if it is "about a property that is subject to an exclusive listing agreement
without the permission of the listing broker and without disclosing the name of the listing broker
unless the listing broker has expressly agreed in writing to waive disclosure"; if it is "offering a
listed property that is not discontinued within 10 days after the listing agreement is no longer in
effect"; or if it is "about a property 10 days or more after the closing of a transaction unless the
current status of the property is included in the advertisement". So when your Pflugerville listing
expires, you have ten days to take down the sign, the landing page, the boosted post and the
syndicated feed. When it closes, your "Just sold!" post is fine for ten days, and after that it
needs the current status included.

**Step six: presenting offers.** 22 TAC § 535.156, taught in Module 02, requires the license holder
to convey to the principal all known information affecting the principal's decision whether to make,
accept or reject offers, subject to one escape: a written agreement by the principal that offers are
not to be submitted after the principal has entered into a contract.

For the working week the default is unconditional — every offer goes to the seller, on the day it
arrives. There is no oral version of the exception. If your Pflugerville seller does not want back-up
offers presented after going under contract, that instruction is signed and in the file before the
first offer arrives, not negotiated afterwards.

Two authority limits govern how you negotiate, both grounds for discipline under § 1101.652 and
taught in Module 10: you may not offer to sell or lease the property without the owner's knowledge
and consent, § 1101.652(b)(19), and you may not offer it on terms the owner has not authorised,
§ 1101.652(b)(20). Between them they dispose of the common temptation to tell a buyer's agent that
"they'd probably take 375."

**Step seven: closing.** Two items belong to the seller's agent at the end. The first is money: any
earnest money or other trust money that reaches the brokerage is handled under 22 TAC § 535.146, and
Unit 2 walks the handling in sequence because the buyer's side usually delivers it. The second is
compensation, which § 1101.651 confines to license holders and to out-of-state brokers who conduct
none of the negotiations in Texas, and which reaches a sales agent only through the sponsoring
broker; Module 09 teaches § 1101.651 and 22 TAC § 535.3.

### Statute reading exercise: Reading § 535.17 and § 535.16 against each other

Open 22 TAC § 535.17 and 22 TAC § 535.16 and answer from the text alone.

1. Section 535.17 requires a specific sentence to accompany a broker price opinion or CMA. Copy the
sentence exactly, then state the two formatting requirements the rule imposes on it and where in the
document the rule says it must appear. 2. Section 535.17 says a sales agent may prepare, sign and
present a CMA. Whose name must it be submitted in, and who does the rule say is responsible for it?
What does that tell you about why the answer is not "the person who did the work"? 3. Section 535.16
creates an obligation to provide a BPO or CMA in two situations. Name both, and identify the words
in the second situation that limit it — the rule does not say a license holder must produce a CMA
before buying any property at all. 4. Section 535.16 permits a net listing only in stated
circumstances. List every condition the rule attaches, including the two terms the listing agreement
itself must contain. Does the rule say a net listing is prohibited? 5. Neither section states a
required format, comparable count or adjustment method for a CMA. What follows for a licence holder
who wants to know how many comparables to use?

### Applied exercise: The Pflugerville listing

You list 1408 Wilbarger Court in Pflugerville on 3 March at 385,000 dollars under a written listing
agreement with a definite termination date of 3 September. The agreed commission is 5.5 percent of
the sales price, of which your brokerage will offer 2.5 percent to a cooperating broker. Your seller
completes the § 5.008 notice on 3 March and answers "Unknown" to the age of the roof. On 14 April a
buyer's agent emails an offer of 372,000 dollars. On 15 April, before your seller responds, a second
buyer's agent calls to say a written offer of 380,000 dollars is coming "later today." Your seller
has told you on the phone, but not in writing, "don't bring me anything under 380."

1. Under 22 TAC § 535.156, may you decline to present the 372,000 dollar offer because of the
seller's phone instruction? Identify the only exception the rule states and say why it does not
apply on these facts. 2. Under 22 TAC § 535.157, by when must you respond to the second buyer's
agent's call? State the period the rule gives and how it is counted. 3. The seller accepts the
380,000 dollar offer. Compute the total commission at 5.5 percent, the amount going to the
cooperating broker at 2.5 percent, and the amount retained by your brokerage. 4. The § 5.008 notice
was delivered on 3 March, well before the contract. Suppose instead it had been delivered on 20
April, after the contract's effective date. State the buyer's right under § 5.008(f), including its
length and the reasons required to exercise it. 5. The listing expires on 3 September without a
sale, and your boosted Facebook post advertising the property is still live on 20 September.
Identify the provision of 22 TAC § 535.155 you have violated and the deadline you missed.

## Unit: The Broker Working for/with the Buyer

The buyer side of Texas practice changed on 1 January 2026, and it changed in a way that moves
paperwork to the very front of the relationship. Two sections added by Acts 2025, 89th Leg., R.S.,
Ch. 1172 (S.B. 1968) — § 1101.562, Real Property Showings Without Representation, and § 1101.563,
Written Agreement Required — are taught in full in Module 05, with their definitions, the contents
the agreement must state, and the 2026 changes read together. What this unit does is put them in
order against everything else that must happen on the buyer's side, because the order is what an
investigator reconstructs.

**The first hour, in sequence.** A prospective buyer calls about a specific listing in Round Rock.

*Before you say anything substantive.* That call is very likely your first substantive communication
about specific real property, so § 1101.558(b-1) and 22 TAC § 531.20 require the IABS, Module 02. If
you are the listing agent on the Round Rock house, § 1101.558(b) also requires you to disclose that
you represent the seller, at the time of that first contact, orally or in writing.

*Before you unlock a door.* Section 1101.563(b) requires a signed written agreement before you show
any residential real property to a prospective buyer, or — where no property will be shown — before
you present an offer on their behalf. The operative word is "before." There is no grace period, no
"we'll sign it back at the office," no "we'll paper it when we write the offer." Section
1101.652(b)(34) makes the failure a ground for suspension or revocation, and there is nothing
theoretical about the exposure: it is charged once per showing.

*Choosing which agreement.* Either a representation agreement, or a showing-only agreement for a
§ 1101.562 showing. If it is showing-only, § 1101.563(e) caps it at fourteen days and forbids
exclusivity — an outer limit, not a default term you must use — and § 1101.563(d) requires a
separate agreement before you perform any further act of brokerage for that buyer. A showing-only
agreement cannot be allowed to grow quietly into a representation.

*Standing in the kitchen.* Section 1101.562(a) preserves non-representation status only while you
have not agreed, orally or in writing, to represent the party, are not otherwise acting as their
agent, give no opinions or advice about the property or about real estate transactions in general,
and perform no other act of brokerage for them. Subsection (c) is the narrow safe harbour: a broker
showing under the section "may confirm information to a party regarding the size, price, and terms
of the real property available for sale or lease." Three items, and the verb is "confirm". You may
confirm that the house is 2,140 square feet, that the list price is 419,000 dollars, and that the
seller wants a 30-day close. You may not tell them what it is worth — and "is this a fair price?" is
the question you will actually be asked, in the kitchen, with nobody else present.

*If you end up on both sides.* A broker who agrees to represent both a buyer and a seller in the
transaction must act as an intermediary, § 1101.561(b), on written consent from each party stating
the source of any expected compensation, § 1101.559(a). The machinery is Module 06's. What matters
in the sequence is that intermediary status follows from the fact of representing both rather than
from anyone's preference, so the consents are obtained before the buyer writes, not produced at the
closing table.

**The open house.** TREC's own explanatory article on the 2026 changes — guidance, not law, used
here only to describe practice — works this case through. Where the agent hosting the open house is
a member of the listing broker's office, the article states the agent does not need to provide the
IABS or enter into a written agreement and needs only to disclose representation of the owner as
§ 1101.558(b) requires. Where the hosting agent is not with the listing brokerage and does not
represent the seller, the article states the agent must provide the IABS and enter into a written
agreement as described by § 1101.563(c) with prospective buyers, even where the visitor is already
represented by another broker. The statutory basis for the first half is § 1101.558(c)(3), which
relieves the notice requirement where "the communication occurs at a property that is held open for
any prospective buyer or tenant and the communication concerns that property" — but note that
§ 1101.558(c) relieves only the § 1101.558(b-1) *notice*. It does not touch § 1101.563, which is a
separate statute with its own trigger. That is exactly why an outside agent hosting a stranger's
open house still needs the written agreement.

The same 2025 bill also removed two references to subagency from TRELA. TREC's article says so and
says subagency "has not been eliminated generally as a legal concept." This course holds no held
source that defines the common-law contours of subagency after that change, so those contours were
not held and are not taught here.

**When the offer is signed: § 1101.555.** One short statute applies at signature, and it is worth
committing to memory in full. Section 1101.555: "When an offer to purchase real estate in this state
is signed, a license holder shall advise each buyer, in writing, that the buyer should: (1) have the
abstract covering the real estate that is the subject of the contract examined by an attorney chosen
by the buyer; or (2) be provided with or obtain a title insurance policy."

Three points. It is triggered by signature of the offer, not by acceptance — so it happens at the
table, in the same sitting, not later in the file. It runs to "each buyer", every buyer, including
the one who is unrepresented and merely handed the form. And it must be in writing. Section
1101.652(b)(29) states the same requirement as a disciplinary ground phrased around the closing,
making it a ground to fail "to advise a buyer in writing before the closing of a real estate
transaction" of the same two options: a signature-time duty with a before-closing backstop.

The sanction that actually gets attention is in the compensation section. Section 1101.806(d): "A
license holder who fails to advise a buyer as provided by Section 1101.555 may not receive payment
of or recover any commission agreed to be paid on the sale." Not reduced. Not subject to offset. You
may not receive it. A one-sentence written advisory, given at signature and kept in the file,
protects your entire fee on the transaction.

**Writing the offer: the § 537.11 boundary.** Now you sit down to write. Rule 537.11 opens with the
mandatory-form rule: "When negotiating contracts binding the sale, exchange, option, or lease of any
interest in real property, a license holder shall use only those contract forms approved for
mandatory use by the Commission for that type of transaction with the following exceptions:" and
then lists them. The exceptions are "transactions in which the license holder is functioning solely
as a principal, not as an agent;" "transactions in which an agency of the United States government
requires a different form to be used;" "transactions for which a contract form has been prepared by
the property owner or prepared by an attorney and required by a property owner;" and "transactions
for which no contract form has been approved for mandatory use by the Commission," in which case the
license holder may use a form "prepared by an attorney licensed by this state, or a trade
association in consultation with an attorney licensed by this state" carrying the identifying
content the rule specifies, or a form "prepared by the Texas Real Estate Broker-Lawyer Committee and
approved by the Commission for voluntary use by license holders."

Learn the exceptions precisely, because each has a limit built into it. The principal exception is
available only when you are "functioning solely as a principal, not as an agent". The owner-form
exception requires that the form be "prepared by the property owner or prepared by an attorney and
required by a property owner" — a builder's own contract qualifies; a form your buyer downloaded
does not. And the attorney-or-trade-association form is available only where the Commission has
approved *no* mandatory form for that type of transaction, and the form must contain the attorney's
or association's name, the name of the broker or trade association it was prepared for, a statement
of the transaction type approved, "any restrictions on the use of the form", and, if applicable, "an
explanation of how the form changes the rights, obligations, or remedies of a party under a contract
form approved for mandatory use by the Commission and a statement that license holders may not
practice law or give legal advice".

The Act backs this with § 1101.652(a-1)(3), which makes it a ground for discipline that a license
holder "fails to use a contract form required by the commission under Section 1101.155", and with
§ 1101.155(b), which limits the Commission in the other direction: it "may not prohibit a license
holder from using for the sale, exchange, option, or lease of an interest in real property a
contract form that is: (1) prepared by the property owner; or (2) prepared by an attorney and
required by the property owner."

Rule 537.11 also governs how the form may be reproduced: approved forms "may be reproduced,
including through use of a software application, provided that the text and format of the form,
including the sizing, spacing, and pagination, is identical to the Commission's published version,"
except that "the business name or logo of a broker, trade association, or other organization may
appear outside the form's border" and "a form may be scaled to accommodate viewing on smaller
screens, including mobile devices, as long as the final executed copy of the form otherwise complies
with this subsection." A brokerage that rebuilds the One to Four Family Residential Contract in its
own template engine with different pagination has broken this rule even if every word is right.

**What you may not do while filling it in.** Rule 537.11 states a list of prohibitions. A license
holder may not "practice law"; may not "directly or indirectly offer, give or attempt to give legal
advice"; may not "give advice or opinions as to the legal effect of any contract forms or other such
instruments which may affect the title to real estate"; may not "give opinions concerning the status
or validity of title to real estate"; may not "draft or recommend language to be included in a
contract form defining or affecting the rights, obligations, or remedies of the principals of a real
estate transaction, including escalation, appraisal, or contingency clauses"; may not "add
informational items to a form approved by the Commission if the Commission has approved another form
for mandatory use for that purpose"; may not "attempt to prevent or in any manner whatsoever
discourage any principal to a real estate transaction from employing an attorney"; and may not
"obtain legal advice from an attorney, directly or indirectly, for a principal in a real estate
transaction in which the license holder is acting as an agent."

The fifth of those is the one that catches competent, well-meaning agents in a competitive market.
Escalation clauses, appraisal-gap clauses and bespoke contingency language are named in the rule by
name. Drafting them is prohibited, and so is recommending the language. If your buyer wants an
escalation clause, the answer is that they need a lawyer to draft it, and you say so without
discouraging them from getting one.

The sixth is quieter and just as dangerous: you may not add informational items to a promulgated
form where the Commission has promulgated an addendum for that purpose. If there is a form for the
subject, use the form.

The rule then tells you what you *may* do. It "does not: limit a license holder's fiduciary
obligation to disclose to the license holder's principals all pertinent facts that are within the
knowledge of the license holder, including such facts which might affect the status of or title to
real estate;" and it does not "prevent the license holder from explaining to the license holder's
principals the meaning of informational items or choices in a contract form, as long as the license
holder does not practice law or give legal advice". So explaining what a blank is for is permitted;
telling your buyer what the paragraph will do to their legal position is not.

And it identifies two acts that are affirmatively not the practice of law: "add informational items
to a contract form authorized for use by this section" and, "if specifically instructed in writing
by a principal, add language to or strike language from a contract form, as long as any change is
made conspicuous, including underlining additions, striking through deletions, or employing some
other method which clearly indicates the change being made." Every element of that second permission
matters. The instruction must come from a principal, it must be specific, it must be in writing, and
the change must be conspicuous. A text message from your buyer saying "strike the survey paragraph"
and a printed strike-through with the deletion visible satisfies it. Your own decision to quietly
delete a sentence does not.

Finally, two affirmative advisory duties. "When a transaction involves unusual matters that should
be reviewed by an attorney before an instrument is executed, or if the instrument must be
acknowledged and filed of record, the license holder shall advise the license holder's principals to
consult an attorney before executing the instrument." And: "A license holder shall advise the
license holder's principals that the instrument they are about to execute is binding on them."

Section 1101.654, taught in Module 10, puts a licence-ending penalty behind the same line — the
Commission "shall", not may, suspend or revoke for drafting or advising on legal sufficiency — and
its subsection (b) protects the ordinary act of completing an approved or attorney-prepared form.
Filling in the right form is safe. Writing your own is not.

**Earnest money: the handling, in order.** The buyer's earnest money is trust money, and 22 TAC
§ 535.146 governs what happens to it from the moment it reaches a hand. Four steps.

*Receipt.* "A sales agent shall not maintain a trust account. Any trust money received by a sales
agent must be immediately delivered to the sales agent's sponsoring broker." Not deposited by you.
Not held in your desk over the weekend. Immediately delivered to your broker.

*Deposit.* Trust money accepted by a broker "is held in a fiduciary capacity and must be maintained
in a designated trust account maintained by the broker or delivered to an escrow agent authorized in
Texas in accordance with the agreement of the principals of the transaction." The deadline is the
number to memorise: unless a different time "is expressly agreed upon in writing by the principals
to the transaction," deposit or delivery must occur "not later than the close of business of the
second working day after the date the broker receives the trust money." Second working day, close of
business, counted from receipt *by the broker*.

*Custody.* The broker may not commingle trust money with the broker's personal or other non-trust
money, or hold it in a personal account or any kind of business account, and "placing trust money in
a broker's personal or operating account" is prima facie evidence of commingling. Money the broker
becomes entitled to — an earned commission — must leave the trust account "not later the 30th day
after the date the broker acquires ownership of the money." If a trust-money check is dishonoured,
"the broker shall immediately notify all parties to the transaction in writing."

*Disbursement.* Money leaves the account only "in accordance with the agreement under which the
money was received." On a written demand for payment by any or all parties, the broker must pay the
party or parties entitled "not later than the 30th day after the date the demand is made," and the
same thirty-day limit runs from receipt of a subsequent written agreement of all parties to a
different disbursement. In both cases "The broker must immediately notify all parties in writing of
any disbursement of trust money". And where entitlement genuinely cannot be determined, the rule
provides the exit: "If the broker cannot reasonably determine to which party or parties the trust
money should be paid, the broker may pay the trust money into the registry of a court and interplead
the parties."

Documentation of the account is kept "for four years from the date the document is received or
created by the broker." The Act makes the same failures disciplinary grounds — § 1101.652(b)(9) for
failing to account for or remit money belonging to another, (b)(10) for commingling, (b)(30) for
failing to deposit within a reasonable time, and (b)(31) for disbursing before the transaction is
completed or terminated — and Module 10 teaches them.

### Applied exercise: A Saturday in Cedar Park

At 9:40 on a Saturday morning a stranger calls your cell. She saw your sign at 2203 Silverstone
Drive in Cedar Park, a single-family house you have listed for the seller at 419,000 dollars. She
asks to see it at 11:00. She is not represented. At 11:00 she asks you three questions: how big is
the house, is 419,000 a fair price, and would the seller take 400,000.

1. Before you open the door, what must you deliver and what must you sign? Cite § 1101.558(b), §
1101.563(b) and, if you were not the listing agent, § 1101.558(b-1) and 22 TAC § 531.20, and say
which apply on these facts and which do not. 2. She does not want a representation agreement. May
you show the house under a showing-only agreement? State the two limits § 1101.563(e) places on such
an agreement. 3. Answer each of her three questions consistently with § 1101.562(c) and §
1101.562(a)(3). Which may you answer, which may you not, and what exactly does subsection (c) permit
you to do? 4. She decides to write an offer. You are the listing agent and your broker also intends

## Unit: A Practical Guide to Everyday Practice

Most licences are not lost through fraud. They are lost through habits: an unanswered email, a
promise made in a car and never written down, a file that cannot be produced three years later, a
helpful sentence about what a contract paragraph "really means." This unit converts the rules you
have just read into a set of standing habits, and names the rule behind each one, because a habit
you cannot justify by citation is a habit you will abandon under pressure.

**Habit one: answer within two calendar days.** 22 TAC § 535.157, taught in Module 02, requires a
broker or sales agent to respond within two calendar days to a principal, to a broker or sales agent
representing another party, or to an unrepresented party to a transaction.

Three things about living with it. The list reaches past your own client to the license holder on
the other side of the deal and to an unrepresented party, so the stranger who emails about your
listing is owed a response by rule, not by courtesy. "Calendar days" is not business days: an email
received Friday afternoon is answered by Sunday. And the rule says respond, not resolve — telling
the other agent that you have their offer, that your seller is travelling, and that you will have an
answer on Wednesday is a response; silence while you wait for something better to say is not, and
because the penalty runs per day, a silence that lasts three weeks is not one violation.

Two matching deadlines inside the brokerage come from 22 TAC § 535.2, Module 09: two calendar days
for a broker or delegated supervisor to answer a sponsored sales agent, and three for delivering
Commission mail to them. The second is your protection — a complaint notice addressed to you arrives
at your broker first.

**Habit two: put it in writing, and put it in writing at the time.** There is no general Texas rule
that says "document everything," so the honest way to teach this is to show you how many separate
rules assume a writing exists, and let the pattern make the argument.

The listing and buyer agreements must be written to be enforceable as to your fee: § 1101.806(c).
The buyer agreement must exist before a showing: § 1101.563(b). The intermediary consent must be
written and must state the source of expected compensation: § 1101.559(a). The instruction not to
present post-acceptance offers must be an agreement "in writing": 22 TAC § 535.156. The instruction
to add or strike contract language must be given "specifically instructed in writing by a
principal": 22 TAC § 537.11. Consent to place a sign must be written: § 1101.652(b)(18). Written
consent of the sponsoring broker is required before a sales agent receives compensation: 22 TAC
§ 535.3. A delegation of supervisory responsibility "must be in writing": 22 TAC § 535.2. The
advisory about the abstract or title policy must be "in writing": § 1101.555. Notice of a dishonoured
trust-money check, and notice of any disbursement under a written demand or subsequent agreement,
must be in writing: 22 TAC § 535.146. The disclosure that you are a license holder acting for your
own account must be "in writing": 22 TAC § 535.144.

Once you see the pattern, the working rule writes itself: if a rule gives you a permission that
depends on someone else's instruction, get the instruction in writing before you act on it; and if a
rule imposes a duty to tell someone something, tell them in a medium that survives.

The corollary is that oral promises still bind you even though they are unwritten. Under 22 TAC
§ 535.145, taught in Module 11, a "false promise" for purposes of § 1101.652(b)(5) includes oral as
well as written promises, and neither a written contractual provision disclaiming oral
representations nor the parol evidence rule prevents the Commission from considering what you said.
An integration clause in the contract does not protect you from the Commission. What you said in the
car counts.

**Habit three: keep the file for four years, in the shape the rule names.** 22 TAC § 535.2, taught
in Module 09, requires the broker to keep eight categories of records available to the Commission
for at least four years from closing, termination of the contract, or the end of the transaction.

Three consequences for your own habits. One of those categories is "substantive communications with
parties to the transaction", which covers your text messages with the buyer's agent about the repair
amendment, so a messaging app that deletes after thirty days is a decision not to keep records the
broker is required to hold. The clock runs from the end of the transaction, so a deal that dies in
option starts it that day rather than at some later tidy-up. And the four years is not arbitrary: it
matches the outer limit of the Commission's investigative reach under § 1101.204(c) and the
four-year limitations period Civil Practice and Remedies Code § 16.004(a) sets for fraud and breach
of fiduciary duty.

Two record duties fall on you personally rather than on your broker, both grounds for discipline
under § 1101.652 and taught in Module 10: (a-1)(2), failing or refusing to produce on request, within
a reasonable time, a document in your possession relating to a transaction you conducted; and
(b)(28), failing or refusing to give a copy of a document to a person who signed it. A seller who
asks for a copy of their own listing agreement two years later gets one.

**Habit four: know the line you must not cross when you talk about documents.** Unit 2 set out 22
TAC § 537.11 in full. Reduce it to five questions you ask yourself before you answer a client,
because the prohibited acts arrive disguised as good service:

Am I about to give an opinion about what a contract provision does to someone's legal rights? Am I
about to say something about who owns this property or whether the title is good? Am I about to
compose a sentence to go into the contract — an escalation, appraisal or contingency clause? Am I
about to suggest that a lawyer is an unnecessary expense or a deal-killer? Am I about to call the
brokerage's lawyer to get an answer for my client and relay it? Every one of those is prohibited by
§ 537.11, the last one included; what you may do instead is employ and pay an attorney to represent
only yourself, and you may reimburse a principal for attorney's fees incurred.

The permitted side, set out in Unit 2, is the answer to each of them: explain informational items,
add informational items, act on a principal's specific written instruction, and tell your principal
every pertinent fact within your knowledge. Tell them the fact. Do not opine on its legal effect.

**Habit five: stay inside your competence and your market.** 22 TAC § 531.4, taught in Module 02,
obliges a license holder to be knowledgeable and competent in the local market and in the specific
type of real estate being brokered. The Act turns incompetence into a disciplinary ground twice, at
§ 1101.652(b)(1) for acting negligently or incompetently and § 1101.652(a)(6) for failing to
consider market conditions for the specific geographic area in which the license holder provides a
service. A Dallas agent taking a ranch listing near Sonora, or an agent who has never handled a
commercial lease taking one because a friend asked, is running straight at both.

The other half of that is your training entitlement, and it is worth claiming. 22 TAC § 535.2
requires the sponsoring broker's written policies to ensure each sponsored sales agent is advised of
the scope of their authorised activities and is competent to conduct them, "including having
competence in the geographic market area where the sales agent represents clients", and requires
that when a sales agent performs a type of brokerage activity "for the first three times, the broker
must require that the sales agent receive coaching and assistance from an experienced license holder
competent for that activity." First three times, coaching required, by rule. If your broker does not
offer it, ask for it and put the request in writing.

**Habit six: know the standard you are being judged against.** 22 TAC § 531.2 makes you a fiduciary
who is "scrupulous and meticulous" and places no personal interest above the client's, and 22 TAC
§ 531.3 requires integrity, "including employment of prudence and caution so as to avoid
misrepresentation, in any way, by acts of commission or omission." Module 02 teaches the Canons as
duties and Module 10 reads them as an ethical whole. The four words to carry into the working week
are the last four: misrepresentation by omission is misrepresentation.

**A working checklist for the week.** None of what follows is itself a legal requirement — it is
practice, and it is offered as practice — but every item exists because a rule cited in this module
requires the underlying act. On taking any new consumer contact: send the IABS and log the send.
Before any showing of residential property to a prospective buyer: written agreement signed and
dated. On taking a listing: signed agreement in the broker's name with a definite termination date,
sign consent, § 5.008 notice completed by the seller, CMA with the § 535.17 statement in it at
12-point or larger. On every advertisement: broker's name at least half the size of the largest
contact information. On receiving anything from a principal, a cooperating agent, or an
unrepresented party: respond inside two calendar days. On receiving trust money as a sales agent:
hand it to the broker the same day. On receiving any instruction that changes what you would
otherwise do: get it in writing before acting. At the end of every transaction, dead or closed:
close the file with the eight record categories 22 TAC § 535.2 lists, and keep it four years.

### Statute reading exercise: What 22 TAC § 537.11 permits

Open 22 TAC § 537.11 and answer only from its text.

1. The rule requires use of Commission-approved mandatory forms "with the following exceptions".
List all four exceptions in the rule's own terms, and for the fourth, state both alternative sources
of a form the rule allows. 2. The rule lists acts a license holder may not do. Identify the one that
names three specific clause types, and state those three types. Why does naming them matter more
than a general prohibition would? 3. The rule states two things that are "not the practice of law"
for a license holder. State the second one in full, including every condition attached to it. How
many separate conditions did you count? 4. Two affirmative advisory duties appear near the end of
the section. State both, and state the two circumstances that trigger the first. 5. The rule permits
reproduction of approved forms. What must remain identical to the Commission's published version,
and what two departures are expressly allowed? Does the rule permit a brokerage to re-typeset a form
so it fits on fewer pages?

## Unit: Other Considerations

Four subjects sit slightly outside the transaction itself and generate an outsized share of Texas
discipline: buying and selling for your own account, paying and receiving referral fees and rebates,
the names you trade under, and the addresses the Commission has on file for you. None of them
involves a client complaining about a house. All of them involve the Commission finding something
wrong on its own.

**Acting for your own account: 22 TAC § 535.144.** Sooner or later you will want to buy a property
yourself, or list your mother's house, or sell a rental owned by an LLC in which you hold an
interest. The rule that governs is short, it is this module's own, and every clause of it matters.

It begins by expanding who counts. "For purposes of §1101.652(a-1)(1) of the Act 'license holder'
includes a license holder acting on behalf of: the license holder's spouse, parent or child; a
business entity in which the license holder is more than a 10% owner; or a trust for which the
license holder acts as trustee or of which the license holder or the license holder's spouse, parent
or child is a beneficiary." Three extensions: family in the first degree, a business entity where
your ownership exceeds ten percent, and a trust where you are trustee or where you or your close
family are beneficiaries. Ten percent is a threshold, and "more than" excludes exactly ten.

Then the disclosure duty. "A license holder engaging in a real estate transaction on his or her own
behalf or in a capacity described by subsection (a), is obligated to disclose in writing that he or
she is a licensed real estate broker or sales agent acting on his or her own behalf or in a capacity
described by subsection (a) in any contract of sale or rental agreement or in any other writing
given before entering into any contract of sale or rental agreement."

Take that sentence apart. What must be disclosed: that you are a licensed real estate broker or
sales agent, and that you are acting on your own behalf or in one of the extended capacities. How:
in writing. Where: in the contract of sale or rental agreement itself, or in some other writing
given *before* entering into it. To whom: the rule does not limit the disclosure to consumers, and
it does not exempt transactions with other license holders. Note also what triggers it — engaging in
the transaction, not advertising it. The safest practice, and it is practice rather than rule, is to
disclose in the listing or advertisement as well, because 22 TAC § 535.155 independently prohibits
an advertisement that "causes a member of the public to believe that a person not licensed to
conduct real estate brokerage is engaged in real estate brokerage".

Then the substantive limit: "A license holder acting on his or her own behalf or in a capacity
described by subsection (a) shall not use the license holder's expertise to the disadvantage of a
person with whom the license holder deals." That is a standard, not a checklist, and it is the
clause that turns a hard bargain into a violation. You know what the comparables say; the
unrepresented owner across the table does not. Using that asymmetry to their disadvantage is
prohibited even though you owe them no agency duty at all.

Three provisions ride alongside. Rule 535.16 (Module 04) requires a BPO or CMA "when negotiating a
listing or offering to purchase the property for the license holder's own account as a result of
contact made while acting as a real estate agent" — so if you meet an owner as an agent and then
decide to buy the property yourself, the CMA is mandatory and it goes to the owner. And two grounds
for discipline in § 1101.652 (Module 10) sit behind § 535.144: (a-1)(1), misrepresentation,
dishonesty or fraud when buying, selling, trading or leasing in your own name or that of a spouse or
a person related within the first degree; and (b)(16), acting in a dual capacity as broker and
undisclosed principal.

**Referral fees, rebates and who may be paid.** Section 1101.651, taught in Module 09, confines
compensation: a sales agent may neither accept compensation from anyone but the sponsoring broker
nor pay a commission except through that broker. Rule 535.147 adds the line that matters in
practice — a license holder may not share fees with an unlicensed entity created to collect them on
the license holder's behalf.

Rebates to consumers are permitted, also under 22 TAC § 535.147, on conditions: the written consent
of the sponsoring broker and of the party you represent, and no payment made in a manner that
misleads a broker, lender, title company or governmental agency about the transaction or the buyer's
financial resources or obligations. An undisclosed credit outside closing that changes what the
lender thinks the buyer brought to the table is precisely what the second of those forbids.

If you advertise a rebate, 22 TAC § 535.155 — this module's rule — adds its own disclosure
requirements. An advertisement violates the rule if it offers "to rebate a portion of a license
holder's compensation to a party if the advertisement does not disclose that payment of the rebate
is subject to the consent of the party the license holder represents in the transaction", or if it
offers a rebate "contingent upon a party's use of a specified service provider, or subject to
approval by a third party such as a lender, unless the advertisement also contains a disclosure that
payment of the rebate is subject to restrictions".

Referrals *from* unlicensed people are governed by 22 TAC § 535.20, Module 09: making a referral
with the expectation of receiving valuable consideration is itself an act requiring a licence, and
valuable consideration includes "gifts of merchandise having a retail value greater than $50". Fifty
dollars is the number to carry — a 40-dollar gift card to a past client who sent you a friend is
outside the definition; a 100-dollar one is not.

Payments from settlement service providers are governed by 22 TAC § 535.148, Module 05. Two things
to know at the moment such a payment is offered. You may not receive a commission, rebate or fee
from anyone other than the person you represent without first disclosing the intention to your
client and obtaining the client's consent — a failure the Act reaches twice, at § 1101.652(b)(8) and
(b)(13). And the rule prohibits paying or receiving a fee to or from another settlement service
provider for referrals or for inclusion on a preferred-provider list. Broker-to-broker referral fees
are lawful. A title company buying your marketing is not.

**Teams, assumed names and alternate names.** Rule 535.154 defines the three categories and imposes
a registration duty on each. An "Alternate name" is "a name used by an individual license holder
other than the name shown on the license issued by the Commission, such as a middle name, maiden
name, or nickname", and the rule expressly excludes "a common derivative of a name, such as Kim for
Kimberly or Bill for William, which is considered the same as the name shown on the license." So
Bill for William needs nothing; a maiden name does. "Before a license holder starts using an
alternate name in an advertisement, the license holder must register the name with the Commission
using a process acceptable to the Commission", and the license holder must notify the Commission and
their sponsoring broker "not later than the 10th day after the date the license holder stops using
an alternate name."

A "Team name" is "a name used by a group of one or more license holders sponsored by or associated
with the same broker that performs real estate activities under an exclusive collective name other
than the broker's licensed name or assumed business name." Three rules govern it. It "may not
include any terms that could mislead the public to believe that the team is offering brokerage
services independent from its sponsoring broker." It "must end with the word 'team' or 'group'." And
"Before an associated broker or a sales agent sponsored by a broker starts using a team name in an
advertisement, the broker must register the name with the Commission", with a ten-day notification
when use stops. Rule 535.155 supplies the enforcement edge, treating as misleading any advertisement
"that contains a team name with terms that imply that the team is offering brokerage services
independent from its sponsoring broker, including, but not limited to, 'brokerage', 'company', and
'associates'". "The Alvarez Group" is compliant. "Alvarez & Associates" is not.

An "Assumed business name" is "any name used in business by a broker that meets the requirements of
subsection (d) of this section, other than the name shown on the broker's license issued by the
Commission, a team name, or an alternate name." Before it is used in an advertisement the broker
must both "register the name with the Commission using a process acceptable to the Commission" and
"provide written evidence of legal authority to use the assumed business name in Texas, such as
registration of the name with the Secretary of State or county clerk's office", with the same
ten-day notice when use ceases.

Two responsibility rules from 22 TAC § 535.2 complete the picture, and they matter to anyone who
leads a team. The broker is responsible for ensuring that a sponsored sales agent's advertising
complies with §§ 535.154 and 535.155. And any license holder who leads, supervises, directs or
manages a team must be delegated as a supervisor in writing, with the broker reporting the
delegation to the Commission within 30 days where it has lasted or is expected to last more than
three consecutive months, and again within 30 days after it ends. If you run a team, you are a
delegated supervisor by rule, and the paperwork is the broker's to file.

**The office and the addresses.** Section 1101.552(a) states the office rule: "A resident broker
shall maintain a fixed office in this state. The address of the office shall be designated on the
broker's license." Subsection (b) sets the moving deadline: "Not later than the 10th day after the
date a broker moves from the address designated on the broker's license, the broker shall submit an
application, accompanied by the appropriate fee, for a license that designates the new location of
the broker's office." Subsection (d) exempts non-residents: "A nonresident licensed broker is not
required to maintain a place of business in this state."

Subsection (e) applies to every license holder, sales agents included. A license holder "shall
provide the commission with: (1) the license holder's current: (A) mailing address; (B) business
address; (C) business telephone number; and (D) business e-mail address; and (2) if the license
holder is an associated broker, the name of the broker with whom the license holder is associated."
Subsection (f) requires notice of a change in any of it. Rule 535.21 supplies the deadline — each
license holder "shall report all subsequent changes not later than the 10th day after the date of a
change of any of the listed contact information" — and the consequence of not doing so: "If a
license holder fails to update the contact information, the last known contact information provided
to the Commission is the license holder's contact information."

That last sentence is why this dull obligation belongs in a module about risk. Rule 535.21 provides
that the Commission sends notices to an active broker or an inactive license holder at the address
in its records, and sends notices for an active sales agent to the sales agent's *sponsoring
broker*. A complaint notice sent to a stale address is still effective service against you.

One geographic reminder closes the unit. Under 22 TAC § 535.4, taught in Module 01, the Act applies
to anyone acting as a broker or sales agent while physically within Texas regardless of where the
property or the client is, and to a person conducting brokerage from another state by mail,
telephone, internet or email where the property is located wholly or partly in Texas. You cannot
step outside these rules by working remotely.

## Unit: Risk Management

Risk management in Texas brokerage is not a philosophy. It is the practice of knowing, in advance,
which specific things go wrong, what the Commission does about them, what a court does about them,
and what you are supposed to do on the day you realise you are the one who got it wrong. Almost all
of the law in this unit belongs to other modules — the disciplinary catalogue and the penalty scheme
to Module 10, the Recovery Trust Account and the DTPA to Module 11, the allocation of
misrepresentation liability to Module 03. What is here is where each of them meets your file.

**How a complaint reaches you.** Section 1101.204, taught in Module 10, sets the frame: a signed,
written, non-anonymous complaint, submitted within four years of the incident, with reasonable cause
for an investigation, and prompt written notice to the license holder under investigation. Rule
535.141 then triages it.

Two consequences to absorb now. A complaint that names a sales agent but not the sponsoring broker
is, under 22 TAC § 535.141, also a complaint against that broker for the limited purpose of
determining the broker's involvement and whether the broker met the broker's professional
responsibilities — and § 1101.803, taught in Module 09, makes the broker liable to the Commission,
the public and clients for your conduct. Your mistake is your broker's problem by operation of
statute, which is why brokers write policies and why you follow them. Second, § 1101.652(a)(4) makes
it an independent ground for discipline to fail to provide, within a reasonable time, information
the Commission requests in connection with a complaint. A licence is more often lost for ignoring
the letter than for the original conduct.

**The recurring fact patterns, and where in the week each one is created.** Section 1101.652(b) is
the catalogue, and Module 10 teaches it subdivision by subdivision. Read as a working list, the
patterns you will actually meet are six, and each has a moment on the calendar when it is created:

*Silence about a defect* — § 1101.652(b)(3) and (b)(4), misrepresenting or failing to disclose a
significant defect known to the license holder. Created at the listing appointment, when the seller
mentions something in conversation that never reaches the § 5.008 notice or the file.

*Advertising* — § 1101.652(b)(23), operationalised by 22 TAC §§ 535.154 and 535.155. Created the
moment you press publish. This is the most mechanical violation in the entire scheme and therefore
the easiest to prove: a screenshot of a post with the broker's name too small, or absent, is a
complete case.

*Money* — § 1101.652(b)(9), (10), (30) and (31), operationalised by 22 TAC § 535.146. Created on the
second working day after receipt, and provable from a bank statement.

*Compensation and undisclosed interest* — § 1101.652(b)(8), (11), (13) and (16), with § 1101.651 and
22 TAC §§ 535.147 and 535.148. Created when the payment is arranged, not when it is received.

*Authority and interference* — § 1101.652(b)(18) through (b)(22): a sign without written consent,
offering property without the owner's knowledge and consent, offering on unauthorised terms,
inducing a party to break a contract in order to substitute a new one, and negotiating with a party
you know is under an exclusive agency with another broker. On that last one, 22 TAC § 535.153,
taught in Module 04, draws the line precisely: soliciting a listing from an owner whose property is
subject to another broker's exclusive listing is permitted; negotiating the present transaction is
not.

*Paperwork that was supposed to exist* — § 1101.652(a-1)(3), (b)(12), (b)(28), (b)(29) and (b)(34):
the wrong form, no termination date, no copy provided on request, no written title advisory, no
buyer agreement under § 1101.563. Every one of these is created by an omission on a day when nothing
appeared to go wrong.

Discrimination sits across all of them, at § 1101.652(b)(32) and 22 TAC § 531.19, taught in Module
10 alongside the federal fair housing provisions.

**What it costs.** The Commission's remedies are cumulative, not alternative, and Module 10 teaches
the scheme: suspension or revocation, an administrative penalty capped by § 1101.702(a) at 5,000
dollars "for each violation" with "Each day a violation continues or occurs" capable of being a
separate violation, and the three published ranges of rule 535.191. Note § 1101.702(b), which puts
"efforts to correct the violation" among the factors that set the amount — the reason the last
section of this unit is worth more to you than any other. Beyond penalties there are a cease and
desist order under § 1101.759, an injunctive action under § 1101.751, and, for a wilful violation of
the chapter or a Commission order, a Class A misdemeanour under § 1101.756.

One consequence lands on your calendar rather than in a tier table. Under 22 TAC § 535.141, a person
whose licence is suspended "may not during the period of any suspension perform, attempt to perform,
or advertise to perform any act for which a license is required", and must give written notice "not
later than the third day before the date of the suspension" — a sales agent to the sponsoring
broker; a broker to sponsored sales agents and to any entity for which the person is designated
broker; and, where the person is contractually obliged to perform licensed services, to all other
parties to that contract.

**Beyond the Commission.** Three exposures run alongside discipline, and all three are taught
elsewhere. The Real Estate Recovery Trust Account, §§ 1101.601–.610 and Module 11, pays an aggrieved
person's actual damages after a judgment goes unsatisfied, subject to the caps that module sets out,
and then looks to you: it is your licence that answers for the reimbursement. The DTPA, Business &
Commerce Code ch. 17 and Module 11, adds mental anguish and up to treble damages for knowing or
intentional conduct, and the consumer's attorney's fees under § 17.50(d). And § 1101.805, taught in
Module 03, allocates responsibility for misrepresentation between you and your client on two
elements — knowledge of the falsity and failure to disclose that knowledge. The practical reading:
you are not an insurer of your seller's statements, but the moment you know a statement is false,
staying quiet makes it yours. Suit for fraud or breach of fiduciary duty must be brought within four
years, Civil Practice and Remedies Code § 16.004(a) — the same four years for which your file must
survive.

**The day you realise you got it wrong.** This is the part of risk management that is almost never
taught, and it is the part you will actually need. There is no held Texas source that prescribes a
self-correction procedure, and none is invented here. What the held sources do provide is a set of
obligations that, taken together, describe the only defensible response.

Tell your broker immediately. Rule 535.2 makes the broker responsible for your authorized acts and
for trust funds, and § 1101.803 makes the broker liable to the Commission, the public and clients
for your conduct; concealing a problem from the person who is legally answerable for it converts a
mistake into a betrayal.

Correct the record in writing, and correct it toward everyone who relied on it. Rule 535.156 imposes
an affirmative duty to keep the principal informed of significant information applicable to the
transaction and a duty to convey accurate information to members of the public with whom you deal.
If you quoted the wrong square footage in an advertisement, the fix is a corrected writing to the
people who saw it, not a quiet edit.

If money is involved, use the notice rules that already exist. Under 22 TAC § 535.146 a broker whose
trust-money check is dishonoured "shall immediately notify all parties to the transaction in
writing", must notify all parties in writing of any disbursement made on a written demand or a
subsequent written agreement, and may interplead where entitlement genuinely cannot be determined.

Do not touch the file except to add to it. Section 1101.652(a-1)(2) makes it a ground for discipline
to fail or refuse to produce records for inspection; 22 TAC § 535.2 requires the broker to keep the
eight listed record categories four years in a format readily available to the Commission; and the
only excuse the rule recognises for missing records is destruction "by an 'Act of God' such as a
natural disaster or fire not intentionally caused by the broker".

Answer the Commission. Section 1101.652(a)(4) makes failure to provide requested information within
a reasonable time its own ground for discipline, and § 1101.702(b) lists "efforts to correct the
violation" among the factors that set the penalty amount. Prompt correction is not merely decent; it
is statutorily relevant to what you will pay.

And do not offer legal conclusions about your own mistake to the person affected by it. That is
exactly the advice 22 TAC § 537.11 forbids you to give, and it is the moment when the person harmed
should be talking to a lawyer — whom you may not discourage them from employing.

### Applied exercise: The earnest money that bounced

You are a sales agent sponsored by Bluebonnet Realty. You represent the seller of 812 Comal Street
in San Marcos. On Friday 6 March at 4:30 in the afternoon the buyer's agent hands you a 7,500 dollar
earnest money check made payable to your broker. You put it in your car console over the weekend and
give it to your broker on Monday 9 March. The broker deposits it in the brokerage trust account on
Wednesday 11 March. On Wednesday 18 March the bank returns the check unpaid. The buyer terminates
during the option period on Friday 20 March, and on Monday 23 March both the buyer and the seller
make written demand for the earnest money, each claiming all of it. Nothing in the contract varies
any deadline. Assume Saturdays and Sundays are not working days.

1. Under 22 TAC § 535.146, what should you have done with the check on Friday afternoon? Quote the
words of the rule that answer it, and say whether a sales agent may hold trust money in an account
of their own at any time. 2. State the deposit deadline the rule sets for the broker, the date from
which it is counted, and the only way it may be varied. Taking Monday 9 March as the date the broker
received the money, identify the last permitted day for deposit and say whether the 11 March deposit
met it. Whose conduct in this fact pattern is the violation? 3. The check is dishonoured on 18
March. State the broker's duty on that date, to whom it runs, and in what medium. 4. Both parties
make written demand on 23 March and their claims conflict. State the period within which the rule
requires payment to the party entitled, identify the last day of that period on these facts, and
state the step the rule permits where the broker cannot reasonably determine who is entitled. 5. The
transaction ends without a closing. For how long, and running from what date, must the trust-account
documentation be kept, and which four subdivisions of § 1101.652(b) does the Act use to make
trust-money failures disciplinary grounds?

### Module recap

This module took the whole of the Law of Agency and made it a sequence of acts. The recap is
deliberately a map rather than a second telling: for each step, what you do, and which module
teaches the law behind it.

**The seller-side sequence.** Reply to the first substantive communication with the IABS already
attached, and disclose whom you represent at first contact with the other side — § 1101.558 and 22
TAC § 531.20, taught in Module 02; what is yours here is that the trigger is the communication, not
the appointment. Take a CMA to the listing appointment with the sentence 22 TAC § 535.17 prescribes
reproduced verbatim inside it at no smaller than 12-point, submitted in the broker's name; that rule
is this module's own. Sign a listing in the broker's name with a definite termination date and
written sign consent — 22 TAC § 535.2 in Module 09, § 1101.652(b)(12) and (b)(18) in Module 10 —
and understand that it is written because § 1101.806(c), Module 04, is the only reason your fee is
recoverable. Get the § 5.008 notice completed by the seller that same evening, because § 5.008(f)
gives a buyer who receives it late a seven-day right to terminate for any reason; Module 03 teaches
the notice. Advertise inside 22 TAC § 535.155, this module's rule, including the half-size
broker-name requirement and the ten-day take-down. Convey every offer unless the principal has
agreed *in writing* otherwise, 22 TAC § 535.156 in Module 02.

**The buyer-side sequence.** IABS at the first substantive communication; written agreement before
you show any residential real property or, if nothing is shown, before you present an offer —
§ 1101.563, taught in Module 05, with the showing-only agreement capped at fourteen days and
non-exclusive under § 1101.563(e) and non-representation preserved only within § 1101.562's four
conditions and its narrow permission to confirm size, price and terms. Intermediary consents, if the
brokerage will hold both sides, before the buyer writes; the machinery is Module 06's. At signature
of the offer, the written title advisory § 1101.555 requires, on pain of forfeiting the whole
commission under § 1101.806(d); that pairing is this module's. Then the contract inside 22 TAC
§ 537.11's boundary — promulgated forms, no drafting or recommending of escalation, appraisal or
contingency language, no opinions on legal effect or title, no discouraging a lawyer, and changes
only on a principal's specific written instruction made conspicuous — with § 1101.654 behind it in
Module 10. Earnest money then moves in four steps under 22 TAC § 535.146: immediate delivery to the
broker, deposit by close of business of the second working day, no commingling, disbursement only
per the agreement or a written demand honoured within thirty days.

**The everyday numbers.** Two calendar days to respond, 22 TAC § 535.157, Module 02. Two calendar
days for a broker to answer a sponsored agent and three to pass on Commission correspondence, 22 TAC
§ 535.2, Module 09. Four years of records in the eight categories § 535.2 lists — matching the
four-year investigation window of § 1101.204(c) and the four-year limitations period of Civil
Practice and Remedies Code § 16.004(a). Ten days to register or stop using a name under 22 TAC
§ 535.154, to update contact information under 22 TAC § 535.21, and for a broker to file a new
office address under § 1101.552(b); those last three are this module's own.

**The other considerations.** 22 TAC § 535.144 — the module's own rule and the one nobody else
teaches — requires written disclosure that you are a licensed broker or sales agent acting for your
own account, extends that to a spouse, parent or child, a business entity in which you are more than
a ten percent owner and certain trusts, and forbids using your expertise to the disadvantage of the
person you deal with. Compensation is confined by § 1101.651 and routed through the sponsoring
broker (Module 09); referrals for valuable consideration over fifty dollars require a licence under
22 TAC § 535.20 (Module 09); settlement-service referral fees are barred by 22 TAC § 535.148 (Module
05). Team, alternate and assumed names are registered before use under 22 TAC § 535.154, and the
office and contact-information duties are § 1101.552 and 22 TAC § 535.21 — again this module's.

**Risk management.** Complaints must be signed and written, cannot be anonymous, and cannot reach
back beyond four years (§ 1101.204); they are triaged by 22 TAC § 535.141; a complaint against a
sales agent reaches the sponsoring broker; penalties, tiers and the disciplinary catalogue of
§ 1101.652 are Module 10's; the Recovery Trust Account and the DTPA are Module 11's; the allocation
of misrepresentation liability in § 1101.805 is Module 03's. What is yours is the day it happens:
tell your broker, correct the record in writing to everyone who relied on it, use the notice rules
that already exist if money is involved, add to the file and never subtract, answer the Commission,
and do not offer the injured person a legal conclusion about your own mistake.

**Where each topic lives.** Carry the course out as a map, not as twelve piles of citations. *Agency
Concepts* (Module 01) gives you the vocabulary of the Act and who needs a licence. *Basic Agency
Relationships, Disclosure and Duties to the Client* (Module 02) gives you the fiduciary Canons, the
timely-response and offer-conveyance rules, and the IABS. *Duties and Disclosures to Third Parties*
(Module 03) gives you the lesser but real duty of honesty to everyone, the seller's disclosure
notice, and the misrepresentation-liability allocation. *Seller Agency* (Module 04) gives you the
listing rules, the writing requirements behind your fee, and the CMA obligation. *Buyer Agency*
(Module 05) gives you the 2026 sections on written agreements and non-representation showings.
*Representing More Than One Party: Intermediary Brokerage* (Module 06) gives you the Texas answer to
conflict. *Creation and Termination of Agency* (Module 07) fixes when the relationship begins and
ends and what survives it. *Clarifying Agency Relationships* (Module 08) covers the consumer
information notice and brokerage disclosure policy. *Employment Issues* (Module 09) places you under
a sponsoring broker and sets the record, supervision and compensation rules. *Agency, Ethics and the
Law* (Module 10) supplies the standards, the disciplinary catalogue and the penalty scheme.
*Deceptive Trade Practices and Consumer Protection Act* (Module 11) supplies the consumer's own
weapon and the Recovery Trust Account. And this topic, *Implementation and Presentation*, is where
all of it becomes Tuesday morning.

If you retain nothing else from this module, retain the rules it owns, because they are the ones no
other module will teach you: 22 TAC § 535.17 for the price-opinion sentence you must reproduce
verbatim; 22 TAC §§ 535.154 and 535.155 for how you may present yourself and your team; 22 TAC
§ 537.11 for the practice-of-law line; 22 TAC § 535.144 for acting on your own account; § 1101.555
with § 1101.806(d) for the title advisory and the fee you forfeit without it; and § 1101.552 with 22
TAC § 535.21 for the office and the addresses at which the Commission will find you. For everything
else, retain the sequence and the module that holds the rule.
