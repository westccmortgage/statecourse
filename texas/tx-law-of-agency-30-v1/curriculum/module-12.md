# Texas Law of Agency — Module 12

## TREC Topic: Implementation and Presentation

**Prescribed time: 140 minutes.** This module carries the five units the Commission's LOA-1 course
approval form places under *Implementation and Presentation*.

**Learning objectives.** After completing this module you will be able to: run a Texas seller-side
engagement from the listing appointment to closing and name the statute or rule that compels each
step, including the representation disclosure under Occupations Code § 1101.558, the price-opinion
language required verbatim by 22 TAC § 535.17, the listing obligations of 22 TAC § 535.16, the
seller's disclosure notice under Property Code § 5.008, and the advertising requirements of 22 TAC
§§ 535.154 and 535.155; run a buyer-side engagement from first contact to closing under the
written-agreement requirement of § 1101.563, the non-representation showing rules of § 1101.562, the
title-or-abstract notice of § 1101.555, the contract-form boundary of 22 TAC § 537.11, and the
trust-money rules of 22 TAC § 535.146; build a daily working routine around the two-calendar-day
response duty of 22 TAC § 535.157, the four-year record set of 22 TAC § 535.2, and the acts that 22
TAC § 537.11 and § 1101.654 define as the unauthorized practice of law; handle transactions for your
own account under 22 TAC § 535.144, referral and rebate arrangements under § 1101.651 and 22 TAC §§
535.147 and 535.148, team and assumed names under 22 TAC § 535.154, and the office and
contact-information duties of § 1101.552; and identify the fact patterns that generate Texas
complaints, act correctly after your own mistake, and state the disciplinary, trust-account and
civil consequences that attach under § 1101.652, 22 TAC § 535.191, § 1101.610 and Business &
Commerce Code § 17.50.

## Unit: The Broker Working for/with the Seller

Everything you have studied so far in this course has been law in the abstract: what agency is, whom
you owe duties to, what the Commission may do to you. This module is the same law arranged
differently. It is arranged as a week of work. You will walk a seller from the moment the phone
rings to the moment the funding wire lands, and at every step you will be asked the same question a
Commission investigator asks: *what required you to do that, and where is the paper that proves you
did it?*

Start with a concrete engagement so the sequence has something to attach to. A homeowner in
Pflugerville emails your brokerage's website form: three-bedroom, two-bath, built in 2004, they
think it is worth about 385,000 dollars, they want to know what you would charge. You reply and set
an appointment for Thursday evening. That single email exchange has already put two legal
obligations in motion, and most new license holders miss both.

**The first substantive communication and the two disclosures.** Occupations Code § 1101.558(b)
provides that "A license holder who represents a party in a proposed real estate transaction shall
disclose, orally or in writing, that representation at the time of the license holder's first
contact with: (1) another party to the transaction; or (2) another license holder who represents
another party to the transaction." That subsection is about telling the *other side* whom you work
for, and it governs your dealings with the buyer's agent later. The subsection that governs the
seller in front of you is § 1101.558(b-1): "At the time of a license holder's first substantive
communication with a party relating to a proposed transaction regarding specific real property, the
license holder shall provide to the party written notice in at least a 10-point font" that describes
the ways a broker can represent a party, including as an intermediary; describes the basic duties a
broker owes a party the broker represents; describes the basic obligations a broker owes a party the
broker does *not* represent; and "provides the name, license number, and contact information for the
license holder and the license holder's supervisor and broker, if applicable."

That notice is the Information About Brokerage Services form. Section 1101.558(b-2) hands the
Commission the job of prescribing the text and the delivery methods, and 22 TAC § 531.20 does
exactly that: the Commission "adopts by reference the Information About Brokerage Services Notice,
TREC No. IABS 1-2 (IABS Notice)." Rule 531.20 then tells you how you may deliver it "For purposes of
§1101.558, Texas Occupations Code": "by personal delivery by the license holder; by first class mail
or overnight common carrier delivery service; in the body of an email; or as an attachment to an
email, or a link within the body of an email, with a specific reference to the IABS Notice in the
body of the email." And it tells you one thing you may not do: "The link to a completed IABS Notice
may not be in a footnote or signature block in an email."

Read that last sentence twice, because it is the single most common IABS mistake in Texas practice.
A sales agent puts a permanent IABS link in the email signature block, believes the box is ticked
forever, and has in fact never complied with § 1101.558(b-1) with any client. The rule forecloses
that shortcut by name. If you send the IABS as a link in the body of an email, the body of the email
must specifically refer to the IABS Notice.

The timing trigger is "first substantive communication." The statute does not define the phrase, and
this course does not have a definition to give you, so teach yourself the safe habit instead: the
Pflugerville email named a specific property and discussed a proposed transaction, so treat that
reply as the trigger and attach the completed IABS to it. Section 1101.558(c) lists the three
situations in which the notice is not required — a residential lease for less than one year where a
sale is not being considered, a meeting with a party you know is represented by another license
holder, and a communication at a property "held open for any prospective buyer or tenant" where "the
communication concerns that property" — and a listing appointment is none of them.

The second thing you owe from the outset is the Consumer Protection Notice. Rule 531.18 adopts by
reference "the Consumer Protection Notice, TREC No. CN 1-5" and requires each license holder to
provide it by "displaying it in a readily noticeable location in each place of business the broker
maintains" and by "providing a link to it in a readily noticeable place on the homepage of each
business website," labelled either "Texas Real Estate Commission Consumer Protection Notice", in at
least 10 point font; or "TREC Consumer Protection Notice", in at least 12 point font. Rule 531.20
imposes the parallel homepage-link duty for the IABS with the same two labelling options at the same
two font sizes. If you run your own website or a personal page under your broker's domain, those
links are your responsibility, and § 535.191 lists both 22 TAC § 531.18 and 22 TAC § 531.20 in the
tier carrying "An administrative penalty range of $500 - $3,000 per violation per day."

**The price opinion, and the sentence you must reproduce verbatim.** At the Thursday appointment you
will be asked what the house is worth. Answering that question is regulated, and it is regulated in
two directions at once.

The outer boundary is 22 TAC § 535.17: "A real estate license holder may not perform an appraisal
of, or provide an opinion of value for, real property unless the license holder is licensed or
certified under Texas Occupations Code, Chapter 1103." You are not an appraiser. What you may
prepare instead is described in the title of the rule and in the Act's definition of a broker: §
1101.002(1)(A)(xi) includes within brokerage the act of providing "a written analysis, opinion, or
conclusion relating to the estimated price of real property if the analysis, opinion, or conclusion:
(a) is not referred to as an appraisal; (b) is provided in the ordinary course of the person's
business; and (c) is related to the actual or potential management, acquisition, disposition, or
encumbrance of an interest in real property." A comparative market analysis is brokerage. An
appraisal is not.

Because the two products look alike to a consumer, § 535.17 requires you to say so in writing. If
you provide "a broker price opinion, comparative market analysis, or estimated worth or sale price
under the Act," you "shall also provide the person for whom the opinion, analysis, or estimate is
prepared with a written statement containing the following language: 'This represents an estimated
sale price for this property. It is not the same as the opinion of value in an appraisal developed
by a licensed appraiser under the Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice.'" And the
rule tells you how the sentence must appear: it "must be made part of any written opinion, analysis,
or estimate of worth or sale price and must be reproduced verbatim in at least 12-point font."

Three practical consequences follow. First, "verbatim" means verbatim — you may not tidy the
sentence, shorten it, or paraphrase it into your own listing-presentation voice. Second, "made part
of" means it belongs inside the CMA document, not in a separate email. Third, 12-point is a floor,
and a CMA laid out in 9-point body text with the disclaimer matched to the body text violates the
rule even though the words are right. Rule 535.191 places 22 TAC § 535.17 in the $500 to $3,000 per
violation per day tier.

One more clause of § 535.17 matters enormously to a sales agent: "A sales agent may prepare, sign,
and present a broker price opinion, comparative market analysis, or estimate of worth or sale price
for the sales agent's sponsoring broker, but the sales agent must submit the broker price opinion,
comparative market analysis, or estimate of worth or sale price in the broker's name and the broker
is responsible for it." You may build it and you may present it. It goes out in the broker's name.
This is the same principle 22 TAC § 535.2 states for engagements generally — "Listings and other
agreements for real estate brokerage services must be solicited and accepted in a broker's name" —
and the same principle § 1101.803 states for liability: "A licensed broker is liable to the
commission, the public, and the broker's clients for any conduct engaged in under this chapter by
the broker or by a sales agent associated with or acting for the broker."

There is a second, easily forgotten CMA duty in 22 TAC § 535.16: "A real estate license holder is
obligated to provide a broker price opinion or comparative market analysis on a property when
negotiating a listing or offering to purchase the property for the license holder's own account as a
result of contact made while acting as a real estate agent." Note both halves. The obligation
attaches when you are negotiating a listing — so the Pflugerville CMA is not optional courtesy, it
is required by rule — and it attaches again if you decide you would like to buy the house yourself
after meeting the owner in your capacity as an agent. The second half is the anti-self-dealing half,
and Unit 4 returns to it.

**The listing agreement.** Rule 535.16 opens with the sentence that defines what a listing is for:
"A broker is obligated under a listing contract to negotiate the best possible transaction for the
principal, the broker has agreed to represent." Everything else in the rule follows from that duty.

That is why the same rule constrains net listings. It defines one — "A 'net listing' is a listing
agreement in which the broker's commission is the difference ('net') between the sales proceeds and
an amount desired by the owner of the real property" — and then restricts it: "A broker may not take
net listings unless the principal requires a net listing and the principal appears to be familiar
with current market values of real property." The rule states its own reasoning, which is worth
memorising because it is the clearest statement of conflict-of-interest logic anywhere in the TREC
rules: "The use of a net listing places an upper limit on the principal's expectancy and places the
broker's interest above the principal's interest with reference to obtaining the best possible
price." And if one is used, "the listing agreement must assure the principal of not less than the
principal's desired price and limit the broker to a specified maximum commission." A net listing is
therefore not flatly forbidden in Texas; it is hedged with a condition about who asks for it, a
condition about the principal's sophistication, and two mandatory terms. In everyday residential
practice the honest advice is to decline, and to say why: the arrangement pays you more the worse
you do for your client.

Three further statutory constraints shape the listing paper itself. Section 1101.652(b)(12) makes it
a ground for discipline if a license holder "fails to specify a definite termination date that is
not subject to prior notice in a contract, other than a contract to perform property management
services, in which the license holder agrees to perform services for which a license is required
under this chapter." An open-ended listing "until sold" is a disciplinary problem, not a marketing
preference. Section 1101.155(c) requires that a listing contract form adopted by the Commission
"must include: (1) a provision informing the parties to the contract that real estate commissions
are negotiable; and (2) a provision explaining the availability of Texas coastal natural hazards
information important to coastal residents, if that information is appropriate." And §
1101.652(b)(18) makes it a ground for discipline to place "a sign on real property offering the real
property for sale or lease without obtaining the written consent of the owner of the real property
or the owner's authorized agent." The signed listing agreement is ordinarily where that written
consent lives; if you are marketing under some arrangement that is not a signed listing, get the
sign consent in writing separately.

**Why the writing is not a formality: § 1101.806.** New license holders treat the listing agreement
as an administrative step. It is in fact the only thing standing between you and unpaid work.
Section 1101.806(c) provides: "A person may not maintain an action in this state to recover a
commission for the sale or purchase of real estate unless the promise or agreement on which the
action is based, or a memorandum, is in writing and signed by the party against whom the action is
brought or by a person authorized by that party to sign the document." A seller who shook your hand,
watched you spend 4,000 dollars on photography and staging, and then closed with a neighbour owes
you nothing you can sue for if there is no signed writing.

Section 1101.806(b) adds the licensing condition: a person may not maintain an action to collect
compensation for an act as a broker or sales agent performed in this state "unless the person
alleges and proves that the person was: (1) a license holder at the time the act was commenced; or
(2) an attorney licensed in any state." The test is your status "at the time the act was commenced,"
which is why a lapsed licence during a listing period is a compensation catastrophe and not merely a
renewal problem.

The Business & Commerce Code reinforces the point from the other direction. Section 26.01(a)
provides that a promise or agreement described in subsection (b) "is not enforceable unless the
promise or agreement, or a memorandum of it, is (1) in writing; and (2) signed by the person to be
charged with the promise or agreement or by someone lawfully authorized to sign for him," and §
26.01(b)(4) and (b)(5) apply that rule to "a contract for the sale of real estate" and "a lease of
real estate for a term longer than one year." So the listing must be written to be enforceable as to
your fee under § 1101.806(c), and the sales contract you will later write must be written to be
enforceable at all.

**Property Code § 5.008: the seller's disclosure notice.** Once the listing is signed, the next
thing you do — before the photographs, before the sign — is get the seller's disclosure notice
completed. Section 5.008(a) states the duty: "A seller of residential real property comprising not
more than one dwelling unit located in this state shall give to the purchaser of the property a
written notice as prescribed by this section or a written notice substantially similar to the notice
prescribed by this section which contains, at a minimum, all of the items in the notice prescribed
by this section."

Four features of that sentence control your workflow. It is the **seller's** duty, not yours; your
job is to obtain it, deliver it and never fill it in for them. It applies to residential property
"comprising not more than one dwelling unit," so a duplex is outside it. It permits a substantially
similar form so long as the form contains at a minimum all the items in the statutory notice. And
the notice itself opens with a legend the statute prints in capitals: "THIS NOTICE IS A DISCLOSURE
OF SELLER'S KNOWLEDGE OF THE CONDITION OF THE PROPERTY AS OF THE DATE SIGNED BY SELLER AND IS NOT A
SUBSTITUTE FOR ANY INSPECTIONS OR WARRANTIES THE PURCHASER MAY WISH TO OBTAIN. IT IS NOT A WARRANTY
OF ANY KIND BY SELLER OR SELLER'S AGENTS."

Section 5.008(d) governs how the seller answers: "The notice shall be completed to the best of
seller's belief and knowledge as of the date the notice is completed and signed by the seller. If
the information required by the notice is unknown to the seller, the seller shall indicate that fact
on the notice, and by that act is in compliance with this section." Say that sentence to a nervous
seller. "Unknown" is a lawful answer. Guessing is not, and a guess that turns out wrong becomes the
buyer's misrepresentation claim.

Section 5.008(f) sets the timing and the buyer's remedy: "The notice shall be delivered by the
seller to the purchaser on or before the effective date of an executory contract binding the
purchaser to purchase the property. If a contract is entered without the seller providing the notice
required by this section, the purchaser may terminate the contract for any reason within seven days
after receiving the notice." Seven days, for any reason. That is why a listing agent who leaves the
notice until after an offer is accepted has handed the buyer a free option on the deal. Get it at
the listing appointment.

Section 5.008(e) exempts eleven categories of transfer, including a transfer "pursuant to a court
order or foreclosure sale," a transfer "by a trustee in bankruptcy," a transfer "by a fiduciary in
the course of the administration of a decedent's estate, guardianship, conservatorship, or trust," a
transfer "from one co-owner to one or more other co-owners," a transfer "made to a spouse or to a
person or persons in the lineal line of consanguinity of one or more of the transferors," a transfer
"to or from any governmental entity," and a transfer "of a new residence of not more than one
dwelling unit which has not previously been occupied for residential purposes." Estate sales and new
construction are the two exemptions you will meet most often. An exemption from § 5.008 is not,
however, permission to conceal: § 1101.652(b)(3) and (b)(4) make it a ground for discipline to make
"a material misrepresentation to a potential buyer concerning a significant defect, including a
latent structural defect, known to the license holder" or to fail "to disclose to a potential buyer
a defect described by Subdivision (3) that is known to the license holder," and those duties do not
depend on which form is used.

Two things you are *not* required to disclose. Section 5.008(c) provides that "A seller or seller's
agent shall have no duty to make a disclosure or release information related to whether a death by
natural causes, suicide, or accident unrelated to the condition of the property occurred on the
property or whether a previous occupant had, may have had, has, or may have AIDS, HIV related
illnesses, or HIV infection." Occupations Code § 1101.556 says the same thing to you directly:
"Notwithstanding other law, a license holder is not required to inquire about, disclose, or release
information relating to whether: (1) a previous or current occupant of real property had, may have
had, has, or may have AIDS, an HIV-related illness, or an HIV infection as defined by the Centers
for Disease Control and Prevention of the United States Public Health Service; or (2) a death
occurred on a property by natural causes, suicide, or accident unrelated to the condition of the
property." Read the qualifier in both: the death exemption covers natural causes, suicide, or
accident *unrelated to the condition of the property*. A death caused by a defective balcony rail is
a death related to the condition of the property, and nothing in either section relieves you of the
disclosure duties that attach to a known defect.

**Marketing the listing.** The moment you publish anything about the Pflugerville house, 22 TAC §
535.155 applies. Its scope is deliberately enormous. An "Advertisement" is "any form of
communication by or on behalf of a license holder designed to attract the public to use real estate
brokerage services and includes, but is not limited to, all publications, brochures, radio or
television broadcasts, all electronic media including email, text messages, social media, the
Internet, business stationery, business cards, displays, signs and billboards." The rule carves out
only two things: "a communication from a license holder to the license holder's current client; and
a directional sign that may also contain only the broker's name or logo."

Every advertisement must include, "in a readily noticeable location in the advertisement," two
items: "the name of the license holder or team placing the advertisement" and "the broker's name in
at least half the size of the largest contact information for any sales agent, associated broker, or
team name contained in the advertisement." That is a size ratio, not a mere presence requirement,
and "Contact Information" is broadly defined as "any information that can be used to contact a
license holder featured in the advertisement, including a name, phone number, email address, website
address, social media handle, scan code or other similar information." If your Instagram tile
carries your handle in 60-point type, the broker's name must be at least 30-point. For social media
and text, the rule allows the required information to sit "on a separate page or on the account user
profile page of the license holder," but only if that page is "readily accessible by a direct link
from the social media or text" and "readily noticeable on the separate page or in the account user
profile."

The rule then lists, "For purposes of this section and §1101.652(b)(23) of the Act," the
advertisements that mislead or are likely to deceive. Several of them are ordinary marketing habits.
An advertisement violates the rule if it is "inaccurate in any material fact or representation"; if
it "identifies a sales agent as a broker"; if it "uses a title, such as owner, president, CEO, COO,
or other similar title, email or website address that implies a sales agent is responsible for the
operations of a brokerage"; if it "contains the name of a sales agent that is not the name as shown
on the sales agent's license issued by the Commission or an alternate name registered with the
Commission"; if it "creates confusion regarding the permitted use of a property"; if it is "about
the value of a property, unless it is based on an appraisal that is disclosed and readily available
upon request by a party or it is given in compliance with §535.17"; or if it "implies the person
making the advertisement was involved in a transaction regarding a property when the person had no
such role."

Three of the listed items are pure timing rules, and they catch careful people. An advertisement
violates the rule if it is "about a property that is subject to an exclusive listing agreement
without the permission of the listing broker and without disclosing the name of the listing broker
unless the listing broker has expressly agreed in writing to waive disclosure"; if it is "offering a
listed property that is not discontinued within 10 days after the listing agreement is no longer in
effect"; or if it is "about a property 10 days or more after the closing of a transaction unless the
current status of the property is included in the advertisement." So when your Pflugerville listing
expires, you have ten days to take down the sign, the landing page, the boosted post and the
syndicated feed. When it closes, your "Just sold!" post is fine for ten days, and after that it
needs the current status included.

**Presenting offers.** This is the point at which the fiduciary rules stop being theory. Rule
535.156 states the standard: "A license holder's relationship with the license holder's principal is
that of a fiduciary. A license holder shall convey to the principal all known information which
would affect the principal's decision on whether or not to make, accept or reject offers; however,
if the principal has agreed in writing that offers are not to be submitted after the principal has
entered into a contract to buy, sell, rent, or lease a property, the license holder shall have no
duty to submit offers to the principal after the principal has accepted an offer."

Take the exception seriously, and take its limits seriously. The default is that every offer goes to
the seller. The only escape the rule provides is a written agreement by the principal that offers
are not to be submitted after the principal has entered into a contract, and even then the relief
runs only to offers arriving after acceptance. There is no oral version of this exception, and there
is no "the seller told me not to bother them with lowballs" version. If your Pflugerville seller
does not want back-up offers presented after they go under contract, that instruction goes in
writing, signed, in the file.

Rule 535.156 continues with three sentences that decide most complaints: "The license holder must
put the interest of the license holder's principal above the license holder's own interest. A
license holder must deal honestly and fairly with all parties; however, the license holder
represents only the principal and owes a duty of fidelity to such principal." "A license holder has
an affirmative duty to keep the principal informed at all times of significant information
applicable to the transaction or transactions in which the license holder is acting as agent for the
principal." "A license holder has a duty to convey accurate information to members of the public
with whom the license holder deals." The word "affirmative" is doing work: silence is a breach. Rule
535.191 places 22 TAC § 535.156 in the top penalty tier, "$1,000 - $5,000 per violation per day."

The Act says the same thing in § 1101.557(b). A broker who represents a party or lists real estate
under an exclusive agreement "must inform the party if the broker receives material information
related to a transaction to list, buy, sell, or lease the party's real estate, including the receipt
of an offer by the broker" and "shall, at a minimum, answer the party's questions and present any
offer to or from the party." And 22 TAC § 535.2 states the broker-level version: "A broker owes the
highest fiduciary obligation to the principal and is obliged to convey to the principal all
information known to the agent which may affect the principal's decision unless prohibited by other
law."

Two authority limits govern how you negotiate. Section 1101.652(b)(19) makes it a ground for
discipline to offer "to sell or lease real property without the knowledge and consent of the owner
of the real property or the owner's authorized agent," and § 1101.652(b)(20) to offer "to sell or
lease real property on terms other than those authorized by the owner of the real property or the
owner's authorized agent." You do not float a price your seller has not approved, and you do not
signal flexibility your seller has not authorised — a rule that also disposes of the common
temptation to tell a buyer's agent that "they'd probably take 375."

**Closing.** Two items belong to the seller's agent at the end. The first is money: any earnest
money or other trust money that comes into the brokerage is governed by 22 TAC § 535.146, and Unit 2
works through it in full because the buyer's side usually delivers it. The second is compensation.
Section 1101.651(a) permits a licensed broker to pay a commission only to a license holder or to "a
real estate broker licensed in another state who does not conduct in this state any of the
negotiations for which the commission or other compensation is paid," and § 1101.651(b) provides
that "A sales agent may not accept compensation for a real estate transaction from a person other
than the broker that is sponsoring the sales agent or was sponsoring the sales agent when the sales
agent earned the compensation." Rule 535.3 states the sales agent's side of the same rule: a sales
agent "may not receive a commission or other valuable consideration except with the written consent
of the sales agent's sponsoring broker or the broker who sponsored the sales agent when the sales
agent became entitled to the commission or other valuable consideration."

### Statute reading exercise: Reading § 535.17 and § 535.16 against each other

Open 22 TAC § 535.17 and 22 TAC § 535.16 and answer from the text alone.

1. Section 535.17 requires a specific sentence to accompany a broker price opinion or CMA. Copy the
sentence exactly, then state the two formatting requirements the rule imposes on it and where in the
document the rule says it must appear.
2. Section 535.17 says a sales agent may prepare, sign and present a CMA. Whose name must it be
submitted in, and who does the rule say is responsible for it? What does that tell you about why the
answer is not "the person who did the work"?
3. Section 535.16 creates an obligation to provide a BPO or CMA in two situations. Name both, and
identify the words in the second situation that limit it — the rule does not say a license holder
must produce a CMA before buying any property at all.
4. Section 535.16 permits a net listing only in stated circumstances. List every condition the rule
attaches, including the two terms the listing agreement itself must contain. Does the rule say a net
listing is prohibited?
5. Neither section states a required format, comparable count or adjustment method for a CMA. What
follows for a licence holder who wants to know how many comparables to use?

### Applied exercise: The Pflugerville listing

You list 1408 Wilbarger Court in Pflugerville on 3 March at 385,000 dollars under a written listing
agreement with a definite termination date of 3 September. The agreed commission is 5.5 percent of
the sales price, of which your brokerage will offer 2.5 percent to a cooperating broker. Your seller
completes the § 5.008 notice on 3 March and answers "Unknown" to the age of the roof. On 14 April a
buyer's agent emails an offer of 372,000 dollars. On 15 April, before your seller responds, a second
buyer's agent calls to say a written offer of 380,000 dollars is coming "later today." Your seller
has told you on the phone, but not in writing, "don't bring me anything under 380."

1. Under 22 TAC § 535.156, may you decline to present the 372,000 dollar offer because of the
seller's phone instruction? Identify the only exception the rule states and say why it does not
apply on these facts.
2. Under 22 TAC § 535.157, by when must you respond to the second buyer's agent's call? State the
period the rule gives and how it is counted.
3. The seller accepts the 380,000 dollar offer. Compute the total commission at 5.5 percent, the
amount going to the cooperating broker at 2.5 percent, and the amount retained by your brokerage.
4. The § 5.008 notice was delivered on 3 March, well before the contract. Suppose instead it had
been delivered on 20 April, after the contract's effective date. State the buyer's right under §
5.008(f), including its length and the reasons required to exercise it.
5. The listing expires on 3 September without a sale, and your boosted Facebook post advertising the
property is still live on 20 September. Identify the provision of 22 TAC § 535.155 you have violated
and the deadline you missed.

## Unit: The Broker Working for/with the Buyer

The buyer side of Texas practice changed on 1 January 2026, and it changed in a way that moves
paperwork to the very front of the relationship. Two new sections were added to TRELA by Acts 2025,
89th Leg., R.S., Ch. 1172 (S.B. 1968): § 1101.562, Real Property Showings Without Representation,
and § 1101.563, Written Agreement Required. Both were added by the same section of the same bill,
both took effect 1 January 2026, and they have to be read together, because § 1101.562 tells you
what you may do without representing someone and § 1101.563 tells you that even then you need a
signed piece of paper first.

**Section 1101.563: the written agreement, and when it must exist.** Start with scope. Section
1101.563(a) defines "residential real property" as "(1) a single-family house; (2) a duplex,
triplex, or quadraplex; or (3) a unit in a multiunit residential structure in which title to an
individual unit is transferred to the owner of the unit under a condominium or cooperative system."
Note that this definition is broader than the § 5.008 seller's-disclosure definition, which reaches
only property "comprising not more than one dwelling unit." A fourplex in Waco is residential real
property for § 1101.563 and is outside § 5.008. Getting these two definitions confused is a genuine
trap; they are different statutes doing different jobs.

Now the duty. Section 1101.563(b): "A license holder who performs any act of real estate brokerage
for a prospective buyer of residential real property must enter into a written agreement with the
prospective buyer before: (1) showing any residential real property to the prospective buyer; or (2)
if no residential real property will be shown, presenting an offer to purchase residential real
property on behalf of the prospective buyer."

The operative word is "before." There is no grace period, no "we'll sign it back at the office," no
"we'll paper it when we write the offer." If you unlock a door for a prospective buyer of a
single-family house in Texas and there is no signed written agreement, you have violated § 1101.563,
and § 1101.652(b)(34) makes it a ground for suspension or revocation that a license holder "fails to
enter into a written agreement with a prospective buyer as required by Section 1101.563." Rule
535.191 places § 1101.652(b)(34) in the "$100 - $1,500 per violation per day" tier — the lowest of
the three, which tells you the Commission treats it as a paperwork failure rather than a fraud, and
which is no comfort at all when it is charged once per showing.

Section 1101.563(c) states what the agreement must contain. It must state "(A) the services to be
provided by the license holder; (B) the termination date of the agreement; (C) whether the agreement
is exclusive or non-exclusive; (D) as applicable, that the license holder: (i) represents the
prospective buyer as the buyer's agent; or (ii) does not represent the prospective buyer as the
buyer's agent if the only act of real estate brokerage being performed is showing real property
under Section 1101.562; and (E) the amount or rate of compensation the broker will receive and how
this amount will be determined." And it must "disclose in conspicuous language that broker
compensation is not set by law and is fully negotiable."

Two structural rules follow. Section 1101.563(d): "A license holder who enters into a written
agreement with a prospective buyer for the sole purpose of showing real property under Section
1101.562 shall enter into a separate agreement with the prospective buyer if additional real estate
brokerage acts are to be provided after showing the real property." You cannot let a showing-only
agreement quietly grow into a representation. And § 1101.563(e): a written agreement for showing
under § 1101.562 "may not: (1) be an exclusive agreement with the license holder; or (2) state a
termination date more than fourteen days from the date the agreement is entered into." Fourteen
days, non-exclusive, and it is the outer limit — not a default term you must use.

**Section 1101.562: showing without representing.** Subsection (a) permits a broker to "show real
property available for sale or lease to a party without representing the party" if the broker "(1)
has not agreed with the party, either orally or in writing, to represent the party; (2) is not
otherwise acting as the party's agent at the time of showing the real property; (3) does not provide
to the party opinions or advice regarding the real property or real estate transactions in general;
and (4) does not perform any other act of real estate brokerage for the party."

Condition (1) includes oral agreements, which means you cannot claim non-representation status after
telling someone on the phone that you will "represent them." Condition (3) is the hard one in the
field. You are standing in a kitchen in Katy and the visitor asks whether the neighbourhood is a
good investment, whether the price is fair, whether they should offer under asking. Every one of
those answers is an opinion or advice regarding the property or regarding real estate transactions
in general, and giving it destroys your non-representation status.

Subsection (b) tells you what you must do first, "as applicable": "(1) disclose to the party as
required by Section 1101.558(b) if the broker represents the owner of the real property; or (2)
provide to the party the written notice described by Section 1101.558(b-1) if the broker does not
represent the owner of the real property." So the listing side discloses representation; a showing
agent who represents nobody delivers the IABS.

Subsection (c) is the narrow safe harbour: "Notwithstanding Subsections (a)(3) and (4), a broker
showing real property under this section may confirm information to a party regarding the size,
price, and terms of the real property available for sale or lease." Size, price, terms. Three items,
and the verb is "confirm." You may confirm that the house is 2,140 square feet, that the list price
is 419,000 dollars, and that the seller wants a 30-day close. You may not tell them what it is
worth.

TREC's own explanatory article on the 2026 changes — which is guidance, not law, and is used here
only to describe practice — works the open-house case through. Where the agent hosting the open
house is a member of the listing broker's office, the article states the agent does not need to
provide the IABS or enter into a written agreement, and needs only to disclose representation of the
owner as § 1101.558(b) requires. Where the agent hosting the open house is not with the listing
brokerage and does not represent the seller, the article states the agent must provide the IABS and
enter into a written agreement as described by § 1101.563(c) with prospective buyers, even where the
visitor is already represented by another broker. The statutory basis for the first half of that is
§ 1101.558(c)(3), which relieves the notice requirement where "the communication occurs at a
property that is held open for any prospective buyer or tenant and the communication concerns that
property" — but note carefully that § 1101.558(c) relieves only the § 1101.558(b-1) *notice*. It
does not touch § 1101.563, which is a separate statute with its own trigger. That is exactly why an
outside agent hosting a stranger's open house still needs the written agreement.

The same 2025 bill also removed two references to subagency from TRELA. TREC's article says so and
says subagency "has not been eliminated generally as a legal concept." This course holds no held
source that defines the common-law contours of subagency after that change, so those contours were
not held and are not taught here.

**First contact with a buyer, in order.** Put the pieces in sequence, because sequence is what an
investigator reconstructs. A prospective buyer calls about a specific listing in Round Rock. That
call is very likely your first substantive communication about specific real property, so §
1101.558(b-1) and 22 TAC § 531.20 require the IABS. If they want to see the house, § 1101.563(b)
requires the written agreement first — either a representation agreement or a showing-only agreement
compliant with § 1101.562 and capped at fourteen days and non-exclusive under § 1101.563(e). If you
already represent the seller of that Round Rock house, § 1101.558(b) requires you to disclose that
representation at the time of first contact, orally or in writing; and if you are going to end up
working with both sides through the same brokerage, § 1101.561(b) is unforgiving: "A broker must
agree to act as an intermediary under this subchapter if the broker agrees to represent in a
transaction: (1) a buyer or tenant; and (2) a seller or landlord." Intermediary status is triggered
by the fact of representing both, not by anyone's preference, and § 1101.559(a) requires that the
broker obtain "written consent from each party for the broker to act as an intermediary in the
transaction" and that "the written consent of the parties states the source of any expected
compensation to the broker."

**Section 1101.555: the title notice, and the fee you lose if you skip it.** When an offer is
signed, one short statute applies and it is worth committing to memory in full. Section 1101.555:
"When an offer to purchase real estate in this state is signed, a license holder shall advise each
buyer, in writing, that the buyer should: (1) have the abstract covering the real estate that is the
subject of the contract examined by an attorney chosen by the buyer; or (2) be provided with or
obtain a title insurance policy."

Three points. It is triggered by signature of the offer, not by acceptance. It runs to "each buyer"
— every buyer, including the one who is unrepresented and merely handed the form. And it must be in
writing. Section 1101.652(b)(29) states the same requirement as a disciplinary ground, phrased
around the closing: it is a ground for discipline to fail "to advise a buyer in writing before the
closing of a real estate transaction" of the same two options. So the Act gives you a signature-time
duty and a before-closing backstop, and § 535.191 places § 1101.652(b)(29) in the "$100 - $1,500 per
violation per day" tier.

The sanction that actually gets attention is in the compensation section. Section 1101.806(d): "A
license holder who fails to advise a buyer as provided by Section 1101.555 may not receive payment
of or recover any commission agreed to be paid on the sale." Not reduced. Not subject to offset. You
may not receive it. A one-sentence written advisory, given at signature and kept in the file,
protects your entire fee on the transaction.

**Writing the offer: the § 537.11 boundary.** Now you sit down to write. Rule 537.11 opens with the
mandatory-form rule: "When negotiating contracts binding the sale, exchange, option, or lease of any
interest in real property, a license holder shall use only those contract forms approved for
mandatory use by the Commission for that type of transaction with the following exceptions:" and
then lists them. The exceptions are "transactions in which the license holder is functioning solely
as a principal, not as an agent;" "transactions in which an agency of the United States government
requires a different form to be used;" "transactions for which a contract form has been prepared by
the property owner or prepared by an attorney and required by a property owner;" and "transactions
for which no contract form has been approved for mandatory use by the Commission," in which case the
license holder may use a form "prepared by an attorney licensed by this state, or a trade
association in consultation with an attorney licensed by this state" carrying the identifying
content the rule specifies, or a form "prepared by the Texas Real Estate Broker-Lawyer Committee and
approved by the Commission for voluntary use by license holders."

Learn the exceptions precisely, because each has a limit built into it. The principal exception is
available only when you are "functioning solely as a principal, not as an agent." The owner-form
exception requires that the form be "prepared by the property owner or prepared by an attorney and
required by a property owner" — a builder's own contract qualifies; a form your buyer downloaded
does not. And the attorney-or-trade-association form is available only where the Commission has
approved *no* mandatory form for that type of transaction, and the form must contain the attorney's
or association's name, the name of the broker or trade association it was prepared for, a statement
of the transaction type approved, "any restrictions on the use of the form," and, if applicable, "an
explanation of how the form changes the rights, obligations, or remedies of a party under a contract
form approved for mandatory use by the Commission and a statement that license holders may not
practice law or give legal advice."

The Act backs this with § 1101.652(a-1)(3), which makes it a ground for discipline that a license
holder "fails to use a contract form required by the commission under Section 1101.155," and with §
1101.155(b), which limits the Commission in the other direction: it "may not prohibit a license
holder from using for the sale, exchange, option, or lease of an interest in real property a
contract form that is: (1) prepared by the property owner; or (2) prepared by an attorney and
required by the property owner."

Rule 537.11 also governs how the form may be reproduced: approved forms "may be reproduced,
including through use of a software application, provided that the text and format of the form,
including the sizing, spacing, and pagination, is identical to the Commission's published version,"
except that "the business name or logo of a broker, trade association, or other organization may
appear outside the form's border" and "a form may be scaled to accommodate viewing on smaller
screens, including mobile devices, as long as the final executed copy of the form otherwise complies
with this subsection." A brokerage that rebuilds the One to Four Family Residential Contract in its
own template engine with different pagination has broken this rule even if every word is right.

**What you may not do while filling it in.** Rule 537.11 states a list of prohibitions. A license
holder may not "practice law"; may not "directly or indirectly offer, give or attempt to give legal
advice"; may not "give advice or opinions as to the legal effect of any contract forms or other such
instruments which may affect the title to real estate"; may not "give opinions concerning the status
or validity of title to real estate"; may not "draft or recommend language to be included in a
contract form defining or affecting the rights, obligations, or remedies of the principals of a real
estate transaction, including escalation, appraisal, or contingency clauses"; may not "add
informational items to a form approved by the Commission if the Commission has approved another form
for mandatory use for that purpose"; may not "attempt to prevent or in any manner whatsoever
discourage any principal to a real estate transaction from employing an attorney"; and may not
"obtain legal advice from an attorney, directly or indirectly, for a principal in a real estate
transaction in which the license holder is acting as an agent."

The fifth of those is the one that catches competent, well-meaning agents in a competitive market.
Escalation clauses, appraisal-gap clauses and bespoke contingency language are named in the rule by
name. Drafting them is prohibited, and so is recommending the language. If your buyer wants an
escalation clause, the answer is that they need a lawyer to draft it, and you say so without
discouraging them from getting one.

The sixth is quieter and just as dangerous: you may not add informational items to a promulgated
form where the Commission has promulgated an addendum for that purpose. If there is a form for the
subject, use the form.

The rule then tells you what you *may* do. It "does not: limit a license holder's fiduciary
obligation to disclose to the license holder's principals all pertinent facts that are within the
knowledge of the license holder, including such facts which might affect the status of or title to
real estate;" and it does not "prevent the license holder from explaining to the license holder's
principals the meaning of informational items or choices in a contract form, as long as the license
holder does not practice law or give legal advice." So explaining what a blank is for is permitted;
telling your buyer what the paragraph will do to their legal position is not.

And it identifies two acts that are affirmatively not the practice of law: "add informational items
to a contract form authorized for use by this section" and, "if specifically instructed in writing
by a principal, add language to or strike language from a contract form, as long as any change is
made conspicuous, including underlining additions, striking through deletions, or employing some
other method which clearly indicates the change being made." Every element of that second permission
matters. The instruction must come from a principal, it must be specific, it must be in writing, and
the change must be conspicuous. A text message from your buyer saying "strike the survey paragraph"
and a printed strike-through with the deletion visible satisfies it. Your own decision to quietly
delete a sentence does not.

Finally, two affirmative advisory duties. "When a transaction involves unusual matters that should
be reviewed by an attorney before an instrument is executed, or if the instrument must be
acknowledged and filed of record, the license holder shall advise the license holder's principals to
consult an attorney before executing the instrument." And: "A license holder shall advise the
license holder's principals that the instrument they are about to execute is binding on them."

Section 1101.654 puts a licence-ending penalty behind the same line. The Commission "shall suspend
or revoke" the licence of a license or certificate holder who is not a licensed Texas attorney and
who, for consideration or in connection with the person's employment, agency, or fiduciary
relationship, "(1) drafts an instrument, other than a form described by Section 1101.155, that
transfers or otherwise affects an interest in real property; or (2) advises a person regarding the
validity or legal sufficiency of an instrument or the validity of title to real property." Note
"shall," not "may." Section 1101.654(b) supplies the protection that makes ordinary practice
possible: a license holder "who completes a contract form for the sale, exchange, option, or lease
of an interest in real property incidental to acting as a broker is not engaged in the unauthorized
or illegal practice of law in this state if the form was: (1) adopted by the commission for the type
of transaction for which the form is used; (2) prepared by an attorney licensed in this state and
approved by the attorney for the type of transaction for which the form is used; or (3) prepared by
the property owner or by an attorney and required by the property owner." Filling in the right form
is safe. Writing your own is not.

**Earnest money: 22 TAC § 535.146.** The buyer's earnest money is trust money, and the rule defines
the term broadly: "'Trust money' means client's money, earnest money, rent, unearned fees, security
deposits, or any money held on behalf of another person." A "Trust account" is "an account managed
by one party for the benefit of another in a banking institution authorized to do business in
Texas."

The core acceptance rules are these. "Any trust money accepted by a broker is held in a fiduciary
capacity and must be maintained in a designated trust account maintained by the broker or delivered
to an escrow agent authorized in Texas in accordance with the agreement of the principals of the
transaction." For a sales agent, the rule is absolute: "A sales agent shall not maintain a trust
account. Any trust money received by a sales agent must be immediately delivered to the sales
agent's sponsoring broker." Not deposited by you. Not held in your desk over the weekend.
Immediately delivered to your broker.

The timing rule is the number to memorise: "Unless a different time to deposit trust money is
expressly agreed upon in writing by the principals to the transaction, any trust money received by
the broker must be deposited in a trust account or delivered to an authorized escrow agent within a
reasonable time, which the Commission has determined to be not later than the close of business of
the second working day after the date the broker receives the trust money." Second working day,
close of business, counted from receipt by the broker — and it can be varied only by a writing
agreed to by the principals.

Commingling is treated as its own offence. "The broker shall not: commingle trust money with the
broker's personal money or other non-trust money; or deposit or maintain trust money in a personal
account or any kind of business account." And the rule pre-decides the evidence: "The following is
prima facie evidence of commingling trust money with the broker's own money: placing trust money in
a broker's personal or operating account; or paying operating expenses or making withdrawals from a
trust account for any purpose other than proper disbursement of trust money." One narrow allowance
exists: "A broker may deposit and maintain a reasonable amount of money in the trust account to
cover bank service fees, including fees charged for insufficient funds. Detailed records must be
kept for any funds deposited under this exception." And when the broker becomes entitled to money in
the account — for example, an earned commission — "such money must be removed from the trust account
not later the 30th day after the date the broker acquires ownership of the money."

Disbursement is where earnest-money disputes live. "A broker may only disburse money from the
broker's trust account in accordance with the agreement under which the money was received." If any
or all parties make a written demand for payment, "the broker must pay the trust money to the party
or parties entitled to the money within a reasonable time, which the Commission has determined to be
not later than the 30th day after the date the demand is made." If all parties later agree in
writing to a different disbursement, the same thirty-day outer limit runs from receipt of that
subsequent written agreement. In both cases "The broker must immediately notify all parties in
writing of any disbursement of trust money." And where the broker genuinely cannot tell who is
entitled, the rule provides the exit: "If the broker cannot reasonably determine to which party or
parties the trust money should be paid, the broker may pay the trust money into the registry of a
court and interplead the parties."

Two more operational rules. If a trust-money check is dishonoured, "the broker shall immediately
notify all parties to the transaction in writing." And the broker "must retain a documentary record
of each deposit or withdrawal from the trust account and provide an accounting to each beneficiary
of trust money at least monthly if there has been any activity in the account," with all
trust-account documentation kept "for four years from the date the document is received or created
by the broker."

The Act makes the same failures disciplinary grounds: § 1101.652(b)(9) for failing "within a
reasonable time to properly account for or remit money that is received by the license holder and
that belongs to another person"; § 1101.652(b)(10) for commingling; § 1101.652(b)(30) for failing to
deposit escrow or trust funds within a reasonable time either "in trust with a title company
authorized to do business in this state" or "in a custodial, trust, or escrow account maintained for
that purpose in a banking institution authorized to do business in this state"; and §
1101.652(b)(31) for disbursing such money "before the completion or termination of the real estate
transaction."

### Applied exercise: A Saturday in Cedar Park

At 9:40 on a Saturday morning a stranger calls your cell. She saw your sign at 2203 Silverstone
Drive in Cedar Park, a single-family house you have listed for the seller at 419,000 dollars. She
asks to see it at 11:00. She is not represented. At 11:00 she asks you three questions: how big is
the house, is 419,000 a fair price, and would the seller take 400,000.

1. Before you open the door, what must you deliver and what must you sign? Cite § 1101.558(b), §
1101.563(b) and, if you were not the listing agent, § 1101.558(b-1) and 22 TAC § 531.20, and say
which apply on these facts and which do not.
2. She does not want a representation agreement. May you show the house under a showing-only
agreement? State the two limits § 1101.563(e) places on such an agreement.
3. Answer each of her three questions consistently with § 1101.562(c) and § 1101.562(a)(3). Which
may you answer, which may you not, and what exactly does subsection (c) permit you to do?
4. She decides to write an offer. You are the listing agent and your broker also intends to
represent her. State what § 1101.561(b) requires of the broker and what § 1101.559(a) requires the
written consent to contain.
5. She signs the offer at 4:15 that afternoon. State the written advisory § 1101.555 requires, and
state the consequence under § 1101.806(d) if you never give it.

## Unit: A Practical Guide to Everyday Practice

Most licences are not lost through fraud. They are lost through habits: an unanswered email, a
promise made in a car and never written down, a file that cannot be produced three years later, a
helpful sentence about what a contract paragraph "really means." This unit converts the rules you
have just read into a set of standing habits, and names the rule behind each one, because a habit
you cannot justify by citation is a habit you will abandon under pressure.

**Habit one: answer within two calendar days.** Rule 535.157 is one sentence long: "A broker or
sales agent must respond to his or her principal, a broker or sales agent representing another party
to a real estate transaction, or an unrepresented party to a real estate transaction within two
calendar days."

Notice the breadth of the list. Your own client, yes — but also the license holder on the other side
of the deal, and also an unrepresented party. The unrepresented buyer who emails you about your
listing is owed a response within two calendar days by rule, not by courtesy. Notice also "calendar
days," not business days. An email received Friday afternoon is answered by Sunday. Rule 535.191
places 22 TAC § 535.157 in the "$100 - $1,500 per violation per day" tier, and the phrase "per day"
should be read carefully: a silence that runs for three weeks is not one violation.

The rule does not say you must resolve the matter within two days. It says respond. "I have your
offer, my seller is travelling and I will have an answer to you Wednesday" is a response. Silence
while you wait for something better to say is not.

Inside the brokerage there are two matching deadlines in 22 TAC § 535.2. A broker or a supervisor
delegated under subsection (e) of that rule "must respond to sponsored sales agents within two
calendar days," expressly "In addition to the requirements of §535.157 of this chapter (relating to
Obligation to Respond Timely)." And a sponsoring broker or delegated supervisor "shall deliver mail
and other correspondence from the Commission to their sponsored sales agents within three calendar
days after receipt." If you are a sales agent, that second one is your protection: a complaint
notice addressed to you goes to your broker, and your broker has three calendar days to get it to
you.

**Habit two: put it in writing, and put it in writing at the time.** There is no general Texas rule
that says "document everything," so the honest way to teach this is to show you how many separate
rules assume a writing exists, and let the pattern make the argument.

The listing and buyer agreements must be written to be enforceable as to your fee: § 1101.806(c).
The buyer agreement must exist before a showing: § 1101.563(b). The intermediary consent must be
written and must state the source of expected compensation: § 1101.559(a). The instruction not to
present post-acceptance offers must be an agreement "in writing": 22 TAC § 535.156. The instruction
to add or strike contract language must be given "specifically instructed in writing by a
principal": 22 TAC § 537.11. Consent to place a sign must be written: § 1101.652(b)(18). Written
consent of the sponsoring broker is required before a sales agent receives compensation: 22 TAC §
535.3. A delegation of supervisory responsibility "must be in writing": 22 TAC § 535.2. The advisory
about the abstract or title policy must be "in writing": § 1101.555. Notice of a dishonoured
trust-money check, and notice of any disbursement under a written demand or subsequent agreement,
must be in writing: 22 TAC § 535.146. The disclosure that you are a license holder acting for your
own account must be "in writing": 22 TAC § 535.144.

Once you see the pattern, the working rule writes itself: if a rule gives you a permission that
depends on someone else's instruction, get the instruction in writing before you act on it; and if a
rule imposes a duty to tell someone something, tell them in a medium that survives.

The corollary is that oral promises still bind you even though they are unwritten. Rule 535.145
exists to close that escape: for purposes of § 1101.652(b)(5), "'false promise' includes both oral
and written promises. The fact that a written agreement between the parties to a real estate
transaction does not recite a promise made by a real estate license holder to one of the parties or
that a person did not detrimentally rely on the false promise will not prevent the Commission from
determining that a false promise was made." The rule goes further and forecloses the two evidentiary
defences a lawyer would reach for: "neither a written contractual provision disclaiming oral
representations nor the Texas Rules of Evidence Rule 1004, the parol evidence rule, shall prevent
the Commission from considering oral promises made by a license holder." An integration clause in
the contract does not protect you from the Commission. What you said in the car counts.

**Habit three: keep the file for four years, in the shape the rule names.** Rule 535.2 tells the
broker exactly what to keep and for how long. "Except for records destroyed by an 'Act of God' such
as a natural disaster or fire not intentionally caused by the broker, the broker must, at a minimum,
maintain the following records in a format that is readily available to the Commission for at least
four years from the date of closing, termination of the contract, or end of a real estate
transaction: disclosures; commission agreements such as listing agreements, buyer representation
agreements, or other written agreements relied upon to claim compensation; substantive
communications with parties to the transaction; offers, contracts, and related addenda; receipts and
disbursements of compensation for services subject to the Act; property management contracts;
appraisals, broker price opinions, and comparative market analyses; and sponsorship agreements
between the broker and sponsored sales agents."

Read the third item again: "substantive communications with parties to the transaction." Your text
messages with the buyer's agent about the repair amendment are transaction records. If your
negotiating happens on a messaging app that deletes after thirty days, you are choosing not to keep
records the rule requires the broker to hold for four years.

The four-year clock runs from "the date of closing, termination of the contract, or end of a real
estate transaction," so a deal that dies in option ends the transaction and starts the clock.
Trust-account documentation has its own parallel four-year rule in 22 TAC § 535.146: "A broker must
maintain all documentation regarding a trust account for four years from the date the document is
received or created by the broker."

Four years is not an arbitrary number. Section 1101.204(c) provides that "The commission may not
conduct an investigation of a person licensed under this chapter or Chapter 1102 in connection with
a complaint submitted later than the fourth anniversary of the date of the incident that is the
subject of the complaint." The retention period matches the outer limit of the Commission's
investigative reach. It also matches the four-year limitations period Civil Practice and Remedies
Code § 16.004(a) sets for suits for "fraud" and "breach of fiduciary duty."

Two more record duties fall directly on you. Section 1101.652(a-1)(2) makes it a ground for
discipline if a license holder "fails or refuses to produce on request, within a reasonable time,
for inspection by the commission or a commission representative, a document, book, or record that is
in the license holder's possession and relates to a real estate transaction conducted by the license
holder." And § 1101.652(b)(28) reaches the consumer side: it is a ground for discipline to fail or
refuse "to provide, on request, a copy of a document relating to a real estate transaction to a
person who signed the document." A seller who asks for a copy of their own listing agreement two
years later gets one.

**Habit four: know the line you must not cross when you talk about documents.** The unauthorized
practice of law is the single most common way a technically excellent agent walks into serious
trouble, because the prohibited acts feel like good service. Rule 537.11 gives you the list, and
Unit 2 quoted it in full. Reduce it to four questions you ask yourself before you answer a client:

Am I about to give an opinion about what a contract provision does to someone's legal rights? That
is prohibited: you may not "give advice or opinions as to the legal effect of any contract forms or
other such instruments which may affect the title to real estate."

Am I about to say something about who owns this property or whether the title is good? Prohibited:
you may not "give opinions concerning the status or validity of title to real estate."

Am I about to compose a sentence to go into the contract? Prohibited: you may not "draft or
recommend language to be included in a contract form defining or affecting the rights, obligations,
or remedies of the principals of a real estate transaction, including escalation, appraisal, or
contingency clauses."

Am I about to tell a client that a lawyer is an unnecessary expense or a deal-killer? Prohibited:
you may not "attempt to prevent or in any manner whatsoever discourage any principal to a real
estate transaction from employing an attorney."

And there is a fifth, less obvious one. You may not "obtain legal advice from an attorney, directly
or indirectly, for a principal in a real estate transaction in which the license holder is acting as
an agent." Calling your brokerage's lawyer to get an answer for your client, then relaying it, is
prohibited by the rule. What you may do is employ and pay an attorney "to represent only the license
holder in a real estate transaction," and you may reimburse a principal for attorney's fees incurred
— the rule says so expressly in the same subsection.

The permitted side of the line is genuinely useful, and you should be able to state it without
hesitation. You may explain "the meaning of informational items or choices in a contract form, as
long as the license holder does not practice law or give legal advice." You may add informational
items to an authorized form. You may, on a principal's specific written instruction, add or strike
language so long as the change is conspicuous. And § 537.11 preserves the duty that survives all of
it: nothing in the section limits "a license holder's fiduciary obligation to disclose to the
license holder's principals all pertinent facts that are within the knowledge of the license holder,
including such facts which might affect the status of or title to real estate." You must tell your
principal about the fact. You must not opine on its legal effect.

**Habit five: stay inside your competence and your market.** Rule 531.4 obliges a license holder "to
be knowledgeable and competent as a real estate brokerage practitioner," which the rule breaks into
four requirements: "be informed on local market issues and conditions affecting real estate in the
geographic area where a license holder provides services to a client"; "be informed on national,
state, and local issues and developments in the real estate industry"; "exercise judgment and skill
in the performance of brokerage activities"; and "be educated in the characteristics involved in the
specific type of real estate being brokered for others."

The Act converts incompetence into a disciplinary ground twice: § 1101.652(b)(1) reaches a license
holder who "acts negligently or incompetently" while engaged in real estate brokerage, and §
1101.652(a)(6) reaches a license holder who "fails to consider market conditions for the specific
geographic area in which the license holder is providing a service." A Dallas agent taking a ranch
listing near Sonora, or an agent who has never handled a commercial lease taking one because a
friend asked, is running straight at both.

Your broker has an obligation on the same subject, and you should know it because it is your
training entitlement. Rule 535.2 requires a sponsoring broker to maintain current written policies
and procedures ensuring, among other things, that each sponsored sales agent "is advised of the
scope of the sales agent's authorized activities subject to the Act and is competent to conduct such
activities, including having competence in the geographic market area where the sales agent
represents clients," and that "when a sales agent performs a type of real estate brokerage activity
for the first three times, the broker must require that the sales agent receive coaching and
assistance from an experienced license holder competent for that activity." First three times,
coaching required, by rule. If your broker does not offer it, ask for it and put the request in
writing.

**Habit six: know the standard you are being judged against.** Rule 531.2 is the fidelity rule, and
it is short enough to internalise. A license holder acting as an agent for another "is a fiduciary,"
and the special obligations that creates "demand: that the primary duty of the license holder is to
represent the interests of the client, and the license holder's position, in this respect, should be
clear to all parties concerned in a real estate transaction; that, however, the license holder, in
performing duties to the client, shall treat other parties to a transaction fairly; that the license
holder be faithful and observant to trust placed in the license holder, and be scrupulous and
meticulous in performing the license holder's functions; and that the license holder place no
personal interest above that of the client."

Rule 531.3 adds the integrity standard: a license holder "has a special obligation to exercise
integrity in the discharge of the license holder's responsibilities, including employment of
prudence and caution so as to avoid misrepresentation, in any way, by acts of commission or
omission." The last four words are the ones to keep. Misrepresentation by omission is
misrepresentation.

**A working checklist for the week.** None of what follows is itself a legal requirement — it is
practice, and it is offered as practice — but every item exists because a rule quoted in this module
requires the underlying act. On taking any new consumer contact: send the IABS and log the send.
Before any showing of residential property to a prospective buyer: written agreement signed and
dated. On taking a listing: signed agreement in the broker's name with a definite termination date,
sign consent, § 5.008 notice completed by the seller, CMA with the § 535.17 statement in it at
12-point or larger. On every advertisement: broker's name at least half the size of the largest
contact information. On receiving anything from a principal, a cooperating agent, or an
unrepresented party: respond inside two calendar days. On receiving trust money as a sales agent:
hand it to the broker the same day. On receiving any instruction that changes what you would
otherwise do: get it in writing before acting. At the end of every transaction, dead or closed:
close the file with the eight record categories 22 TAC § 535.2 lists, and keep it four years.

Open 22 TAC § 537.11 and answer only from its text.

## Unit: Other Considerations

Four subjects sit slightly outside the transaction itself and generate an outsized share of Texas
discipline: buying and selling for your own account, paying and receiving referral fees and rebates,
the names you trade under, and the addresses the Commission has on file for you. None of them
involves a client complaining about a house. All of them involve the Commission finding something
wrong on its own.

**Acting for your own account: 22 TAC § 535.144.** Sooner or later you will want to buy a property
yourself, or list your mother's house, or sell a rental owned by an LLC in which you hold an
interest. The rule that governs is short, and every clause of it matters.

It begins by expanding who counts. "For purposes of §1101.652(a-1)(1) of the Act 'license holder'
includes a license holder acting on behalf of: the license holder's spouse, parent or child; a
business entity in which the license holder is more than a 10% owner; or a trust for which the
license holder acts as trustee or of which the license holder or the license holder's spouse, parent
or child is a beneficiary." Three extensions: family in the first degree, a business entity where
your ownership exceeds ten percent, and a trust where you are trustee or where you or your close
family are beneficiaries. Ten percent is a threshold, and "more than" excludes exactly ten.

Then the disclosure duty. "A license holder engaging in a real estate transaction on his or her own
behalf or in a capacity described by subsection (a), is obligated to disclose in writing that he or
she is a licensed real estate broker or sales agent acting on his or her own behalf or in a capacity
described by subsection (a) in any contract of sale or rental agreement or in any other writing
given before entering into any contract of sale or rental agreement."

Take that sentence apart. What must be disclosed: that you are a licensed real estate broker or
sales agent, and that you are acting on your own behalf or in one of the extended capacities. How:
in writing. Where: in the contract of sale or rental agreement itself, or in some other writing
given *before* entering into it. To whom: the rule does not limit the disclosure to consumers, and
it does not exempt transactions with other license holders. Note also what triggers it — engaging in
the transaction, not advertising it. The safest practice, and it is practice rather than rule, is to
disclose in the listing or advertisement as well, because 22 TAC § 535.155 independently prohibits
an advertisement that "causes a member of the public to believe that a person not licensed to
conduct real estate brokerage is engaged in real estate brokerage."

Then the substantive limit: "A license holder acting on his or her own behalf or in a capacity
described by subsection (a) shall not use the license holder's expertise to the disadvantage of a
person with whom the license holder deals." That is a standard, not a checklist, and it is the
clause that turns a hard bargain into a violation. You know what the comparables say; the
unrepresented owner across the table does not. Using that asymmetry to their disadvantage is
prohibited even though you owe them no agency duty at all.

Three other provisions ride alongside. Rule 535.16 requires a BPO or CMA "when negotiating a listing
or offering to purchase the property for the license holder's own account as a result of contact
made while acting as a real estate agent" — so if you meet an owner as an agent and then decide to
buy the property yourself, the CMA is mandatory and it goes to the owner. Section 1101.652(a-1)(1)
makes it a ground for discipline to engage "in misrepresentation, dishonesty, or fraud when selling,
buying, trading, or leasing real property in the name of: (A) the license holder; (B) the license
holder's spouse; or (C) a person related to the license holder within the first degree by
consanguinity." And § 1101.652(b)(16) reaches the classic abuse: acting "in a dual capacity as
broker and undisclosed principal in a real estate transaction." Rule 535.191 places 22 TAC § 535.144
in the "$500 - $3,000 per violation per day" tier and § 1101.652(a-1)(1) in the top tier.

**Referral fees, rebates and who may be paid.** The governing statute is § 1101.651. Subsection (a):
"A licensed broker may not pay a commission to or otherwise compensate a person directly or
indirectly for performing an act of a broker unless the person is: (1) a license holder; or (2) a
real estate broker licensed in another state who does not conduct in this state any of the
negotiations for which the commission or other compensation is paid." Subsections (b) and (c) run
the sales agent's side: a sales agent "may not accept compensation for a real estate transaction
from a person other than the broker that is sponsoring the sales agent or was sponsoring the sales
agent when the sales agent earned the compensation," and "may not pay a commission to a person
except through the broker that is sponsoring the sales agent at that time."

Rule 535.147 restates the prohibition and then draws two useful lines. An unlicensed person "may
share in the income earned by a business entity licensed as a broker or exempted from the licensing
requirements under the Act if the person engages in no acts for which a license is required and does
not lead the public to believe that the person is in the real estate brokerage business" — so a
passive unlicensed owner of a brokerage entity may take profit, but may not do brokerage or look
like they do. And a broker or sales agent "may not share a commission or fees with an unlicensed
business entity created by a license holder for the purpose of collecting a commission or fees on
behalf of the license holder," unless that entity is exempt under 22 TAC § 535.5 and § 1101.355(d)
of the Act.

Rebates to consumers are permitted with conditions. Rule 535.147 provides that a license holder "may
rebate or pay a portion of the license holder's fee or commission to a party in the transaction when
the sales agent has the written consent of the sales agent's sponsoring broker and the party
represented by the license holder." Two limits follow immediately: "A commission or fee may not be
paid to any party to the transaction in a manner that misleads a broker, lender, title company, or
governmental agency regarding the real estate transaction or the financial resources or obligations
of the buyer," and a license holder who intends to rebate to a party the license holder does not
represent "must obtain the written consent of the party represented by the license holder before
making the payment." An undisclosed credit outside closing that changes what the lender thinks the
buyer brought to the table is precisely what the first limit forbids.

If you advertise a rebate, 22 TAC § 535.155 adds disclosure requirements. An advertisement violates
the rule if it offers "to rebate a portion of a license holder's compensation to a party if the
advertisement does not disclose that payment of the rebate is subject to the consent of the party
the license holder represents in the transaction," or if it offers a rebate "contingent upon a
party's use of a specified service provider, or subject to approval by a third party such as a
lender, unless the advertisement also contains a disclosure that payment of the rebate is subject to
restrictions."

Referrals *from* unlicensed people are governed by 22 TAC § 535.20. Making a referral "is an act
requiring the person making the referral to be licensed if the referral is made with the expectation
of receiving valuable consideration," and the rule defines valuable consideration to include "money;
gifts of merchandise having a retail value greater than $50; rent bonuses; and discounts." Fifty
dollars is the number: a 40-dollar gift card to a past client who sent you a friend is outside the
definition; a 100-dollar one is not. The rule then carves out a narrow vendor case: a person is not
required to be licensed if the person is in the business of selling goods or services to the public,
sells those goods or services to a license holder who intends to offer them as an inducement, refers
the person's customers to the license holder afterwards, and "The payment to the person for the
goods or services is not contingent upon the consummation of a real estate transaction by the
person's customers."

Payments from settlement service providers are governed by 22 TAC § 535.148. The base rule: a
license holder "may not receive a commission, rebate, or fee in a transaction from a person other
than the person the license holder represents without first disclosing to the license holder's
client that the license holder intends to receive the commission, rebate, or fee, and obtaining the
consent of the license holder's client." The Act makes the same conduct a disciplinary ground twice
over, at § 1101.652(b)(8) for receiving compensation "from more than one party to a real estate
transaction without the full knowledge and consent of all parties to the transaction" and at §
1101.652(b)(13) for accepting, receiving or charging "an undisclosed commission, rebate, or direct
profit on an expenditure made for a principal."

Rule 535.148 then imposes a flat prohibition that surprises people: a license holder "may not pay or
receive a fee or other valuable consideration to or from any other settlement service provider for,
but not limited to, the following: the referral of inspections, lenders, mortgage brokers, or title
companies; inclusion on a list of inspectors, preferred settlement providers, or similar
arrangements; or inclusion on lists of inspectors or other settlement providers contingent on other
financial agreements." The rule's definition of settlement service provider is long and includes
lenders, title service providers, attorneys, appraisers, inspectors, settlement agents, insurance
and home-warranty providers, and "a real estate agent or broker." What the rule does *not* prohibit
is set out at the end: "normal promotional or educational activity that is not conditioned on the
referral of business and that does not involve the defraying of expenses that otherwise would be
incurred"; "a payment at market rates to any person for goods actually furnished or for services
actually performed"; and "a payment pursuant to a cooperative brokerage or referral arrangement or
agreement between active licensed real estate agents and real estate brokers." Broker-to-broker
referral fees are lawful. A title company buying your marketing is not.

Residential service contracts have their own form: a license holder "must use TREC No. RSC-4,
Disclosure of Relationship with Residential Service Contract Provider or Administrator" to disclose
payments received for services provided for or on behalf of such a provider, in a transaction where
the license holder represents one or both parties.

**Teams, assumed names and alternate names.** Rule 535.154 defines the three categories and imposes
a registration duty on each. An "Alternate name" is "a name used by an individual license holder
other than the name shown on the license issued by the Commission, such as a middle name, maiden
name, or nickname," and the rule expressly excludes "a common derivative of a name, such as Kim for
Kimberly or Bill for William, which is considered the same as the name shown on the license." So
Bill for William needs nothing; a maiden name does. "Before a license holder starts using an
alternate name in an advertisement, the license holder must register the name with the Commission
using a process acceptable to the Commission," and the license holder must notify the Commission and
their sponsoring broker "not later than the 10th day after the date the license holder stops using
an alternate name."

A "Team name" is "a name used by a group of one or more license holders sponsored by or associated
with the same broker that performs real estate activities under an exclusive collective name other
than the broker's licensed name or assumed business name." Three rules govern it. It "may not
include any terms that could mislead the public to believe that the team is offering brokerage
services independent from its sponsoring broker." It "must end with the word 'team' or 'group'." And
"Before an associated broker or a sales agent sponsored by a broker starts using a team name in an
advertisement, the broker must register the name with the Commission," with a ten-day notification
when use stops. Rule 535.155 supplies the enforcement edge, treating as misleading any advertisement
"that contains a team name with terms that imply that the team is offering brokerage services
independent from its sponsoring broker, including, but not limited to, 'brokerage', 'company', and
'associates'." "The Alvarez Group" is compliant. "Alvarez & Associates" is not.

An "Assumed business name" is "any name used in business by a broker that meets the requirements of
subsection (d) of this section, other than the name shown on the broker's license issued by the
Commission, a team name, or an alternate name." Before it is used in an advertisement the broker
must both "register the name with the Commission using a process acceptable to the Commission" and
"provide written evidence of legal authority to use the assumed business name in Texas, such as
registration of the name with the Secretary of State or county clerk's office," with the same
ten-day notice when use ceases.

Two responsibility rules complete the picture. Rule 535.2 makes the broker "responsible to ensure
that a sponsored sales agent's advertising complies with §535.154 of this chapter … and §535.155 of
this chapter." And the same rule provides that "Any license holder who leads, supervises, directs,
or manages a team must be delegated as a supervisor," that "Any such delegation must be in writing,"
and that the broker must give the Commission the name of each delegated supervisor "within 30 days
of any such delegation that has lasted or is anticipated to last more than three consecutive
months," with notice again "within 30 days after the delegation of a supervisor has ended." If you
run a team, you are a delegated supervisor by rule, and the paperwork is the broker's to file.

**The office and the addresses.** Section 1101.552(a) states the office rule: "A resident broker
shall maintain a fixed office in this state. The address of the office shall be designated on the
broker's license." Subsection (b) sets the moving deadline: "Not later than the 10th day after the
date a broker moves from the address designated on the broker's license, the broker shall submit an
application, accompanied by the appropriate fee, for a license that designates the new location of
the broker's office." Subsection (d) exempts non-residents: "A nonresident licensed broker is not
required to maintain a place of business in this state."

Subsection (e) applies to every license holder, sales agents included. A license holder "shall
provide the commission with: (1) the license holder's current: (A) mailing address; (B) business
address; (C) business telephone number; and (D) business e-mail address; and (2) if the license
holder is an associated broker, the name of the broker with whom the license holder is associated."
Subsection (f) requires notice of a change in any of it. Rule 535.21 supplies the deadline — each
license holder "shall report all subsequent changes not later than the 10th day after the date of a
change of any of the listed contact information" — and the consequence of not doing so: "If a
license holder fails to update the contact information, the last known contact information provided
to the Commission is the license holder's contact information."

That last sentence is why this dull obligation belongs in a module about risk. Rule 535.21 provides
that the Commission sends notices to an active broker or an inactive license holder at the address
in its records, and sends notices for an active sales agent to the sales agent's *sponsoring
broker*. A complaint notice sent to a stale address is still effective service against you. Rule
535.191 lists both § 1101.552 and 22 TAC § 535.21 in the "$100 - $1,500 per violation per day" tier.

One geographic reminder closes the unit. Rule 535.4 provides that the Act "applies to any person
acting as a real estate broker or sales agent while physically within Texas, regardless of the
location of the real estate involved or the residence of the person's customers or clients," and
that a person "conducting brokerage business from another state by mail, telephone, the Internet,
email, or other medium is acting within Texas if the real property concerned is located wholly or
partly in Texas." You cannot step outside these rules by working remotely.

## Unit: Risk Management

Risk management in Texas brokerage is not a philosophy. It is the practice of knowing, in advance,
which specific things go wrong, what the Commission does about them, what a court does about them,
and what you are supposed to do on the day you realise you are the one who got it wrong.

**Where the complaints come from.** Section 1101.204(b) tells you how a complaint starts: the
Commission "shall investigate the actions and records of a license holder if: (1) a person submits a
signed, written complaint; and (2) the complaint and any evidence presented with the complaint
provide reasonable cause for an investigation." Subsection (f) provides that "An investigation or
other action against a person licensed under this chapter or Chapter 1102 may not be initiated on
the basis of an anonymous complaint." Subsection (c) caps the reach: no investigation "in connection
with a complaint submitted later than the fourth anniversary of the date of the incident that is the
subject of the complaint." Subsection (d) requires the Commission to "promptly provide a written
notice to a person licensed under this chapter or Chapter 1102 who is the subject of an
investigation unless after deliberation the commission decides against notification."

Complaints are triaged. Rule 535.141 sets three levels using the criteria of § 1101.204. Level 1 is
"Fraud or misrepresentation that involves loss of $10,000 or more," a "Continuing threat to public
welfare," and "Unlicensed activity." Level 2 is "Fraud or misrepresentation that involves loss of
less than $10,000" and "Negligence," together with certain inspector violations. Level 3 is
"Technical violations," other Chapter 1102 complaints, and "Allegations involving education
providers." The dollar line between Level 1 and Level 2 is 10,000 dollars, and the practical lesson
is that unlicensed activity sits at the top tier regardless of amount.

If you are a sales agent, understand who else is in the room. Rule 535.141 provides that a complaint
naming a sales agent but not naming the sponsoring broker "is a complaint against the broker
sponsoring the sales agent at the time of any alleged violation for the limited purposes of
determining the broker's involvement in any alleged violation and whether the broker fulfilled the
broker's professional responsibilities provided the complaint concerns the conduct of the sales
agent as an agent for the broker." The same rule extends that to the designated broker of a licensed
business entity. And § 1101.803 states the liability rule in one line: "A licensed broker is liable
to the commission, the public, and the broker's clients for any conduct engaged in under this
chapter by the broker or by a sales agent associated with or acting for the broker." Your mistake is
your broker's problem by operation of statute, which is why brokers write policies and why you
follow them.

Rule 535.141 also requires you to cooperate with the investigation, and § 1101.652(a)(4) makes it an
independent disciplinary ground to fail "to provide, within a reasonable time, information requested
by the commission that relates to a formal or informal complaint to the commission that would
indicate a violation of this chapter or Chapter 1102." A licence is more often lost for ignoring the
letter than for the original conduct.

**The recurring fact patterns.** Section 1101.652(b) is, read practically, a catalogue of the ways
Texas licence holders get into trouble, and the ones you will actually meet cluster into six
patterns.

*Silence about a defect.* Subsections (b)(3) and (b)(4): making "a material misrepresentation to a
potential buyer concerning a significant defect, including a latent structural defect, known to the
license holder that would be a significant factor to a reasonable and prudent buyer in making a
decision to purchase real property," and failing to disclose such a defect known to the license
holder. Note the knowledge element in both. Note also 22 TAC § 531.3, under which misrepresentation
can be "by acts of commission or omission."

*Advertising.* Subsection (b)(23), operationalised by 22 TAC § 535.155 and § 535.154. This is the
most mechanical violation in the entire scheme and therefore the easiest to prove: a screenshot of a
post with the broker's name too small, or absent, is a complete case.

*Money.* Subsections (b)(9), (b)(10), (b)(30) and (b)(31), operationalised by 22 TAC § 535.146.
Commingling is provable from a bank statement.

*Compensation and disclosure of interest.* Subsections (b)(8), (b)(11), (b)(13) and (b)(16), with §
1101.651 and 22 TAC §§ 535.147 and 535.148.

*Authority and interference.* Subsections (b)(18) through (b)(22): signs without written consent,
offering property without the owner's knowledge and consent, offering on unauthorised terms,
inducing a party "to break the contract for the purpose of substituting a new contract," and
negotiating with a party you know is under an exclusive agency with another broker. On that last
one, 22 TAC § 535.153 draws the line precisely: although a license holder "may not attempt to
negotiate a sale, exchange, lease, or rental of property under exclusive listing with another
broker, the Act does not prohibit a license holder from soliciting a listing from the owner while
the owner's property is subject to an exclusive listing with another broker." Soliciting the future
listing is permitted; negotiating the present transaction is not.

*Paperwork that was supposed to exist.* Subsections (a-1)(3), (b)(12), (b)(28), (b)(29) and (b)(34):
the wrong form, no termination date, no copy provided on request, no written title advisory, and no
buyer agreement under § 1101.563.

Discrimination sits across all of them at § 1101.652(b)(32), which reaches a license holder who
discriminates "against an owner, potential buyer, landlord, or potential tenant on the basis of
race, color, religion, sex, disability, familial status, national origin, or ancestry, including
directing a prospective buyer or tenant interested in equivalent properties to a different area
based on the race, color, religion, sex, disability, familial status, national origin, or ancestry
of the potential owner or tenant." Rule 531.19 states the Commission's parallel prohibition on
inquiring about, responding to, facilitating inquiries about, or disclosing information about an
owner, occupant, purchaser, lessor or lessee that indicates or is intended to indicate a preference,
limitation or discrimination.

**What happens to you.** The Commission's options are cumulative, not alternative. Section
1101.652(a) allows it to "suspend or revoke a license issued under this chapter or Chapter 1102 or
take other disciplinary action authorized by this chapter or Chapter 1102." Section 1101.702(a) sets
the administrative penalty ceiling: "The amount of an administrative penalty may not exceed $5,000
for each violation. Each day a violation continues or occurs may be considered a separate violation
for purposes of imposing a penalty." Section 1101.702(b) lists the factors: "the seriousness of the
violation, including the nature, circumstances, extent, and gravity of the prohibited acts; the
history of previous violations; the amount necessary to deter a future violation; efforts to correct
the violation; and any other matter that justice may require."

Rule 535.191 turns those factors into three published ranges. The lowest, "$100 - $1,500 per
violation per day," covers § 1101.552, § 1101.652(a)(3), § 1101.652(a)(8), § 1101.652(a-1)(3), §
1101.652(b)(23), § 1101.652(b)(29), § 1101.652(b)(33), § 1101.652(b)(34), 22 TAC § 535.21, 22 TAC §
535.154, 22 TAC § 535.155 and 22 TAC § 535.157, among others. The middle range, "$500 - $3,000 per
violation per day," covers § 1101.652(b)(1), § 1101.652(b)(7) and (8), § 1101.652(b)(12), §
1101.652(b)(22), § 1101.652(b)(28), § 1101.652(b)(30) and (31), § 1101.654(a), 22 TAC § 531.18, 22
TAC § 531.20, 22 TAC § 535.2, 22 TAC § 535.16 and 22 TAC § 535.17, among others. The highest,
"$1,000 - $5,000 per violation per day," covers § 1101.351, § 1101.557(b), § 1101.558, § 1101.559(a)
and (c), § 1101.560, § 1101.561(b), § 1101.651, § 1101.652(a-1)(1), § 1101.652(b)(2) through (6), §
1101.652(b)(9) through (11), § 1101.652(b)(13), § 1101.652(b)(15) through (21), § 1101.652(b)(24)
through (27), § 1101.652(b)(32), 22 TAC §§ 535.145 through 535.148, and 22 TAC § 535.156. Rule
535.191 also warns that the Commission "may assess an additional administrative penalty of up to two
times that assessed under subsections (c), (d) and (e) of this section, subject to the maximum
penalties authorized under §1101.702(a) of the Act, if a person has a history of previous
violations," and reminds you that it "may suspend or revoke a license or take other disciplinary
action authorized by the Act in addition to or instead of assessing the administrative penalties."

There are three further tools. Section 1101.759(a) permits a cease and desist order "after notice
and opportunity for a hearing," and violating one "constitutes grounds for imposing an
administrative penalty under Subchapter O." Section 1101.751(a) permits the Commission to bring an
injunctive action in its own name, and subsection (b) removes the usual equitable burdens: the
Commission "is not required to allege or prove that: (1) an adequate remedy at law does not exist;
or (2) substantial or irreparable damage would result from the continued violation." And § 1101.756
provides that "A person commits an offense if the person wilfully violates or fails to comply with
this chapter or a commission order," an offence that "is a Class A misdemeanor."

A suspension has practical consequences the day it lands. Rule 535.141 provides that a person whose
licence has been suspended "may not during the period of any suspension perform, attempt to perform,
or advertise to perform any act for which a license is required," and requires written notice "not
later than the third day before the date of the suspension" — a sales agent to the sponsoring
broker; a broker to sponsored sales agents and any entity for which the person is designated broker;
and, where the person has a contractual obligation to perform licensed services, to all other
parties to the contract.

**What happens to the consumer's money.** The Real Estate Recovery Trust Account is the backstop.
Section 1101.601(a) requires the Commission to maintain it "to reimburse aggrieved persons who
suffer actual damages caused by an act described by Section 1101.602" committed by a license holder,
a certificate holder, or "a person who does not hold a license or certificate and who is an employee
or agent of a license or certificate holder," provided under subsection (b) that "The license or
certificate holder must have held the license or certificate at the time the act was committed."
Section 1101.602 ties entitlement to conduct "described by Section 1101.652(a-1)(1) or (b)" for a
license holder — that is, the same catalogue you just studied.

Recovery is not automatic. Section 1101.606(a) requires an aggrieved person to obtain a court
judgment, and then, "after final judgment is entered, execution returned nulla bona, and a judgment
lien perfected," to file a verified claim in the court that entered the judgment. Subsection (b)
requires written notice to the Commission and the judgment debtor, allows application to the court
after the twentieth day, and provides that "If the aggrieved person does not schedule a hearing
before the first anniversary of the date the application was filed, recovery is waived."

The caps are in § 1101.610. Payments "for claims, including attorney's fees, interest, and court
costs, arising out of a single transaction may not exceed a total of $125,000, regardless of the
number of claimants." Payments "for claims based on judgments against a single license or
certificate holder may not exceed a total of $250,000 until the license or certificate holder has
reimbursed the trust account for all amounts paid." Where claims exceed those limits "the court
shall proportionately reduce the amount payable on each claim." And for purposes of the section, "a
business entity and the broker who is the business entity's designated broker are considered a
single license holder." Note the words "until the license or certificate holder has reimbursed the
trust account": the fund pays the consumer and then looks to you.

**Civil exposure.** The Commission is not the only forum. Business & Commerce Code § 17.50(a) allows
a consumer to maintain an action where certain conduct is "a producing cause of economic damages or
damages for mental anguish," including the use of a false, misleading or deceptive act specifically
enumerated in § 17.46(b) and relied on by the consumer to the consumer's detriment, breach of an
express or implied warranty, and "any unconscionable action or course of action by any person." The
damages provision is § 17.50(b)(1): the consumer may obtain "the amount of economic damages found by
the trier of fact," and "If the trier of fact finds that the conduct of the defendant was committed
knowingly, the consumer may also recover damages for mental anguish, as found by the trier of fact,
and the trier of fact may award not more than three times the amount of economic damages; or if the
trier of fact finds the conduct was committed intentionally, the consumer may recover damages for
mental anguish, as found by the trier of fact, and the trier of fact may award not more than three
times the amount of damages for mental anguish and economic damages." Section 17.50(d) adds that
"Each consumer who prevails shall be awarded court costs and reasonable and necessary attorneys'
fees."

Section 1101.805 allocates responsibility for misstatements between you and your client, and it says
it "prevails over any other law, including common law." A party "is not liable for a
misrepresentation or a concealment of a material fact made by a license holder in a real estate
transaction unless the party: (1) knew of the falsity of the misrepresentation or concealment; and
(2) failed to disclose the party's knowledge of the falsity." And symmetrically, a license holder
"is not liable for a misrepresentation or a concealment of a material fact made by a party to a real
estate transaction unless the license holder: (1) knew of the falsity of the misrepresentation or
concealment; and (2) failed to disclose the license holder's knowledge of the falsity." Subsection
(c) preserves broker responsibility for sales agents. The practical reading: you are not an insurer
of your seller's statements, but the moment you know a statement is false, staying quiet makes it
yours.

Limitations set the outer horizon. Civil Practice and Remedies Code § 16.004(a) requires suit "not
later than four years after the day the cause of action accrues" on actions including "specific
performance of a contract for the conveyance of real property," "debt," "fraud," and "breach of
fiduciary duty." Section 16.003(a) sets a two-year period for the listed actions including trespass
for injury to property and personal injury. Four years for fraud and breach of fiduciary duty is the
number that matters to you, and it is the number that explains the four-year retention rule in 22
TAC § 535.2.

**The day you realise you got it wrong.** This is the part of risk management that is almost never
taught, and it is the part you will actually need. There is no held Texas source that prescribes a
self-correction procedure, and none is invented here. What the held sources do provide is a set of
obligations that, taken together, describe the only defensible response.

Tell your broker immediately. Rule 535.2 makes the broker responsible for your authorized acts and
for trust funds, and § 1101.803 makes the broker liable to the Commission, the public and clients
for your conduct; concealing a problem from the person who is legally answerable for it converts a
mistake into a betrayal.

Correct the record in writing, and correct it toward everyone who relied on it. Rule 535.156 imposes
"an affirmative duty to keep the principal informed at all times of significant information
applicable to the transaction" and "a duty to convey accurate information to members of the public
with whom the license holder deals." If you quoted the wrong square footage in an advertisement, the
fix is a corrected writing to the people who saw it, not a quiet edit.

If money is involved, use the notice rules that already exist. Under 22 TAC § 535.146 a broker whose
trust-money check is dishonoured "shall immediately notify all parties to the transaction in
writing," must notify all parties in writing of any disbursement made on a written demand or a
subsequent written agreement, and may interplead where entitlement genuinely cannot be determined.

Do not touch the file except to add to it. Section 1101.652(a-1)(2) makes it a ground for discipline
to fail or refuse to produce records for inspection; 22 TAC § 535.2 requires the broker to keep the
eight listed record categories four years in a format readily available to the Commission; and the
only excuse the rule recognises for missing records is destruction "by an 'Act of God' such as a
natural disaster or fire not intentionally caused by the broker."

Answer the Commission. Section 1101.652(a)(4) makes failure to provide requested information within
a reasonable time its own ground for discipline, and § 1101.702(b) lists "efforts to correct the
violation" among the factors that set the penalty amount. Prompt correction is not merely decent; it
is statutorily relevant to what you will pay.

And do not offer legal conclusions about your own mistake to the person affected by it. That is
exactly the advice 22 TAC § 537.11 forbids you to give, and it is the moment when the person harmed
should be talking to a lawyer — whom you may not discourage them from employing.

### Applied exercise: The commingled deposit

You are the broker at a two-agent brokerage in Amarillo. On Monday 6 April your sales agent receives
a 5,000 dollar earnest money check from a buyer and puts it in her desk drawer. She hands it to you
on Thursday 9 April. On Friday 10 April, short on cash, you deposit it into the brokerage operating
account, intending to move it Monday. On Monday 13 April the seller's broker asks in writing where
the money is. On 20 April the buyer terminates and makes a written demand for the deposit.

1. Identify the rule the sales agent broke on 6 April and quote the words that state what she was
required to do with the check.
2. State the deposit deadline 22 TAC § 535.146 imposes on you, how it is measured, and the only way
the principals could have changed it.
3. Placing the money in the operating account has a specific evidentiary consequence under 22 TAC §
535.146. State it, and name the two subsections of § 1101.652(b) that make the same conduct a
disciplinary ground.
4. By what date must the trust money be paid to the party entitled to it after the 20 April written
demand? Give the rule's period and the resulting date, and state what you must do immediately after
disbursing.
5. Using 22 TAC § 535.191 and § 1101.702(a), state the penalty range applicable to a violation of §
1101.652(b)(30), the statutory ceiling per violation, and how the Act treats each day a violation
continues.

### Module recap

This module took the whole of the Law of Agency and made it a sequence of acts. On the seller's
side, the sequence is: IABS at the first substantive communication about specific real property
under § 1101.558(b-1) and 22 TAC § 531.20, delivered by one of the four permitted methods and never
by a signature-block link; representation disclosed to the other side at first contact under §
1101.558(b); a CMA carrying, verbatim and at no smaller than 12-point, the sentence 22 TAC § 535.17
prescribes, submitted in the broker's name with the broker responsible for it, and required by 22
TAC § 535.16 whenever you are negotiating a listing; a written listing accepted in the broker's name
under 22 TAC § 535.2, with a definite termination date under § 1101.652(b)(12) and written sign
consent under § 1101.652(b)(18), and enforceable as to your fee only because it is written and
signed as § 1101.806(c) demands; the seller's § 5.008 notice completed by the seller, with "Unknown"
a lawful answer under § 5.008(d), delivered on or before the effective date under § 5.008(f) or the
buyer holds a seven-day right to terminate for any reason; advertising that satisfies 22 TAC §
535.155's half-size broker-name rule and comes down within ten days of the listing ending; and every
offer conveyed under 22 TAC § 535.156 unless the principal has agreed *in writing* that offers are
not to be submitted after a contract is signed.

On the buyer's side the sequence begins earlier than most people expect. Section 1101.563(b)
requires a written agreement *before* you show any residential real property or, if nothing is
shown, before you present an offer, and § 1101.652(b)(34) makes failing to do it a disciplinary
ground. Section 1101.563(c) lists the five things the agreement must state plus the conspicuous
disclosure that broker compensation is not set by law and is fully negotiable; § 1101.563(e) caps a
showing-only agreement at fourteen days and forbids exclusivity. Section 1101.562 permits showing
without representing only where you have not agreed to represent, are not otherwise acting as agent,
give no opinions or advice about the property or transactions generally, and perform no other
brokerage act — with a narrow permission in § 1101.562(c) to confirm size, price and terms. When the
offer is signed, § 1101.555 requires a written advisory about an abstract examined by an attorney of
the buyer's choosing or a title insurance policy, and § 1101.806(d) provides that a license holder
who fails to give it "may not receive payment of or recover any commission agreed to be paid on the
sale."

The contract itself is written inside a boundary. Rule 537.11 requires promulgated forms subject to
four exceptions, forbids you to draft or recommend escalation, appraisal or contingency language,
forbids opinions on legal effect or on title, forbids discouraging a party from hiring an attorney,
and permits you to add or strike language only on a principal's specific written instruction with
the change made conspicuous. Section 1101.654(a) says the Commission "shall" suspend or revoke for
drafting an instrument affecting an interest in real property or advising on the validity or legal
sufficiency of an instrument or of title; § 1101.654(b) protects you when you complete an approved,
attorney-prepared, or owner-required form. Earnest money is trust money under 22 TAC § 535.146: a
sales agent may never hold a trust account and must deliver it to the broker immediately; the broker
deposits by close of business of the second working day unless the principals agree otherwise in
writing; commingling is prima facie proved by an operating-account deposit; written demands are
honoured within thirty days; and all trust-account documentation is kept four years.

The everyday habits are five numbers and one line. Two calendar days to respond to a principal, a
cooperating license holder or an unrepresented party under 22 TAC § 535.157, and two calendar days
for a broker or delegated supervisor to answer a sponsored sales agent under 22 TAC § 535.2, which
also gives three calendar days to pass on Commission correspondence. Four years to keep the eight
categories of records 22 TAC § 535.2 lists — including "substantive communications with parties to
the transaction" — which matches both the four-year investigation window of § 1101.204(c) and the
four-year limitations period of Civil Practice and Remedies Code § 16.004(a) for fraud and breach of
fiduciary duty. Ten days to register or de-register a name under 22 TAC § 535.154, to update contact
information under 22 TAC § 535.21, and for a broker to file a new office address under §
1101.552(b). And the line is the one 22 TAC § 537.11 draws: explain the form, never opine on its
legal effect.

The other considerations are the ones the Commission finds without a consumer's help. Rule 535.144
requires written disclosure that you are a licensed broker or sales agent acting for your own
account — a category that includes your spouse, parent or child, a business entity in which you are
more than a ten percent owner, and a trust where you are trustee or where you or close family are
beneficiaries — in the contract or in a writing given before it, and forbids using your expertise to
the disadvantage of the person you deal with. Section 1101.651 confines compensation to license
holders and qualifying out-of-state brokers and routes every sales agent's compensation through the
sponsoring broker. Rule 535.20 makes an unlicensed referral for valuable consideration an act
requiring a licence, with "gifts of merchandise having a retail value greater than $50" inside the
definition. Rule 535.148 bars fees between settlement service providers for referrals or list
placement while preserving market-rate payment for goods actually furnished and cooperative
brokerage referral arrangements. Team names must end in "team" or "group," must not imply
independence from the sponsoring broker, and must be registered by the broker before use.

Risk management closes the loop. Complaints must be signed and written, cannot be anonymous, and
cannot reach back beyond four years. They are triaged by 22 TAC § 535.141 with a 10,000 dollar line
between Level 1 and Level 2 fraud and with unlicensed activity always at Level 1. A complaint
against a sales agent is, for limited purposes, a complaint against the sponsoring broker, and §
1101.803 makes the broker liable for the agent's conduct. Penalties run to 5,000 dollars per
violation under § 1101.702(a), with each day capable of being a separate violation, sorted by 22 TAC
§ 535.191 into three published ranges and doubled for a history of violations. Beyond the Commission
lie the Recovery Trust Account with its 125,000 dollar per-transaction and 250,000 dollar
per-license-holder caps under § 1101.610, DTPA damages that can be trebled under Business & Commerce
Code § 17.50(b)(1) with mandatory costs and fees to a prevailing consumer under § 17.50(d), and the
allocation of misrepresentation liability in § 1101.805 that turns on knowledge of falsity and
failure to disclose it.

Now stand back and look at the twelve topics as one argument, because that is how you should carry
them out of this course. *Agency Concepts* and *Basic Agency Relationships, Disclosure and Duties to
the Client* establish that a license holder acting for another is a fiduciary — 22 TAC § 531.2 — and
that the duties are loyalty, disclosure and obedience within the law. *Duties and Disclosures to
Third Parties* establishes the second, lesser but real duty: honesty and accuracy to everyone, under
22 TAC § 535.156 and § 1101.652(b)(3) and (4). *Seller Agency* and *Buyer Agency* give those duties
their two concrete shapes, now governed on the buyer side by the 2026 sections § 1101.562 and §
1101.563. *Representing More Than One Party in a Transaction: Intermediary Brokerage* supplies the
Texas answer to conflict — § 1101.559's written consent stating the source of expected compensation,
§ 1101.560's appointments, § 1101.561(b)'s mandatory intermediary status, and § 1101.651(d)'s five
prohibitions on the intermediary. *Creation and Termination of Agency* and *Clarifying Agency
Relationships* fix when the relationship exists and how everyone is told, through § 1101.558, §
1101.557 and § 1101.652(b)(7). *Employment Issues* places you under a sponsoring broker with §
1101.351(c), 22 TAC § 535.2 and § 1101.803 defining who answers for what. *Agency, Ethics and the
Law* supplies the standards — 22 TAC §§ 531.2, 531.3, 531.4 and 531.19 — and the catalogue of §
1101.652. *Deceptive Trade Practices and Consumer Protection Act* supplies the consumer's own weapon
in Business & Commerce Code ch. 17. And this topic, *Implementation and Presentation*, is where all
of it becomes Tuesday morning.

If you retain nothing else, retain these: § 1101.558 and 22 TAC § 531.20 for the IABS; § 1101.563
and § 1101.562 for the buyer agreement and non-representation showings; § 1101.555 and § 1101.806(d)
for the title advisory and the fee you forfeit without it; § 1101.806(c) and Business & Commerce
Code § 26.01 for why everything is in writing; 22 TAC § 535.17 for the price-opinion sentence and 22
TAC § 535.16 for the listing duties; Property Code § 5.008 for the seller's notice and its seven-day
consequence; 22 TAC § 537.11 and § 1101.654 for the practice-of-law line; 22 TAC § 535.146 for trust
money and the second-working-day rule; 22 TAC §§ 535.154 and 535.155 for how you may present
yourself; 22 TAC § 535.157 for two calendar days; 22 TAC § 535.2 for four years of records; 22 TAC §
535.144 for your own account; and § 1101.652 with 22 TAC § 535.191 for what it costs when you get
one of them wrong.
