# Texas Law of Agency — Module 05

## TREC Topic: Buyer Agency

**Prescribed time: 150 minutes.** This module carries the six units the Commission's LOA-1 course
approval form places under *Buyer Agency*, whose scope note is *"Disclosure of agency; listing or
buying representation procedures."*

**Learning objectives.** After completing this module you will be able to: state what a buyer
representation agreement is, whose contract it is, and every element Occupations Code
§ 1101.563(c) requires it to contain; describe how buyer agency is created, distinguish it from
the non-representation showing authorised by § 1101.562 and its four conjunctive conditions, and
recognise the conduct that turns an unrepresented prospect into a client by accident; state the
concrete benefits a represented buyer receives and trace each to a duty in the held sources; work
out how a buyer's broker is paid and why 22 TAC § 535.3 routes every sales agent's compensation
through the sponsoring broker; and carry out a buyer's broker's disclosure obligations, including
the commission-or-rebate disclosure required by 22 TAC § 535.148.

**What this module teaches in full:** Occupations Code §§ 1101.562 and 1101.563 and the whole 2026
buyer-representation change, together with 22 TAC §§ 535.3 and 535.148. Other authorities appear
here only as they operate on buyer agency, and each is taught in the module named at the citation.

**Every legal statement in this module comes from a source that was read for this course.** Where
a statement is practice rather than law, the module says so and cites nothing for it.

## Unit: Buyer Representation Agreement

Start with the date, because this unit is different from the one your predecessors studied. Two
new sections were added to The Real Estate License Act by Acts 2025, 89th Leg., R.S., Ch. 1172
(S.B. 1968), Sec. 10, effective **January 1, 2026**: Occupations Code § 1101.562, "REAL PROPERTY
SHOWINGS WITHOUT REPRESENTATION," and Occupations Code § 1101.563, "WRITTEN AGREEMENT REQUIRED."
The Texas Real Estate Commission published an explanatory article on those changes, "What Changes
in 2026 About Buyer/Tenant Representation in Texas," dated 01-07-2026, which says of the two
sections that "While these two sections are separate, they go together and should be read
together." That article is guidance, not law; the statutory text controls, and this unit works
from the text. But the Commission's advice to read the two sections together is sound, and this
module follows it.

Before 2026, a Texas license holder could work with a buyer for weeks without any signed
agreement, and many did. That is no longer permissible for residential buyers. If you learned the
business before January 1, 2026, or if you are being trained by someone who did, you will hear
habits described as normal that the statute now forbids. Learn the current text.

**Whose agreement is it?** A buyer representation agreement is a contract between the prospective
buyer and a **broker**. It is not a contract between the buyer and you, if you are a sales agent.
Commission rule 22 TAC § 535.2 requires that listings and other agreements for real estate
brokerage services "must be solicited and accepted in a broker's name"; Module 09 teaches that
rule. The consequence for this topic is that when you sit at a kitchen table in Cedar Park and ask
a buyer to sign, the buyer is engaging your broker, your broker's name goes on the agreement, your
broker is answerable for its performance, and you sign as the broker's associate. Students
routinely get this wrong and speak of *my buyer agreement*. The client is the brokerage's client.
That matters when you change sponsorship, when a complaint is filed, and when compensation is
claimed.

**What the agreement does.** In substance, a buyer representation agreement is an employment
contract that engages the broker to perform acts of real estate brokerage for the buyer, defines
what those acts are, sets how long the engagement lasts, states whether the buyer may engage other
brokers at the same time, and fixes what the broker will be paid and by whom. Signing one carries
a statutory consequence: "A broker who represents a party in a real estate transaction or who
lists real estate for sale under an exclusive agreement for a party is that party's agent,"
§ 1101.557(a), taught in Module 02. Once the broker represents the buyer, the whole apparatus of
agency duties attaches.

**The § 1101.563 mandate — the centre of this module.** Section 1101.563 is short, and every
clause of it does work. Read it in order.

Subsection (a) defines the term that limits the section's reach. "In this section, 'residential
real property' means" three things, and only three: "a single-family house"; "a duplex, triplex,
or quadraplex"; and "a unit in a multiunit residential structure in which title to an individual
unit is transferred to the owner of the unit under a condominium or cooperative system." That is
the whole definition. Notice what is not in it. Unimproved land is not in it — the ten-acre tract
outside Bastrop your buyer wants for a future homesite is not a single-family house. A fifty-unit
apartment building held in one ownership is not in it, because title to individual units is not
transferred to unit owners under a condominium or cooperative system. An office suite, a retail
strip centre, a warehouse in Pharr, a working ranch in Coleman County — none of these fall inside
the definition. TREC's article puts the same limit in plain words: "The written agreement
requirement does not apply to commercial purchasers or residential/commercial tenants."

That last clause deserves emphasis because it is the most common misreading. **Section 1101.563
reaches prospective buyers only.** Its operative sentence in subsection (b) speaks of "a
prospective buyer of residential real property," and every subsequent reference is to "the
prospective buyer." A tenant is not a buyer. A person looking to lease a house in Lubbock for a
year is outside § 1101.563 entirely, no matter how residential the house is. Do not tell a leasing
prospect that state law requires them to sign a representation agreement before you can show them
a rental. That is not what the statute says.

Subsection (b) states the mandate and the moment it attaches: "A license holder who performs any
act of real estate brokerage for a prospective buyer of residential real property must enter into
a written agreement with the prospective buyer before" one of two events. The first is "showing
any residential real property to the prospective buyer." The second is "if no residential real
property will be shown, presenting an offer to purchase residential real property on behalf of the
prospective buyer."

Take those triggers apart. The duty falls on "a license holder," which by § 1101.002(4) means "a
broker or sales agent licensed under this chapter" — so it binds you personally as a sales agent,
not merely your broker. The condition that puts you inside the section is that you are performing
"any act of real estate brokerage" for a prospective residential buyer, and the meaning of that
phrase is broad: § 1101.004(a) and the acts described by § 1101.002(1)(A), which include aiding
"in locating or obtaining real estate for purchase or lease" and negotiating "the listing, sale,
exchange, purchase, or lease of real estate," are taught in Module 01. Helping a buyer find a
house is an act of real estate brokerage. That is the whole business.

The two "before" clauses fix the outer limit. You must have the written agreement in hand **before
you show any residential real property** to that prospective buyer. Not before you write the
offer; not before you open escrow; before the showing. And in the case where you are never going
to show anything — the buyer has already found the house on their own, or is buying from a
relative, or is buying at a distance — the agreement must be in place before you present the offer
to purchase on the buyer's behalf.

Notice the exact wording of the first trigger: "showing any residential real property." Not
*showing the residential real property*. If you have a written agreement with a Frisco buyer that
covers only a specific address, and you then take them to look at a second house, you have shown
residential real property to a prospective buyer, and you need a written agreement that covers it.
Write the agreement to cover the engagement, not one door.

Subsection (c) tells you what must be in the writing. The agreement "must" state each of the
following, and must make one disclosure:

- "the services to be provided by the license holder";
- "the termination date of the agreement";
- "whether the agreement is exclusive or non-exclusive";
- "as applicable," that the license holder either "represents the prospective buyer as the buyer's
  agent" or "does not represent the prospective buyer as the buyer's agent if the only act of real
  estate brokerage being performed is showing real property under Section 1101.562";
- "the amount or rate of compensation the broker will receive and how this amount will be
  determined."

And the agreement must "disclose in conspicuous language that broker compensation is not set by
law and is fully negotiable."

Work through those. The **services** clause forces you to say what you are actually agreeing to
do. TREC's article notes that representation agreements "also can limit the services a broker
agrees to provide to prospective buyers—such as limiting their services to showing only—but they
cannot waive the minimum duties owed to clients which are provided by existing TREC rules and
law." That last qualification is the one to remember: a limited-service agreement narrows the
scope of the engagement; it does not shrink the fiduciary standard within whatever scope you
accepted. You may agree to do less. You may not agree to do it worse.

The **termination date** requirement is not new in substance, and it has independent teeth.
Occupations Code § 1101.652(b)(12) has long made it a disciplinary ground to fail "to specify a
definite termination date that is not subject to prior notice in a contract, other than a contract
to perform property management services, in which the license holder agrees to perform services
for which a license is required under this chapter"; Module 10 teaches § 1101.652. Read the
qualifier carefully, because it is the part students drop. The date must be **definite**, and it
must be "not subject to prior notice" — meaning an agreement that runs until one side gives thirty
days' notice does not satisfy the section. A buyer representation agreement is not a property
management contract, so it needs a definite termination date, and now § 1101.563(c)(1)(B) requires
the date to be stated in the writing as well.

The **exclusive or non-exclusive** clause tells the buyer whether they may engage another broker
at the same time. Under an exclusive agreement, the buyer commits to work through your brokerage
for the term; under a non-exclusive one, they do not. Neither is unlawful; the statute requires
only that the agreement say which it is. There is a hard limit on exclusivity for one narrow kind
of agreement, which we come to in a moment.

The **representation status** clause is the hinge between § 1101.563 and § 1101.562. There are two
boxes, and they are not both available in every case. If you are representing the buyer, the
agreement says you represent the prospective buyer as the buyer's agent. The other box — "does not
represent the prospective buyer as the buyer's agent" — is available only "if the only act of real
estate brokerage being performed is showing real property under Section 1101.562." That is a
narrow gate. You may check the non-representation box only when showing is genuinely the only
brokerage act you are performing for that person.

The **compensation** clause requires "the amount or rate of compensation the broker will receive
and how this amount will be determined." Both halves matter: the number or the rate, and the
method. *To be determined* is not compliance. *Three percent of the sales price* states a rate
and, read with a definition of sales price, states the method. We return to compensation at length
in Unit 5.

The **negotiability disclosure** must be in "conspicuous language" and must say "that broker
compensation is not set by law and is fully negotiable." A comparable statement has long been
required in listing contract forms the Commission adopts — § 1101.155(c)(1) — but the Commission
has not adopted a buyer representation form, and § 1101.563(c)(2) reaches the agreement itself
regardless of who drafted it. Put the sentence in, make it conspicuous, and do not bury it in a
paragraph of recitals.

**Showing-only agreements.** Subsections (d) and (e) govern the special case of an agreement
entered into solely so that you can show property to someone you do not represent. Subsection (d)
provides: "A license holder who enters into a written agreement with a prospective buyer for the
sole purpose of showing real property under Section 1101.562 shall enter into a separate agreement
with the prospective buyer if additional real estate brokerage acts are to be provided after
showing the real property." So the showing-only agreement does not silently upgrade itself. The
moment you begin doing more than showing — advising, negotiating, drafting an offer — you need a
separate agreement, and that separate agreement is the one where the representation box gets
checked differently.

Subsection (e) puts two hard limits on the showing-only agreement. It "may not" "be an exclusive
agreement with the license holder," and it may not "state a termination date more than fourteen
days from the date the agreement is entered into." Note that the statute writes **fourteen days**
in words, not as a numeral, and note that fourteen days is a ceiling, not a default: nothing stops
a shorter term, and for a single Saturday of showings in Round Rock a one-day or three-day term is
perfectly lawful and often cleaner. What you may not do is sign a person to a ninety-day exclusive
showing-only arrangement. The Legislature plainly did not want the non-representation posture used
to tie consumers up.

**The consequence of getting it wrong.** The same 2025 act that created § 1101.563 added a
matching disciplinary ground. Occupations Code § 1101.652(b)(34), added by Acts 2025, 89th Leg.,
R.S., Ch. 1172 (S.B. 1968), Sec. 11, effective January 1, 2026, lets the Commission suspend or
revoke a license or take other disciplinary action if the license holder, while engaged in real
estate brokerage, "fails to enter into a written agreement with a prospective buyer as required by
Section 1101.563." The writing requirement is not a best practice or a trade-association rule. It
is a licensing obligation with its own enumerated sanction.

**Which form do you use?** The Commission does not supply one. TREC's contracts page states: "TREC
does not promulgate listing or buyer representation agreements, property management contracts,
forms for commercial property, or residential leases (other than temporary residential leases used
in connection with a sale). Contact your attorney or a real estate trade association for such
forms." Practically, you will use a form your broker supplies, drawn either by an attorney or by a
trade association. What you may not do is draft one yourself: 22 TAC § 537.11(b) forbids a license
holder to practice law, to offer or give legal advice, or to draft or recommend contract language
defining or affecting the rights, obligations or remedies of the principals to a real estate
transaction; Module 04 teaches § 537.11. Use your broker's form. If the buyer wants it changed in
a way that alters legal rights, the buyer needs a lawyer.

**Keep the paper.** Commission rule 22 TAC § 535.2 requires the broker to maintain "commission
agreements such as listing agreements, buyer representation agreements, or other written
agreements relied upon to claim compensation," along with "disclosures" and "substantive
communications with parties to the transaction," "in a format that is readily available to the
Commission for at least four years from the date of closing, termination of the contract, or end
of a real estate transaction"; Module 09 teaches the record-keeping rule. A buyer representation
agreement that expired unused still starts a four-year clock on the date it terminated.

## Unit: Deciding to Represent the Buyer

The preceding unit assumed a decision had already been made: that your brokerage would represent
this buyer. That decision is not automatic, and treating it as automatic is how brokerages get
into trouble.

**The decision belongs to the broker.** A sales agent does not decide, on the agent's own
authority, whom the firm will represent. Under 22 TAC § 535.2, agreements for brokerage services
are solicited and accepted in the broker's name, the broker must notify each sponsored sales agent
in writing of the scope of that agent's authorised activities, and the broker is responsible for
the agent's authorised acts and for acts beyond the authorised scope that the broker permits;
Module 09 teaches the rule. So the first question in deciding whether to represent a buyer is a
firm question: does our policy allow this engagement, and am I authorised to accept it?

**Competence is a threshold, not an aspiration.** Rule 22 TAC § 531.4 obliges a license holder "to
be knowledgeable and competent as a real estate brokerage practitioner," and requires among other
things that the license holder be informed on local market conditions "in the geographic area
where a license holder provides services to a client" and be "educated in the characteristics
involved in the specific type of real estate being brokered for others"; Module 02 teaches the
Canon, and § 1101.652(b)(1), which permits discipline where a license holder "acts negligently or
incompetently," is taught in Module 10.

Apply that concretely. You are a San Antonio sales agent in your first year. A buyer asks you to
represent them in purchasing a 300-acre place near Junction with a producing well and an existing
grazing lease. You know suburban resale. You do not know surface-and-mineral severance,
agricultural valuation, or livestock leases. The right answer is not to sign the agreement and
learn on the buyer's money. It is either to decline, or to bring in someone competent and disclose
the arrangement, with your broker's authorisation. Note too that 22 TAC § 535.2 requires the
broker's written policies to ensure that a sales agent performing a type of brokerage activity for
the first three times receives coaching and assistance from an experienced license holder
competent for that activity. Your first three of anything are supervised. That is a rule, not a
courtesy.

**The conflict the decision creates: your own listings.** The sharpest conflict arrives the moment
your buyer wants to see a property your brokerage has listed. If your broker represents the seller
under an exclusive listing, § 1101.557(a) makes your broker the seller's agent; if your broker
also agrees to represent the buyer, the broker is on both sides, and § 1101.561(b) makes
intermediary status compulsory: "A broker must agree to act as an intermediary under this
subchapter if the broker agrees to represent in a transaction" both "a buyer or tenant" and "a
seller or landlord." The word is *must*; there is no third posture available under that
subchapter. The intermediary machinery — §§ 1101.559, 1101.560 and 1101.561, and the conduct
§ 1101.651(d) prohibits — is taught in Module 06.

What belongs to buyer agency is where the consent is captured. Section 1101.559(a) permits a
broker to act as an intermediary only if the broker "obtains written consent from each party" and
"the written consent of the parties states the source of any expected compensation to the broker,"
and § 1101.559(b) makes a written agreement to represent a buyer sufficient to establish that
party's consent "if the written agreement specifies in conspicuous bold or underlined print the
conduct that is prohibited under Section 1101.651(d)." So the intermediary authorisation, and the
recital of prohibited conduct in conspicuous bold or underlined print, belong in the buyer
representation agreement at signing — long before anyone knows whether an in-house property will
come up, and in every case before the broker acts as an intermediary. A brokerage that has not
built this into its buyer agreement will be scrambling on a Saturday afternoon.

So the honest way to present the decision to a buyer in Sugar Land is this: if you engage our
brokerage and you later want to buy one of our listings, our broker becomes an intermediary, our
broker's advocacy narrows to what § 1101.651(d) permits, and — if the broker makes appointments
under § 1101.560 and both parties consented in writing to appointments — you may still receive
opinions and advice from the associate appointed to you. A buyer who does not want that outcome
should be told plainly that a brokerage with no listings, or a firm that does not take in-house
transactions, is an alternative.

**Other conflicts to weigh before you sign.** Two buyers you represent may want the same house in
the same week. Nothing in the held sources forbids representing both, but rule 22 TAC § 531.2
requires "that the primary duty of the license holder is to represent the interests of the client,
and the license holder's position, in this respect, should be clear to all parties concerned in a
real estate transaction," and requires "that the license holder place no personal interest above
that of the client"; Module 02 teaches the Canon. Two clients bidding against each other puts you
in a position where advancing one client's interest damages another's. Decide your policy in
advance, disclose it in the agreement, and do not improvise it at midnight.

A second conflict is your own pocketbook. Section 1101.652(b)(8) reaches a license holder who
receives compensation from more than one party to a transaction without the full knowledge and
consent of all parties; § 1101.652(b)(13) reaches an undisclosed commission, rebate, or direct
profit on an expenditure made for a principal; and § 1101.652(b)(16) reaches acting in a dual
capacity as broker and undisclosed principal. Module 10 teaches § 1101.652. If you or your spouse
might end up bidding on the same property, if you take referral money from a lender, if you have
any economic interest in the outcome beyond the commission stated in the agreement, that belongs
on the table before the buyer signs, not after. Unit 6 works through the disclosure mechanics.

**Declining is a legitimate outcome.** Nothing in Chapter 1101 obliges a broker to represent
anyone. Rule 22 TAC § 531.19 does constrain *how* you decline: it forbids a license holder to
inquire about, respond to or facilitate inquiries about, or disclose information about an owner,
occupant, potential purchaser, lessor or potential lessee that indicates or is intended to
indicate any preference, limitation, or discrimination on the grounds the rule lists; Module 10
teaches it. You may decline an engagement because it is outside your competence, outside your
market, outside your firm's policy, or because the buyer will not agree to terms. You may never
decline, or steer, on a protected basis.

## Unit: The Creation of Buyer Agency

Buyer agency is created by agreement. That sentence is the whole unit in compressed form, and the
rest of this unit is about the ways students and license holders get it wrong in both directions —
believing they have an agency relationship when they do not, and, far more dangerously, believing
they do not have one when they do.

**The statutory rule.** Occupations Code § 1101.557(a) states the creation rule for both sides of
a transaction: "A broker who represents a party in a real estate transaction or who lists real
estate for sale under an exclusive agreement for a party is that party's agent." For sellers, the
statute names a document — an exclusive listing agreement. For buyers, the statute uses the
broader phrase "represents a party." Module 02 teaches § 1101.557. Since January 1, 2026,
§ 1101.563 has supplied the missing formality on the buy side for residential transactions: a
license holder performing any act of real estate brokerage for a prospective buyer of residential
real property "must enter into a written agreement with the prospective buyer" before showing any
residential real property or, if none will be shown, before presenting an offer on the buyer's
behalf. The intended result is that residential buyer agency now begins with a signed document and
a date, not with a conversation whose legal effect nobody noticed at the time.

**Why the change happened.** The Commission's article says the reason directly: "The intent behind
this change was to help protect consumers by requiring use of written agreements that clearly
outline the obligations the license holder and prospective buyer owe to each other." The consumer
problem the Legislature addressed was not that buyers were being represented badly. It was that
buyers frequently could not say whether they were being represented at all, and neither could the
license holder, and the two of them often held different views of the same relationship. A written
agreement forces the question to be answered in advance.

**The mistake to name: agency by conduct.** Here is the failure mode. A license holder in Denton
meets a prospect at an open house. Over the next three weeks they exchange forty text messages.
The license holder pulls comparable sales, tells the prospect that a listing is *overpriced by
about fifteen thousand*, suggests offering $12,000 under list with a shortened option period,
calls the listing agent to ask what the seller wants on closing date, and drafts an offer. No
agreement was ever signed. Ask the license holder whom they represent and they will say *nobody —
they never signed anything*.

That answer is wrong twice over. First, it is wrong as a matter of licensing law after January 1,
2026: § 1101.652(b)(34) makes it a disciplinary ground to fail "to enter into a written agreement
with a prospective buyer as required by Section 1101.563," so the absence of the writing is itself
the violation, not a defence to one. Second, it is wrong because the conduct described is exactly
the conduct § 1101.562 says a broker showing property without representation may not engage in.
That section permits the non-representation posture only where the broker "does not provide to the
party opinions or advice regarding the real property or real estate transactions in general" and
"does not perform any other act of real estate brokerage for the party." Pricing opinions are
opinions about the real property. Offer strategy is advice about real estate transactions in
general. Drafting and presenting an offer is another act of real estate brokerage. The license
holder in Denton was doing the work of a buyer's agent and simply had not papered it.

The older provision that has always punished this confusion is § 1101.652(b)(7), which reaches a
license holder who "fails to make clear to all parties to a real estate transaction the party for
whom the license holder is acting" (Module 10). Ambiguity about whom you represent is itself a
violation. Whether or not a court would find an agency relationship arose by conduct in a given
case is a question of common law, and **the Texas common-law elements of implied or apparent
agency were not held for this course and are not taught here.** What is held, and what is
sufficient for your licence, is this: TRELA requires you to make the relationship clear, and since
2026 it requires the residential buyer relationship to be written.

**The lawful alternative: § 1101.562 showings without representation.** The 2025 legislation did
not force every license holder into a representation relationship with every prospect. It created
an express, narrow, non-representation posture — and then fenced it. Section 1101.562(a) provides
that "A broker may show real property available for sale or lease to a party without representing
the party if the broker" satisfies four conditions, stated conjunctively. The broker must have
"not agreed with the party, either orally or in writing, to represent the party." The broker must
be "not otherwise acting as the party's agent at the time of showing the real property." The
broker must not "provide to the party opinions or advice regarding the real property or real
estate transactions in general." And the broker must not "perform any other act of real estate
brokerage for the party."

Read condition (1) again: "either orally or in writing." An oral agreement to represent someone
destroys the non-representation posture even though it would not satisfy § 1101.563. This is the
trap. You cannot tell a prospect in a Katy driveway *sure, I'll look out for you on this one* and
then claim, at showing time, that you had not agreed to represent them. You had. The statute
counts oral agreements for the purpose of taking the non-representation posture away from you,
while requiring a writing for the purpose of establishing representation. Those two rules point
the same direction: say nothing to a prospect about representing them that you are not prepared to
reduce to writing.

Condition (3) is the operative daily constraint. No opinions, no advice — not about the property
in front of you, and not about "real estate transactions in general." That second phrase is
broader than students expect. *The market's been slowing, you could probably get this for less.*
*You should get a survey.* *Do the inspection before you spend money on the appraisal.* Every one
of those is advice about real estate transactions in general.

Subsection (c) tells you exactly how much you may say, and it is a short list: "Notwithstanding
Subsections (a)(3) and (4), a broker showing real property under this section may confirm
information to a party regarding the size, price, and terms of the real property available for
sale or lease." Size, price, terms. You may confirm that the house is 2,140 square feet, that it
is listed at $389,900, and that the seller wants a fifteen-day close with a leaseback. That is the
extent of it. The verb matters too: **confirm**, not evaluate. Confirming that the price is
$389,900 is permitted. Saying the price is fair is not.

**The disclosure gate in § 1101.562(b).** Before a broker may show property under this section,
the broker must do one of two things, "as applicable." If the broker represents the owner, the
broker must "disclose to the party as required by Section 1101.558(b) if the broker represents the
owner of the real property." If the broker does not represent the owner, the broker must "provide
to the party the written notice described by Section 1101.558(b-1)" — that is, the Information
About Brokerage Services notice, which Module 02 teaches in full.

The interaction is the point that belongs to this module. Section 1101.558(c)(3) relieves the
§ 1101.558(b-1) duty where "the communication occurs at a property that is held open for any
prospective buyer or tenant and the communication concerns that property." Section 1101.562(b)(2)
imposes its own duty, as a condition of showing property without representation, on a broker who
does not represent the owner. The § 1101.558(c) exceptions by their terms relieve only "the notice
required by Subsection (b-1)"; they do not relieve the free-standing gate in § 1101.562(b). TREC's
article reads the interaction the same way, and works it through the open-house case. Where the
agent hosting the open house "is a member of a listing broker's office (e.g., a sales agent at the
brokerage holding the listing), the agent does not need to provide the IABS or enter into a
written agreement with prospective buyers," but "will need to disclose he or she represents the
owner either orally or in writing as required by 1101.558(b)." Where the agent hosting the open
house "is not a member of the listing broker's office and does not represent the seller (e.g., a
showing agent)," the agent must "Provide the IABS" and "Enter into a written agreement as
described by 1101.563(c) with prospective buyers (e.g., visitors of the open house)." The article
adds a sentence that surprises people: "This is true even when a prospective buyer has a written
representation agreement with another broker." The statute keys the duty to what *you* are doing,
not to what some other broker has already signed.

The article also states the practical timing: "By law, the IABS must be provided and agreement
entered into before the showing. In practical terms, what this means for an open house is that
ideally, the agreement should be presented and entered into before the buyer enters the property,
but at the very least before the buyer starts viewing the property (e.g., the agent greets the
prospective buyer near the door)." And it answers the question every new license holder asks next:
"If a buyer refuses to sign the agreement, then the agent cannot show the property, meaning a
consumer cannot view the open house. License holders can face disciplinary action if they fail to
enter into a written agreement as required by state law under 1101.563." That consequence follows
from § 1101.563(b) plus § 1101.652(b)(34), and it is the single most operationally significant
thing in this module. A showing agent hosting an open house in a residential subdivision who
cannot get a signature cannot let the visitor tour the house.

**Keeping the posture honest.** If you are going to use § 1101.562, use it deliberately. In
practice — this is practice, not law — that means deciding before the appointment which posture
you are in, having the correct paper with you, and having a short, rehearsed sentence for the
moment the prospect asks the question you are not allowed to answer. *I am not able to give you an
opinion on that, because I am showing you this house without representing you. If you would like
advice, we can talk about a representation agreement.* Then stop. The most common way license
holders blow the non-representation posture is by answering one friendly question in the hallway.

And understand the trapdoor: the posture is not permanent. Section 1101.563(d) requires a separate
agreement "if additional real estate brokerage acts are to be provided after showing the real
property." If you show a house in New Braunfels on Saturday under a fourteen-day non-exclusive
showing agreement and the buyer calls Monday wanting to make an offer, you do not amend the
showing agreement in your head. You enter into a new agreement that states the services and the
representation status accurately.

**Subagency.** One structural change deserves a note, because older materials discuss it at
length. Section 1101.002(8) of the Occupations Code now reads, in the version held for this
course, "Repealed by Acts 2025, 89th Leg., R.S., Ch. 1172 (S.B. 1968), Sec. 13(1), eff. January 1,
2026." The word "subagent" does not appear anywhere in Chapter 1101 as held. TREC's article
describes the change this way: "Two references to subagency were removed from TRELA. This means
the circumstances in which subagency could arise in your real estate practice are limited.
However, subagency has not been eliminated generally as a legal concept. If you are in a situation
where it seems subagency may be involved, contact your broker and/or attorney." The text of the
repealed definition **was not held** for this course and is not taught here. What you should take
away is narrow and safe: TRELA no longer defines subagency, and a Texas license holder working
with a buyer today operates in one of the two statutory postures this module has described —
representation under a written agreement, or a § 1101.562 showing without representation.

## Unit: Benefits of Buyer-Agency Relationships

This unit is easy to teach badly. The temptation is to recite advantages the way a recruiting
brochure would. Resist it. The correct way to state the benefits of buyer agency is to identify
the specific duties the law imposes on a broker who represents a buyer, and then to describe what
the buyer does *not* get when no such relationship exists. Every benefit below is a duty somewhere
in the held sources, and where a supposed benefit turns out to be something the buyer would
receive anyway, this unit says so.

**Benefit one: someone is legally required to put the buyer's interests first.** A license holder
acting as an agent for another is a fiduciary whose primary duty is to represent the interests of
the client and who must place no personal interest above the client's, while treating other
parties to the transaction fairly and honestly: 22 TAC §§ 531.2 and 535.156, taught in Module 02.
Read the qualifier rather than the headline. A buyer without a representation agreement is
entitled to fair and honest treatment from every license holder in the transaction. What that
buyer does not have is anyone whose duty of *fidelity* runs to them. That is the difference, and
it is worth stating in exactly those terms to a prospect who asks why they should sign anything.

**Benefit two: affirmative disclosure of everything that bears on the buyer's decision.** Rule
22 TAC § 535.156 requires a license holder to convey to the principal all known information that
would affect the principal's decision on whether to make, accept or reject offers — subject to one
recognised limit, which operates only where the principal has agreed **in writing** that offers
are not to be submitted after the principal has entered into a contract, in which case there is no
duty to submit offers after the principal has accepted an offer — and imposes an affirmative duty
to keep the principal informed of significant information applicable to the transaction. The statute adds
that a broker who represents a party must inform the party of material information related to the
transaction, "including the receipt of an offer by the broker," and "shall, at a minimum, answer
the party's questions and present any offer to or from the party": § 1101.557(b)(2) and (b)(3),
taught with the rule in Module 02. That floor is what matters here. A limited-service buyer
agreement can shrink the list of services in many directions, but it cannot go below answering the
client's questions and presenting offers.

Now compare the unrepresented buyer. A license holder still owes them something. Sections
1101.652(b)(3) and (b)(4) reach a material misrepresentation to, or a failure to disclose to, a
"potential buyer" a known significant defect including a latent structural defect (Module 10), and
22 TAC § 535.156 imposes on every license holder "a duty to convey accurate information to members
of the public with whom the license holder deals." Notice the phrase in the statute: *potential
buyer*, not client. So known material defects must be disclosed to a buyer whether or not you
represent them. The benefit of representation is not that you will be told about the foundation.
It is that you will be told about everything else — the seller's timeline, the second offer that
fell through, the fact that the house was withdrawn and relisted, the pattern in the comparable
sales — because significant information about the transaction must be conveyed to a principal and
need not be volunteered to a stranger.

**Benefit three: opinions and advice at all.** This is the benefit the 2026 statute made concrete.
A broker showing property under § 1101.562 may not "provide to the party opinions or advice
regarding the real property or real estate transactions in general," and may only "confirm
information to a party regarding the size, price, and terms of the real property available for
sale or lease." A buyer who declines representation and tours houses under a showing-only
agreement is buying a door-opening service. They are not entitled to be told whether the price is
defensible, whether the option period is too short, or whether a repair amendment is a good idea.
A represented buyer is. That is not marketing; it is the plain difference between two statutory
postures.

The point extends to valuation. A license holder may provide a broker price opinion, comparative
market analysis, or estimate of worth or sale price, subject to the required written statement and
the sponsoring-broker conditions in 22 TAC § 535.17, which Module 12 teaches. A represented buyer
in Amarillo can be handed a comparative market analysis before deciding what to offer. A buyer
being shown the same house under § 1101.562 cannot, because a CMA is an opinion regarding the real
property.

**Benefit four: undivided advocacy.** A buyer's broker who represents only buyers, and does not
hold the listing, faces no structural constraint on advocacy: the broker can press the seller's
agent for information without simultaneously owing the seller a duty of impartiality. A broker on
both sides is differently placed — an intermediary "shall act fairly and impartially"
(§ 1101.559(c)), and § 1101.651(d) bars the intermediary from telling either side that the other
will move off its stated price. Module 06 teaches both. Impartiality is a virtue in a referee and
a defect in an advocate, and buyers are entitled to understand which one they have engaged.

**Benefit five: competence in the right market and the right property type.** The competence Canon
is expressed in terms of the area "where a license holder provides services to a client" (22 TAC
§ 531.4, Module 02), and the broker's written policies must ensure each sponsored sales agent is
competent, "including having competence in the geographic market area where the sales agent
represents clients" (22 TAC § 535.2, Module 09). Both obligations are framed around clients. A
buyer who becomes a client acquires the benefit of a rule that measures the license holder's
knowledge against the market the buyer is actually shopping in.

**Benefit six: the exclusive agreement is protected against interference.** If the buyer signs an
exclusive agreement, § 1101.652(b)(22) becomes a shield: it permits discipline where a license
holder negotiates or attempts to negotiate with a person known to be "a party to an outstanding
written contract that grants exclusive agency to another broker in connection with the
transaction," and § 1101.557(b)(1) forbids a broker who represents a party to instruct another
broker to violate it (Modules 10 and 02). The practical benefit to the buyer is continuity. Note
the three conditions built into (b)(22): the contract must be **written**, must grant **exclusive**
agency, and the other license holder must have **knowledge** of it. A non-exclusive buyer agreement
does not trigger the provision, and neither does an exclusive one that nobody knows about.

**Benefit seven: an accountable, supervised counterparty and a record.** Because the agreement
runs to a broker, the buyer's counterparty is a firm subject to the broker responsibility rule, to
record retention for "at least four years from the date of closing, termination of the contract, or
end of a real estate transaction," and to the Commission's disciplinary jurisdiction; 22 TAC
§ 535.2 also makes the broker "responsible for the proper handling of trust funds placed with the
broker" (Module 09). If something goes wrong, there is a written scope of engagement to measure
conduct against — which, before 2026, frequently did not exist in residential buyer transactions.

**What buyer agency does not do.** Three honest limits, so that you do not oversell. First, it
does not make you a lawyer: 22 TAC § 537.11(b) forbids a license holder to practice law, to give
advice or opinions on the legal effect of contract forms or on the status or validity of title,
and to discourage any principal from employing an attorney (Module 04). Second, it does not make
you an appraiser: 22 TAC § 535.17 provides that a real estate license holder "may not perform an
appraisal of, or provide an opinion of value for, real property" unless licensed or certified
under Occupations Code Chapter 1103 (Module 12). Third, representation does not create disclosure
duties the Legislature has expressly withheld: § 1101.556 provides that, notwithstanding other
law, a license holder is not required to inquire about, disclose, or release information relating
to whether an occupant had or has AIDS, an HIV-related illness or an HIV infection, or whether a
death occurred on the property by natural causes, suicide, or accident unrelated to the condition
of the property (Module 03). Your buyer client may ask. The statute does not require you to
inquire or disclose.

## Unit: Written Notification of Compensation to Broker

The compensation term is the part of a buyer representation agreement that most often gets written
loosely and most often gets litigated. This unit is about why the writing is decisive, what must
be in it, and how the money is permitted to move once it is earned.

**Why the writing decides everything.** Occupations Code § 1101.806(c) bars a person from
maintaining an action in this state to recover a commission for the sale or purchase of real
estate "unless the promise or agreement on which the action is based, or a memorandum, is in
writing and signed by the party against whom the action is brought or by a person authorized by
that party to sign the document." Module 04 teaches § 1101.806, together with the general statute
of frauds in Business and Commerce Code § 26.01, whose only commission promises are for oil, gas
and mineral interests — so the writing rule for a house commission is TRELA's, not chapter 26's.
The consequence for buyer agency is direct. A broker suing a buyer for an unpaid fee needs a
writing the **buyer** signed; a memorandum signed by your own broker proves nothing. Section
1101.806(b) separately requires the claimant to have been a license holder when the act was
commenced, and § 1101.806(a) puts agreements to share compensation among license holders outside
the section altogether.

**What § 1101.563 now requires the writing to say.** For residential buyers, the compensation term
is no longer a matter of drafting preference. Section 1101.563(c)(1)(E) requires the written
agreement to state "the amount or rate of compensation the broker will receive and how this amount
will be determined." Both elements. The amount or rate is the number: $6,000, or two and a half
percent. How the amount "will be determined" is the method: percent of what, measured when,
payable on what event. A clause reading *Broker's fee shall be as agreed at the time of contract*
states neither and does not comply. And § 1101.563(c)(2) requires the agreement to "disclose in
conspicuous language that broker compensation is not set by law and is fully negotiable."
Conspicuous means it must stand out; a sentence in the same type as the surrounding paragraph, on
page four, is not conspicuous. Your broker's form will handle this. Your job is to notice when a
form does not.

**Where the money may come from, and where it may not.** Nothing in the held sources requires that
a buyer's broker be paid by the buyer, and nothing requires that the broker be paid by the seller
or the listing broker. What the sources require is that the arrangement be disclosed and
documented. Where the broker is acting as an intermediary, § 1101.559(a)(2) allows that status
only if "the written consent of the parties states the source of any expected compensation to the
broker" (Module 06). Where the license holder is receiving money from someone other than their own
client, 22 TAC § 535.148 applies, and Unit 6 works through the whole of it.

The statutory routing rules live in § 1101.651, taught in Module 09. In outline: a broker may
compensate only a license holder, or a broker licensed in another state "who does not conduct in
this state any of the negotiations for which the commission or other compensation is paid"
(§ 1101.651(a)); a sales agent may not accept compensation for a real estate transaction "from a
person other than the broker that is sponsoring the sales agent or was sponsoring the sales agent
when the sales agent earned the compensation" (§ 1101.651(b)); and a sales agent "may not pay a
commission to a person except through the broker that is sponsoring the sales agent at that time"
(§ 1101.651(c)). Carry the clause after the "or" in subsection (b), because it is the one that
saves you when you change firms: if you earned the fee while sponsored by Brazos Bend Realty and
you move before closing, Brazos Bend Realty may still pay you, your new broker may not accept it
on your behalf and pass it along, and no one else may pay you directly.

**The written-consent overlay: 22 TAC § 535.3.** This module owns the rule, and it runs in both
directions: "A sales agent may not receive a commission or other valuable consideration except
with the written consent of the sales agent's sponsoring broker or the broker who sponsored the
sales agent when the sales agent became entitled to the commission or other valuable
consideration. A sales agent may not pay a commission or other valuable consideration to another
person except with the written consent of the sales agent's sponsoring broker."

Alongside it, 22 TAC § 535.2 requires the sponsoring broker's written policies and procedures to
ensure that "Any and all compensation paid to a sponsored sales agent for acts or services subject
to the Act is paid by, through, or with the written consent of the sponsoring broker" (Module 09).
Between the statute and the two rules, the answer to the question *can the title company just cut
me a check at closing?* is no, unless it is being paid by, through, or with the written consent of
your sponsoring broker.

**Paying people who are not license holders.** Rule 22 TAC § 535.147 governs, and Module 09
teaches it. In outline: a broker or sales agent may not share a commission or fees with a person
who engages in acts for which a license is required and is not actively licensed, while an
unlicensed person may share in the income earned by a licensed business entity if that person
"engages in no acts for which a license is required and does not lead the public to believe that
the person is in the real estate brokerage business." An unlicensed spouse who is a passive owner
of the brokerage entity and does no brokerage may share in its income. An unlicensed assistant who
finds and negotiates deals may not be paid a share of the commission.

Referral fees to the public are the classic trap. Rule 22 TAC § 535.20 makes referring a
prospective buyer, seller, landlord or tenant an act requiring a licence "if the referral is made
with the expectation of receiving valuable consideration," and defines that term non-exhaustively
to include money, gifts of merchandise having a retail value greater than $50, rent bonuses and
discounts, subject to a narrow exception whose four conditions must all be met. Module 09 teaches
the rule. The point that recurs in buyer work is the last of those conditions: the moment payment
turns on whether a deal closes, the arrangement is a referral for valuable consideration and the
referrer needs a licence.

**Rebating part of your fee to the buyer.** This is lawful in Texas, with conditions. Rule 22 TAC
§ 535.147(d) provides that "A license holder may rebate or pay a portion of the license holder's
fee or commission to a party in the transaction when the sales agent has the written consent of
the sales agent's sponsoring broker and the party represented by the license holder," requires a
license holder who intends to pay a party the license holder does not represent to obtain the
represented party's written consent first, and forbids paying a commission or fee to any party "in
a manner that misleads a broker, lender, title company, or governmental agency regarding the real
estate transaction or the financial resources or obligations of the buyer." Module 09 teaches
§ 535.147. Rebates must appear where the lender can see them; concealing a rebate that changes the
buyer's effective cash to close is precisely what the rule forbids.

**Disclosing a commission or rebate you receive.** Rule 22 TAC § 535.148(a) states the governing
principle: "A license holder may not receive a commission, rebate, or fee in a transaction from a
person other than the person the license holder represents without first disclosing to the license
holder's client that the license holder intends to receive the commission, rebate, or fee, and
obtaining the consent of the license holder's client." Two acts are required and both come first:
disclosure of the intention, and consent. This module owns § 535.148 and Unit 6 works through the
whole of it. The statutory counterparts are § 1101.652(b)(13), which reaches an undisclosed
commission, rebate, or direct profit on an expenditure made for a principal, and § 1101.652(b)(8),
which reaches compensation from more than one party without the full knowledge and consent of all
parties (Module 10).

### Applied exercise: Working the compensation numbers in Pflugerville

Deshawn is a sales agent sponsored by Blackland Prairie Brokers. On February 2 the firm signs an

states that the broker's compensation is 2.5% of the sales price, states how it is determined and

at $415,000. The listing broker has agreed in writing to pay a cooperating broker $8,000.
Deshawn's split with Blackland Prairie Brokers is 70/30 in the agent's favour, and Deshawn holds
written consent from the broker to receive compensation.

1. Compute the broker's total compensation under the agreement, the amount coming from the listing
   broker, and the shortfall Ana owes under her agreement. Show the arithmetic.
2. Compute Deshawn's share and the broker's retained share of the total, and state which statute
   and which Commission rule together require the money to reach Deshawn through Blackland Prairie
   Brokers. Quote the operative sentence from each.
3. Suppose Ana disputes the shortfall and refuses to pay. Under § 1101.806(c), what must Blackland
   Prairie Brokers allege and prove to maintain an action against her, and whose signature must
   appear on the writing?
4. Deshawn wants to rebate $1,500 of his share to Ana at closing. Identify the two written

   how the rebate may be handled.
5. Ana's neighbour Rico, who holds no licence, sent Ana to Deshawn and expects a $75 gift card as
   thanks. Under 22 TAC § 535.20, is that "valuable consideration," and what does the rule say

## Unit: Buyer's Broker Disclosures

A buyer's broker makes disclosures to three different audiences at three different moments: to the
buyer, about how brokerage works and about the license holder's own interests; to the other side,
about whom the license holder represents; and to the buyer again, at closing-adjacent moments,
about title and about money the license holder is receiving from third parties. This unit
organises them by trigger, because the exam and the disciplinary file both turn on timing.

**Disclosure one: representation, at first contact with the other side.** Occupations Code
§ 1101.558(b) requires a license holder who represents a party in a proposed real estate
transaction to disclose that representation, "orally or in writing," at the time of the license
holder's first contact with another party to the transaction or with another license holder who
represents another party. Module 02 teaches § 1101.558. Three things matter to a buyer's broker.
The disclosure may be **oral**. It is owed at **first contact**, not first substantive
communication. And it runs to the other side — the seller, or the seller's license holder — not to
your own client. If you telephone a listing agent in Corpus Christi to ask about a property, you
tell them at that moment that you represent a buyer. If you call the owner of a for-sale-by-owner
house in Odessa, you tell the owner. Section 1101.652(b)(7), which reaches a license holder who
"fails to make clear to all parties to a real estate transaction the party for whom the license
holder is acting" (Module 10), sets the continuing standard behind that single moment.

**Disclosure two: the written notice at first substantive communication.** Section 1101.558(b-1)
requires a license holder, at the time of a first substantive communication with a party relating
to a proposed transaction regarding specific real property, to provide the party written notice in
at least a 10-point font describing the ways a broker can represent a party including as an
intermediary, the duties owed to a represented party and to an unrepresented one, and the
identifying and contact details of the license holder, the supervisor and the broker. Section
1101.558(b-2) hands the text and the delivery methods to the Commission, and 22 TAC § 531.20
adopts by reference the Information About Brokerage Services Notice, TREC No. IABS 1-2, sets the
four permitted delivery methods, forbids a link to the notice in an email footnote or signature
block, requires a labelled link on the homepage of each business website, and constrains how the
form may be reproduced. Both the statute and the rule are taught in full in Module 02.

For a buyer's broker the two triggers are what to watch: the communication must be **substantive**,
and it must relate to a proposed transaction **regarding specific real property**. A conversation
at a barbecue in Georgetown about whether now is a good time to buy meets neither. A conversation
about the house at a stated address, in which you begin discussing the transaction, meets both.

**The § 1101.558(c) exceptions do not open the § 1101.562(b) gate.** Section 1101.558(c) relieves
the (b-1) notice in three cases: a residential lease for less than one year where a sale is not
being considered; a meeting with a party the license holder knows is represented by another
license holder; and a communication that "occurs at a property that is held open for any
prospective buyer or tenant and the communication concerns that property." Module 02 teaches the
exceptions and their qualifiers.

Now hold them against § 1101.562(b). A broker who wants to show property without representing the
party must, before showing, either "disclose to the party as required by Section 1101.558(b) if
the broker represents the owner of the real property" or "provide to the party the written notice
described by Section 1101.558(b-1) if the broker does not represent the owner of the real
property." Section 1101.562(b) is a condition on showing, and it is not subject to the
§ 1101.558(c) exceptions, which by their terms relieve only "the notice required by Subsection
(b-1)." TREC's article reaches the same conclusion for open houses: the hosting agent from the
listing brokerage discloses that the agent represents the owner and need not deliver the IABS or
sign an agreement, while the hosting agent from outside the listing brokerage must deliver the
IABS and enter into a § 1101.563(c) agreement. If you host an open house for another firm, the
exception in § 1101.558(c)(3) will not carry you.

**Disclosure three: the Consumer Protection Notice.** Separate from the IABS, 22 TAC § 531.18
adopts by reference the Consumer Protection Notice, TREC No. CN 1-5, and requires each license
holder to display it at each place of business the broker maintains and to link it in a readily
noticeable place on the homepage of each business website; Module 08 teaches the rule. Students
confuse the two notices constantly. The IABS explains representation; the Consumer Protection
Notice tells consumers how to complain.

**Disclosure four: your own interest in the deal.** If you or someone close to you is on the other
side of the transaction, or is the buyer, disclosure is mandatory and must be in writing. Rule
22 TAC § 535.144 extends "license holder" to a license holder acting on behalf of a spouse, parent
or child, a business entity in which the license holder is more than a 10% owner, or a trust for
which the license holder acts as trustee or of which the license holder or a spouse, parent or
child is a beneficiary; requires written disclosure of that capacity "in any contract of sale or
rental agreement or in any other writing given before entering into any contract of sale or rental
agreement"; and forbids using the license holder's expertise "to the disadvantage of a person with
whom the license holder deals." Module 12 teaches § 535.144. The statutory backstops are
§ 1101.652(b)(16), which reaches acting in a dual capacity as broker and undisclosed principal,
and § 1101.652(a-1)(1), which reaches misrepresentation, dishonesty or fraud in transactions in
the name of the license holder, the license holder's spouse, or a person related within the first
degree by consanguinity (Module 10). Note the difference in scope: the rule sweeps in a
more-than-10%-owned entity and certain trusts, which the statutory language does not name.

One further own-interest rule belongs here. If, while acting as an agent, you make contact that
leads you to want the property for yourself, 22 TAC § 535.16(c) obliges you to provide a broker
price opinion or comparative market analysis when negotiating a listing or offering to purchase
the property for your own account as a result of that contact (Module 04). You may not use
information gathered as an agent to buy quietly at a price the owner does not understand.

**Disclosure five: money from third parties.** Rule 22 TAC § 535.148 is the section to know cold,
this module owns it, and it has more in it than its title suggests. Subsection (a) is the general
rule already quoted in the previous unit: no commission, rebate, or fee from someone other than
the person you represent without first disclosing your intention to your client and obtaining the
client's consent. Subsection (b) adds a second consent in a specific case: "If a party the license
holder does not represent agrees to pay a service provider in the transaction, the license holder
must also obtain the consent of that party to accept a fee, commission, or rebate from the service
provider," and it defines the term narrowly for this purpose — "As used in this section, the term
'service provider' does not include a person acting in the capacity of a real estate broker or
sales agent."

Subsection (c) forbids exclusive-dealing arrangements: a license holder may not enter into a
contract or agreement with a service provider to a real estate transaction in which the license
holder represents one or both of the parties if, under that contract, "the license holder provides
services for or on behalf of the service provider" and "the contract or agreement prohibits the
license holder from offering similar services for or on behalf of a competing service provider."
Both conditions must be present.

Subsection (d) is a flat prohibition, and it is the one that catches new license holders: "A
license holder may not pay or receive a fee or other valuable consideration to or from any other
settlement service provider for, but not limited to, the following: the referral of inspections,
lenders, mortgage brokers, or title companies; inclusion on a list of inspectors, preferred
settlement providers, or similar arrangements; or inclusion on lists of inspectors or other
settlement providers contingent on other financial agreements." The rule's definition of
"settlement service provider" is long and expressly non-exhaustive, and it includes mortgage loan
originators, mortgage brokers, lenders, title service providers, attorneys, document preparers,
credit reporting services, appraisers, inspectors, settlement agents, mortgage insurance
providers, providers of hazard, flood or other casualty insurance, homeowner's warranties or
residential service contracts, "a real estate agent or broker," and "a person who provides any
other services for which a settlement service provider requires a borrower or seller to pay." So:
no money for sending your buyer to a particular inspector, and no money for appearing on a
preferred-vendor list.

Subsection (f) requires a specific form for one recurring situation: "A license holder must use
TREC No. RSC-4, Disclosure of Relationship with Residential Service Contract Provider or
Administrator, to disclose to a party to a real estate transaction in which the license holder
represents one or both of the parties any payments received for services provided for or on behalf
of a residential service contract provider or administrator licensed under Texas Occupations Code
Chapter 1304."

And subsection (h) states three things the section does **not** prohibit, which you must carry
with the prohibitions or you will over-read them: "normal promotional or educational activity that
is not conditioned on the referral of business and that does not involve the defraying of expenses
that otherwise would be incurred"; "a payment at market rates to any person for goods actually
furnished or for services actually performed"; and "a payment pursuant to a cooperative brokerage
or referral arrangement or agreement between active licensed real estate agents and real estate
brokers." Cooperative brokerage splits and licensee-to-licensee referral fees are not what this
rule is aimed at. A title company buying your lunch to teach a class is not what this rule is
aimed at. Payment contingent on where you send your buyer is.

**Disclosure six: the abstract-or-title-policy advice, and the fee consequence of skipping it.**
Section 1101.555 requires a license holder, when an offer to purchase real estate in this state is
signed, to advise each buyer in writing that the buyer should either "have the abstract covering
the real estate that is the subject of the contract examined by an attorney chosen by the buyer"
or "be provided with or obtain a title insurance policy"; Module 12 teaches it. Note that it is
owed to **each buyer** by a **license holder**, without regard to whom the license holder
represents, and that it is triggered when the offer is signed. Section 1101.652(b)(29) makes
failure a disciplinary ground (Module 10), and § 1101.806(d) attaches a money penalty that has
nothing to do with discipline: a license holder who fails to advise a buyer as provided by
§ 1101.555 "may not receive payment of or recover any commission agreed to be paid on the sale"
(Module 04). Skip a one-sentence written advisory and you forfeit the fee.

**Putting the timing together.** For a buyer's license holder working a residential purchase, the
sequence is: at first substantive communication about specific property, deliver the IABS; before
showing any residential real property, have the § 1101.563 written agreement signed; at first
contact with the seller or the seller's license holder, disclose that you represent a buyer;
before entering into any contract of sale where you or a person described by 22 TAC § 535.144(a)
is a principal, disclose that in writing; before accepting any fee from anyone other than your
client, disclose the intention and obtain the client's consent under 22 TAC § 535.148(a); and when
the offer is signed, give the written abstract-or-title-policy advice. Every one of those has a
source, and every one of them has been the subject of discipline.

### Applied exercise: Disclosures on a Corpus Christi purchase

Priya is a sales agent sponsored by Nueces Bay Realty. On May 4 she meets Tom at a public open
house hosted by a different brokerage and they talk about that house. On May 6 Tom emails Priya
asking her to help him buy; on the call that follows they discuss a specific listing at 1412 Ocean
Drive. Priya later shows Tom three houses. One of them, in Flour Bluff, is owned by an LLC in
which Priya holds a 25% interest. Priya also receives $400 a year from a residential service
contract provider for services she performs for them.

1. Did the May 4 conversation trigger the § 1101.558(b-1) written notice? Identify the exception
   in § 1101.558(c) that bears on it and state its two limiting conditions. Would that same
   exception relieve a showing agent of the gate in § 1101.562(b)? Explain.
2. On the May 6 call, does the § 1101.558(b-1) duty attach? Identify the two conditions in the
   subsection's opening clause and apply each to these facts.
3. Before Priya shows Tom any of the three houses, what does § 1101.563(b) require, and what does
   § 1101.652(b)(34) provide if she skips it? If Tom will not sign, may she show him the houses?
4. What must Priya disclose about the Flour Bluff house, in what form, and by when? Identify the
   subsection of 22 TAC § 535.144 that brings a 25%-owned entity within the rule, and the
   subsection that fixes the timing.
5. What must Priya do about the $400 from the residential service contract provider? Name the form
   22 TAC § 535.148 requires and the subsection that requires it, and state separately what
   § 535.148(a) requires before she accepts a fee from a person other than Tom.

### Module recap

The through-line of this module is a single change of posture in Texas law. Before January 1,
2026, a Texas license holder could work with a residential buyer indefinitely without any signed
document, and the question *who represents this buyer?* was often answered only after something
went wrong. Acts 2025, 89th Leg., R.S., Ch. 1172 (S.B. 1968), Sec. 10 added two sections to The
Real Estate License Act effective January 1, 2026 — Occupations Code § 1101.562, real property
showings without representation, and § 1101.563, written agreement required — and Sec. 11 of the
same act added § 1101.652(b)(34) so that failing to enter into the required agreement is itself a
ground for discipline. The Commission's own article on the change is dated 01-07-2026 and says the
two sections "go together and should be read together." Everything else in this module hangs off
that pair.

Section 1101.563 reaches prospective **buyers** of **residential real property**, and its
definition of that term in subsection (a) has exactly three items: a single-family house; a
duplex, triplex, or quadraplex; and a unit in a multiunit residential structure in which title to
an individual unit is transferred to the owner under a condominium or cooperative system. It does
not reach tenants, commercial buyers, or land, and TREC's article says the same. The writing must
be in place before showing any residential real property to the prospective buyer or, if none will
be shown, before presenting an offer on the buyer's behalf. Subsection (c) fixes the contents: the
services to be provided; the termination date; whether the agreement is exclusive or
non-exclusive; the representation status, with the non-representation option available only where
showing under § 1101.562 is the only brokerage act being performed; the amount or rate of
compensation and how it will be determined; and, in conspicuous language, that broker compensation
is not set by law and is fully negotiable. A showing-only agreement may not be exclusive and may
not run more than **fourteen days**, and under subsection (d) a separate agreement is required if
additional brokerage acts follow the showing. Section 1101.652(b)(12), taught in Module 10,
independently requires a definite termination date "that is not subject to prior notice," excepting
only property management contracts.

Section 1101.562 is the other half. A broker may show property for sale or lease without
representing a party only if all four of its conditions hold: no agreement to represent, "either
orally or in writing"; not otherwise acting as the party's agent at the time; no opinions or
advice about the property or about real estate transactions in general; and no other act of real
estate brokerage for the party. Subsection (c) permits exactly one thing back — the broker may
**confirm** information "regarding the size, price, and terms" of the property. And subsection (b)
is a gate: before showing, disclose representation of the owner under § 1101.558(b), or, if you do
not represent the owner, deliver the § 1101.558(b-1) notice. That gate is why a showing agent
hosting an open house for another brokerage must deliver the IABS and get an agreement signed even
though § 1101.558(c)(3) would otherwise excuse the notice at an open house.

Buyer agency is created by agreement, not by conversation; § 1101.557(a), taught in Module 02,
supplies the consequence, and the agreement runs to the **broker**, not to the sales agent. Section
1101.002(8) of the Occupations Code was repealed effective January 1, 2026, and the word
"subagent" no longer appears in Chapter 1101 as held for this course; the repealed definition's
text was not held and is not taught here, and neither are the common-law elements of implied or
apparent buyer agency, which were not held either. The decision to represent a buyer is the
broker's; where the broker will represent both sides of one transaction, intermediary status is
compulsory under § 1101.561(b), and the buyer representation agreement is where § 1101.559(b) lets
the parties' written consent be captured — provided it specifies "in conspicuous bold or underlined
print" the conduct prohibited by § 1101.651(d). Module 06 teaches the intermediary machinery.

On money, this module owns 22 TAC § 535.3: a sales agent may neither receive nor pay a commission
or other valuable consideration without the written consent of the sponsoring broker — for money
coming in, either the current sponsoring broker or the broker who sponsored the agent when the
agent became entitled to it. Section 1101.563(c)(1)(E) requires the agreement itself to state the
amount or rate and how it will be determined. The rest of the compensation apparatus lives
elsewhere: § 1101.806's writing requirement for a suit to recover a commission in Module 04, and
§ 1101.651 and 22 TAC §§ 535.147 and 535.20 in Module 09.

This module also owns 22 TAC § 535.148. Disclose your intention and obtain your client's consent
before receiving any commission, rebate, or fee from a person other than the person you represent;
obtain the unrepresented paying party's consent as well in the § 535.148(b) case; do not enter the
exclusive-dealing arrangements subsection (c) forbids; never take or pay a fee to or from a
settlement service provider for a referral or for a place on a preferred list under subsection
(d); use TREC No. RSC-4 under subsection (f); and read subsection (h), which preserves normal
promotional and educational activity, payments at market rates for goods and services actually
furnished, and cooperative brokerage and licensee-to-licensee referral arrangements.
