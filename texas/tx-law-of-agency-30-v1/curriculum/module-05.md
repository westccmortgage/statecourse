# Texas Law of Agency — Module 05

## TREC Topic: Buyer Agency

**Prescribed time: 150 minutes.** This module carries the six units the Commission's LOA-1 course
approval form places under *Buyer Agency*, whose scope note is *"Disclosure of agency; listing or
buying representation procedures."*

**Learning objectives.** After completing this module you will be able to: state what a buyer
representation agreement is, whose contract it is, and every element Occupations Code
§ 1101.563(c) requires it to contain; explain how a broker decides whether to represent a buyer at
all and identify the conflicts that decision creates under §§ 1101.559, 1101.560 and 1101.561;
describe exactly how buyer agency is created under § 1101.557(a) and § 1101.563, distinguish it
from the non-representation showing authorised by § 1101.562, and recognise the conduct that turns
an unrepresented prospect into a client by accident; state the concrete benefits a represented
buyer receives, each traced to a duty imposed by § 1101.557(b), § 1101.651(d), or 22 TAC §§ 531.2,
531.4 and 535.156; work out how a buyer's broker is paid, why § 1101.806(c) makes the writing
decisive, and why 22 TAC § 535.3 and § 1101.651(b) route every sales agent's compensation through
the sponsoring broker; and carry out a buyer's broker's disclosure obligations, including the
§ 1101.558(b) representation disclosure, the § 1101.558(b-1) written notice delivered under 22 TAC
§ 531.20, the own-interest disclosure required by 22 TAC § 535.144, and the commission-or-rebate
disclosure required by 22 TAC § 535.148.

**Every legal statement in this module comes from a source that was read for this course.** Where
a statement is practice rather than law, the module says so and cites nothing for it.

## Unit: Buyer Representation Agreement

Start with the date, because this unit is different from the one your predecessors studied. Two
new sections were added to The Real Estate License Act by Acts 2025, 89th Leg., R.S., Ch. 1172
(S.B. 1968), Sec. 10, effective **January 1, 2026**: Occupations Code § 1101.562, "REAL PROPERTY
SHOWINGS WITHOUT REPRESENTATION," and Occupations Code § 1101.563, "WRITTEN AGREEMENT REQUIRED."
The Texas Real Estate Commission published an explanatory article on those changes, "What Changes
in 2026 About Buyer/Tenant Representation in Texas," dated 01-07-2026, which says of the two
sections that "While these two sections are separate, they go together and should be read
together." That article is guidance, not law; the statutory text controls, and this unit works
from the text. But the Commission's advice to read the two sections together is sound, and this
module follows it.

Before 2026, a Texas license holder could work with a buyer for weeks without any signed
agreement, and many did. That is no longer permissible for residential buyers. If you learned the
business before January 1, 2026, or if you are being trained by someone who did, you will hear
habits described as normal that the statute now forbids. Learn the current text.

**Whose agreement is it?** A buyer representation agreement is a contract between the prospective
buyer and a **broker**. It is not a contract between the buyer and you, if you are a sales agent.
Commission rule 22 TAC § 535.2 states the point flatly: "Listings and other agreements for real
estate brokerage services must be solicited and accepted in a broker's name." A sales agent is
defined by Occupations Code § 1101.002(7) as "a person who is sponsored by a licensed broker for
the purpose of performing an act described by Subdivision (1)" — that is, a person who performs
brokerage acts under a broker's sponsorship, not on the agent's own account. So when you sit at a
kitchen table in Cedar Park and ask a buyer to sign, the buyer is engaging your broker, your
broker's name goes on the agreement, and your broker is the one who will be answerable for its
performance. You sign as the broker's associate. Students routinely get this wrong and speak of
*my buyer agreement*. The client is the brokerage's client. That matters when you change
sponsorship, when a complaint is filed, and when compensation is claimed.

**What the agreement does.** In substance, a buyer representation agreement is an employment
contract that engages the broker to perform acts of real estate brokerage for the buyer, defines
what those acts are, sets how long the engagement lasts, states whether the buyer may engage other
brokers at the same time, and fixes what the broker will be paid and by whom. Occupations Code
§ 1101.557(a) supplies the legal consequence of signing one: "A broker who represents a party in a
real estate transaction or who lists real estate for sale under an exclusive agreement for a party
is that party's agent." Once the broker represents the buyer, the broker is the buyer's agent, and
the whole apparatus of agency duties you have been studying attaches.

**The § 1101.563 mandate — the centre of this module.** Section 1101.563 is short, and every
clause of it does work. Read it in order.

Subsection (a) defines the term that limits the section's reach. "In this section, 'residential
real property' means" three things, and only three: "a single-family house"; "a duplex, triplex,
or quadraplex"; and "a unit in a multiunit residential structure in which title to an individual
unit is transferred to the owner of the unit under a condominium or cooperative system." That is
the whole definition. Notice what is not in it. Unimproved land is not in it — the ten-acre tract
outside Bastrop your buyer wants for a future homesite is not a single-family house. A fifty-unit
apartment building held in one ownership is not in it, because title to individual units is not
transferred to unit owners under a condominium or cooperative system. An office suite, a retail
strip centre, a warehouse in Pharr, a working ranch in Coleman County — none of these fall inside
the definition. TREC's article puts the same limit in plain words: "The written agreement
requirement does not apply to commercial purchasers or residential/commercial tenants."

That last clause deserves emphasis because it is the most common misreading. **Section 1101.563
reaches prospective buyers only.** Its operative sentence in subsection (b) speaks of "a
prospective buyer of residential real property," and every subsequent reference is to "the
prospective buyer." A tenant is not a buyer. A person looking to lease a house in Lubbock for a
year is outside § 1101.563 entirely, no matter how residential the house is. Do not tell a leasing
prospect that state law requires them to sign a representation agreement before you can show them
a rental. That is not what the statute says.

Subsection (b) states the mandate and the moment it attaches: "A license holder who performs any
act of real estate brokerage for a prospective buyer of residential real property must enter into
a written agreement with the prospective buyer before" one of two events. The first is "showing
any residential real property to the prospective buyer." The second is "if no residential real
property will be shown, presenting an offer to purchase residential real property on behalf of the
prospective buyer."

Take those triggers apart. The duty falls on "a license holder," which by § 1101.002(4) means "a
broker or sales agent licensed under this chapter" — so it binds you personally as a sales agent,
not merely your broker. The condition that puts you inside the section is that you are performing
"any act of real estate brokerage" for a prospective residential buyer, and the meaning of that
phrase is broad. Section 1101.004(a) provides that "A person is engaged in real estate brokerage
if the person, with the expectation of receiving valuable consideration, directly or indirectly
performs or offers, attempts, or agrees to perform for another person any act described by Section
1101.002(1), as a part of a transaction or as an entire transaction." And the acts described by
§ 1101.002(1)(A) include, among others, one who "aids or offers or attempts to aid in locating or
obtaining real estate for purchase or lease" and one who "negotiates or attempts to negotiate the
listing, sale, exchange, purchase, or lease of real estate." Helping a buyer find a house is an
act of real estate brokerage. That is the whole business.

The two "before" clauses fix the outer limit. You must have the written agreement in hand **before
you show any residential real property** to that prospective buyer. Not before you write the
offer; not before you open escrow; before the showing. And in the case where you are never going
to show anything — the buyer has already found the house on their own, or is buying from a
relative, or is buying at a distance — the agreement must be in place before you present the offer
to purchase on the buyer's behalf.

Notice the exact wording of the first trigger: "showing any residential real property." Not
*showing the residential real property*. If you have a written agreement with a Frisco buyer that
covers only a specific address, and you then take them to look at a second house, you have shown
residential real property to a prospective buyer, and you need a written agreement that covers it.
Write the agreement to cover the engagement, not one door.

Subsection (c) tells you what must be in the writing. The agreement "must" state each of the
following, and must make one disclosure:

- "the services to be provided by the license holder";
- "the termination date of the agreement";
- "whether the agreement is exclusive or non-exclusive";
- "as applicable," that the license holder either "represents the prospective buyer as the buyer's
  agent" or "does not represent the prospective buyer as the buyer's agent if the only act of real
  estate brokerage being performed is showing real property under Section 1101.562";
- "the amount or rate of compensation the broker will receive and how this amount will be
  determined."

And the agreement must "disclose in conspicuous language that broker compensation is not set by
law and is fully negotiable."

Work through those. The **services** clause forces you to say what you are actually agreeing to
do. TREC's article notes that representation agreements "also can limit the services a broker
agrees to provide to prospective buyers—such as limiting their services to showing only—but they
cannot waive the minimum duties owed to clients which are provided by existing TREC rules and
law." That last qualification is the one to remember: a limited-service agreement narrows the
scope of the engagement; it does not shrink the fiduciary standard within whatever scope you
accepted. You may agree to do less. You may not agree to do it worse.

The **termination date** requirement is not new in substance, and it has independent teeth.
Occupations Code § 1101.652(b)(12) has long made it a ground for suspension or revocation if a
license holder "fails to specify a definite termination date that is not subject to prior notice
in a contract, other than a contract to perform property management services, in which the license
holder agrees to perform services for which a license is required under this chapter." Read that
qualifier carefully, because it is the part students drop. The date must be **definite**, and it
must be, in the statute's words, "not subject to prior notice" — meaning an agreement that runs
until one side gives thirty days' notice does not satisfy the section. And there is a carve-out:
contracts to perform property management services are excepted. A buyer representation agreement
is not a property management contract, so it needs a definite termination date, and now
§ 1101.563(c)(1)(B) requires the date to be stated in the writing as well.

The **exclusive or non-exclusive** clause tells the buyer whether they may engage another broker
at the same time. Under an exclusive agreement, the buyer commits to work through your brokerage
for the term; under a non-exclusive one, they do not. Neither is unlawful; the statute requires
only that the agreement say which it is. There is a hard limit on exclusivity for one narrow kind
of agreement, which we come to in a moment.

The **representation status** clause is the hinge between § 1101.563 and § 1101.562. There are two
boxes, and they are not both available in every case. If you are representing the buyer, the
agreement says you represent the prospective buyer as the buyer's agent. The other box — "does not
represent the prospective buyer as the buyer's agent" — is available only "if the only act of real
estate brokerage being performed is showing real property under Section 1101.562." That is a
narrow gate. You may check the non-representation box only when showing is genuinely the only
brokerage act you are performing for that person.

The **compensation** clause requires "the amount or rate of compensation the broker will receive
and how this amount will be determined." Both halves matter: the number or the rate, and the
method. *To be determined* is not compliance. *Three percent of the sales price* states a rate
and, read with a definition of sales price, states the method. We return to compensation at length
in Unit 5.

The **negotiability disclosure** must be in "conspicuous language" and must say "that broker
compensation is not set by law and is fully negotiable." Texas has required a comparable statement
in listing forms adopted by the Commission for years — § 1101.155(c)(1) requires that a listing
contract form adopted by the commission include "a provision informing the parties to the contract
that real estate commissions are negotiable" — but § 1101.155 governs forms *the commission
adopts*, and the Commission has not adopted a buyer representation form. Section 1101.563(c)(2)
reaches the agreement itself regardless of who drafted it. Put the sentence in, make it
conspicuous, and do not bury it in a paragraph of recitals.

**Showing-only agreements.** Subsections (d) and (e) govern the special case of an agreement
entered into solely so that you can show property to someone you do not represent. Subsection (d)
provides: "A license holder who enters into a written agreement with a prospective buyer for the
sole purpose of showing real property under Section 1101.562 shall enter into a separate agreement
with the prospective buyer if additional real estate brokerage acts are to be provided after
showing the real property." So the showing-only agreement does not silently upgrade itself. The
moment you begin doing more than showing — advising, negotiating, drafting an offer — you need a
separate agreement, and that separate agreement is the one where the representation box gets
checked differently.

Subsection (e) puts two hard limits on the showing-only agreement. It "may not" "be an exclusive
agreement with the license holder," and it may not "state a termination date more than fourteen
days from the date the agreement is entered into." Note that the statute writes **fourteen days**
in words, not as a numeral, and note that fourteen days is a ceiling, not a default: nothing stops
a shorter term, and for a single Saturday of showings in Round Rock a one-day or three-day term is
perfectly lawful and often cleaner. What you may not do is sign a person to a ninety-day exclusive
showing-only arrangement. The Legislature plainly did not want the non-representation posture used
to tie consumers up.

**The consequence of getting it wrong.** The same 2025 act that created § 1101.563 added a
matching disciplinary ground. Occupations Code § 1101.652(b)(34), added by Acts 2025, 89th Leg.,
R.S., Ch. 1172 (S.B. 1968), Sec. 11, effective January 1, 2026, lets the Commission suspend or
revoke a license or take other disciplinary action if the license holder, while engaged in real
estate brokerage, "fails to enter into a written agreement with a prospective buyer as required by
Section 1101.563." The writing requirement is not a best practice or a trade-association rule. It
is a licensing obligation with its own enumerated sanction.

**Which form do you use?** The Commission does not supply one. TREC's contracts page states: "TREC
does not promulgate listing or buyer representation agreements, property management contracts,
forms for commercial property, or residential leases (other than temporary residential leases used
in connection with a sale). Contact your attorney or a real estate trade association for such
forms." That is TREC's own published guidance, and it aligns with the structure of 22 TAC
§ 537.11, whose mandatory-form requirement applies "When negotiating contracts binding the sale,
exchange, option, or lease of any interest in real property" — which a buyer representation
agreement is not. Practically, you will use a form your broker supplies, drawn either by an
attorney or by a trade association. What you may not do is draft one yourself: 22 TAC § 537.11(b)
provides that a license holder may not "practice law," may not "directly or indirectly offer, give
or attempt to give legal advice," and may not "draft or recommend language to be included in a
contract form defining or affecting the rights, obligations, or remedies of the principals of a
real estate transaction, including escalation, appraisal, or contingency clauses." Use your
broker's form. If the buyer wants it changed in a way that alters legal rights, the buyer needs a
lawyer.

**Keep the paper.** Commission rule 22 TAC § 535.2 requires that, except for records destroyed by
an "Act of God" such as a natural disaster or fire not intentionally caused by the broker, the
broker maintain a list of records "in a format that is readily available to the Commission for at
least four years from the date of closing, termination of the contract, or end of a real estate
transaction." That list expressly includes "commission agreements such as listing agreements,
buyer representation agreements, or other written agreements relied upon to claim compensation,"
and separately "disclosures" and "substantive communications with parties to the transaction."
Four years, from the later of closing, contract termination, or the end of the transaction. A
buyer representation agreement that expired unused still starts a four-year clock on the date it
terminated.

## Unit: Deciding to Represent the Buyer

Everything in the preceding unit assumed a decision had already been made: that your brokerage
would represent this buyer. That decision is not automatic, and treating it as automatic is how
brokerages get into trouble. This unit is about the judgment that precedes the signature.

**The decision belongs to the broker.** A sales agent does not decide, on the agent's own
authority, whom the firm will represent. Commission rule 22 TAC § 535.2 requires that "Listings
and other agreements for real estate brokerage services must be solicited and accepted in a
broker's name," and it makes the broker responsible in a way that should shape how you think about
your own discretion: "A broker is required to notify a sponsored sales agent in writing of the
scope of the sales agent's authorized activities under the Act. Unless such scope is limited or
revoked in writing, a broker is responsible for the authorized acts of the broker's sales agents,
but the broker is not required to supervise the sales agents directly. If a broker permits a
sponsored sales agent to conduct activities beyond the scope explicitly authorized by the broker,
those are acts for which the broker is responsible." The same rule requires a sponsoring broker to
maintain, on a current basis, written policies and procedures to ensure that "Each sponsored sales
agent is advised of the scope of the sales agent's authorized activities subject to the Act and is
competent to conduct such activities, including having competence in the geographic market area
where the sales agent represents clients." So the first question in deciding whether to represent
a buyer is a firm question: does our policy allow this engagement, and am I authorised to accept
it?

**Competence is a threshold, not an aspiration.** Rule 22 TAC § 531.4 makes competency an
obligation: "It is the obligation of a license holder to be knowledgeable and competent as a real
estate brokerage practitioner." It then specifies four requirements — the license holder must "be
informed on local market issues and conditions affecting real estate in the geographic area where
a license holder provides services to a client"; must "be informed on national, state, and local
issues and developments in the real estate industry"; must "exercise judgment and skill in the
performance of brokerage activities"; and must "be educated in the characteristics involved in the
specific type of real estate being brokered for others." Read alongside § 1101.652(b)(1), which
permits discipline where a license holder, while engaged in real estate brokerage, "acts
negligently or incompetently," these are not aspirational sentences.

Apply them concretely. You are a San Antonio sales agent in your first year. A buyer asks you to
represent them in purchasing a 300-acre place near Junction with a producing well and an existing
grazing lease. You know suburban resale. You do not know surface-and-mineral severance,
agricultural valuation, or livestock leases. Rule 531.4 requires competence in "the specific type
of real estate being brokered for others" and in "the geographic area where a license holder
provides services to a client." The right answer is not to sign the agreement and learn on the
buyer's money. It is either to decline, or to bring in someone competent and disclose the
arrangement, with your broker's authorisation. Note that 22 TAC § 535.2 also requires the broker's
written policies and procedures to ensure that "At a minimum, when a sales agent performs a type
of real estate brokerage activity for the first three times, the broker must require that the
sales agent receive coaching and assistance from an experienced license holder competent for that
activity." Your first three of anything are supervised. That is a rule, not a courtesy.

**The conflict the decision creates: your own listings.** The sharpest conflict arrives the moment
your buyer wants to see a property your brokerage has listed. If your broker represents the seller
under an exclusive listing, § 1101.557(a) makes your broker the seller's agent. If your broker
also agrees to represent the buyer, the broker is on both sides. Texas resolves that with the
intermediary statute, and the resolution is mandatory, not optional: § 1101.561(b) provides that
"A broker must agree to act as an intermediary under this subchapter if the broker agrees to
represent in a transaction" both "a buyer or tenant" and "a seller or landlord." The word is
*must*. A Texas broker who represents both sides of the same transaction is an intermediary; there
is no third posture available under this subchapter.

Acting as intermediary has its own written-consent prerequisites. Section 1101.559(a) permits a
broker to act as an intermediary "if" the broker "obtains written consent from each party for the
broker to act as an intermediary in the transaction" and "the written consent of the parties
states the source of any expected compensation to the broker." Subsection (b) tells you how that
consent is normally captured: "A written listing agreement to represent a seller or landlord or a
written agreement to represent a buyer or tenant that authorizes a broker to act as an
intermediary in a real estate transaction is sufficient to establish written consent of the party
to the transaction if the written agreement specifies in conspicuous bold or underlined print the
conduct that is prohibited under Section 1101.651(d)." So the intermediary authorisation, and the
recital of prohibited conduct in conspicuous bold or underlined print, belong in the buyer
representation agreement at signing — long before anyone knows whether an in-house property will
come up. A brokerage that has not built this into its buyer agreement will be scrambling on a
Saturday afternoon, and the consent must be obtained before the broker acts as an intermediary,
not after.

The conduct § 1101.651(d) prohibits is worth reading before you decide to take on an engagement
that may become an intermediary transaction, because it is a real narrowing of what you can do for
the buyer. A broker acting as an intermediary, and any broker or sales agent appointed under
§ 1101.560, "may not" "disclose to the buyer or tenant that the seller or landlord will accept a
price less than the asking price, unless otherwise instructed in a separate writing by the seller
or landlord"; may not "disclose to the seller or landlord that the buyer or tenant will pay a
price greater than the price submitted in a written offer to the seller or landlord, unless
otherwise instructed in a separate writing by the buyer or tenant"; may not "treat a party to a
transaction dishonestly"; and may not "violate this chapter." The confidentiality prohibition is
stated with its own exceptions, and you must carry them with the rule: the intermediary may not
"disclose any confidential information or any information a party specifically instructs the
broker or sales agent in writing not to disclose, unless" "the broker or sales agent is otherwise
instructed in a separate writing by the respective party," or "the broker or sales agent is
required to disclose the information by this chapter or a court order," or "the information
materially relates to the condition of the property." That third exception is the one that saves
you: material information about the condition of the property is not protectable as confidential,
and you are not silenced about a defect because a party asked you to be.

Section 1101.559(c) adds that "An intermediary shall act fairly and impartially," and that
appointing associated license holders under § 1101.560 to communicate with, carry out instructions
of, and provide opinions and advice to the parties to whom they are appointed "is a fair and
impartial act." Section 1101.560 permits the broker to appoint one associated license holder to
communicate with and carry out the instructions of one party and another to do the same for the
other party, but only if the parties' written consent "authorizes the broker to make the
appointment" and "the broker provides written notice of the appointment to all parties involved in
the real estate transaction." A license holder so appointed "may provide opinions and advice
during negotiations to the party to whom the license holder is appointed." And § 1101.561(a) tells
you which duty set governs when they collide: "The duties of a license holder acting as an
intermediary under this subchapter supersede the duties of a license holder established under any
other law, including common law."

So the honest way to present the decision to a buyer in Sugar Land is this: if you engage our
brokerage and you later want to buy one of our listings, our broker becomes an intermediary, our
broker's advocacy narrows to what § 1101.651(d) permits, and — if the broker makes appointments
under § 1101.560 and both parties consented in writing to appointments — you may still receive
opinions and advice from the associate appointed to you. A buyer who does not want that outcome
should be told plainly that a brokerage with no listings, or a firm that does not take in-house
transactions, is an alternative.

**Other conflicts to weigh before you sign.** Two buyers you represent may want the same house in
the same week. Nothing in the held sources forbids representing both, but rule 22 TAC § 531.2
requires "that the primary duty of the license holder is to represent the interests of the client,
and the license holder's position, in this respect, should be clear to all parties concerned in a
real estate transaction," and requires "that the license holder place no personal interest above
that of the client." Two clients bidding against each other puts you in a position where advancing
one client's interest damages another's. Decide your policy in advance, disclose it in the
agreement, and do not improvise it at midnight.

A second conflict is your own pocketbook. Section 1101.652(b)(8) permits discipline where a
license holder "receives compensation from more than one party to a real estate transaction
without the full knowledge and consent of all parties to the transaction." Section 1101.652(b)(13)
reaches a license holder who "accepts, receives, or charges an undisclosed commission, rebate, or
direct profit on an expenditure made for a principal." And § 1101.652(b)(16) reaches one who "acts
in a dual capacity as broker and undisclosed principal in a real estate transaction." If you or
your spouse might end up bidding on the same property, if you take referral money from a lender,
if you have any economic interest in the outcome beyond the commission stated in the agreement,
that belongs on the table before the buyer signs, not after. Unit 6 works through the disclosure
mechanics.

**Declining is a legitimate outcome.** Nothing in Chapter 1101 obliges a broker to represent
anyone. Rule 22 TAC § 531.19 does constrain *how* you decline: no license holder may "inquire
about, respond to or facilitate inquiries about, or make a disclosure of an owner, previous or
current occupant, potential purchaser, lessor, or potential lessee of real property which
indicates or is intended to indicate any preference, limitation, or discrimination based on" race,
colour, religion, sex, national origin, ancestry, familial status, or disability — and the rule
specifies that for its purposes "disability includes AIDS, HIV-related illnesses, or HIV infection
as defined by the Centers for Disease Control of the United States Public Health Service." You may
decline an engagement because it is outside your competence, outside your market, outside your
firm's policy, or because the buyer will not agree to terms. You may never decline, or steer, on a
protected basis.

### Applied exercise: The in-house listing in Sugar Land

Marisol is a sales agent sponsored by Brazos Bend Realty. On March 3 she signs a buyer
representation agreement, in Brazos Bend Realty's name, with the Okonkwos, who are looking for a
house in Fort Bend County up to $525,000. The agreement is exclusive, runs to June 30, and states
that Brazos Bend Realty will be compensated 3% of the sales price. On March 20 the Okonkwos ask to
see a Sugar Land listing that Brazos Bend Realty holds under an exclusive listing agreement with
the seller, listed at $499,000. The seller's agent is Dev, another Brazos Bend sales agent.

1. Under § 1101.557(a), what is Brazos Bend Realty's relationship to the seller, and what is it to
   the Okonkwos? Cite the sentence.
2. If Brazos Bend Realty agrees to represent both, what does § 1101.561(b) require the broker to
   do, and is that a choice?
3. Under § 1101.559(a), what two things must be true before the broker may act as intermediary? If
   the March 3 buyer agreement is silent about intermediary status, can § 1101.559(b) supply the
   consent? Explain what subsection (b) requires the agreement to contain.
4. Suppose consent is in order and the broker appoints Dev to the seller and Marisol to the
   Okonkwos under § 1101.560. Marisol learns from a prior conversation that the Okonkwos would pay
   $510,000. The Okonkwos submit a written offer of $487,000. May Marisol tell Dev the buyers
   would go higher? Identify the prohibition and any exception that could apply.
5. During the option period Marisol learns that the house's foundation was repaired twice and the
   seller instructed Dev in writing not to discuss it. Does the confidentiality prohibition in
   § 1101.651(d)(3) cover that instruction? Quote the exception that decides it.

## Unit: The Creation of Buyer Agency

Buyer agency is created by agreement. That sentence is the whole unit in compressed form, and the
rest of this unit is about the ways students and license holders get it wrong in both directions —
believing they have an agency relationship when they do not, and, far more dangerously, believing
they do not have one when they do.

**The statutory rule.** Occupations Code § 1101.557(a) states the creation rule for both sides of
a transaction: "A broker who represents a party in a real estate transaction or who lists real
estate for sale under an exclusive agreement for a party is that party's agent." For sellers, the
statute names a document — an exclusive listing agreement. For buyers, the statute uses the
broader phrase "represents a party." Since January 1, 2026, § 1101.563 has supplied the missing
formality on the buy side for residential transactions: a license holder performing any act of
real estate brokerage for a prospective buyer of residential real property "must enter into a
written agreement with the prospective buyer" before showing any residential real property or, if
none will be shown, before presenting an offer on the buyer's behalf. The intended result is that
residential buyer agency now begins with a signed document and a date, not with a conversation
whose legal effect nobody noticed at the time.

**Why the change happened.** The Commission's article says the reason directly: "The intent behind
this change was to help protect consumers by requiring use of written agreements that clearly
outline the obligations the license holder and prospective buyer owe to each other." The consumer
problem the Legislature addressed was not that buyers were being represented badly. It was that
buyers frequently could not say whether they were being represented at all, and neither could the
license holder, and the two of them often held different views of the same relationship. A written
agreement forces the question to be answered in advance.

**The mistake to name: agency by conduct.** Here is the failure mode. A license holder in Denton
meets a prospect at an open house. Over the next three weeks they exchange forty text messages.
The license holder pulls comparable sales, tells the prospect that a listing is *overpriced by
about fifteen thousand*, suggests offering $12,000 under list with a shortened option period,
calls the listing agent to ask what the seller wants on closing date, and drafts an offer. No
agreement was ever signed. Ask the license holder whom they represent and they will say *nobody —
they never signed anything*.

That answer is wrong twice over. First, it is wrong as a matter of licensing law after January 1,
2026: § 1101.652(b)(34) makes it a disciplinary ground to fail "to enter into a written agreement
with a prospective buyer as required by Section 1101.563," so the absence of the writing is itself
the violation, not a defence to one. Second, it is wrong because the conduct described is exactly
the conduct § 1101.562 says a broker showing property without representation may not engage in.
That section permits the non-representation posture only where the broker "does not provide to the
party opinions or advice regarding the real property or real estate transactions in general" and
"does not perform any other act of real estate brokerage for the party." Pricing opinions are
opinions about the real property. Offer strategy is advice about real estate transactions in
general. Drafting and presenting an offer is another act of real estate brokerage. The license
holder in Denton was doing the work of a buyer's agent and simply had not papered it.

Section 1101.652(b)(7) is the older provision that has always punished this confusion: the
Commission may discipline a license holder who, while engaged in real estate brokerage, "fails to
make clear to all parties to a real estate transaction the party for whom the license holder is
acting." Ambiguity about whom you represent is itself a violation. Whether or not a court would
find an agency relationship arose by conduct in a given case is a question of common law, and
**the Texas common-law elements of implied or apparent agency were not held for this course and
are not taught here.** What is held, and what is sufficient for your licence, is this: TRELA
requires you to make the relationship clear, and since 2026 it requires the residential buyer
relationship to be written.

**The lawful alternative: § 1101.562 showings without representation.** The 2025 legislation did
not force every license holder into a representation relationship with every prospect. It created
an express, narrow, non-representation posture — and then fenced it. Section 1101.562(a) provides
that "A broker may show real property available for sale or lease to a party without representing
the party if the broker" satisfies four conditions, stated conjunctively. The broker must have
"not agreed with the party, either orally or in writing, to represent the party." The broker must
be "not otherwise acting as the party's agent at the time of showing the real property." The
broker must not "provide to the party opinions or advice regarding the real property or real
estate transactions in general." And the broker must not "perform any other act of real estate
brokerage for the party."

Read condition (1) again: "either orally or in writing." An oral agreement to represent someone
destroys the non-representation posture even though it would not satisfy § 1101.563. This is the
trap. You cannot tell a prospect in a Katy driveway *sure, I'll look out for you on this one* and
then claim, at showing time, that you had not agreed to represent them. You had. The statute
counts oral agreements for the purpose of taking the non-representation posture away from you,
while requiring a writing for the purpose of establishing representation. Those two rules point
the same direction: say nothing to a prospect about representing them that you are not prepared to
reduce to writing.

Condition (3) is the operative daily constraint. No opinions, no advice — not about the property
in front of you, and not about "real estate transactions in general." That second phrase is
broader than students expect. *The market's been slowing, you could probably get this for less.*
*You should get a survey.* *Do the inspection before you spend money on the appraisal.* Every one
of those is advice about real estate transactions in general.

Subsection (c) tells you exactly how much you may say, and it is a short list: "Notwithstanding
Subsections (a)(3) and (4), a broker showing real property under this section may confirm
information to a party regarding the size, price, and terms of the real property available for
sale or lease." Size, price, terms. You may confirm that the house is 2,140 square feet, that it
is listed at $389,900, and that the seller wants a fifteen-day close with a leaseback. That is the
extent of it. The verb matters too: **confirm**, not evaluate. Confirming that the price is
$389,900 is permitted. Saying the price is fair is not.

**The disclosure gate in § 1101.562(b).** Before a broker may show property under this section,
the broker must do one of two things, "as applicable." If the broker represents the owner, the
broker must "disclose to the party as required by Section 1101.558(b) if the broker represents the
owner of the real property." If the broker does not represent the owner, the broker must "provide
to the party the written notice described by Section 1101.558(b-1)" — that is, the Information
About Brokerage Services notice. This is a free-standing precondition to showing, and it matters
because § 1101.558(c) contains an exception that would otherwise appear to swallow it:
§ 1101.558(c)(3) says a license holder is not required to provide the (b-1) notice if "the
communication occurs at a property that is held open for any prospective buyer or tenant and the
communication concerns that property."

Hold both provisions at once. Section 1101.558(c)(3) relieves the § 1101.558(b-1) duty for
open-house conversations. Section 1101.562(b)(2) imposes its own duty, as a condition of showing
property without representation, on a broker who does not represent the owner. TREC's article
reads the interaction the same way, and works it through the open-house case. Where the agent
hosting the open house "is a member of a listing broker's office (e.g., a sales agent at the
brokerage holding the listing), the agent does not need to provide the IABS or enter into a
written agreement with prospective buyers," but "will need to disclose he or she represents the
owner either orally or in writing as required by 1101.558(b)." Where the agent hosting the open
house "is not a member of the listing broker's office and does not represent the seller (e.g., a
showing agent)," the agent must "Provide the IABS" and "Enter into a written agreement as
described by 1101.563(c) with prospective buyers (e.g., visitors of the open house)." The article
adds a sentence that surprises people: "This is true even when a prospective buyer has a written
representation agreement with another broker." The statute keys the duty to what *you* are doing,
not to what some other broker has already signed.

The article also states the practical timing: "By law, the IABS must be provided and agreement
entered into before the showing. In practical terms, what this means for an open house is that
ideally, the agreement should be presented and entered into before the buyer enters the property,
but at the very least before the buyer starts viewing the property (e.g., the agent greets the
prospective buyer near the door)." And it answers the question every new license holder asks next:
"If a buyer refuses to sign the agreement, then the agent cannot show the property, meaning a
consumer cannot view the open house. License holders can face disciplinary action if they fail to
enter into a written agreement as required by state law under 1101.563." That consequence follows
from § 1101.563(b) plus § 1101.652(b)(34), and it is the single most operationally significant
thing in this module. A showing agent hosting an open house in a residential subdivision who
cannot get a signature cannot let the visitor tour the house.

**Keeping the posture honest.** If you are going to use § 1101.562, use it deliberately. In
practice — this is practice, not law — that means deciding before the appointment which posture
you are in, having the correct paper with you, and having a short, rehearsed sentence for the
moment the prospect asks the question you are not allowed to answer. *I am not able to give you an
opinion on that, because I am showing you this house without representing you. If you would like
advice, we can talk about a representation agreement.* Then stop. The most common way license
holders blow the non-representation posture is by answering one friendly question in the hallway.

And understand the trapdoor: the posture is not permanent. Section 1101.563(d) requires a separate
agreement "if additional real estate brokerage acts are to be provided after showing the real
property." If you show a house in New Braunfels on Saturday under a fourteen-day non-exclusive
showing agreement and the buyer calls Monday wanting to make an offer, you do not amend the
showing agreement in your head. You enter into a new agreement that states the services and the
representation status accurately.

**Subagency.** One structural change deserves a note, because older materials discuss it at
length. Section 1101.002(8) of the Occupations Code now reads, in the version held for this
course, "Repealed by Acts 2025, 89th Leg., R.S., Ch. 1172 (S.B. 1968), Sec. 13(1), eff. January 1,
2026." The word "subagent" does not appear anywhere in Chapter 1101 as held. TREC's article
describes the change this way: "Two references to subagency were removed from TRELA. This means
the circumstances in which subagency could arise in your real estate practice are limited.
However, subagency has not been eliminated generally as a legal concept. If you are in a situation
where it seems subagency may be involved, contact your broker and/or attorney." The text of the
repealed definition **was not held** for this course and is not taught here. What you should take
away is narrow and safe: TRELA no longer defines subagency, and a Texas license holder working
with a buyer today operates in one of the two statutory postures this module has described —
representation under a written agreement, or a § 1101.562 showing without representation.

### Statute reading exercise: § 1101.562 and the four conditions

Open Occupations Code § 1101.562 and 22 TAC § 531.20, and read both.

1. Subsection (a) lists four conditions. Are they joined by "and" or by "or," and what practical
   difference does that make to a broker who satisfies three of them?
2. Subsection (a)(1) uses a phrase about the form an agreement to represent may take. Quote it.
   Why does it matter that the phrase is not limited to written agreements, given that § 1101.563
   requires a writing?
3. Subsection (c) begins with "Notwithstanding Subsections (a)(3) and (4)." What three categories
   of information does it permit, and what verb does the statute use to describe what the broker

4. Under § 1101.562(b), a broker who does *not* represent the owner must provide a particular
   notice before showing. Which notice, identified by section? Now read 22 TAC § 531.20 and list

5. Does § 1101.562 say anything about whether the property being shown is residential? Compare
   with § 1101.563(a). What does the difference mean for a broker showing a warehouse in Laredo?

## Unit: Benefits of Buyer-Agency Relationships

This unit is easy to teach badly. The temptation is to recite advantages the way a recruiting
brochure would. Resist it. The correct way to state the benefits of buyer agency is to identify
the specific duties the law imposes on a broker who represents a buyer, and then to describe what
the buyer does *not* get when no such relationship exists. Every benefit below is a duty somewhere
in the held sources, and where a supposed benefit turns out to be something the buyer would
receive anyway, this unit says so.

**Benefit one: someone is legally required to put the buyer's interests first.** Commission rule
22 TAC § 531.2 provides that "A license holder, while acting as an agent for another, is a
fiduciary," and that the fiduciary relationship demands "that the primary duty of the license
holder is to represent the interests of the client, and the license holder's position, in this
respect, should be clear to all parties concerned in a real estate transaction; that, however, the
license holder, in performing duties to the client, shall treat other parties to a transaction
fairly." It further demands "that the license holder be faithful and observant to trust placed in
the license holder, and be scrupulous and meticulous in performing the license holder's
functions," and "that the license holder place no personal interest above that of the client."
Rule 22 TAC § 535.156 repeats the point in operational terms: "The license holder must put the
interest of the license holder's principal above the license holder's own interest. A license
holder must deal honestly and fairly with all parties; however, the license holder represents only
the principal and owes a duty of fidelity to such principal."

Read the qualifier in both rules rather than the headline. The license holder must treat everyone
fairly and honestly; the license holder owes *fidelity* to one side. A buyer without a
representation agreement is entitled to fair and honest treatment from every license holder in the
transaction. What that buyer does not have is anyone whose duty of loyalty runs to them. That is
the difference, and it is worth stating in exactly those terms to a prospect who asks why they
should sign anything.

**Benefit two: affirmative disclosure of everything that bears on the buyer's decision.** Rule
22 TAC § 535.156 imposes a duty that is broader than most people assume: "A license holder shall
convey to the principal all known information which would affect the principal's decision on
whether or not to make, accept or reject offers; however, if the principal has agreed in writing
that offers are not to be submitted after the principal has entered into a contract to buy, sell,
rent, or lease a property, the license holder shall have no duty to submit offers to the principal
after the principal has accepted an offer." Carry the "however" clause with the rule; it is the
one recognised limit, and it operates only where the principal has agreed **in writing**. The same
rule adds that "A license holder has an affirmative duty to keep the principal informed at all
times of significant information applicable to the transaction or transactions in which the
license holder is acting as agent for the principal."

The statute reinforces this for any broker who represents a party. Section 1101.557(b)(2) provides
that a broker who represents a party "must inform the party if the broker receives material
information related to a transaction to list, buy, sell, or lease the party's real estate,
including the receipt of an offer by the broker." Section 1101.557(b)(3) sets a floor beneath
everything else: the broker "shall, at a minimum, answer the party's questions and present any
offer to or from the party." That is a statutory minimum. A limited-service buyer agreement can
shrink the list of services in many directions, but it cannot go below answering the client's
questions and presenting offers.

Now compare the unrepresented buyer. A license holder still owes them something. Section
1101.652(b)(3) permits discipline where a license holder "makes a material misrepresentation to a
potential buyer concerning a significant defect, including a latent structural defect, known to
the license holder that would be a significant factor to a reasonable and prudent buyer in making
a decision to purchase real property," and § 1101.652(b)(4) reaches a license holder who "fails to
disclose to a potential buyer a defect described by Subdivision (3) that is known to the license
holder." Notice the phrase: *potential buyer*, not client. Rule 22 TAC § 535.156 likewise imposes
on every license holder "a duty to convey accurate information to members of the public with whom
the license holder deals." So known material defects must be disclosed to a buyer whether or not
you represent them. The benefit of representation is not that you will be told about the
foundation. It is that you will be told about everything else — the seller's timeline, the second
offer that fell through, the fact that the house was withdrawn and relisted, the pattern in the
comparable sales — because significant information about the transaction must be conveyed to a
principal and need not be volunteered to a stranger.

**Benefit three: opinions and advice at all.** This is the benefit the 2026 statute made concrete.
A broker showing property under § 1101.562 may not "provide to the party opinions or advice
regarding the real property or real estate transactions in general," and may only "confirm
information to a party regarding the size, price, and terms of the real property available for
sale or lease." A buyer who declines representation and tours houses under a showing-only
agreement is buying a door-opening service. They are not entitled to be told whether the price is
defensible, whether the option period is too short, or whether a repair amendment is a good idea.
A represented buyer is. That is not marketing; it is the plain difference between two statutory
postures.

The point extends to valuation. Rule 22 TAC § 535.17 permits a license holder to provide "a broker
price opinion, comparative market analysis, or estimated worth or sale price under the Act" so
long as the license holder also provides a written statement containing the required language —
"This represents an estimated sale price for this property. It is not the same as the opinion of
value in an appraisal developed by a licensed appraiser under the Uniform Standards of
Professional Appraisal Practice" — reproduced verbatim in at least 12-point font and made part of
the written opinion. The same rule provides that "A sales agent may prepare, sign, and present a
broker price opinion, comparative market analysis, or estimate of worth or sale price for the
sales agent's sponsoring broker, but the sales agent must submit the broker price opinion,
comparative market analysis, or estimate of worth or sale price in the broker's name and the
broker is responsible for it." A represented buyer in Amarillo can be handed a comparative market
analysis before deciding what to offer. A buyer being shown the same house under § 1101.562
cannot, because a CMA is an opinion regarding the real property.

**Benefit four: confidentiality with a defined limit.** The intermediary provisions tell you, by
contrast, what full representation preserves. When a broker becomes an intermediary,
§ 1101.651(d)(1) forbids the broker to "disclose to the buyer or tenant that the seller or
landlord will accept a price less than the asking price, unless otherwise instructed in a separate
writing by the seller or landlord," and § 1101.651(d)(2) forbids disclosing "to the seller or
landlord that the buyer or tenant will pay a price greater than the price submitted in a written
offer to the seller or landlord, unless otherwise instructed in a separate writing by the buyer or
tenant." Those prohibitions exist because, absent them, a broker on both sides could leak each
side's reservation price to the other. A buyer whose broker represents only buyers, and does not
hold the listing, faces no such structural constraint on advocacy: the broker can press the
seller's agent for information without simultaneously owing the seller a duty of impartiality.
Section 1101.559(c) requires an intermediary to "act fairly and impartially." A buyer's agent who
is not an intermediary is not required to be impartial as between the buyer and the seller.
Impartiality is a virtue in a referee and a defect in an advocate, and buyers are entitled to
understand which one they have engaged.

**Benefit five: competence in the right market and the right property type.** Rule 22 TAC § 531.4
requires a license holder to "be informed on local market issues and conditions affecting real
estate in the geographic area where a license holder provides services to a client" and to "be
educated in the characteristics involved in the specific type of real estate being brokered for
others." Read the first clause closely: it is expressed in terms of the area "where a license
holder provides services to a client" — a client, not a prospect. Competence obligations under
this rule are framed around clients. So is the broker's supervisory obligation under 22 TAC
§ 535.2, which requires written policies ensuring each sponsored sales agent "is competent to
conduct such activities, including having competence in the geographic market area where the sales
agent represents clients." A buyer who becomes a client acquires the benefit of a rule that
measures the license holder's knowledge against the market the buyer is actually shopping in.

**Benefit six: the exclusive agreement is protected against interference.** If the buyer signs an
exclusive agreement, § 1101.652(b)(22) becomes a shield. It permits discipline where a license
holder "negotiates or attempts to negotiate the sale, exchange, or lease of real property with an
owner, landlord, buyer, or tenant with knowledge that that person is a party to an outstanding
written contract that grants exclusive agency to another broker in connection with the
transaction." Section 1101.557(b)(1) adds that a broker who represents a party "may not instruct
another broker to directly or indirectly violate Section 1101.652(b)(22)." The practical benefit
to the buyer is continuity: once the exclusive agreement exists, other license holders who know
about it are not supposed to work around your broker. Note the two conditions built into (b)(22) —
the contract must be **written** and must grant **exclusive** agency, and the other license holder
must have **knowledge** of it. A non-exclusive buyer agreement does not trigger the provision, and
neither does an exclusive one that nobody knows about.

**Benefit seven: an accountable, insured, supervised counterparty and a record.** Because the
agreement runs to a broker (22 TAC § 535.2: "Listings and other agreements for real estate
brokerage services must be solicited and accepted in a broker's name"), the buyer's counterparty
is a firm subject to the broker responsibility rule, to record retention for "at least four years
from the date of closing, termination of the contract, or end of a real estate transaction," and
to the Commission's disciplinary jurisdiction. If money changes hands, 22 TAC § 535.2 makes the
broker "responsible for the proper handling of trust funds placed with the broker," and requires
compliance with § 535.146 relating to maintaining trust money. If something goes wrong, there is a
written scope of engagement to measure conduct against — which, before 2026, frequently did not
exist in residential buyer transactions.

**What buyer agency does not do.** Three honest limits, so that you do not oversell. First, it
does not make you a lawyer: 22 TAC § 537.11(b) forbids a license holder to "practice law," to
"give advice or opinions as to the legal effect of any contract forms or other such instruments
which may affect the title to real estate," or to "give opinions concerning the status or validity
of title to real estate," and forbids any attempt to "prevent or in any manner whatsoever
discourage any principal to a real estate transaction from employing an attorney." Second, it does
not make you an appraiser: 22 TAC § 535.17 provides that "A real estate license holder may not
perform an appraisal of, or provide an opinion of value for, real property unless the license
holder is licensed or certified under Texas Occupations Code, Chapter 1103." Third, representation
does not create disclosure duties the Legislature has expressly withheld. Section 1101.556
provides that "Notwithstanding other law, a license holder is not required to inquire about,
disclose, or release information relating to whether" "a previous or current occupant of real
property had, may have had, has, or may have AIDS, an HIV-related illness, or an HIV infection as
defined by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention of the United States Public Health
Service," or whether "a death occurred on a property by natural causes, suicide, or accident
unrelated to the condition of the property." Your buyer client may ask. The statute does not
require you to inquire or disclose, and 22 TAC § 531.19 independently forbids disclosures that
indicate or are intended to indicate a preference, limitation, or discrimination on protected
grounds, a category the rule expressly extends to disability including "AIDS, HIV-related
illnesses, or HIV infection."

## Unit: Written Notification of Compensation to Broker

The compensation term is the part of a buyer representation agreement that most often gets written
loosely and most often gets litigated. This unit is about why the writing is decisive, what must
be in it, and how the money is permitted to move once it is earned.

**Why the writing decides everything.** Occupations Code § 1101.806(c) provides: "A person may not
maintain an action in this state to recover a commission for the sale or purchase of real estate
unless the promise or agreement on which the action is based, or a memorandum, is in writing and
signed by the party against whom the action is brought or by a person authorized by that party to
sign the document." Read every clause. The bar is on *maintaining an action* — it is a rule about
suing, not a rule that makes an oral promise morally acceptable. The writing may be "the promise
or agreement on which the action is based, or a memorandum" of it. It must be "signed by the party
against whom the action is brought or by a person authorized by that party to sign the document."
So a broker suing a buyer for an unpaid fee needs a writing the **buyer** signed. A broker suing a
seller needs a writing the seller signed. A memorandum signed by your own broker proves nothing
under this subsection.

Two further limits sit in the same section. Section 1101.806(b) provides that "A person may not
maintain an action to collect compensation for an act as a broker or sales agent that is performed
in this state unless the person alleges and proves that the person was" either "a license holder
at the time the act was commenced" or "an attorney licensed in any state." The licence must have
been in place when the act commenced; getting licensed afterwards does not repair it. And
§ 1101.806(a) carves two things out of the whole section: it "does not" "apply to an agreement to
share compensation among license holders" or "limit a cause of action among brokers for
interference with business relationships." That first carve-out is important and frequently
misquoted: a co-brokerage or referral split between license holders is not governed by
§ 1101.806's writing requirement. Which is not a reason to leave it oral. It is only a reason to
know precisely what the section covers.

Students often assume the general statute of frauds supplies this rule. It does not, for ordinary
real estate commissions. Business and Commerce Code § 26.01(b) lists the promises and agreements
that are unenforceable without a signed writing, and the only commission promise on that list is
at subdivision (7): "a promise or agreement to pay a commission for the sale or purchase of" "an
oil or gas mining lease," "an oil or gas royalty," "minerals," or "a mineral interest." A
commission on the sale of a house in Temple is not on the § 26.01(b) list. The writing requirement
that matters to you lives in TRELA at § 1101.806(c). Note also that § 26.01(b)(4) does require a
signed writing for "a contract for the sale of real estate" and § 26.01(b)(5) for "a lease of real
estate for a term longer than one year" — those govern the buyer's contract with the seller, not
the broker's fee.

**What § 1101.563 now requires the writing to say.** For residential buyers, the compensation term
is no longer a matter of drafting preference. Section 1101.563(c)(1)(E) requires the written
agreement to state "the amount or rate of compensation the broker will receive and how this amount
will be determined." Both elements. The amount or rate is the number: $6,000, or two and a half
percent. How the amount "will be determined" is the method: percent of what, measured when,
payable on what event. A clause reading *Broker's fee shall be as agreed at the time of contract*
states neither and does not comply. And § 1101.563(c)(2) requires the agreement to "disclose in
conspicuous language that broker compensation is not set by law and is fully negotiable."
Conspicuous means it must stand out; a sentence in the same type as the surrounding paragraph, on
page four, is not conspicuous. Your broker's form will handle this. Your job is to notice when a
form does not.

**Where the money may come from, and where it may not.** Nothing in the held sources requires that
a buyer's broker be paid by the buyer, and nothing requires that the broker be paid by the seller
or the listing broker. What the sources require is that the arrangement be disclosed and
documented. Where the broker is acting as an intermediary, the requirement is explicit:
§ 1101.559(a)(2) allows intermediary status only if "the written consent of the parties states the
source of any expected compensation to the broker." Where the license holder is receiving money
from someone other than their own client, 22 TAC § 535.148 applies, and we take it up in the next
unit and again below.

The structural rules on payment are three, and you must be able to state them cold.

First, § 1101.651(a): "A licensed broker may not pay a commission to or otherwise compensate a
person directly or indirectly for performing an act of a broker unless the person is" "a license
holder" or "a real estate broker licensed in another state who does not conduct in this state any
of the negotiations for which the commission or other compensation is paid." Carry that second
qualifier. An Oklahoma broker may be paid a referral fee by a Texas broker only if the Oklahoma
broker conducted none of the Texas negotiations. Section 1101.652(b)(11) supplies the disciplinary
counterpart: it reaches a license holder who "pays a commission or a fee to or divides a
commission or a fee with a person other than a license holder or a real estate broker or sales
agent licensed in another state for compensation for services as a real estate agent."

Second, § 1101.651(b): "A sales agent may not accept compensation for a real estate transaction
from a person other than the broker that is sponsoring the sales agent or was sponsoring the sales
agent when the sales agent earned the compensation." The clause after "or" is the one people
forget, and it is the one that saves you when you change firms. If you earned the fee while
sponsored by Brazos Bend Realty and you move to another brokerage before closing, Brazos Bend
Realty — the broker who was sponsoring you when you earned it — may still pay you. Your new broker
may not accept it on your behalf and pass it along, and no one else may pay you directly.

Third, § 1101.651(c): "A sales agent may not pay a commission to a person except through the
broker that is sponsoring the sales agent at that time." Money out, like money in, runs through
the sponsoring broker.

Commission rule 22 TAC § 535.3 adds a written-consent overlay to both directions: "A sales agent
may not receive a commission or other valuable consideration except with the written consent of
the sales agent's sponsoring broker or the broker who sponsored the sales agent when the sales
agent became entitled to the commission or other valuable consideration. A sales agent may not pay
a commission or other valuable consideration to another person except with the written consent of
the sales agent's sponsoring broker." And 22 TAC § 535.2 requires the sponsoring broker's written
policies and procedures to ensure that "Any and all compensation paid to a sponsored sales agent
for acts or services subject to the Act is paid by, through, or with the written consent of the
sponsoring broker." Between the statute and the two rules, the answer to the question *can the
title company just cut me a check at closing?* is no, unless it is being paid by, through, or with
the written consent of your sponsoring broker.

**Paying people who are not license holders.** Rule 22 TAC § 535.147 governs. Subsection (a):
"Except as otherwise provided by the Act or Commission rules, a broker or sales agent may not
share a commission or fees with any person who engages in acts for which a license is required and
is not actively licensed as a broker or sales agent." Note the condition — the prohibition is
aimed at sharing with a person who *engages in acts for which a license is required*. Subsection
(b) makes the corollary explicit: "An unlicensed person may share in the income earned by a
business entity licensed as a broker or exempted from the licensing requirements under the Act if
the person engages in no acts for which a license is required and does not lead the public to
believe that the person is in the real estate brokerage business." An unlicensed spouse who is a
passive owner of the brokerage entity and does no brokerage may share in its income. An unlicensed
assistant who finds and negotiates deals may not be paid a share of the commission.

Referral fees to the public are the classic trap, and 22 TAC § 535.20 draws the line with a
number. "Referring a prospective buyer, seller, landlord, or tenant to another person in
connection with a proposed real estate transaction is an act requiring the person making the
referral to be licensed if the referral is made with the expectation of receiving valuable
consideration." The rule then says that for its purposes "the term 'valuable consideration'
includes but is not limited to" "money," "gifts of merchandise having a retail value greater than
$50," "rent bonuses," and "discounts." So a $50 threshold applies to merchandise, and the list is
expressly non-exhaustive. The rule does contain a narrow exception, and all four of its conditions
must be met: the person "is engaged in the business of selling goods or services to the public";
"sells goods or services to a real estate license holder who intends to offer the goods or
services as an inducement to potential buyers, sellers, landlords or tenants"; "After selling the
goods or services to the real estate license holder, the person refers the person's customers to
the real estate license holder"; and "The payment to the person for the goods or services is not
contingent upon the consummation of a real estate transaction by the person's customers." That
last condition is the heart of it. The moment payment turns on whether a deal closes, the
arrangement is a referral for valuable consideration and the referrer needs a licence.

**Rebating part of your fee to the buyer.** This is lawful in Texas, with conditions. Rule 22 TAC
§ 535.147(d) provides that "A license holder may rebate or pay a portion of the license holder's
fee or commission to a party in the transaction when the sales agent has the written consent of
the sales agent's sponsoring broker and the party represented by the license holder." It then
imposes a limit that exists to protect lenders and title companies: "A commission or fee may not
be paid to any party to the transaction in a manner that misleads a broker, lender, title company,
or governmental agency regarding the real estate transaction or the financial resources or
obligations of the buyer." And it adds a further consent requirement for the cross-side case: "A
license holder who intends to pay a portion of the license holder's fee or commission to a party
the license holder does not represent must obtain the written consent of the party represented by
the license holder before making the payment." Rebates must also appear where the lender can see
them; concealing a rebate that changes the buyer's effective cash to close is precisely what the
rule forbids.

**Disclosing a commission or rebate you receive.** Rule 22 TAC § 535.148(a) states the governing
principle: "A license holder may not receive a commission, rebate, or fee in a transaction from a
person other than the person the license holder represents without first disclosing to the license
holder's client that the license holder intends to receive the commission, rebate, or fee, and
obtaining the consent of the license holder's client." Two acts are required and both come first:
disclosure of the intention, and consent. The statutory counterpart is § 1101.652(b)(13), which
reaches a license holder who "accepts, receives, or charges an undisclosed commission, rebate, or
direct profit on an expenditure made for a principal," and § 1101.652(b)(8), which reaches one who
"receives compensation from more than one party to a real estate transaction without the full
knowledge and consent of all parties to the transaction." Note the difference in whose consent is
required: § 535.148(a) requires the client's consent; § 1101.652(b)(8) requires the full knowledge
and consent of *all* parties where you are being compensated by more than one of them.

## Unit: Buyer's Broker Disclosures

A buyer's broker makes disclosures to three different audiences at three different moments: to the
buyer, about how brokerage works and about the license holder's own interests; to the other side,
about whom the license holder represents; and to the buyer again, at closing-adjacent moments,
about title and about money the license holder is receiving from third parties. This unit
organises them by trigger, because the exam and the disciplinary file both turn on timing.

**Disclosure one: representation, at first contact with the other side.** Occupations Code
§ 1101.558(b) provides that "A license holder who represents a party in a proposed real estate
transaction shall disclose, orally or in writing, that representation at the time of the license
holder's first contact with" either "another party to the transaction" or "another license holder
who represents another party to the transaction." Note three things. The disclosure may be
**oral**. It is owed at **first contact**, not first substantive communication. And it runs to the
other side — the seller, or the seller's license holder — not to your own client. If you represent
a buyer and you telephone a listing agent in Corpus Christi to ask about a property, you tell them
at that moment that you represent a buyer. If you call the owner of a for-sale-by-owner house in
Odessa, you tell the owner.

The related prohibition is § 1101.652(b)(7), which reaches a license holder who "fails to make
clear to all parties to a real estate transaction the party for whom the license holder is
acting." Section 1101.558(b) fixes a moment; § 1101.652(b)(7) sets a continuing standard. Both are
satisfied by the same habit: say whom you represent, early, out loud, and again in writing.

**Disclosure two: the written notice at first substantive communication.** Section 1101.558(b-1)
provides that "At the time of a license holder's first substantive communication with a party
relating to a proposed transaction regarding specific real property, the license holder shall
provide to the party written notice in at least a 10-point font" that does four things: it
"describes the ways in which a broker can represent a party to a real estate transaction,
including as an intermediary"; it "describes the basic duties and obligations a broker has to a
party to a real estate transaction that the broker represents"; it "describes the basic
obligations a broker has to a party to a real estate transaction that the broker does not
represent"; and it "provides the name, license number, and contact information for the license
holder and the license holder's supervisor and broker, if applicable."

Two triggers must both be present: the communication must be **substantive**, and it must relate
to a proposed transaction **regarding specific real property**. A conversation at a barbecue in
Georgetown about whether now is a good time to buy meets neither. A conversation about the house
at a stated address, in which you begin discussing the transaction, meets both. Section
1101.558(b-2) hands the details to the Commission: "The commission by rule shall prescribe the
text of the notice required under Subsections (b-1)(1) and (2) and establish the methods by which
a license holder shall provide the notice." That rule is 22 TAC § 531.20, which "adopts by
reference the Information About Brokerage Services Notice, TREC No. IABS 1-2 (IABS Notice)." The
notice itself is published by the Commission; this course holds the rule that adopts it, not the
form.

Rule 531.20 imposes two distinct obligations. The first is passive and continuous: each license
holder shall provide "a link to a completed IABS Notice in a readily noticeable place on the
homepage of each business website," labelled either "Texas Real Estate Commission Information
About Brokerage Services", in at least 10 point font; or "TREC Information About Brokerage
Services", in at least 12 point font. A "business website" for this purpose is a website that "is
accessible to the public," that "contains information about a license holder's real estate
brokerage services," and whose "content of the website is controlled by the license holder." For a
social media platform, the rule permits the link to be located on "the account holder profile" or
on "a separate page or website through a direct link from the social media platform or account
holder profile."

The second obligation is the transactional one: each license holder shall provide "the completed
IABS Notice at the first substantive communication as required under §1101.558, Texas Occupations
Code." The rule then lists the permitted delivery methods, and the list is closed: "by personal
delivery by the license holder"; "by first class mail or overnight common carrier delivery
service"; "in the body of an email"; or "as an attachment to an email, or a link within the body
of an email, with a specific reference to the IABS Notice in the body of the email." And it
forbids one placement outright: "The link to a completed IABS Notice may not be in a footnote or
signature block in an email." That prohibition exists because a permanent link in your signature
block would let you claim delivery on every email you ever sent without the recipient ever
noticing. If you use a link, the body of the email must specifically refer to the IABS Notice.

Finally, the rule constrains how the form may be reproduced: "License holders may reproduce the
IABS Notice published by the Commission, provided that the text of the IABS Notice is copied
verbatim and the spacing, borders and placement of text on the page must appear to be identical to
that in the published version of the IABS Notice, except that the Broker Contact Information
section may be prefilled." Prefill the broker contact box. Do not redesign the form to match your
branding.

**The three exceptions in § 1101.558(c) — and their limit.** A license holder "is not required to
provide the notice required by Subsection (b-1) if" any of the following applies: "the proposed
transaction is for a residential lease for less than one year and a sale is not being considered";
"the license holder meets with a party who the license holder knows is represented by another
license holder"; or "the communication occurs at a property that is held open for any prospective
buyer or tenant and the communication concerns that property."

Read each with its qualifiers. The lease exception requires **both** that the term be less than
one year **and** that a sale is not being considered; a nine-month lease with a purchase option is
outside it. The represented-party exception turns on **knowledge** — you must know the party is
represented, not merely suspect it. The open-house exception is confined to communications that
occur **at** the held-open property and that **concern that property**; a conversation at an open
house about a different listing across town is not covered.

Now hold this against § 1101.562(b). A broker who wants to show property without representing the
party must, before showing, either "disclose to the party as required by Section 1101.558(b) if
the broker represents the owner of the real property" or "provide to the party the written notice
described by Section 1101.558(b-1) if the broker does not represent the owner of the real
property." Section 1101.562(b) is a condition on showing, and it is not subject to the
§ 1101.558(c) exceptions, which by their terms relieve only "the notice required by Subsection
(b-1)." TREC's article reaches the same conclusion for open houses: the hosting agent from the
listing brokerage discloses that the agent represents the owner and need not deliver the IABS or
sign an agreement, while the hosting agent from outside the listing brokerage must deliver the
IABS and enter into a § 1101.563(c) agreement. If you host an open house for another firm, the
exception in § 1101.558(c)(3) will not carry you.

**Disclosure three: the Consumer Protection Notice.** Separate from the IABS, 22 TAC § 531.18
adopts by reference "the Consumer Protection Notice, TREC No. CN 1-5," and requires each license
holder to provide it by "displaying it in a readily noticeable location in each place of business
the broker maintains" and by "providing a link to it in a readily noticeable place on the homepage
of each business website," labelled "Texas Real Estate Commission Consumer Protection Notice", in
at least 10 point font, or "TREC Consumer Protection Notice", in at least 12 point font. Two
notices, two rules, two labels, and different fonts for the long and short forms of each label.
Students confuse them constantly. The IABS explains representation; the Consumer Protection Notice
tells consumers how to complain.

**Disclosure four: your own interest in the deal.** If you or someone close to you is on the other
side of the transaction, or is the buyer, disclosure is mandatory and must be in writing. Rule
22 TAC § 535.144(a) extends the reach of § 1101.652(a-1)(1) by providing that "license holder"
includes a license holder acting on behalf of "the license holder's spouse, parent or child," "a
business entity in which the license holder is more than a 10% owner," or "a trust for which the
license holder acts as trustee or of which the license holder or the license holder's spouse,
parent or child is a beneficiary." Subsection (b) then states the duty: "A license holder engaging
in a real estate transaction on his or her own behalf or in a capacity described by subsection
(a), is obligated to disclose in writing that he or she is a licensed real estate broker or sales
agent acting on his or her own behalf or in a capacity described by subsection (a) in any contract
of sale or rental agreement or in any other writing given before entering into any contract of
sale or rental agreement." And subsection (c) adds a substantive constraint that is easy to
overlook: "A license holder acting on his or her own behalf or in a capacity described by
subsection (a) shall not use the license holder's expertise to the disadvantage of a person with
whom the license holder deals."

The statutory backstops are § 1101.652(b)(16), which reaches a license holder who "acts in a dual
capacity as broker and undisclosed principal in a real estate transaction," and
§ 1101.652(a-1)(1), which reaches one who "engages in misrepresentation, dishonesty, or fraud when
selling, buying, trading, or leasing real property in the name of" the license holder, the license
holder's spouse, or "a person related to the license holder within the first degree by
consanguinity." Note the difference in scope between the rule and the statute: § 535.144(a) sweeps
in a more-than-10%-owned entity and certain trusts, which the statutory language does not name.

One further own-interest rule belongs here. If, while acting as an agent, you make contact that
leads you to want the property for yourself, 22 TAC § 535.16(c) provides that "A real estate
license holder is obligated to provide a broker price opinion or comparative market analysis on a
property when negotiating a listing or offering to purchase the property for the license holder's
own account as a result of contact made while acting as a real estate agent." You may not use
information gathered as an agent to buy quietly at a price the owner does not understand.

**Disclosure five: money from third parties.** Rule 22 TAC § 535.148 is the section to know cold,
and it has more in it than its title suggests. Subsection (a) is the general rule already quoted
in the previous unit: no commission, rebate, or fee from someone other than the person you
represent without first disclosing your intention to your client and obtaining the client's
consent. Subsection (b) adds a second consent in a specific case: "If a party the license holder
does not represent agrees to pay a service provider in the transaction, the license holder must
also obtain the consent of that party to accept a fee, commission, or rebate from the service
provider," and it defines the term narrowly for this purpose — "As used in this section, the term
'service provider' does not include a person acting in the capacity of a real estate broker or
sales agent."

Subsection (c) forbids exclusive-dealing arrangements: a license holder may not enter into a
contract or agreement with a service provider to a real estate transaction in which the license
holder represents one or both of the parties if, under that contract, "the license holder provides
services for or on behalf of the service provider" and "the contract or agreement prohibits the
license holder from offering similar services for or on behalf of a competing service provider."
Both conditions must be present.

Subsection (d) is a flat prohibition, and it is the one that catches new license holders: "A
license holder may not pay or receive a fee or other valuable consideration to or from any other
settlement service provider for, but not limited to, the following: the referral of inspections,
lenders, mortgage brokers, or title companies; inclusion on a list of inspectors, preferred
settlement providers, or similar arrangements; or inclusion on lists of inspectors or other
settlement providers contingent on other financial agreements." The rule's definition of
"settlement service provider" is long and expressly non-exhaustive, and it includes mortgage loan
originators, mortgage brokers, lenders, title service providers, attorneys, document preparers,
credit reporting services, appraisers, inspectors, settlement agents, mortgage insurance
providers, providers of hazard, flood or other casualty insurance, homeowner's warranties or
residential service contracts, "a real estate agent or broker," and "a person who provides any
other services for which a settlement service provider requires a borrower or seller to pay." So:
no money for sending your buyer to a particular inspector, and no money for appearing on a
preferred-vendor list.

Subsection (f) requires a specific form for one recurring situation: "A license holder must use
TREC No. RSC-4, Disclosure of Relationship with Residential Service Contract Provider or
Administrator, to disclose to a party to a real estate transaction in which the license holder
represents one or both of the parties any payments received for services provided for or on behalf
of a residential service contract provider or administrator licensed under Texas Occupations Code
Chapter 1304."

And subsection (h) states three things the section does **not** prohibit, which you must carry
with the prohibitions or you will over-read them: "normal promotional or educational activity that
is not conditioned on the referral of business and that does not involve the defraying of expenses
that otherwise would be incurred"; "a payment at market rates to any person for goods actually
furnished or for services actually performed"; and "a payment pursuant to a cooperative brokerage
or referral arrangement or agreement between active licensed real estate agents and real estate
brokers." Cooperative brokerage splits and licensee-to-licensee referral fees are not what this
rule is aimed at. A title company buying your lunch to teach a class is not what this rule is
aimed at. Payment contingent on where you send your buyer is.

**Disclosure six: the abstract-or-title-policy advice, and the fee consequence of skipping it.**
Section 1101.555 provides: "When an offer to purchase real estate in this state is signed, a
license holder shall advise each buyer, in writing, that the buyer should" either "have the
abstract covering the real estate that is the subject of the contract examined by an attorney
chosen by the buyer" or "be provided with or obtain a title insurance policy." Note that it is
owed to **each buyer** by a **license holder**, without regard to whom the license holder
represents, and note that it is triggered when the offer is signed. Section 1101.652(b)(29) makes
failure a disciplinary ground where the license holder "fails to advise a buyer in writing before
the closing of a real estate transaction" of the same two options. And § 1101.806(d) attaches a
money penalty that has nothing to do with discipline: "A license holder who fails to advise a
buyer as provided by Section 1101.555 may not receive payment of or recover any commission agreed
to be paid on the sale." Skip a one-sentence written advisory and you forfeit the fee.

**Putting the timing together.** For a buyer's license holder working a residential purchase, the
sequence is: at first substantive communication about specific property, deliver the IABS by one
of the four § 531.20 methods; before showing any residential real property, have the § 1101.563
written agreement signed; at first contact with the seller or the seller's license holder,
disclose that you represent a buyer; before entering into any contract of sale where you or a
person described by 22 TAC § 535.144(a) is a principal, disclose that in writing; before accepting
any fee from anyone other than your client, disclose the intention and obtain the client's
consent; and when the offer is signed, give the written abstract-or-title-policy advice. Every one
of those has a source, and every one of them has been the subject of discipline.

### Module recap

The through-line of this module is a single change of posture in Texas law. Before January 1,
2026, a Texas license holder could work with a residential buyer indefinitely without any signed
document, and the question *who represents this buyer?* was often answered only after something
went wrong. Acts 2025, 89th Leg., R.S., Ch. 1172 (S.B. 1968), Sec. 10 added two sections to The
Real Estate License Act effective January 1, 2026 — Occupations Code § 1101.562, real property
showings without representation, and § 1101.563, written agreement required — and Sec. 11 of the
same act added § 1101.652(b)(34) so that failing to enter into the required agreement is itself a
ground for discipline. The Commission's own article on the change is dated 01-07-2026 and says the
two sections "go together and should be read together." Everything else in this module hangs off
that pair.

Section 1101.563 reaches prospective **buyers** of **residential real property**, and its
definition of that term in subsection (a) has exactly three items: a single-family house; a
duplex, triplex, or quadraplex; and a unit in a multiunit residential structure in which title to
an individual unit is transferred to the owner under a condominium or cooperative system. It does
not reach tenants, commercial buyers, or land, and TREC's article says the same. The writing must
be in place before showing any residential real property to the prospective buyer or, if none will
be shown, before presenting an offer on the buyer's behalf. Subsection (c) fixes the contents: the
services to be provided; the termination date; whether the agreement is exclusive or
non-exclusive; the representation status, with the non-representation option available only where
showing under § 1101.562 is the only brokerage act being performed; the amount or rate of
compensation and how it will be determined; and, in conspicuous language, that broker compensation
is not set by law and is fully negotiable. A showing-only agreement may not be exclusive and may
not run more than **fourteen days**, and under subsection (d) a separate agreement is required if
additional brokerage acts follow the showing. Section 1101.652(b)(12) independently requires a
definite termination date "that is not subject to prior notice," excepting only property
management contracts.

Section 1101.562 is the other half. A broker may show property for sale or lease without
representing a party only if all four of its conditions hold: no agreement to represent, "either
orally or in writing"; not otherwise acting as the party's agent at the time; no opinions or
advice about the property or about real estate transactions in general; and no other act of real
estate brokerage for the party. Subsection (c) permits exactly one thing back — the broker may
**confirm** information "regarding the size, price, and terms" of the property. And subsection (b)
is a gate: before showing, disclose representation of the owner under § 1101.558(b), or, if you do
not represent the owner, deliver the § 1101.558(b-1) notice. That gate is why a showing agent
hosting an open house for another brokerage must deliver the IABS and get an agreement signed even
though § 1101.558(c)(3) would otherwise excuse the notice at an open house.

Buyer agency is created by agreement, not by conversation, and § 1101.557(a) supplies the
consequence: a broker who represents a party, or lists under an exclusive agreement, "is that
party's agent." The agreement runs to the **broker**, because 22 TAC § 535.2 requires that
listings and other agreements for brokerage services "be solicited and accepted in a broker's
name," and the broker must keep it, along with disclosures and substantive communications, for "at
least four years from the date of closing, termination of the contract, or end of a real estate
transaction." Section 1101.002(8) of the Occupations Code was repealed effective January 1, 2026,
and the word "subagent" no longer appears in Chapter 1101 as held for this course; the repealed
definition's text was not held and is not taught here.

The decision to represent a buyer is the broker's, is constrained by competence under 22 TAC
§ 531.4 and by the broker's written policies under 22 TAC § 535.2, and creates conflicts that must
be handled in advance. If the broker will represent both a buyer and a seller in one transaction,
§ 1101.561(b) says the broker **must** agree to act as an intermediary; § 1101.559(a) requires
written consent from each party and requires that consent to state the source of any expected
compensation; § 1101.559(b) lets the buyer representation agreement itself supply the consent if
it authorises intermediary status and specifies "in conspicuous bold or underlined print" the
conduct prohibited by § 1101.651(d); and § 1101.560 governs appointments. Remember the exception
inside § 1101.651(d)(3): confidential information may still be disclosed where "the information
materially relates to the condition of the property."

The benefits of representation are duties, not slogans. Fidelity under 22 TAC § 531.2 and
§ 535.156 means the client's interest comes before the license holder's while everyone else is
treated fairly and honestly. Section 1101.557(b)(3) sets an irreducible floor — the broker "shall,
at a minimum, answer the party's questions and present any offer to or from the party" — and
§ 1101.557(b)(2) requires the broker to pass on material information. Opinions, advice, and a
comparative market analysis under 22 TAC § 535.17 are available to a client and unavailable to a
person being shown property under § 1101.562. An exclusive written agreement engages the
protection of § 1101.652(b)(22) against other license holders who know about it. But
representation does not make you a lawyer (22 TAC § 537.11(b)) or an appraiser (22 TAC
§ 535.17(a)), and it does not create disclosure duties § 1101.556 expressly withholds.

On money: § 1101.806(c) bars an action to recover a real estate commission unless the promise,
agreement, or a memorandum is "in writing and signed by the party against whom the action is
brought or by a person authorized by that party to sign the document"; § 1101.806(b) requires the
claimant to have been a license holder when the act commenced, or an attorney; and § 1101.806(a)
excludes agreements to share compensation among license holders from the section. The general
statute of frauds at Business and Commerce Code § 26.01(b) lists commission promises only for oil,
gas, and mineral interests, so the writing rule for a house commission is TRELA's, not chapter
26's. A broker may compensate only a license holder or an out-of-state broker who conducted none
of the Texas negotiations (§ 1101.651(a)); a sales agent may accept compensation only from the
sponsoring broker or the broker who was sponsoring when the compensation was earned
(§ 1101.651(b)) and may pay a commission only through the sponsoring broker (§ 1101.651(c)); and
22 TAC § 535.3 layers written consent onto both directions. Rebates to a party are permitted under
22 TAC § 535.147(d) with the sponsoring broker's and the represented party's written consent, and
may never be arranged so as to mislead a broker, lender, title company, or governmental agency
about the transaction or the buyer's financial resources or obligations.

Finally, the disclosure calendar. Deliver the IABS at first substantive communication about
specific real property, in at least a 10-point font, by one of the four methods in 22 TAC
§ 531.20, never by a link in a footnote or signature block. Disclose your representation orally or
in writing at first contact with the other party or that party's license holder under
§ 1101.558(b). Get the § 1101.563 agreement signed before you show. Disclose in writing, under
22 TAC § 535.144(b), any transaction in which you or a spouse, parent, child, a
more-than-10%-owned entity, or a covered trust is a principal, and do it in the contract or in a
writing given before it. Disclose and obtain your client's consent before receiving any
commission, rebate, or fee from anyone other than your client (22 TAC § 535.148(a);
§ 1101.652(b)(13)), use TREC No. RSC-4 for residential service contract payments, and never take a
fee for referring an inspector, lender, mortgage broker, or title company. And when the offer is
signed, give each buyer the written abstract-or-title-policy advice required by § 1101.555 —
because § 1101.806(d) says a license holder who fails to do so "may not receive payment of or
recover any commission agreed to be paid on the sale."
