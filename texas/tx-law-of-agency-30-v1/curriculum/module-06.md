# Texas Law of Agency — Module 06

## TREC Topic: Representing More Than One Party in a Transaction: Intermediary Brokerage

**Prescribed time: 165 minutes.** This module carries the five units the Commission's LOA-1 course
approval form places under *Representing More Than One Party in a Transaction: Intermediary
Brokerage*, whose scope note is *"Listing or buying representation procedures; agency disclosure."*

**Learning objectives.** After completing this module you will be able to: explain why a single agent
cannot deliver undivided loyalty to two adverse principals, and state what capacity Chapter 1101 of the
Occupations Code actually provides for a broker who ends up representing both sides; state, from
§ 1101.551(1), § 1101.559 and § 1101.561, who the intermediary is, what written consent the statute
demands, what that consent must contain, what typography the statute requires and what typography it
does not require, and precisely which duties the intermediary's duties supersede; apply § 1101.651(d)
to decide what an intermediary and an appointed license holder may and may not disclose, including the
three exceptions written into subdivision (3); carry out an appointment under § 1101.560 — identify who
may appoint, who may be appointed, the two conditions the statute imposes, the written notice the
broker owes the parties, and what an appointed license holder may do that the intermediary may not; and
distinguish an intermediary arrangement entered into deliberately from unintended dual representation,
name the specific behaviours that produce the latter, and state the disciplinary exposure under
§ 1101.652 and 22 TAC § 535.191 when it happens.

**Every legal statement in this module comes from a source that was read for this course.** Where a
statement is practice rather than law, the module says so and cites nothing for it.

## Unit: The Path from Dual Agency to Intermediary Brokerage

You are about to learn the single most Texas-specific idea in this course. Almost every other subject
in agency law travels: fiduciary duty, disclosure, the difference between a client and a customer.
Those ideas look broadly similar in Oklahoma, in Colorado, in Florida. What Texas does when one
brokerage firm ends up standing between a buyer and a seller who are both its clients does not look
like what most other states do, and the vocabulary Texas uses for it is statutory, narrow, and
unforgiving of approximation. If you learn one thing from this module, learn to stop saying the words
*dual agent* about a Texas transaction. There is a correct word. It is *intermediary*, and it means
something specific.

**Start with what agency actually promises.** Section 1101.557(a) of the Occupations Code states the
rule that makes everything else in this module necessary: "A broker who represents a party in a real
estate transaction or who lists real estate for sale under an exclusive agreement for a party is that
party's agent." That is a status, not a courtesy. Once you are the party's agent, the Commission's
rules attach obligations that run in one direction only. Rule 531.2 — the rule the Commission titles
*Fidelity* — says that "A license holder, while acting as an agent for another, is a fiduciary," and
then spells out what that fiduciary posture demands. The first demand is the one that matters here:
"that the primary duty of the license holder is to represent the interests of the client, and the
license holder's position, in this respect, should be clear to all parties concerned in a real estate
transaction; that, however, the license holder, in performing duties to the client, shall treat other
parties to a transaction fairly." The last demand in the rule is blunter still: "that the license
holder place no personal interest above that of the client."

Rule 535.156 says the same thing from a different angle and adds the information duty. "A license
holder's relationship with the license holder's principal is that of a fiduciary. A license holder
shall convey to the principal all known information which would affect the principal's decision on
whether or not to make, accept or reject offers." Read the qualifier that follows in the same rule,
because truncating it would leave you with a false absolute: "however, if the principal has agreed in
writing that offers are not to be submitted after the principal has entered into a contract to buy,
sell, rent, or lease a property, the license holder shall have no duty to submit offers to the
principal after the principal has accepted an offer." The rule then states the loyalty rule and the
fairness rule in one breath: "The license holder must put the interest of the license holder's
principal above the license holder's own interest. A license holder must deal honestly and fairly with
all parties; however, the license holder represents only the principal and owes a duty of fidelity to
such principal." And it adds a continuing obligation: "A license holder has an affirmative duty to keep
the principal informed at all times of significant information applicable to the transaction or
transactions in which the license holder is acting as agent for the principal."

Put those together and you have the promise a Texas agency relationship makes. Everything you learn
about the other side, you tell your client. Everything your client tells you that would hurt him, you
keep. His interest outranks yours and outranks the other side's. You are not neutral; you are his.

**Now try to make that promise twice, to two people who want opposite things.** Suppose you list a
house on Chisholm Trail in Round Rock at $415,000. The seller, over coffee at the listing appointment,
tells you the truth every listing agent hears eventually: she has already closed on a house in
Georgetown, she is carrying two notes, and she will take $392,000 rather than carry a third month. That
number — $392,000 — is now yours to protect. Rule 535.156 says you must put her interest above your
own and owe her a duty of fidelity. If you leak $392,000, you have cost her twenty-three thousand
dollars and you have breached the most basic thing a client buys when she signs a listing.

Three weeks later a buyer walks into your open house. He likes the property. He is pre-approved to
$430,000 and he tells you, because he trusts you, that he would go to $421,000 for this house before he
would lose it. Suppose you now purport to represent him too, in the full sense — as *his* agent, with
the same duties Rule 531.2 and Rule 535.156 describe. His interest is that you tell him the seller will
take $392,000. Her interest is that you never say it. His interest is that you keep his $421,000
ceiling secret. Her interest is that you disclose it. Rule 535.156 obliges you to convey to the
principal all known information which would affect the principal's decision on whether or not to make,
accept or reject offers — and you now have two principals, each of whom is entitled under that rule to
information you are simultaneously obliged to withhold from the other.

This is not a hard case. It is an *impossible* case, and the impossibility is structural rather than
ethical. A scrupulous, honest, hard-working license holder cannot solve it by trying harder. There is
no version of "advise the buyer to offer $395,000" that is loyal to the seller, and no version of
"advise the seller to hold at $415,000" that is loyal to the buyer. Whatever you do next, you breach a
duty to somebody. The only question is which one.

**What *dual agency* was, as a concept.** The concept the unit title names — one licensee, two
principals, full agency duties to each, made lawful by consent — is the arrangement some states permit
and label *dual agency*. The theory is that the conflict is curable by disclosure: tell both parties
they will get a diminished form of representation, obtain their informed agreement, and proceed. Even
in the states that allow it, the practical result is that the agent's duties to each party are cut back
to something much thinner than ordinary agency, because a duty that cannot be performed for both cannot
be owed to both. The consent does not dissolve the conflict. It just relocates the loss onto the
parties.

**What Texas law actually says.** Here is the finding you should take from the statute itself. Search
Chapter 1101 of the Occupations Code — the Real Estate License Act — and you will not find the words
*dual agent* or *dual agency* anywhere in it. There is no subchapter defining the role, no section
authorizing it, no consent form the Commission prescribes for it, and no set of reduced duties assigned
to it. **A Texas license holder may not act as a dual agent, because Texas law does not create the
capacity.** Representation in Texas is a status conferred by § 1101.557(a) and carrying the full
fiduciary content Rule 531.2 and Rule 535.156 describe; the statute never provides a diluted second
version of it that a licensee could occupy toward two adverse parties at once.

What the Legislature provided instead is a different role with a different name and different rules.
Section 1101.551(1), the definitions section for Subchapter L of the Act, provides that an intermediary
is "a broker who is employed to negotiate a transaction between the parties to a transaction and for
that purpose may act as an agent of the parties." Read that with care, because the drafting is
deliberate. The intermediary is *employed to negotiate a transaction between the parties*. The object
of the employment is the transaction, not the victory of one side over the other. And the intermediary
"may act as an agent of the parties" — plural, jointly, in the capacity Subchapter L defines, not as a
separate full agent of each.

Then comes the provision that closes the door on any alternative. Section 1101.561(b): "A broker must
agree to act as an intermediary under this subchapter if the broker agrees to represent in a
transaction: (1) a buyer or tenant; and (2) a seller or landlord." The verb is *must*. If your broker
takes on both sides of a transaction, the intermediary posture is not one option among several. It is
the only lawful posture the Act supplies, and choosing anything else is a violation of a statute that
22 TAC § 535.191 places in the Commission's highest administrative-penalty band — $1,000 to $5,000 per
violation per day.

**Do not confuse "dual capacity" with dual agency.** Chapter 1101 does use the word "dual" once in a
disciplinary context, and students trip over it every year. Section 1101.652(b)(16) makes it grounds
for suspension or revocation if a license holder, while engaged in real estate brokerage, "acts in a
dual capacity as broker and undisclosed principal in a real estate transaction." That subdivision is
about a licensee who is secretly a *party* to the deal — buying for herself through a nominee, taking a
hidden interest in the property — not about a licensee representing two sides. The vice there is the
concealed self-interest, which is why Rule 535.144 requires a license holder transacting on her own
behalf, or on behalf of a spouse, parent or child, a business entity in which she is more than a ten
percent owner, or a trust she serves as trustee or in which she or a close family member is a
beneficiary, to "disclose in writing that he or she is a licensed real estate broker or sales agent
acting on his or her own behalf" in the contract or in a writing given before the contract. Different
problem, different rule. Section 1101.652(b)(16) is not the Texas dual-agency prohibition, because
Texas does not need one: the capacity was never created.

**The two disciplinary hooks that predate everything.** Two subdivisions of § 1101.652(b) do the real
work when a licensee muddles the sides, and they will reappear in every unit of this module. The
Commission may suspend or revoke a license, or take other disciplinary action, if the license holder,
while engaged in real estate brokerage, "fails to make clear to all parties to a real estate
transaction the party for whom the license holder is acting" — § 1101.652(b)(7) — or "receives
compensation from more than one party to a real estate transaction without the full knowledge and
consent of all parties to the transaction" — § 1101.652(b)(8). Notice how general those are. They do
not require proof that anybody was cheated out of money. Subdivision (7) is violated by ambiguity
itself. If a buyer at your open house cannot tell whose agent you are, you have already supplied the
element. Under 22 TAC § 535.191 those two subdivisions carry an administrative penalty range of $500 to
$3,000 per violation per day.

**When the intermediary provisions entered Texas law.** The held text of Chapter 1101 records the
enactment history at the foot of each section. Sections 1101.559, 1101.560 and 1101.561 were each
"Added by Acts 2001, 77th Leg., ch. 1421, Sec. 2, eff. June 1, 2003" — the act that codified the Real
Estate License Act into the Occupations Code. Section 1101.561 was then "Amended by: Acts 2005, 79th
Leg., Ch. 825 (S.B. 810), Sec. 8, eff. September 1, 2005," and § 1101.557 was amended by section 7 of
that same 2005 act. The legislative history of the intermediary concept before that codification — the
amendments to the former Real Estate License Act in the 1990s that first introduced it — was not held
for this course, and this module therefore does not narrate it. What the held statute does establish is
that the framework you are about to study has been settled Texas law in its codified form since June 1,
2003, with the mandatory-intermediary sentence in § 1101.561(b) reaching its current form by
September 1, 2005.

**One more piece of vocabulary that just disappeared.** If you have read an older Texas text, or talked
to a licensee who has been in the business twenty years, you may have heard about *subagency* — the
arrangement in which a cooperating broker worked for the seller through the listing broker rather than
for the buyer. Look at the definitions section of the current statute: § 1101.002(8) now reads only
"Repealed by Acts 2025, 89th Leg., R.S., Ch. 1172 (S.B. 1968), Sec. 13(1), eff. January 1, 2026." TREC's
own explanatory article on the 2026 changes — which is guidance, not law, and which this course uses
only to describe what changed — states that "Two references to subagency were removed from TRELA" and
adds that "subagency has not been eliminated generally as a legal concept," advising a license holder
who thinks subagency may be involved to contact the broker or an attorney. The word *subagent* and the
word *subagency* appear nowhere in the current statutory text held for this course, and nowhere in the
Commission rules held for it either. Which is to say: the menu of relationships you can lawfully name in
a Texas transaction has gotten shorter, not longer, and the intermediary occupies the middle of it.

**Why the Legislature's answer is better than consent-cured dual agency.** Consider what the
intermediary framework does that a diluted double agency does not. First, it moves the role up to the
broker. Section 1101.551(1) says the intermediary is "a broker," and Subchapter L never says otherwise.
The individual who has been sitting in the seller's kitchen is not the one asked to hold two loyalties
in one head. Second, it converts confidentiality from a judgment call into a rule list: § 1101.651(d)
tells the intermediary exactly which four things may not be said and exactly what it takes to unlock
each of them. Third, it permits the brokerage to restore genuine advice to each party through
appointments under § 1101.560, without pretending that any one human being is advocating for both.
Fourth, it makes the arrangement consensual in advance and in writing, with the compensation source
disclosed, under § 1101.559(a). And fifth, § 1101.561(a) removes the trap that would otherwise
destroy the whole scheme — the argument that a broker who complied perfectly with Subchapter L
nevertheless breached some common-law duty of undivided loyalty by not leaking the seller's floor to
the buyer.

That fifth point deserves a sentence of its own, because it is what makes the framework coherent
instead of merely well-intentioned. Section 1101.561(a): "The duties of a license holder acting as an
intermediary under this subchapter supersede the duties of a license holder established under any other
law, including common law." Without that sentence, a Texas broker acting as an intermediary would be
obeying a statute and breaching a fiduciary standard at the same moment. With it, the statute governs.

## Unit: Intermediary Brokerage

Now build the thing piece by piece. This unit is the operating manual: who the intermediary is, how the
capacity is created, what it forbids, what it permits, and how far the supersession in § 1101.561(a)
actually reaches. Read it slowly. Every sentence in the next several pages corresponds to something a
Commission investigator can ask you to produce.

**The intermediary is the broker. Not you.** Section 1101.551(1) is explicit: an intermediary is
"a broker who is employed to negotiate a transaction between the parties to a transaction." Chapter 1101
defines the two license types separately and does not let them trade places. Section 1101.002(4)
provides that a license holder means "a broker or sales agent licensed under this chapter," and
§ 1101.002(7) provides that a sales agent means "a person who is sponsored by a licensed broker for the
purpose of performing an act described by Subdivision (1)" — that is, an act of brokerage. Section
1101.351(c) closes the loop: "A licensed sales agent may not engage or attempt to
engage in real estate brokerage unless the sales agent is sponsored by a licensed broker and is acting
for that broker." Everything you do as a sales agent, you do for and through your broker.

Two further sources make the point unmistakable. Rule 535.2, the broker responsibility rule, provides
that "Listings and other agreements for real estate brokerage services must be solicited and accepted
in a broker's name," and that a broker "owes the highest fiduciary obligation to the principal and is
obliged to convey to the principal all information known to the agent which may affect the principal's
decision unless prohibited by other law." And § 1101.803 states the liability rule in one sentence: "A
licensed broker is liable to the commission, the public, and the broker's clients for any conduct
engaged in under this chapter by the broker or by a sales agent associated with or acting for the
broker." So when a Round Rock brokerage lists the Chisholm Trail house and a sales agent in the same
office is working with a buyer who wants it, the client relationships on both sides belong to the
broker, the conflict is the broker's conflict, and the decision about how to proceed is the broker's
decision to make.

This is the error to inoculate yourself against right now: a sales agent never "acts as intermediary."
You may be *appointed* by an intermediary under § 1101.560, and that is an entirely different thing
which the next unit takes apart. But if you tell a client "I'm going to act as the intermediary here,"
you have described a role Chapter 1101 does not give you. Say instead: "My broker will be acting as
intermediary, and if the required consents and appointments are in place, my broker may appoint me to
work with you." Those are two different legal facts and the difference shows up in what you are allowed
to say next.

**Creating the capacity: § 1101.559(a).** The statute states two conditions and joins them with "and."
"A broker may act as an intermediary between parties to a real estate transaction if: (1) the broker
obtains written consent from each party for the broker to act as an intermediary in the transaction;
and (2) the written consent of the parties states the source of any expected compensation to the
broker."

Take the conditions one at a time.

*Written.* Not oral. Not implied from conduct. Not inferred from the fact that both parties knew the
brokerage was on both sides. The Act uses "orally or in writing" when it means to allow either — look
at § 1101.558(b), which requires a license holder who represents a party to disclose that
representation "orally or in writing" at first contact with the other party. Section 1101.559(a)(1)
does not offer that choice. It says "written consent," and it says the broker must obtain it before
acting: the section is phrased as a permission conditioned on the consent — a broker "may act as an
intermediary between parties to a real estate transaction if" the two listed conditions are met — so the
consent is a precondition to the conduct, not a paper to be collected afterward.

*From each party.* Both of them. A consent from the seller and a shrug from the buyer is not
compliance. If one party will not consent, the broker cannot act as intermediary in that transaction,
and since § 1101.561(b) forbids the alternative of representing both sides in any other posture,
something has to give — the broker declines the second client, or the second party goes to another
brokerage, or the arrangement does not happen. That is a real business consequence and it is why the
consent is normally captured months earlier, in the listing agreement and in the buyer representation
agreement, before anybody knows whether the two clients will ever collide.

*Consent to the broker acting as an intermediary in the transaction.* The consent is to a named
capacity, not to a vague willingness to be flexible. A clause saying "Client agrees Broker may work with
other clients" is not consent under this section.

*Stating the source of any expected compensation to the broker.* This is the second condition and it is
the one most often skipped. The parties must be told, in the same written consent, where the broker's
money is expected to come from. Notice why the Legislature put it there rather than somewhere else.
Section 1101.652(b)(8) makes it grounds for discipline to receive "compensation from more than one party
to a real estate transaction without the full knowledge and consent of all parties to the transaction."
An intermediary transaction is precisely the situation in which a broker is most likely to be paid from
both sides, or from one side on account of both. Section 1101.559(a)(2) forces the disclosure into the
consent instrument so that the knowledge is documented before the conflict matures.

**The safe harbour in § 1101.559(b), and the typography question.** Students always want to know
whether Texas requires a particular type size on the intermediary consent. Read the subsection:

> "A written listing agreement to represent a seller or landlord or a written agreement to represent a
> buyer or tenant that authorizes a broker to act as an intermediary in a real estate transaction is
> sufficient to establish written consent of the party to the transaction if the written agreement
> specifies in conspicuous bold or underlined print the conduct that is prohibited under Section
> 1101.651(d)."

Three things follow. First, the ordinary listing agreement and the ordinary buyer or tenant
representation agreement are the intended vehicles. Texas does not contemplate a separate mid-
transaction consent ceremony as the normal case; it contemplates the authorization being obtained at the
front end, when the client is calm and not yet in love with a particular house.

Second, the safe harbour is conditional, and the condition is substantive. The agreement is "sufficient
to establish written consent" only "if the written agreement specifies in conspicuous bold or underlined
print the conduct that is prohibited under Section 1101.651(d)." The party must be shown, in emphasised
type, the four things the intermediary will not be permitted to tell the other side. That is the
Legislature making sure the consent is informed about the actual cost of the arrangement: you are
consenting to a broker who will not tell you what the other side would accept.

Third — and answer the question precisely — **§ 1101.559 prescribes no point size.** It requires
"conspicuous bold or underlined print," which is a description of emphasis, not a measurement. Contrast
that with the places where Chapter 1101 and the Commission's rules do specify a size. Section
1101.558(b-1) requires the representation notice to be given "in at least a 10-point font." Rule 531.20
requires the homepage link to the Information About Brokerage Services Notice to be labelled either
*Texas Real Estate Commission Information About Brokerage Services*, "in at least 10 point font," or
*TREC Information About Brokerage Services*, "in at least 12 point font." Rule 535.17(c) requires the
disclaimer that
accompanies a broker price opinion to be "reproduced verbatim in at least 12-point font." The
Legislature knows how to specify a font size. In § 1101.559(b) it chose a conspicuousness standard
instead. Do not tell a client Texas requires ten-point type on the intermediary consent. It does not.

**What the intermediary may not disclose: § 1101.651(d), in full and with its qualifiers.** This is the
heart of the unit and the provision most often quoted wrongly, because people drop the "unless"
clauses. Section 1101.651(d) provides that "A broker and any broker or sales agent appointed under
Section 1101.560 who acts as an intermediary under Subchapter L may not:"

> "(1) disclose to the buyer or tenant that the seller or landlord will accept a price less than the
> asking price, unless otherwise instructed in a separate writing by the seller or landlord;"

> "(2) disclose to the seller or landlord that the buyer or tenant will pay a price greater than the
> price submitted in a written offer to the seller or landlord, unless otherwise instructed in a
> separate writing by the buyer or tenant;"

> "(3) disclose any confidential information or any information a party specifically instructs the
> broker or sales agent in writing not to disclose, unless: (A) the broker or sales agent is otherwise
> instructed in a separate writing by the respective party; (B) the broker or sales agent is required
> to disclose the information by this chapter or a court order; or (C) the information materially
> relates to the condition of the property;"

> "(4) treat a party to a transaction dishonestly; or"

> "(5) violate this chapter."

Work through what each one actually does.

Subdivision (1) is the seller's floor. On the Chisholm Trail facts, the $392,000 is locked. You may not
tell the buyer that the seller will accept less than the $415,000 asking price — not the number, and not
the fact. "She's motivated" is a disclosure that the seller will accept less than the asking price,
dressed up in a word. So is "I'd come in under list if I were you." The prohibition is on communicating
the substance, not on uttering a particular sentence.

The qualifier matters just as much. "[U]nless otherwise instructed in a separate writing by the seller
or landlord." The seller *can* unlock it. If she decides in June, after two months on the market, that
she wants buyers told she will consider $395,000, she may instruct the broker to say so — but the
instruction must be in a *separate writing*. Not a phone call. Not a text saying "do whatever you have
to." Not a clause tucked into the original listing. A separate writing, signed, from that party. Learn
that phrase, because it appears twice more in the subsection and it is the mechanism by which a
confidence is deliberately released.

Subdivision (2) is the buyer's ceiling, stated with a precision people misread. The prohibition is on
disclosing that the buyer or tenant "will pay a price greater than the price submitted in a written
offer to the seller or landlord." The benchmark is the price in the written offer, not the buyer's
approval letter and not what he told you in the car. If the buyer submits at $400,000 and told you he
would go to $421,000, you may not tell the seller he would go higher. The same unless-clause applies: a
separate writing from the buyer can release it, which is exactly what a buyer sometimes wants at the
escalation stage of a competitive negotiation.

Subdivision (3) is the general confidentiality rule and it has two limbs and three exceptions. The two
limbs: "any confidential information" and "any information a party specifically instructs the broker or
sales agent in writing not to disclose." The second limb is the client's tool — a written instruction
converts an ordinary fact into a protected one. The three exceptions are where students get careless.
Exception (A) is the separate-writing release again. Exception (B) is compulsion — where "the broker or
sales agent is required to disclose the information by this chapter or a court order." Exception (C) is
the one that saves lives and licences: confidentiality does not cover information that "materially
relates to the condition of the property."

Sit with exception (C) for a moment. A seller cannot use the intermediary's confidentiality duty as a
lid on a defect. If the seller tells you the foundation was underpinned twice and the second contractor
walked off, that information materially relates to the condition of the property and subdivision (3)
does not permit you to withhold it. Independently, § 1101.652(b)(3) makes it a disciplinary ground to
make "a material misrepresentation to a potential buyer concerning a significant defect, including a
latent structural defect, known to the license holder that would be a significant factor to a reasonable
and prudent buyer in making a decision to purchase real property," and § 1101.652(b)(4) reaches the
license holder who "fails to disclose to a potential buyer a defect described by Subdivision (3) that is
known to the license holder." Confidentiality in Texas protects negotiating position. It has never
protected physical facts about the house.

Subdivision (4) — "treat a party to a transaction dishonestly" — is short and total. There is no
qualifier and no unless. Whatever else the intermediary framework permits, it never permits lying to
either side. Rule 535.156 says the same thing about all license holders in all postures: "A license
holder has a duty to convey accurate information to members of the public with whom the license holder
deals." Rule 531.3 adds that "A license holder has a special obligation to exercise integrity in the
discharge of the license holder's responsibilities, including employment of prudence and caution so as
to avoid misrepresentation, in any way, by acts of commission or omission." Note "omission." A
half-truth engineered by silence is a misrepresentation.

Subdivision (5) — "violate this chapter" — folds the whole Act into the intermediary prohibition. It
means that an intermediary who breaks any other provision of Chapter 1101 has, by that fact, also
violated § 1101.651(d). Under 22 TAC § 535.191, § 1101.651 sits in the top penalty band: $1,000 to
$5,000 per violation per day.

Notice who § 1101.651(d) binds. The opening words are "A broker and any broker or sales agent appointed
under Section 1101.560 who acts as an intermediary under Subchapter L." The four confidentiality
prohibitions run to the intermediary broker *and* to every license holder the broker appoints. An
appointment does not release a sales agent from subdivisions (1) through (5). We will come back to this
in the next unit, because it is the point at which the appointment system is most often misdescribed.

**The impartiality duty and what the statute deems consistent with it.** Section 1101.559(c) contains
two sentences and you need both. "An intermediary shall act fairly and impartially. Appointment by a
broker acting as an intermediary of an associated license holder under Section 1101.560 to communicate
with, carry out the instructions of, and provide opinions and advice to the parties to whom that
associated license holder is appointed is a fair and impartial act."

The first sentence sets the standard: fairly and impartially. The intermediary broker does not push. He
does not tell the seller the offer is low, or the buyer the price is fair. He carries information,
answers questions, and keeps the deal moving without tilting it.

The second sentence answers an objection before it can be made. Someone was always going to argue that
appointing one licensee to advise the buyer and another to advise the seller is inherently *partial* —
after all, the appointees are advocating. The Legislature settled it: making the appointment is, as a
matter of statute, "a fair and impartial act." Impartiality attaches to the broker's own conduct, not to
the conduct of the appointees, whose entire function is to be useful to one party each.

**How far does § 1101.561(a) actually go?** Read the supersession clause with maximum care, because
it is the most over-read sentence in Subchapter L. "The duties of a license holder acting as an
intermediary under this subchapter supersede the duties of a license holder established under any other
law, including common law."

What it displaces: duties established under *other law* — the fiduciary duties a court would otherwise
imply, the common-law obligation of undivided loyalty, the argument that a broker's failure to disclose
the seller's floor to a buyer-client is a breach of trust. Those yield to Subchapter L. That is the
whole purpose of the section, and it is why an intermediary who complies with § 1101.651(d) is not
thereby breaching a duty of loyalty: the statutory duty has taken the place of the common-law one.

What it does not displace, on the face of the text:

Duties imposed by *this* subchapter. The supersession runs against "any other law." Sections 1101.559,
1101.560 and 1101.561 are the source of the intermediary's duties, not their casualty. Section
1101.559(c) still requires fair and impartial action.

Duties imposed elsewhere in Chapter 1101. Section 1101.651(d) is drafted expressly to govern brokers and
appointees "who acts as an intermediary under Subchapter L," so it plainly survives — indeed it would be
absurd to read § 1101.561(a) as abolishing the very confidentiality rules that define the role. Section
1101.652 continues to supply grounds for discipline; § 1101.652(b)(2) still reaches conduct "that is
dishonest or in bad faith or that demonstrates untrustworthiness," and § 1101.652(b)(1) still reaches a
license holder who "acts negligently or incompetently." Section 1101.555 still requires a license holder,
when an offer to purchase Texas real estate is signed, to advise each buyer in writing to have the
abstract examined by an attorney chosen by the buyer or to be provided with or obtain a title insurance
policy. Section 1101.557(b) still requires a broker who represents a party to inform the party of
material information related to the transaction, including the receipt of an offer, and to "at a
minimum, answer the party's questions and present any offer to or from the party."

Timeliness rules still apply too. Rule 535.157 provides that "A broker or sales agent must respond to
his or her principal, a broker or sales agent representing another party to a real estate transaction,
or an unrepresented party to a real estate transaction within two calendar days," and Rule 535.2 adds
that a broker or a delegated supervisor "must respond to sponsored sales agents within two calendar
days." An intermediary who goes quiet for a week has not become impartial; he has become
non-responsive.

Where the boundary is genuinely uncertain, say so rather than guessing. The phrase "any other law,
including common law" is broad, and the held sources do not contain a Texas court decision construing
its outer edge. **No Texas case law construing § 1101.561(a) was held for this course, and none is cited
here.** The safe professional reading — and the one the rest of this module teaches — is that
§ 1101.561(a) resolves the conflict between Subchapter L and the common law in Subchapter L's favour,
and does nothing else. It is not a general amnesty.

**A practical sequence.** Putting the unit together, an intermediary transaction in a Texas brokerage
runs like this, and the order is not decorative. The broker obtains written intermediary authorization
in the listing agreement, with the § 1101.651(d) prohibitions set out in conspicuous bold or underlined
print, and the source of expected compensation stated (§ 1101.559(a)(2), (b)). The broker obtains the
same authorization in the buyer or tenant representation agreement. The § 1101.558(b-1) notice has
already gone to each party at first substantive communication. The two clients collide on a property.
Because the broker has agreed to represent both a buyer and a seller in the transaction, § 1101.561(b)
requires the broker to act as intermediary. If the consents authorize appointments, the broker makes
them and gives written notice to all parties under § 1101.560(b)(2). From that point the broker acts
fairly and impartially, and everyone — broker and appointees alike — lives inside § 1101.651(d). If any
piece of that chain is missing, the brokerage is not in a lawful intermediary posture, and it is in a
posture Texas law does not recognise at all.

## Unit: Representation of More Than One Party in a Transaction

The previous unit ended with the broker standing between two clients, bound to fairness and silence.
That is a lawful place to stand, and for some transactions it is where the brokerage stays. But it has
an obvious cost: neither party has anybody advising them. Section 1101.560 is the Legislature's answer
to that cost, and this unit is about how it works, who it binds, and what the parties actually get
under each of the two available configurations.

**The text, clause by clause.** Section 1101.560 is titled "ASSOCIATED LICENSE HOLDER ACTING AS
INTERMEDIARY," which is itself a slightly misleading heading — as you will see, the associated license
holder does not become the intermediary. Subsection (a) provides:

> "A broker who complies with the written consent requirements of Section 1101.559 may appoint: (1) a
> license holder associated with the broker to communicate with and carry out instructions of one party
> to a real estate transaction; and (2) another license holder associated with the broker to
> communicate with and carry out instructions of any other party to the transaction."

Subsection (b):

> "A license holder may be appointed under this section only if: (1) the written consent of the parties
> under Section 1101.559 authorizes the broker to make the appointment; and (2) the broker provides
> written notice of the appointment to all parties involved in the real estate transaction."

Subsection (c):

> "A license holder appointed under this section may provide opinions and advice during negotiations to
> the party to whom the license holder is appointed."

**Who may appoint.** "A broker who complies with the written consent requirements of Section 1101.559."
Only the broker appoints, and only a broker who has already satisfied § 1101.559 — both parties' written
consent, with the compensation source stated. A sales agent cannot appoint herself, cannot appoint a
colleague, and cannot appoint anybody by agreeing with the other side's agent that they will "each just
work our own client." That agreement, without the broker's act, is nothing.

Note the ordering the statute imposes. Compliance with § 1101.559 comes first; appointment second.
There is no lawful sequence in which appointments precede consent.

**Who may be appointed.** "[A] license holder associated with the broker." Two constraints live in
those five words. The appointee must be a *license holder* — a broker or sales agent licensed under
Chapter 1101, per § 1101.002(4). And the appointee must be *associated with the broker* — an unlicensed
assistant cannot be appointed, a licensee at a different firm cannot be appointed, and a licensee whose
sponsorship has lapsed cannot be appointed, because § 1101.351(c) bars a sales agent from engaging in
brokerage at all unless "sponsored by a licensed broker and is acting for that broker."

Read subsection (a)(2) for what it implies about *numbers*: "another license holder associated with the
broker" is to be appointed to "any other party." The statute contemplates a different license holder for
each party. It does not describe appointing one person to both sides, and it would defeat the purpose if
it did — the entire point of the mechanism is to give each party a person who is permitted to advise
them, which is impossible if that person is the same human being holding both sets of instructions.

**The two conditions in subsection (b), and the word "only."** "A license holder may be appointed under
this section **only if**" both conditions are met. This is a closed gate, not a list of good practices.

The first condition: "the written consent of the parties under Section 1101.559 authorizes the broker to
make the appointment." So the consent must do two distinct jobs — authorize intermediary status, and
authorize appointments. A consent that does the first but is silent on the second permits an
intermediary without appointments and nothing more. This is why brokerage listing and representation
agreements ask the client to authorize appointments explicitly; the authorization is not implied by the
intermediary authorization.

The second condition: "the broker provides written notice of the appointment to all parties involved in
the real estate transaction." Four features of that clause repay attention. It is the *broker* who must
provide it — not the appointed agent. It must be *written* — the statute does not offer the "orally or
in writing" alternative that § 1101.558(b) offers for the representation disclosure. It concerns the
*appointment* — who has been appointed to whom. And it goes to *all parties involved in the real estate
transaction*, not merely to the party whose appointee is being named. Each side is entitled to know that
the other side has an appointed licensee, and who it is.

Now note what the subsection does *not* say, because the exam-relevant discipline here is reading
restraint. It does not prescribe a form. It does not set a deadline expressed in days or hours; it says
the broker "provides" the notice, and since subsection (b) makes it a condition of a valid appointment,
the sound reading is that it must be given for the appointment to be effective. It does not require the
parties to sign the notice, or to consent again at the moment of appointment — the consent was obtained
earlier under § 1101.559. And it does not require the notice to explain the appointee's duties. The
Commission's promulgated forms are not held for this course, and this module therefore does not describe
any particular form's contents. What is held is the statute, and the statute requires written notice of
the appointment to everyone in the transaction.

Under 22 TAC § 535.191 the whole of § 1101.560 sits in the Commission's top administrative-penalty
band, $1,000 to $5,000 per violation per day — the same band as § 1101.559(a) and (c), § 1101.561(b),
§ 1101.558 and § 1101.651.

**What the appointment actually buys: subsection (c).** "A license holder appointed under this section
may provide opinions and advice during negotiations to the party to whom the license holder is
appointed." Compare that with § 1101.559(c)'s command that "An intermediary shall act fairly and
impartially," and you have the architecture in a sentence: **the broker stays neutral; the appointees
advise.**

Be precise about the scope of the permission. It is to provide "opinions and advice" — the very things
§ 1101.562(a)(3) forbids a broker who is showing property *without* representing the party from giving.
It is "during negotiations." And it runs only "to the party to whom the license holder is appointed."
An appointed agent who starts coaching the other side has stepped outside subsection (c) and, since the
other side has its own appointee, has almost certainly also crossed § 1101.651(d).

And be equally precise about what the appointment does not buy. It does not lift § 1101.651(d). Reread
the opening words of that subsection: it binds "A broker and any broker or sales agent appointed under
Section 1101.560 who acts as an intermediary under Subchapter L." The appointee may advise her party
about strategy, about the condition of the property, about whether the price is supportable — but she
may not tell her buyer that the seller will take less than list, may not tell her seller that the buyer
would pay more than the written offer, may not disclose confidential information or information a party
has instructed in writing not to be disclosed except under exceptions (A), (B) and (C), may not treat
either party dishonestly, and may not violate the chapter. An appointed agent is an advisor operating
inside a confidentiality cage. If you remember nothing else from this unit, remember that.

**Configuration one: intermediary without appointments.** Either the consent did not authorize
appointments, or the broker chose not to make them, or the brokerage is too small to make them — a
situation the next unit takes up. The broker is intermediary; nobody is appointed.

What the parties get: a broker who acts fairly and impartially under § 1101.559(c); who informs each
party of material information related to the transaction, including the receipt of an offer, under
§ 1101.557(b)(2); who at a minimum answers each party's questions and presents any offer to or from
each party under § 1101.557(b)(3); who responds within two calendar days under Rule 535.157; and who
conveys accurate information to the public under Rule 535.156. What they do not get is advice. Nobody in
the brokerage will tell the buyer whether $398,000 is a good number, or tell the seller whether to
counter at $409,000 or take it. The parties supply their own judgment, or they retain their own counsel
or advisors outside the brokerage.

That is not a failure of the system; it is the honest price of the situation. But it is a price the
parties should understand at the front end rather than discovering it on the night an offer arrives, and
explaining it clearly is one of the things a competent Texas licensee does at the listing appointment.

**Configuration two: intermediary with appointments.** The broker is intermediary and remains fair and
impartial. Agent A is appointed to the seller; Agent B to the buyer. Written notice of both appointments
has gone to both parties. Now each party has a licensee who may give opinions and advice during
negotiations — Agent A may tell the seller that $398,000 is below what the comparables support and that
a counter at $407,500 is defensible; Agent B may tell the buyer that the roof is at the end of its life
and that he should not go above $405,000 without a repair credit. Neither may cross the § 1101.651(d)
lines. The seller's floor stays with the seller's side. The buyer's ceiling stays with the buyer's side.
The broker knows both and says neither.

The practical difference between the two configurations is therefore not about confidentiality — that is
identical, because § 1101.651(d) governs both — but about *advice*. Without appointments, the parties
negotiate unadvised through an impartial broker. With appointments, each party negotiates with a
licensee permitted to be useful to them, inside the same silence.

**Two more constraints that survive the intermediary posture.** First, broker responsibility. Rule 535.2
requires a broker to notify a sponsored sales agent in writing of the scope of the sales agent's
authorized activities, provides that "Unless such scope is limited or revoked in writing, a broker is
responsible for the authorized acts of the broker's sales agents, but the broker is not required to
supervise the sales agents directly," and warns that "If a broker permits a sponsored sales agent to
conduct activities beyond the scope explicitly authorized by the broker, those are acts for which the
broker is responsible." The same rule permits a broker to delegate to another license holder the
responsibility to assist in administering compliance, but says the broker "may not relinquish overall
responsibility for the supervision of license holders sponsored by the broker," requires any such
delegation to be in writing, and requires the broker to give the Commission the name of each delegated
supervisor within thirty days of a delegation that has lasted or is anticipated to last more than three
consecutive months, and to notify the Commission within thirty days after a delegation ends. In an
intermediary transaction the broker's exposure is doubled, because § 1101.803 makes the broker liable
"to the commission, the public, and the broker's clients" for conduct engaged in by the broker or by a
sales agent associated with or acting for the broker — and there are now clients on both sides.

Second, records. Rule 535.2 requires the broker, except for records destroyed by an "Act of God" such as
a natural disaster or fire not intentionally caused by the broker, to maintain in a format readily
available to the Commission, for at least four years from the date of closing, termination of the
contract, or end of a real estate transaction, a list that includes "disclosures," "commission
agreements such as listing agreements, buyer representation agreements, or other written agreements
relied upon to claim compensation," "substantive communications with parties to the transaction," and
"offers, contracts, and related addenda." Every document this module has described — the consents, the
appointment notices, the separate writings that release a confidence — falls inside that list, and four
years is the floor.

## Unit: Specialized Intermediary Applications

The framework is now built. This unit walks it into the awkward corners of real Texas practice, where
students most often discover that the tidy two-agent example does not fit the office they actually work
in.

**The one-agent brokerage.** Priya holds a broker licence and works alone in Fredericksburg. She has no
sponsored sales agents. She lists a limestone cottage on Milam Street, and separately she has a signed
buyer representation agreement with a couple relocating from Houston who fall in love with that cottage.

Section 1101.561(b) applies to her exactly as it applies to a two-hundred-agent firm: she "must agree to
act as an intermediary under this subchapter" if she agrees to represent both a buyer and a seller in
the transaction. She may act as intermediary if — and only if — she has satisfied § 1101.559(a): written
consent from each party for her to act as intermediary, stating the source of any expected compensation.

Can she appoint? No. Section 1101.560(a) authorizes the broker to appoint "a license holder associated
with the broker" for one party and "another license holder associated with the broker" for any other
party. Priya has no associated license holders. She cannot appoint herself; the subsection is written in
terms of a broker appointing someone else, and appointing herself to both parties would produce exactly
the undivided-loyalty impossibility that Unit 1 showed cannot be performed. So Priya's only lawful
configuration is intermediary without appointments: fair and impartial under § 1101.559(c), silent under
§ 1101.651(d), still bound by § 1101.557(b) to inform each party of material information including the
receipt of an offer and at a minimum to answer each party's questions and present offers, still bound to
respond within two calendar days under Rule 535.157.

What that means for her clients is concrete and she should say it out loud before the offer, not after:
"From this point I will not advise either of you on price or strategy. I will carry information
accurately, present every offer, answer questions about the property and the process, and keep each of
your negotiating positions confidential. If you want advice on what to offer, you need to get it
somewhere other than from me." Saying that plainly is practice, not law — no held source prescribes the
script — but the substance of every sentence in it comes from § 1101.559(c), § 1101.651(d) and
§ 1101.557(b).

**The listing agent whose own buyer client wants the listing.** This is the most common version of the
problem in a large brokerage, and it is the one people get wrong. Marisol is a sales agent at a Plano
firm. She lists a house on Legacy Drive for the Nguyens. She also has a buyer representation agreement
with the Halls. The Halls want the Legacy Drive house.

First, the broker — not Marisol — decides the posture, because the clients are the broker's clients
(Rule 535.2: listings and other agreements for real estate brokerage services must be solicited and
accepted in a broker's name) and § 1101.561(b) speaks to the broker. Assuming both written consents
comply with § 1101.559(a), the broker acts as intermediary.

Second, can Marisol be appointed to both the Nguyens and the Halls? Read § 1101.560(a) again: one
license holder for one party, "another license holder" for any other party. The statute describes two
different appointees. And ask what an appointment even means: subsection (c) permits the appointee to
"provide opinions and advice during negotiations to the party to whom the license holder is appointed."
An appointee to both parties would be permitted to advise both parties in the same negotiation, which is
the impossibility the whole subchapter exists to avoid. So the workable configurations are two: the
broker acts as intermediary with no appointments at all, and Marisol simply stops advising anybody about
this transaction; or the broker appoints Marisol to one party and a different associated license holder
to the other, with written notice of both appointments to all parties under § 1101.560(b)(2).

There is a business consequence students should see coming. If the broker appoints another agent to the
Halls, Marisol is no longer the Halls' advisor for this house, and the split of whatever compensation
the firm earns is a matter for the brokerage's own policies and the sponsorship agreement. Rule 535.3
provides that "A sales agent may not receive a commission or other valuable consideration except with
the written consent of the sales agent's sponsoring broker or the broker who sponsored the sales agent
when the sales agent became entitled to the commission or other valuable consideration," and
§ 1101.651(b) provides that "A sales agent may not accept compensation for a real estate transaction
from a person other than the broker that is sponsoring the sales agent or was sponsoring the sales agent
when the sales agent earned the compensation." How the firm divides the money is the firm's policy; that
it flows through the broker is law.

**Commercial transactions.** Nothing in Subchapter L limits the intermediary provisions to houses.
Section 1101.551(2) defines "Party" to mean "a prospective buyer, seller, landlord, or tenant or an
authorized representative of a buyer, seller, landlord, or tenant, including a trustee, guardian,
executor, administrator, receiver, or attorney-in-fact," and adds that "The term does not include a
license holder who represents a party." A broker representing both the seller and the buyer of a
warehouse on Highway 290 in Brenham is in exactly the same statutory position as a residential broker:
§ 1101.561(b) requires the intermediary posture, § 1101.559 supplies the consent requirements, and
§ 1101.651(d) supplies the silence.

What differs is a neighbouring 2026 requirement, and you should be able to state its boundary. Section
1101.563(b) requires a license holder who performs any act of real estate brokerage for a prospective
buyer of *residential real property* to enter into a written agreement with that buyer before showing
any residential real property, or, if none will be shown, before presenting an offer to purchase
residential real property on the buyer's behalf. Section 1101.563(a) defines "residential real property"
for that section as "(1) a single-family house; (2) a duplex, triplex, or quadraplex; or (3) a unit in a
multiunit residential structure in which title to an individual unit is transferred to the owner of the
unit under a condominium or cooperative system." A warehouse is none of those. TREC's own explanatory
article on the 2026 changes — guidance, not law — puts the same boundary in a sentence: "The written
agreement requirement does not apply to commercial purchasers or residential/commercial tenants." So the
§ 1101.563 written-agreement mandate does not reach your Brenham warehouse buyer, while the intermediary
requirements of § 1101.559 through § 1101.561 reach him fully. Two different rules, two different
scopes; do not merge them.

**Lease transactions.** The statute is written in paired terms throughout — "seller or landlord" and
"buyer or tenant" in § 1101.651(d)(1) and (2), "a buyer or tenant" and "a seller or landlord" in
§ 1101.561(b). A broker who represents both the landlord and the tenant of a retail suite in San Marcos
is squarely inside Subchapter L. The confidentiality rules translate directly: the intermediary may not
tell the tenant that the landlord will accept a rent less than the asking rate, absent a separate writing
from the landlord, and may not tell the landlord that the tenant will pay more than the rent stated in
the tenant's written offer, absent a separate writing from the tenant.

One adjacent provision has a lease-specific exception that students misapply. Section 1101.558(b-1)
requires the written representation notice at first substantive communication, but § 1101.558(c)(1)
excuses it where "the proposed transaction is for a residential lease for less than one year and a sale
is not being considered." Subsection (c) also excuses the notice where "the license holder meets with a
party who the license holder knows is represented by another license holder" and where "the
communication occurs at a property that is held open for any prospective buyer or tenant and the
communication concerns that property." Those are exceptions to the *notice* requirement in
§ 1101.558(b-1). They are not exceptions to § 1101.559. A short residential lease where the brokerage
represents both landlord and tenant still requires written intermediary consent from each party, because
§ 1101.559 contains no lease exception at all.

**When the license holder is on one side as a principal.** Sometimes the two-parties problem is not
two clients but one client and yourself. Section 1101.557(c)(1) provides that for purposes of that
section, "a license holder who has the authority to bind a party to a lease or sale under a power of
attorney or a property management agreement is also a party to the lease or sale." If you hold that
authority, you are not merely the agent; you are on the other side of the table.

Rule 535.144 governs the related situation. For purposes of § 1101.652(a-1)(1), "license holder"
includes a license holder acting on behalf of "the license holder's spouse, parent or child," "a
business entity in which the license holder is more than a 10% owner," or "a trust for which the license
holder acts as trustee or of which the license holder or the license holder's spouse, parent or child is
a beneficiary." Such a license holder "is obligated to disclose in writing that he or she is a licensed
real estate broker or sales agent acting on his or her own behalf or in a capacity described by
subsection (a) in any contract of sale or rental agreement or in any other writing given before entering
into any contract of sale or rental agreement," and "shall not use the license holder's expertise to the
disadvantage of a person with whom the license holder deals." Section 1101.652(a-1)(1) itself makes it
grounds for discipline to engage "in misrepresentation, dishonesty, or fraud when selling, buying,
trading, or leasing real property" in the name of the license holder, the license holder's spouse, or a
person related within the first degree by consanguinity. Rule 535.16(c) adds an obligation people forget:
a real estate license holder "is obligated to provide a broker price opinion or comparative market
analysis on a property when negotiating a listing or offering to purchase the property for the license
holder's own account as a result of contact made while acting as a real estate agent."

The teaching point is that this is not an intermediary situation at all. Intermediary status addresses a
broker with two *clients*. A licensee buying for her own account has one client, or none, and a
disclosure obligation of a different kind. Do not paper a self-dealing transaction with an intermediary
consent and think you have solved it.

**Two teams inside one large firm.** A Houston brokerage with three hundred agents will routinely have
both sides of a transaction in house without anyone noticing until the offer arrives. Nothing about size
changes the analysis: the clients are the broker's clients, § 1101.561(b) is triggered by the broker's
agreement to represent both, and the consents must already be in the two representation agreements. Many
firms maintain internal information barriers so that appointed agents on opposite sides do not share
files, sit in on the same pipeline meetings, or use a shared transaction folder. Rule 535.2 requires a
broker who sponsors sales agents to "maintain, on a current basis, written policies and procedures" that
ensure, among other things, that each sponsored sales agent "is advised of the scope of the sales
agent's authorized activities subject to the Act and is competent to conduct such activities." The
detailed content of any particular firm's information barrier is practice, not law, and no held source
prescribes one; what is law is that the broker must have written policies and procedures covering the
listed subjects, and that under § 1101.803 the broker answers for what the agents do.

### Applied exercise: Three awkward corners in one week

You are the sponsoring broker of a nine-agent firm in New Braunfels. In one week the following land on
your desk. Answer each using the sections cited in this module.

1. Your agent Dario has the listing on a duplex on Landa Street and also holds a buyer representation
   agreement with a purchaser who wants it. Both agreements authorize intermediary status and
   appointments. Dario asks to be appointed to both parties so he can "keep it simple." Answer him
   using the text of § 1101.560(a) and (c), and state the two configurations that are in fact
   available to you.

## Unit: Intentional versus Unintended Dual Representation

Everything in this module so far has described a brokerage that *decided* to represent two parties and
built the paperwork to do it lawfully. That is intentional. It is planned at the listing appointment,
consented to in writing under § 1101.559(a), documented with appointment notices under § 1101.560(b)(2),
and governed by § 1101.651(d) from the moment the two clients collide.

The far more dangerous case is the one nobody decided. A licensee who intends to represent one party
slides, conversation by conversation, into behaving like the agent of the other, until a Commission
investigator or a plaintiff's lawyer asks the question § 1101.652(b)(7) asks: did the license holder fail
"to make clear to all parties to a real estate transaction the party for whom the license holder is
acting"? This unit is about that slide — how it happens, what it costs, and how to stop it.

**Where the line is drawn in the first place.** Section 1101.558(b) requires that "A license holder who
represents a party in a proposed real estate transaction shall disclose, orally or in writing, that
representation at the time of the license holder's first contact with: (1) another party to the
transaction; or (2) another license holder who represents another party to the transaction." First
contact. Not first offer, not first showing — first contact.

Section 1101.558(b-1) then requires the written notice: "At the time of a license holder's first
substantive communication with a party relating to a proposed transaction regarding specific real
property, the license holder shall provide to the party written notice in at least a 10-point font
that: (1) describes the ways in which a broker can represent a party to a real estate transaction,
including as an intermediary; (2) describes the basic duties and obligations a broker has to a party to
a real estate transaction that the broker represents; (3) describes the basic obligations a broker has
to a party to a real estate transaction that the broker does not represent; and (4) provides the name,
license number, and contact information for the license holder and the license holder's supervisor and
broker, if applicable." Subsection (b-2) provides that "The commission by rule shall prescribe the text
of the notice required under Subsections (b-1)(1) and (2) and establish the methods by which a license
holder shall provide the notice."

The Commission has done so. Rule 531.20 "adopts by reference the Information About Brokerage Services
Notice, TREC No. IABS 1-2 (IABS Notice)," requires each license holder to provide "a link to a completed
IABS Notice in a readily noticeable place on the homepage of each business website," labelled either
*Texas Real Estate Commission Information About Brokerage Services*, "in at least 10 point font," or
*TREC Information About Brokerage Services*, "in at least 12 point font," and to provide "the completed
IABS Notice at the first substantive communication as required under §1101.558, Texas Occupations Code." The
rule lists the permitted delivery methods — "by personal delivery by the license holder," "by first
class mail or overnight common carrier delivery service," "in the body of an email," or "as an
attachment to an email, or a link within the body of an email, with a specific reference to the IABS
Notice in the body of the email" — and then closes a favourite loophole: "The link to a completed IABS
Notice may not be in a footnote or signature block in an email." The rule also requires that any
reproduction of the notice copy the text verbatim, with "the spacing, borders and placement of text on
the page" appearing "to be identical to that in the published version of the IABS Notice, except that
the Broker Contact Information section may be prefilled." The published text of TREC No. IABS 1-2 itself
was not held for this course, so this module describes what the statute and the rule require of the
notice and does not quote the form.

Notice what § 1101.558(b-1)(1) tells you about the Legislature's design. The notice must describe "the
ways in which a broker can represent a party to a real estate transaction, **including as an
intermediary**." The intermediary posture is not a surprise sprung on a consumer at contract time; the
statute puts it in front of every Texas buyer, seller, landlord and tenant at the first substantive
conversation. Three exceptions appear in § 1101.558(c): the notice is not required where "the proposed
transaction is for a residential lease for less than one year and a sale is not being considered," where
"the license holder meets with a party who the license holder knows is represented by another license
holder," or where "the communication occurs at a property that is held open for any prospective buyer or
tenant and the communication concerns that property."

**The 2026 non-representation lane, and its hard edges.** Since January 1, 2026, Texas law has provided
an explicit way to show property to someone you do not represent. Section 1101.562(a) provides that "A
broker may show real property available for sale or lease to a party without representing the party if
the broker: (1) has not agreed with the party, either orally or in writing, to represent the party; (2)
is not otherwise acting as the party's agent at the time of showing the real property; (3) does not
provide to the party opinions or advice regarding the real property or real estate transactions in
general; and (4) does not perform any other act of real estate brokerage for the party." Subsection (b)
requires that before such a showing the broker must, as applicable, "disclose to the party as required
by Section 1101.558(b) if the broker represents the owner of the real property" or "provide to the party
the written notice described by Section 1101.558(b-1) if the broker does not represent the owner of the
real property." Subsection (c) supplies the narrow permission: "Notwithstanding Subsections (a)(3) and
(4), a broker showing real property under this section may confirm information to a party regarding the
size, price, and terms of the real property available for sale or lease."

Read (a)(3) as the trap it is. "[D]oes not provide to the party opinions or advice regarding the real
property **or real estate transactions in general**." Not just opinions about this house — opinions about
transactions generally. "You should really get an inspection." "Around here people usually offer about
two percent under list." "I'd ask for a survey." Each of those is advice regarding real estate
transactions in general, and each of them, given to a party you are not representing, takes you outside
§ 1101.562. Subsection (c) tells you what you *may* do: confirm size, price and terms. That is a short
list, and it is the whole list.

Section 1101.563 sits alongside it. For a prospective buyer of residential real property as defined in
§ 1101.563(a), a license holder who performs any act of real estate brokerage must enter into a written
agreement before showing residential real property or, if none will be shown, before presenting an offer
on the buyer's behalf. The agreement must state "the services to be provided by the license holder,"
"the termination date of the agreement," "whether the agreement is exclusive or non-exclusive," and, as
applicable, that the license holder "represents the prospective buyer as the buyer's agent" or "does not
represent the prospective buyer as the buyer's agent if the only act of real estate brokerage being
performed is showing real property under Section 1101.562," together with "the amount or rate of
compensation the broker will receive and how this amount will be determined"; and it must "disclose in
conspicuous language that broker compensation is not set by law and is fully negotiable." Two
constraints apply specifically to the showing-only agreement: under § 1101.563(e) it "may not: (1) be an
exclusive agreement with the license holder; or (2) state a termination date more than fourteen days
from the date the agreement is entered into." And under § 1101.563(d), a license holder who entered into
a showing-only agreement "shall enter into a separate agreement with the prospective buyer if additional
real estate brokerage acts are to be provided after showing the real property." Failure to enter into
the required written agreement is itself a disciplinary ground — § 1101.652(b)(34), added by the same
2025 act.

Why does a module on intermediary brokerage care about all this? Because the showing-only lane is the
single most common place where a licensee drifts into representing someone she never meant to represent.
The agreement says "does not represent." Then she answers three questions in the kitchen and the conduct
says otherwise.

**The behaviours that cause unintended dual representation.** Here they are, named, so that you can hear
yourself doing them.

*Advising the other side on price.* You represent the seller. The unrepresented buyer asks what he
should offer. You say "I'd start around $405,000 and you'll probably get it." You have just given
negotiating advice to the person your client is negotiating against. If you are inside § 1101.562, you
have violated subsection (a)(3). If you are not, you have created a factual record that you were acting
for him, which is precisely the material § 1101.652(b)(7) is made of. And you have almost certainly told
him something about your seller's flexibility, which — had you been an intermediary — § 1101.651(d)(1)
would forbid outright.

*"Helping" an unrepresented buyer write the offer.* Filling in the blanks, recommending an option fee,
suggesting a closing date that suits him, telling him what to ask for in repairs. Each of those is an act
of brokerage performed for him. Under § 1101.562(a)(4) it puts you outside the non-representation lane.
Under § 1101.563(b) it triggers the written-agreement requirement if the property is residential. And
under Rule 531.2 it is flatly inconsistent with the rule's first demand, that "the primary duty of the
license holder is to represent the interests of the client, and the license holder's position, in this
respect, should be clear to all parties concerned in a real estate transaction."

*Disclosing your client's motivation.* "They've already moved to Georgetown." "They need to close by the
fifteenth." "They've had it on the market a while." Each sentence hands the other side leverage. If you
are your seller's agent, Rule 535.156 requires you to put her interest above your own and owe her a duty
of fidelity, and you have done the opposite. If you are an intermediary or an appointed agent, § 1101.
651(d)(1) forbids disclosing that the seller will accept less than the asking price and § 1101.651(d)(3)
forbids disclosing confidential information or information the party has instructed you in writing not
to disclose — with the only exits being a separate writing from that party, a legal requirement or court
order, or information that materially relates to the condition of the property. A moving date is none of
those.

*Answering "what will they take?"* This is the same violation in interrogative form, and the correct
answer is a sentence you should have ready: "I'm not able to share that. If you'd like to make an offer,
I'll present it promptly." Section 1101.557(b)(3) obliges a broker who represents a party to at a
minimum answer the party's questions and present any offer to or from the party — that is your duty to
your own client. It is not a duty to answer the other side's questions about your client's position.

*Letting the relationship drift over weeks.* You show a buyer eleven houses on a showing-only agreement
that, under § 1101.563(e)(2), cannot state a termination date more than fourteen days out and, under
§ 1101.563(e)(1), cannot be exclusive. Three weeks in, you are sending him listings, discussing lenders,
and negotiating on his behalf. The paper says one thing; the conduct says another. Section 1101.563(d)
required a separate agreement the moment additional brokerage acts were to be provided.

*Talking directly to the other side's client.* Section 1101.652(b)(22) makes it grounds for discipline
to negotiate or attempt to negotiate the sale, exchange, or lease of real property "with an owner,
landlord, buyer, or tenant with knowledge that that person is a party to an outstanding written contract
that grants exclusive agency to another broker in connection with the transaction." Section 1101.557(b)(1)
forbids a broker who represents a party from instructing another broker to violate that subdivision
directly or indirectly. Section 1101.557(c)(3) supplies a narrow carve-out for delivery alone: the sole
delivery of an offer to a party does not violate § 1101.652(b)(22) if the party's broker consents to the
delivery, a copy of the offer is sent to the party's broker unless a governmental agency using a sealed
bid process does not allow it, and the person delivering the offer does not engage in another activity
that directly or indirectly violates § 1101.652(b)(22). Note how tightly drawn that is: consent, a copy
to the broker, and nothing else. Rule 535.153 draws a related line worth remembering — a license holder
"may not attempt to negotiate a sale, exchange, lease, or rental of property under exclusive listing with
another broker," but "the Act does not prohibit a license holder from soliciting a listing from the
owner while the owner's property is subject to an exclusive listing with another broker."

**The disciplinary exposure, quantified.** Unintended dual representation is not a soft error. Walk the
grounds it triggers.

Section 1101.652(b)(7): failing "to make clear to all parties to a real estate transaction the party for
whom the license holder is acting." Section 1101.652(b)(8): receiving "compensation from more than one
party to a real estate transaction without the full knowledge and consent of all parties to the
transaction." Under 22 TAC § 535.191 both sit in the $500 to $3,000 per violation per day band. Section
1101.652(b)(1) — acting "negligently or incompetently" — and § 1101.652(b)(2) — engaging "in conduct
that is dishonest or in bad faith or that demonstrates untrustworthiness" — sit at $1,000 to $5,000 per
violation per day, as do § 1101.558, § 1101.559(a) and (c), § 1101.560, § 1101.561(b), § 1101.651 and
22 TAC § 535.156. Section 1101.652(b)(34) — failing to enter into a written agreement with a prospective
buyer as required by § 1101.563 — sits at $100 to $1,500 per violation per day, as does 22 TAC
§ 535.157. Rule 535.191 adds that the Commission "may assess an additional administrative penalty of up
to two times that assessed under subsections (c), (d) and (e) of this section, subject to the maximum
penalties authorized under §1101.702(a) of the Act, if a person has a history of previous violations,"
and states at the outset that the Commission "may suspend or revoke a license or take other disciplinary
action authorized by the Act in addition to or instead of assessing the administrative penalties set
forth in this section."

The statutory cap is in § 1101.702(a): "The amount of an administrative penalty may not exceed $5,000
for each violation. Each day a violation continues or occurs may be considered a separate violation for
purposes of imposing a penalty." Read the second sentence again and do the arithmetic on a transaction
that ran forty days from first substantive communication to closing without the required written
consent. In setting the amount, § 1101.702(b) directs the executive director to consider "the seriousness
of the violation, including the nature, circumstances, extent, and gravity of the prohibited acts," "the
history of previous violations," "the amount necessary to deter a future violation," "efforts to correct
the violation," and "any other matter that justice may require."

Two more exposures complete the picture. Section 1101.803: "A licensed broker is liable to the
commission, the public, and the broker's clients for any conduct engaged in under this chapter by the
broker or by a sales agent associated with or acting for the broker." Your drift is your broker's
problem too. And § 1101.801: "Disciplinary action taken against a person under Section 1101.652 does not
relieve the person from civil or criminal liability." The Commission's penalty is not the end of it.

**Telling the two situations apart in practice.** The intentional case has documents: an intermediary
authorization in each representation agreement with the § 1101.651(d) prohibitions in conspicuous bold or
underlined print, the source of expected compensation stated, appointment notices in writing to all
parties, and a file that Rule 535.2 requires the broker to keep for at least four years from closing,
termination of the contract, or the end of the transaction. The unintended case has none of that, because
nobody decided anything — there are only text messages in which a licensee gave advice to somebody she
had never agreed to represent.

**What to do when you realise you have drifted.** The following is professional practice, not a statutory
procedure, and no held source prescribes it. Stop the conversation. Tell your broker the same day —
Rule 535.2 makes the broker responsible for your authorized acts and for acts beyond the scope she
authorized if she permitted them, and Rule 535.157 and Rule 535.2 both put a two-calendar-day response
obligation on the broker's side of that conversation. Then let the broker decide the posture: either the
brokerage obtains the written consents § 1101.559(a) requires and proceeds as intermediary — with
appointments if the consents authorize them and written notice under § 1101.560(b)(2) — or the brokerage
withdraws from one side. What you may not do is keep going and hope. Section 1101.651(d)(5) makes any
violation of the chapter a violation of the intermediary prohibition, and § 1101.652(b)(2) reaches
conduct that merely "demonstrates untrustworthiness."

**The sentence to keep in your mouth.** When a stranger at your listing asks you a question that only
their own agent should answer, the safe reply names your side and offers the service you are permitted to
give: "I represent the seller here, so I'm not able to advise you on what to offer. I can confirm the
size, the price and the terms, and I'll present any offer you make promptly." Every clause of that
sentence is doing statutory work — the first from § 1101.558(b), the second from § 1101.562(a)(3), the
third from § 1101.562(c), the fourth from § 1101.557(b)(3). Practise it until it is automatic, because
the moment you need it is never a moment you saw coming.

### Applied exercise: The open house on Guadalupe Street

1. Identify each of the four things you did in that forty minutes that took you outside the

2. Your sellers had told you about the Cedar Park purchase in confidence. Assume for this question

3. Section 1101.558(c)(3) excuses the written notice where "the communication occurs at a property that

4. The listing pays your broker three percent of the sales price. Compute the brokerage's fee at the

### Module recap

The through-line of this module is a single structural fact and the statutory machine Texas built around
it. One person cannot deliver undivided loyalty to two people who want opposite things. Rule 531.2
requires that a license holder's primary duty is to represent the interests of the client and that the
license holder place no personal interest above that of the client; Rule 535.156 requires the license
holder to convey to the principal all known information which would affect the principal's decision on
whether or not to make, accept or reject offers, subject to the written no-further-offers exception the
rule itself states, and to put the principal's interest above the license holder's own. Owed to two
adverse parties at once, those obligations contradict each other. No amount of consent makes them
performable.

Texas therefore did not create a diluted second form of agency. Search Chapter 1101 and you will find no
*dual agent* and no *dual agency* — only § 1101.652(b)(16), which concerns a license holder acting in a
dual capacity as broker and undisclosed principal, a different problem entirely. What the Act supplies is
the intermediary: under § 1101.551(1), "a broker who is employed to negotiate a transaction between the
parties to a transaction and for that purpose may act as an agent of the parties." And it is not
optional. Section 1101.561(b) provides that a broker "must agree to act as an intermediary under this
subchapter" if the broker agrees to represent both a buyer or tenant and a seller or landlord in a
transaction.

The capacity is created by written consent. Section 1101.559(a) permits a broker to act as intermediary
only if the broker obtains written consent from each party for the broker to act as intermediary in the
transaction and the written consent states the source of any expected compensation to the broker.
Section 1101.559(b) lets an ordinary listing agreement or buyer or tenant representation agreement carry
that consent, but only if the agreement "specifies in conspicuous bold or underlined print the conduct
that is prohibited under Section 1101.651(d)." Conspicuous bold or underlined print — not a point size.
The Act reserves point sizes for other things: at least a 10-point font for the § 1101.558(b-1) notice,
10-point and 12-point label options for the website link under 22 TAC § 531.20, and 12-point for the
Rule 535.17(c) price-opinion disclaimer.

Section 1101.651(d) is the confidentiality core, and it binds the intermediary broker *and* every license
holder appointed under § 1101.560. No disclosing that the seller or landlord will accept less than the
asking price, unless otherwise instructed in a separate writing by that party. No disclosing that the
buyer or tenant will pay more than the price submitted in a written offer, unless otherwise instructed in
a separate writing by that party. No disclosing confidential information or information a party has
instructed in writing not to be disclosed — unless a separate writing from that party says otherwise, the
chapter or a court order requires disclosure, or the information materially relates to the condition of
the property. No treating a party dishonestly. No violating the chapter. Learn the three exceptions in
subdivision (3) as carefully as you learn the prohibition, and never forget that property condition is
outside the cage: § 1101.652(b)(3) and (b)(4) stand behind it.

Appointments are the mechanism that restores advice. Only a broker who has complied with § 1101.559 may
appoint; only "a license holder associated with the broker" may be appointed; one licensee to one party
and another to any other party; and the appointment is valid only if the parties' written consent
authorized it and the broker provides written notice of the appointment to all parties involved in the
transaction. What the appointment buys is § 1101.560(c) — the appointed license holder "may provide
opinions and advice during negotiations to the party to whom the license holder is appointed." The broker
meanwhile "shall act fairly and impartially" under § 1101.559(c), which also deems the act of appointing
to be fair and impartial. Without appointments, the parties get an impartial conduit and no advice; with
appointments, each party gets an advisor operating inside the same § 1101.651(d) silence. Section
1101.561(a) makes the whole scheme coherent by providing that the intermediary's duties "supersede the
duties of a license holder established under any other law, including common law" — displacing other law,
not the rest of Chapter 1101, and not the Commission's rules adopted under it.

The specialised applications follow from the text. A sole-practitioner broker may be an intermediary but
cannot appoint, because she has no associated license holders. One sales agent cannot be appointed to
both parties, because § 1101.560(a) describes one license holder for one party and "another license
holder" for the other. Commercial and lease transactions are fully inside Subchapter L, while the
§ 1101.563 written-agreement mandate reaches only prospective buyers of residential real property as
defined in § 1101.563(a). A licensee dealing on her own account, or for a spouse, parent, child, a
business entity she owns more than ten percent of, or a trust she serves or benefits from, is in Rule
535.144 territory and must disclose her licence status in writing in the contract or in a writing given
before it — that is not an intermediary situation at all.

Unintended dual representation is the failure mode to fear. It is built out of ordinary friendliness:
advising the other side on price, helping an unrepresented buyer draft an offer, volunteering that your
seller has already moved, answering "what will they take?" Since January 1, 2026, § 1101.562 gives you a
defined non-representation lane, but its walls are close — no opinions or advice regarding the property
or real estate transactions in general, no other act of brokerage, with subsection (c) permitting you
only to confirm size, price and terms — and § 1101.563 requires a written agreement with a residential
buyer before you show anything, non-exclusive and no more than fourteen days if it is showing-only. When
you step outside, § 1101.652(b)(7) and (b)(8) are waiting, at $500 to $3,000 per violation per day under
22 TAC § 535.191, with § 1101.558, § 1101.559(a) and (c), § 1101.560, § 1101.561(b), § 1101.651 and
22 TAC § 535.156 in the $1,000 to $5,000 band, a statutory ceiling of $5,000 per violation under
§ 1101.702(a) with each day capable of being a separate violation, the broker liable alongside you under
§ 1101.803, and § 1101.801 confirming that discipline "does not relieve the person from civil or criminal
liability."

Two things were looked for and not found, and you should know it. The published text of the Information
About Brokerage Services notice, TREC No. IABS 1-2, was not held for this course; this module teaches
what § 1101.558(b-1) and 22 TAC § 531.20 require of that notice and does not quote the form. And no Texas
appellate decision construing § 1101.561(a) was held; the module therefore reads the supersession clause
narrowly, as displacing other law in favour of Subchapter L and doing nothing more.
