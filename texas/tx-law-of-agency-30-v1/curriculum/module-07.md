# Texas Law of Agency — Module 07

## TREC Topic: Creation and Termination of Agency

**Prescribed time: 85 minutes.** This module carries the three units the Commission's LOA-1 course
approval form places under *Creation and Termination of Agency*, whose scope note is *"Termination of
an agent's authority."*

**Learning objectives.** After completing this module you will be able to: identify the moment a
Texas agency relationship comes into existence; separate the three writing requirements Texas law
imposes and say which question each answers — Occupations Code § 1101.563 (may I act),
§ 1101.806(c) (may I sue for the fee), and Business & Commerce Code § 26.01 (is the promise
enforceable at all); explain why an agency created by conduct is a hazard rather than a technique;
list the events that end an agency and say which of them end the agent's authority immediately and
without notice; apply Occupations Code § 1101.367 to the end of a sales agent's sponsorship; and
state, from the held sources, which duties continue after the relationship ends — in particular the
accounting duty for trust money under 22 TAC § 535.146.

**Every legal statement in this module comes from a source that was read for this course.** Where a
statement is practice rather than law, the module says so and cites nothing for it.

**Where the underlying rules are taught.** This module is about beginnings and endings, and it
applies authorities that other modules teach in full. Those are cited here, not re-taught: the
fiduciary Canons and the IABS notice in Module 02; § 1101.806 in Module 04; the 2026
buyer-representation scheme of §§ 1101.562 and 1101.563 in Module 05; the intermediary machinery of
§§ 1101.559–.561 and § 1101.651(d) in Module 06; broker responsibility and record retention under
22 TAC § 535.2 and the compensation rules of § 1101.651(a)–(c) in Module 09; and § 1101.652 in
Module 10.

## Unit: How and When Agency is Created

Almost every disciplinary case and almost every lawsuit begins with a disagreement about one
question: *whose agent were you?* Duties attach to a relationship, so if you cannot say precisely
when the relationship began, you cannot say when the duties began.

Occupations Code § 1101.557(a), taught in Module 02, fixes the moment: "A broker who represents a
party in a real estate transaction or who lists real estate for sale under an exclusive agreement for
a party is that party's agent." Two independent triggers, joined by "or." The second is the
comfortable one — an exclusive listing, signed and sitting in the file. The first is the dangerous
one: it describes what you are actually doing, and it contains no signature requirement at all.

Two structural points follow, and both are taught elsewhere. The agency runs to the *broker*, not to
you: § 1101.351(c) bars a sales agent from brokerage "unless the sales agent is sponsored by a
licensed broker and is acting for that broker," and 22 TAC § 535.2 requires listings to be "solicited
and accepted in a broker's name" (Module 09). And the conduct that pulls you in is the list of
brokerage acts in § 1101.002(1)(A) with the definition of showing in 22 TAC § 535.4(c) (Module 01),
whose thresholds sit very low — attempting to negotiate, offering to aid in locating. Opening a
lockbox is a regulated act.

### Creation by written agreement — and where Texas now compels the writing

Texas has moved decisively toward the written agreement as the ordinary instrument of creation. Since
January 1, 2026, § 1101.563(b) has required a license holder who performs "any act of real estate
brokerage for a prospective buyer of residential real property" to "enter into a written agreement
with the prospective buyer before: (1) showing any residential real property to the prospective
buyer; or (2) if no residential real property will be shown, presenting an offer to purchase
residential real property on behalf of the prospective buyer." Two words carry the freight. "Any" —
any act of brokerage, not the important ones. And "before" — before the showing, not at the closing
table. If a buyer texts you on a Saturday morning in Frisco asking to see a house at two o'clock, the
agreement is signed before two o'clock or you do not open the door.

Module 05 teaches that section in full, together with § 1101.562, which lets a broker show property
without representing the party so long as the broker has not agreed to represent them "either orally
or in writing," is not otherwise acting as their agent, gives no "opinions or advice regarding the
real property or real estate transactions in general," and performs no other act of brokerage for
them. Take three consequences for creation. First, on residential buyer-side work Texas has largely
replaced the question *when did an agency arise* with the question *was there a writing before the
showing*, and § 1101.652(b)(34), effective the same date, makes the missing agreement its own ground
for discipline. Second, the contents § 1101.563(c) requires include the agreement's termination date,
so a creation statute now carries a termination term — the point the second unit picks up. Third, the
non-representation status is fragile: say that the price looks soft and the buyer should offer
$375,000 and you have given advice, lost the safe harbour, and performed brokerage for that buyer.

One further Texas writing creates a status rather than merely recording one: § 1101.559 permits a
broker to act as an intermediary only on written consent from each party stating the source of any
expected compensation. Intermediary status never arises by accident; it arises from that writing, and
Module 06 teaches the machinery.

### The writings that make a promise enforceable — a different question

Here is the distinction that separates a licensee who understands Texas law from one who has memorised
a list. A writing that TRELA requires *before you act* is not the same thing as a writing the law
requires *before you can sue*. Section 1101.563 is the first kind. Two other provisions are the
second kind, and they operate whether or not you complied with § 1101.563.

Occupations Code § 1101.806(c) provides: "A person may not maintain an action in this state to recover
a commission for the sale or purchase of real estate unless the promise or agreement on which the
action is based, or a memorandum, is in writing and signed by the party against whom the action is
brought or by a person authorized by that party to sign the document." That is the commission statute
of frauds, and it lives in the License Act, not in the Business & Commerce Code. Notice exactly who
must have signed: "the party against whom the action is brought." Your own signature proves nothing. A
seller in Waco who shakes your hand, tells three neighbors you have the listing, and then sells to
his brother-in-law has given you nothing you can sue on, because he never signed. The rest of
§ 1101.806 — the licensure gate in subsection (b), the carve-out in (a) for agreements to share
compensation among license holders, and the forfeiture in (d) — is taught in Module 04.

Then there is the general statute of frauds. Business & Commerce Code § 26.01(a) provides that "[a]
promise or agreement described in Subsection (b) of this section is not enforceable unless the promise
or agreement, or a memorandum of it, is (1) in writing; and (2) signed by the person to be charged
with the promise or agreement or by someone lawfully authorized to sign for him." Subsection (b)
applies that rule to, among other things, "a contract for the sale of real estate," "a lease of real
estate for a term longer than one year," and "an agreement which is not to be performed within one
year from the date of making the agreement." The last of those has teeth for listings: a fifteen-month
exclusive right to sell agreed orally in Midland is unenforceable on its face.

Now read § 26.01(b)(7) with care, because this is where courses routinely misstate Texas law.
Subdivision (7) covers "a promise or agreement to pay a commission for the sale or purchase of: (A) an
oil or gas mining lease; (B) an oil or gas royalty; (C) minerals; or (D) a mineral interest." That
subdivision is about *mineral* commissions. It is not the source of the writing requirement for an
ordinary residential commission; § 1101.806(c) is. If you are asked where Texas law requires a
commission agreement to be in writing, the honest answer names both provisions and keeps them
straight: § 1101.806(c) for the sale or purchase of real estate generally, § 26.01(b)(7) for
commissions on the mineral interests it lists.

Three writings, three questions, three different failures: § 1101.563 answers *may I act*, and
failing it is a disciplinary count; § 1101.806(c) answers *may I sue for the fee*, and failing it
loses the suit; § 26.01 answers *is the promise enforceable at all*. A file can satisfy one and fail
another.

### Creation without a signature: the common-law routes and why you must not rely on them

The general law of agency recognizes that agency can arise other than by a signed, formal
appointment — commonly grouped as express agreement, agency implied from conduct, ratification, and
agency by estoppel. **This course does not hold a Texas source that states the elements of
ratification or of agency by estoppel; those common-law elements were not held and are not taught
here as law.** What this course *does* hold is a set of Texas provisions that show the Legislature and
the Commission plainly contemplating agency that arises without a signature.

Section 1101.557(a) makes a broker "who represents a party" that party's agent, with no signature
requirement in the sentence. Section 1101.562(a)(1) conditions non-representation showings on the
broker not having "agreed with the party, either orally or in writing, to represent the party" — the
Legislature wrote "orally or in writing" because an oral agreement to represent someone is a real
thing with real consequences. Section 1101.562(a)(2) separately requires that the broker "is not
otherwise acting as the party's agent at the time of showing the real property," a condition that
would be redundant if agency could arise only by agreement. 22 TAC § 531.2 attaches fiduciary status
to a license holder "while acting as an agent for another" — the trigger is acting, not signing. And
under 22 TAC § 535.145, taught in Module 11, a "false promise" for purposes of § 1101.652(b)(5)
"includes both oral and written promises," and neither a contractual disclaimer of oral
representations nor the parol evidence rule stops the Commission considering what you said out loud.

So the honest teaching is this. Do not think of the non-written routes as *strategies*. Think of them
as *hazards*. Under § 1101.806(c) you cannot sue for a commission on an agency you created by conduct,
because there is no signed promise from the party you would be suing. Under § 1101.652(b)(34) the
missing § 1101.563 agreement is itself a ground for discipline. An accidentally created agency is the
worst of both worlds: full fiduciary exposure, no enforceable fee, and a disciplinary count.

### What not to do, concretely

Module 02 teaches the disclosure steps that keep the record clean — the representation disclosure at
first contact under § 1101.558(b) and the written notice at the first substantive communication under
§ 1101.558(b-1), in the form 22 TAC § 531.20 adopts as the Information About Brokerage Services
Notice, TREC No. IABS 1-2. **The IABS form is held for this course, but its text is not taught here;
Module 02 teaches 22 TAC § 531.20, the rule that adopts it, and this module does not quote the
form.** Two applications belong here rather than there. The exemption in § 1101.558(c)(3) for a
communication at a property "held open for any prospective buyer or tenant"
excuses the *notice* at an open house; it does not excuse the § 1101.563 writing when the property is
residential and the prospective buyer is unrepresented, nor § 1101.652(b)(7), which reaches a license
holder who "fails to make clear to all parties to a real estate transaction the party for whom the
license holder is acting." And do not create an agency on top of somebody else's: § 1101.652(b)(22)
reaches negotiating with a party you know is bound by an outstanding written exclusive agency to
another broker, though 22 TAC § 535.153 confirms you may still solicit the *future* listing.

### Statute reading exercise: The two 2026 sections, read side by side

   Reading them together, does § 1101.562 relieve a license holder of the § 1101.563 writing

2. Section 1101.563(a) defines "residential real property" in three subdivisions. Name two property

3. Section 1101.562(c) begins "Notwithstanding Subsections (a)(3) and (4)." Which two prohibitions
   does that clause qualify, and what exactly does it let you say?
4. Section 1101.563(e) limits showing-only agreements in two ways. State both, and explain why a

You are a sales agent sponsored by Vela Realty in Corpus Christi. Your brokerage does not have the

## Unit: How Agency is Terminated

An agency relationship is not a possession; it is a state of affairs that persists only while its
conditions persist. It can end because the parties finished what they set out to do, because a date
arrived, because somebody quit, or because a fact in the world changed and there is nothing left to be
an agent about. The scope note the Commission attached to this topic — "Termination of an agent's
authority" — is worded precisely. What ends is *authority*. Your right to be paid, your duty of
confidentiality, and your obligation to account for money can all outlive the authority, and the third
unit takes those up. This unit is about the authority itself.

### A distinction to carry through the whole unit: the right and the power

General agency law distinguishes a principal's *right* to end the relationship from the principal's
*power* to end it. The power is the raw ability to strip the agent of authority; the right is the
question of whether doing so breaches the contract. In the ordinary case a principal has both, and an
agent likewise has the power to walk away and may or may not have the right. **The common-law rule
that a principal always retains the power to revoke, and the exception traditionally described as an
agency coupled with an interest, were not held for this course.** No held Texas statute or Commission
rule states either proposition, and this module therefore does not state them as Texas law. What the
distinction is good for — and the reason it is worth carrying — is that it explains a pattern you can
verify in the held sources: repeatedly, Texas ends the *authority* the moment a triggering fact
occurs, and then handles the money separately.

The clearest held example is a sales agent's sponsorship, below. The clearest everyday example is the
difference between § 1101.652(b)(12), which forces every brokerage service contract other than
property management to carry "a definite termination date that is not subject to prior notice," and
§ 1101.806(c), which lets a broker sue on a signed commission promise after the authority under that
contract has expired. The contract's authority ends on the date. The signed promise is still a signed
promise.

### Completion of the purpose

The ordinary and happy way an agency ends is that it does what it was created to do. The listing
broker sells the house; the transaction closes; there is nothing further to perform. Chapter 1101 does
not announce this as a rule, because it does not need to — a contract to accomplish a thing is
discharged when the thing is accomplished. But the held sources show the Commission thinking in those
terms: 22 TAC § 535.2 measures record retention from "the date of closing, termination of the
contract, or end of a real estate transaction," treating those as three distinct ending events, and
§ 1101.652(b)(31) speaks of disbursement "before the completion or termination of the real estate
transaction."

One frequent misunderstanding: the acceptance of an offer does not end a listing. It fixes the
transaction being performed under it. Rule 22 TAC § 535.156(a), taught in Module 02, switches off
exactly one duty and only by writing — the license holder stops having to submit offers after
acceptance only "if the principal has agreed in writing that offers are not to be submitted after the
principal has entered into a contract to buy, sell, rent, or lease a property." Absent that written
agreement, back-up offers keep coming to you and you keep delivering them.

### Expiration of the stated term

Because § 1101.652(b)(12) requires a definite termination date in almost every brokerage service
contract, expiration is the second-most common ending. Read the qualifier, because students skip it
every time. The date must be *definite*, and it must *not be subject to prior notice* — so a clause
providing that the agreement continues until either party gives thirty days' notice does not satisfy
the section. And the section carves out "a contract to perform property management services," which
may run open-ended. An agreement that expires on December 31, 2026 is definite. One that expires
ninety days after either party notifies the other is not. A property management agreement may run
without a definite termination date; a listing may not.

When the date passes, the authority to act under that agreement is gone. You are not the seller's
agent on the day after the listing expires; if you show that property the next week, you are showing
it without authority.

Practise the arithmetic on a real agreement. A Lubbock listing signed on February 1, 2026, with a
stated termination date of July 31, 2026, at a total compensation of six percent on a home that would
sell for $240,000, is an authority that runs 181 days and a fee of $14,400 if the sale closes within
it. On August 1 the authority is gone and the $14,400 is gone with it — unless the agreement contains
a protection or override clause, which is a contractual survival term the third unit takes up. Notice
how naturally students slide from the listing having expired to the commission having been lost. Those
are two separate questions governed by two separate instruments: the termination date governs
authority; the signed writing governs the fee under § 1101.806(c).

### Mutual agreement

Parties who created a relationship by agreement may end it by agreement. Nothing in the held sources
forbids it, and the Commission assumes it happens: § 535.2 speaks of "termination of the contract" as
a record-retention trigger, and § 1101.652(b)(31) speaks of "termination of the real estate
transaction." Two practical rules follow, both of which are practice rather than statute and are
labelled as such. First, put the release in writing and date it, because you will otherwise be
arguing about the date on which your authority ended and on which the four-year retention clock under
§ 535.2 started. Second, deal with the money in the same document. A mutual release that says nothing
about a protection period or about earnest money in the broker's trust account has resolved the easy
half of the problem and left the hard half.

### Revocation by the principal and renunciation by the agent

A principal who tells the broker to stop has revoked. An agent who tells the principal it is quitting
has renounced. Texas law as held here does not enumerate these as terminating events, and **the Texas
case law fixing the measure of damages for a wrongful revocation or a wrongful renunciation was not
held for this course.** What can be said from the held sources is more useful than a half-remembered
damages formula.

First, a revocation does not vaporize a signed promise to pay. Section 1101.806(c) conditions an
action for a commission on a written, signed promise or memorandum; it does not condition it on the
agency still being alive when suit is filed. If the seller signed and then revoked, the writing still
exists and the seller is still the party against whom the action would be brought.

Second, the limitations clock is generous and it runs both ways. Civil Practice & Remedies Code
§ 16.004(a) provides that "[a] person must bring suit on the following actions not later than four
years after the day the cause of action accrues," and the list includes "debt" and "breach of
fiduciary duty." A broker's suit for an unpaid commission and a client's suit for breach of fiduciary
duty sit in the same four-year window. A relationship you ended in March 2026 can produce a fiduciary
duty petition in February 2030.

Third, renouncing badly is a regulatory problem, not only a contractual one. A license holder who
abandons a client mid-transaction is exposed under § 1101.652(b)(1), which reaches negligence or
incompetency in brokerage, and § 1101.652(b)(2), which reaches conduct "that is dishonest or in bad
faith or that demonstrates untrustworthiness." And the two-calendar-day response obligation in 22 TAC
§ 535.157 does not stop applying because you have decided to withdraw. Silence is not a method of
resignation.

### Death or incapacity of a party

Here the held sources give you something concrete, and it is worth more than the doctrine most courses
recite. When a Texas seller dies, the property does not sit in limbo waiting for probate. Estates Code
§ 101.001(a) provides that if a person dies leaving a lawful will, "all of the person's estate that is
devised by the will vests immediately in the devisees" and "all of the person's estate that is not
devised by the will vests immediately in the person's heirs at law"; subsection (b) provides that "the
estate of a person who dies intestate vests immediately in the person's heirs at law." Both are
subject to § 101.051, under which the estate vests subject to the payment of "the debts of the
decedent, except as exempted by law" and "any court-ordered child support payments that are delinquent
on the date of the decedent's death."

Follow the consequence for your listing. Your listing agreement was signed by a person who owned the
property. At the instant of death, title vests in someone else — devisees or heirs. The person who
gave you authority no longer owns the thing you were authorized to sell, and the new owners never
agreed to anything with you. That is why a listing does not survive the owner's death as a practical
matter, whatever a course tells you about common-law rules, and this course does not need those rules
to reach the answer. Estates Code § 101.003 completes the picture: on issuance of letters testamentary
or of administration, "the executor or administrator has the right to possession of the estate as the
estate existed at the death of the testator or intestate," and shall recover and hold the estate "in
trust to be disposed of in accordance with the law."

Chapter 1101 anticipates that you will end up dealing with those representatives. Section 1101.551(2)
defines "Party," for the representation subchapter, as "a prospective buyer, seller, landlord, or
tenant or an authorized representative of a buyer, seller, landlord, or tenant, including a trustee,
guardian, executor, administrator, receiver, or attorney-in-fact." The term "does not include a
license holder who represents a party." So the executor of the Hidalgo County estate is a party with
whom you may contract; but you contract afresh, with a new signature and a new definite termination
date under § 1101.652(b)(12).

**On incapacity, the held sources are thinner and you should know it.** Section 1101.551(2) recognizes
a guardian and an attorney-in-fact as authorized representatives, and § 1101.005(2) exempts from
Chapter 1101 "an attorney-in-fact authorized under a power of attorney to conduct not more than three
real estate transactions annually." **The Texas statute governing durable powers of attorney, and any
rule about whether an agency survives a principal's incapacity, were not held for this course and are
not stated here.** If a client's capacity becomes doubtful mid-listing, that is a question for the
broker and for counsel, not for your judgment in the field.

### Destruction of the subject property

If the four-plex in Beaumont that you listed burns to the foundation, there is nothing left to sell in
the condition the agreement described. **A Texas statute or Commission rule stating that destruction
of the subject matter terminates an agency was not held for this course**, and this module does not
manufacture one. What the held sources do tell you is what your obligations look like the morning
after the fire. Rule 22 TAC § 535.156(c) imposes "an affirmative duty to keep the principal informed
at all times of significant information applicable to the transaction or transactions in which the
license holder is acting as agent for the principal," and a total loss is significant information.
Section 1101.652(b)(3) and (b)(4) continue to make it a disciplinary matter to misrepresent to a
potential buyer a significant defect known to the license holder, or to fail to disclose one. Whatever
happens to the agency, the property's condition does not become somebody else's problem.

### Bankruptcy

**Chapter 1101 does not state that a party's bankruptcy terminates an agency, and no held source in
this course does; that rule was not held and is not taught here as Texas law.** The only place
bankruptcy appears in the held chapter is the Real Estate Recovery Trust Account subchapter —
§ 1101.606(c) addresses an aggrieved person "precluded by action of a bankruptcy court from executing
a judgment or perfecting a judgment lien." That is a recovery-fund provision, not an
agency-termination provision. If a seller files, the practical reality is that the property becomes
subject to a federal proceeding and the person who signed your listing may no longer control it — but
the governing law is federal bankruptcy law, which this course does not hold, and the correct
professional response is to escalate to your broker.

### Termination of the sales agent's sponsorship

This is the terminating event most likely to happen to *you*, and it is the one Texas law states with
the most precision. Section 1101.351(c) makes sponsorship a condition of acting at all: a sales agent
"may not engage or attempt to engage in real estate brokerage unless the sales agent is sponsored by a
licensed broker and is acting for that broker." Section 1101.367(a) then supplies the mechanics: "When
the relationship of a sales agent with the sales agent's sponsoring broker terminates, the terminating
party shall immediately notify in writing both the other party and the commission. On receiving the
written notice, the commission shall place the sales agent license on inactive status."

Take that apart. The duty to notify falls on "the terminating party" — whichever side ended it. The
notice is written, it goes to *two* recipients, the other party and the Commission, and the timing
word is "immediately," not *promptly* and not *within thirty days*. And the consequence is automatic:
on receipt, the license goes inactive. An inactive sales agent has no authority to perform brokerage
for anyone, because § 1101.351(c) is unsatisfied. Every agency in which you were acting for the
brokerage ends as to you at that moment — not because you resigned from the client, but because you
lost the only lawful channel through which you could act.

Note the direction of travel. The client's agency was with the *broker*, and it does not follow you
out the door. The listing on the ranch outside Bandera belongs to your former brokerage, and the
seller's relationship is with that firm unless the seller and the firm agree otherwise. Section
1101.367(b) tells you how to get back to work: the Commission may return the license to active status
if, before the expiration date of the sales agent license, a licensed broker files a request advising
the Commission that the broker assumes sponsorship, accompanied by the appropriate fee. Subsection (c)
adds that an inactive sales agent whose license is not subject to the § 1101.454 education
requirements must, as a condition of returning to active status, provide proof of attending
continuing education as specified by § 1101.455 during the two years preceding the date the
application to return to active status is filed.

The mirror image also exists. Section 1101.366(c) provides that "[a] broker applying for inactive
status shall terminate the broker's association with each sales agent sponsored by the broker by
giving written notice to each sales agent before the 30th day preceding the date the broker applies
for inactive status." Thirty days' written notice, running backward from the application date. A
broker on inactive status, under § 1101.366(d), "may not perform any activity regulated under this
chapter" and "must pay renewal fees."

**Delegated supervision ends the same way — in writing, on the record.** Broker responsibility is
Module 09's subject, but the endings inside it belong here: under 22 TAC § 535.2 a sponsored sales
agent's authorized scope may be limited or revoked only *in writing*, any delegation of supervisory
responsibility must itself be in writing, and the broker must notify the Commission within 30 days
after a reportable delegation ends, just as at its start. An oral instruction not to handle
commercial leases leaves the broker responsible for commercial leases.

### Suspension or revocation of the license

A license the Commission suspends stops functioning as authority. Under 22 TAC § 535.141(g), taught
in Module 10, a suspended license holder "may not during the period of any suspension perform,
attempt to perform, or advertise to perform any act for which a license is required by the Act or
Commission rules," and subsection (h) requires written notice not later than the third day before the
suspension takes effect to everyone it will cut off. The item to remember is the last on that list: a
person who "holds money in trust in any transaction in which the person is acting as a broker" must
"remit such money in accordance with the instructions of the principals." Even where the regulator is
taking the authority away, the trust money must still be dealt with. That is the bridge into the
final unit.

### Applied exercise: Leaving Delgado & Finch

You are a sales agent at Delgado & Finch in Round Rock. On Monday, June 8, you accept a position at
another brokerage and email your broker your resignation. You are the listing agent on 703 Crestwood
Loop, listed by Delgado & Finch at $415,000 with a total compensation of 5.5 percent, listing signed
March 2 with a termination date of September 2. A buyer's offer on Crestwood is due to be presented
Tuesday, June 9. You also hold a $5,000 earnest money check a buyer handed you on Friday, June 5, for
a different property.

1. Under § 1101.367(a), who must notify the Commission, in what form, and by when? Name both
   recipients of the notice.
2. Once the Commission places your license on inactive status, may you present Tuesday's offer on
   Crestwood? Answer using § 1101.351(c), and say who at Delgado & Finch may present it instead.
3. Whose listing is 703 Crestwood Loop after your departure, and why? Use § 1101.557(a) and the

## Unit: Duties of Agency that Survive Termination

The most expensive mistake in this module is the assumption that a closed file means the duties are
over. They are not, and the held sources tell you exactly which ones continue. Three survive as
matters of law — confidentiality, accounting for trust money, and record retention — and one survives
because you wrote it into a contract, namely the protection or override period. A fifth, your
exposure to suit, survives on the four-year limitations clock in Civil Practice & Remedies Code
§ 16.004(a).

### Confidentiality

The one held provision that squarely prohibits disclosure of a client's confidential information is
Occupations Code § 1101.651(d), taught in Module 06 with the intermediary machinery it belongs to. It
binds a broker, and any broker or sales agent appointed under § 1101.560, "who acts as an
intermediary under Subchapter L." What matters after termination is subdivision (d)(3), so read it
whole: such a person may not "disclose any confidential information or any information a party
specifically instructs the broker or sales agent in writing not to disclose, unless: (A) the broker or
sales agent is otherwise instructed in a separate writing by the respective party; (B) the broker or
sales agent is required to disclose the information by this chapter or a court order; or (C) the
information materially relates to the condition of the property." Those three exceptions are the whole
of it, and (C) is the one that saves careers and ends them: confidentiality never covers the condition
of the house. Outside the intermediary role, the held sources rest the obligation on the fiduciary
standard rather than on a specific non-disclosure statute — 22 TAC §§ 531.2 and 535.156(b), taught in
Module 02. Using a former client's negotiating limits to advantage a new client, or yourself, places
an interest above the former client's.

Now the honest limit, and state it exactly this way in an exam answer. **No held source in this course
fixes a duration for the duty of confidentiality after an agency terminates. A period was not held.**
Section 1101.651(d) states no expiry. Rule 531.2 states no expiry. Rule 535.156 states no expiry. Any
number you have heard — one year, two years, forever — does not come from a source held for this
course, and you should not repeat it as Texas law. What you can say with authority is that
§ 1101.651(d)(3) permits disclosure in exactly three circumstances, none of which is the passage of
time. Absent one of those, the safe and defensible professional position — practice, not statute — is
that you do not disclose, and that if a former client's information becomes relevant in a new
transaction, you take the question to your broker before you open your mouth. One further
confidentiality provision survives by relieving you of an obligation rather than imposing one:
§ 1101.556, taught in Module 03, on AIDS, HIV and deaths on the property. It does not expire with the
listing either.

### The accounting duty for trust money

Money is where the claim that the deal is over gets tested, and 22 TAC § 535.146 is the rule that
tests it. Start with its definition, which is broader than most licensees expect: "Trust money" means
"client's money, earnest money, rent, unearned fees, security deposits, or any money held on behalf of
another person." Unearned fees and security deposits are in that list. So is any money at all held on
behalf of another person.

Section 535.146(b)(1) states the character of the holding: "Any trust money accepted by a broker is
held in a fiduciary capacity and must be maintained in a designated trust account maintained by the
broker or delivered to an escrow agent authorized in Texas in accordance with the agreement of the
principals of the transaction." Subsection (b)(2) is absolute for sales agents: "A sales agent shall
not maintain a trust account. Any trust money received by a sales agent must be immediately delivered
to the sales agent's sponsoring broker." Subsection (b)(3) sets the deposit deadline: unless the
principals expressly agree in writing on a different time, trust money the broker receives must be
deposited in a trust account or delivered to an authorized escrow agent within a reasonable time,
"which the Commission has determined to be not later than the close of business of the second working
day after the date the broker receives the trust money."

Those are duties during the relationship. Here is what continues after it. Section 535.146(c)(6)
requires that the broker "retain a documentary record of each deposit or withdrawal from the trust
account and provide an accounting to each beneficiary of trust money at least monthly if there has
been any activity in the account." Read the trigger: activity, not an open transaction. A terminated
listing whose earnest money is still sitting in the account and moves for any reason generates an
accounting to the beneficiary that month. Section 535.146(c)(5) governs the broker's own money: "If a
broker acquires ownership of trust money held in a trust account, including entitlement to
compensation, such money must be removed from the trust account not later the 30th day after the date
the broker acquires ownership of the money." That is a survival duty in the sharpest sense — the
transaction is over, the broker has earned the fee, and the rule now runs a clock against the broker,
because leaving earned commission in a trust account is a commingling risk. Section 535.146(b)(5)
provides that placing trust money in a broker's personal or operating account, or paying operating
expenses or making withdrawals from a trust account for any purpose other than proper disbursement of
trust money, is prima facie evidence of commingling.

Disbursement after a dead deal is the scenario to master. Section 535.146(d)(1) provides that "[a]
broker may only disburse money from the broker's trust account in accordance with the agreement under
which the money was received." Section 535.146(d)(2) then supplies the demand rule: "If any or all of
the parties to a real estate transaction make a written demand for payment of trust money, the broker
must pay the trust money to the party or parties entitled to the money within a reasonable time, which
the Commission has determined to be not later than the 30th day after the date the demand is made."
Work it: a written demand delivered on June 12 must be answered with payment to whoever is entitled no
later than July 12. Section 535.146(d)(3) applies the same thirty-day clock where all parties later
agree in writing to a different disbursement, measured from the date the broker receives the
subsequent written agreement, and § 535.146(d)(4) requires the broker to "immediately notify all
parties in writing of any disbursement of trust money" under either provision. And when the broker
genuinely cannot tell who is entitled, § 535.146(d)(5) provides the exit: "If the broker cannot
reasonably determine to which party or parties the trust money should be paid, the broker may pay the
trust money into the registry of a court and interplead the parties." Interpleader is permissive, not
mandatory, and it is what a careful broker does instead of guessing between two angry parties.

The statute stands behind the rule: § 1101.652(b)(9), (b)(10), (b)(30) and (b)(31) reach, in turn, a
failure to account for or remit another person's money within a reasonable time, commingling,
depositing outside an authorized trust or escrow arrangement, and disbursing "before the completion or
termination of the real estate transaction." Note the phrasing of the last — the trust obligation is
defined by the *transaction's* life, not the agency's.

Finally, § 535.146(e) — subsection (e), the rule's own "Records" subsection, not (f): "A broker must
maintain all documentation regarding a trust account for four years from the date the document is
received or created by the broker." Four years, per document, from receipt or creation. That is a
different measuring point from the general brokerage records rule below, and the two are worth keeping
separate in your head.

### Contractual survival: protection and override periods

A protection period — sometimes called an override or a carryover — is a clause in a listing or
representation agreement providing that if the property is sold within a stated number of days after
the agreement ends to a person the broker introduced during the term, compensation is still owed.
**The held sources for this course do not prescribe protection-period language, a maximum length, or a
naming requirement; those terms were not held and this module does not state any.** What the held
sources do tell you is the framework that makes such a clause enforceable or worthless. It must be in
the signed writing, because § 1101.806(c) conditions any commission action on a signed promise or
memorandum; a protection period agreed to orally when the seller asked you to stop marketing is not a
promise you can sue on. It runs from the definite termination date § 1101.652(b)(12) requires, since a
period measured from an indefinite date is not measurable. And the paperwork proving the claim is
exactly what 22 TAC § 535.2 already obliges the broker to keep — "commission agreements such as
listing agreements, buyer representation agreements, or other written agreements relied upon to claim
compensation."

**Compensation across a change of sponsorship survives too, and Texas says so expressly.** Module 09
teaches the compensation rules; the survival clause inside them is this module's business. Section
1101.651(b) provides that "[a] sales agent may not accept compensation for a real estate transaction
from a person other than the broker that is sponsoring the sales agent or was sponsoring the sales
agent when the sales agent earned the compensation." That final clause is a survival rule written
into a prohibition. 22 TAC § 535.3 says the same in terms of consent, requiring "the written consent
of the sales agent's sponsoring broker or the broker who sponsored the sales agent when the sales
agent became entitled to the commission or other valuable consideration." So a fee earned at your old
brokerage may still reach you, through that brokerage and through no one else — not from the client,
not from the title company as a direct payee, and not from the other side's broker.

### Records — and the documents that belong to the client

Record retention is taught in Module 09 with the rest of broker responsibility. What this module
needs is the measuring point: 22 TAC § 535.2 requires the listed brokerage records to be kept "for at
least four years from the date of closing, termination of the contract, or end of a real estate
transaction" — so a listing that terminates without a sale on August 31, 2026 starts its own clock to
August 31, 2030 even though nothing closed. Two entries on that list bite hardest at the end of a
relationship: *substantive communications*, which means the texts and emails and not just the signed
forms, so that a departing sales agent who wipes a phone has destroyed the broker's records; and
*sponsorship agreements between the broker and sponsored sales agents*, so that the paperwork of the
relationship you just ended is itself a four-year record.

Keeping records is not the same as keeping the client's property. Two grounds in § 1101.652, taught
in Module 10, survive termination on their face because neither is limited to a live agency:
subsection (a)(5) reaches a failure to surrender to the owner, "without just cause," a document the
owner requests and the license holder holds, and (b)(28) reaches a refusal to give a person who
signed a transaction document a copy of it on request. Refusing to hand back a former seller's survey
because you are in a fee dispute is precisely the conduct (a)(5) describes, and being owed money is
unlikely to be the "just cause" the statute contemplates.

### Module recap

Agency in Texas is a state of affairs with a beginning, an end, and a tail. The beginning is fixed by
Occupations Code § 1101.557(a): a broker "who represents a party in a real estate transaction or who
lists real estate for sale under an exclusive agreement for a party is that party's agent" — two
triggers, one of which needs no signature at all. Since January 1, 2026, § 1101.563(b) has required a
written agreement before showing any residential real property to a prospective buyer or, if none will
be shown, before presenting an offer on the buyer's behalf; the section itself is taught in Module 05,
and § 1101.652(b)(34) makes the failure a disciplinary ground.

Keep the three writing rules separate, because they answer three different questions. Section 1101.563
answers *may I act*. Section 1101.806(c) answers *may I sue for the fee*, and it demands a writing
signed by "the party against whom the action is brought." Business & Commerce Code § 26.01 answers *is
this promise enforceable at all*, reaching a contract for the sale of real estate, a lease longer than
one year, an agreement not to be performed within one year, and — a point courses get wrong —
commissions on oil and gas leases, royalties, minerals and mineral interests under § 26.01(b)(7), not
ordinary real estate commissions. Section 1101.559 adds a fourth writing that creates a status:
written consent from each party stating the source of any expected compensation, before a broker may
act as intermediary.

Agency you did not intend is a hazard, not a technique: § 1101.562 tolerates a showing without
representation only inside four conditions, 22 TAC § 531.2 attaches fiduciary status to "acting as an
agent" rather than to signing, and 22 TAC § 535.145 tells you that neither a disclaimer of oral
representations nor the parol evidence rule will stop the Commission from considering what you said
out loud. **The common-law elements of ratification and of agency by estoppel were not held for this
course and were not taught here as Texas law.**

Termination comes in a predictable set: completion of the purpose; expiration of the definite
termination date § 1101.652(b)(12) requires in every brokerage service contract except property
management; mutual agreement; revocation by the principal; renunciation by the agent; death, where
Estates Code § 101.001 vests the estate immediately in devisees or heirs so that the person who signed
no longer owns the property; destruction of the subject property; bankruptcy; suspension or revocation
of the license under 22 TAC § 535.141(g); and — the one most likely to happen to you — the end of a
sales agent's sponsorship. Section 1101.367(a) requires the terminating party to notify in writing
both the other party and the Commission "immediately," whereupon the license goes inactive, and
§ 1101.351(c) then bars the sales agent from engaging or attempting to engage in brokerage. Section
1101.366(c) gives the mirror rule for a broker going inactive: written notice to each sponsored sales
agent before the 30th day preceding the application.

**Four things were not held and are not stated here as Texas law: any authority that bankruptcy
terminates an agency; any authority that destruction of the subject property terminates an agency;
the common-law power-versus-right distinction, including the agency coupled with an interest; and the
measure of damages for wrongful revocation or renunciation. The Texas durable power of attorney
statute, and any rule about whether an agency survives a principal's incapacity, were not held
either.**

Three duties survive as law. Confidentiality: § 1101.651(d)(3) forbids disclosure of confidential
information or information a party instructed be withheld in writing, unless separately instructed in
writing by that party, unless required to disclose by Chapter 1101 or a court order, or unless "the
information materially relates to the condition of the property." **No held source fixes a duration
for post-termination confidentiality; a period was not held.** Trust money: 22 TAC § 535.146 defines
trust money to include client's money, earnest money, rent, unearned fees, security deposits and any
money held on behalf of another person; requires deposit not later than the close of business of the
second working day after receipt; requires an accounting to each beneficiary at least monthly if there
has been activity; requires the broker to remove money the broker comes to own, including earned
compensation, not later than the 30th day after acquiring ownership; requires payment on a written
demand not later than the 30th day after the demand; permits interpleader where the broker cannot
reasonably determine entitlement; and, in subsection (e), requires trust account documentation to be
kept four years from receipt or creation. Records: 22 TAC § 535.2 requires the listed brokerage
records to be kept at least four years from closing, termination of the contract, or end of the
transaction.

Two more survivals round it out. Compensation: § 1101.651(b) lets a sales agent accept compensation
from "the broker that is sponsoring the sales agent or was sponsoring the sales agent when the sales
agent earned the compensation," and 22 TAC § 535.3 says the same in terms of written consent — so a
fee earned at your old brokerage may still be paid through that brokerage, and through no one else.
**The held sources prescribe no protection-period language, maximum length or naming requirement;
those terms were not held.** Liability: Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 16.004(a) gives four years
from accrual for "debt" and for "breach of fiduciary duty," which is the real answer to a licensee who
asks how long an old file can come back. The authority ends on a date; the obligations of the
relationship keep running long after it.
