# Texas Law of Agency — Module 09

## TREC Topic: Employment Issues

**Prescribed time: 120 minutes.** This module carries the six units the Commission's LOA-1 course
approval form places under *Employment Issues*, whose scope note is *"Employment law."*

**Learning objectives.** After completing this module you will be able to: state the four
conditions in Labor Code § 201.072 and the three conditions in 26 U.S.C. § 3508(b)(1) that
together determine whether a licensed Texas agent's service is excluded from "employment," and
identify the clauses a written independent contractor agreement must carry to satisfy them;
describe what Labor Code chapter 21 prohibits, who counts as an "employer" and an "employee" under
§ 21.002, and state honestly where chapter 21 stops at the edge of a genuine independent
contractor relationship; explain why the broker — not the sales agent — is the person the client
employs under § 1101.557(a), and separate that sense of "employment" from labour-law employment;
determine, from 22 TAC §§ 535.4, 535.5, 535.20 and 535.147, exactly which tasks an
unlicensed personal assistant may perform, which are prohibited, and who may lawfully pay the
assistant; apply every subsection of 22 TAC § 535.2 to a working brokerage, including the written
scope notice, the delegation and Commission-notice mechanics, the four-year records list and the
written policies and procedures; and route every dollar of compensation correctly under 22 TAC
§ 535.3, § 1101.651(b), § 535.147 and § 535.148, including compensation earned before a change of
sponsorship.

**Every legal statement in this module comes from a source that was read for this course.** Where
a statement is practice rather than law, the module says so and cites nothing for it.

## Unit: Independent Contract Agreements

Within a week or two of activating your licence you will be handed a document with a title like
*Independent Contractor Agreement*, and you will be asked to sign it along with the sponsorship
paperwork. Most new agents sign it without reading it, on the assumption that it is an
administrative formality that describes an arrangement the industry has already settled. That
assumption is exactly backwards. Two statutes — one Texas, one federal — make the existence of a
signed written contract a *condition* of the status each of them confers, and each confers it for
one narrow purpose only. The Texas statute decides whether your service counts as "employment" for
**unemployment compensation** purposes. The federal statute decides **federal tax treatment**.
Neither decides whether you are an employee for any other purpose, and — this is the point most
courses get wrong — neither one touches your broker's duty to supervise you. If the written
document is missing, or if it omits one particular sentence, the status it was meant to secure is
unavailable for those two purposes no matter what everyone involved believed.

Start with why the category exists at all. Texas Labor Code chapter 201 is the state's
unemployment compensation statute, and the word that carries the tax in that statute is
"employment." Employers pay unemployment tax on wages paid for "employment"; if a service is not
"employment," no unemployment tax is owed on the remuneration for it and the person performing it
builds no unemployment benefit entitlement from it. Because that single word decides who pays and
who collects, the Legislature wrote a long run of sections carving specific trades out of it.
Section 201.070 excludes product demonstrators and direct sellers. Section 201.071 excludes an
insurance agent whose only remuneration is a commission. Section 201.073 excludes certain private
delivery drivers and youth newspaper carriers. Section 201.074 excludes inmates. Section 201.075
excludes crew on small fishing vessels. Sitting in the middle of that run, real estate has a
section of its own.

**The Texas provision, and exactly what it decides.** Labor Code § 201.072, headed SERVICE AS REAL
ESTATE BROKER, sits inside the unemployment compensation subtitle and opens with the words "In
this subtitle". Everything it does is confined to that subtitle. It reads in full:

> Sec. 201.072. SERVICE AS REAL ESTATE BROKER. In this subtitle, "employment" does not include:
> (1) service performed by an individual as a real estate broker or salesperson if: (A) the
> individual engages in activity described by the definition of " broker" in Section 1101.002,
> Occupations Code; (B) the individual is licensed as a broker or salesperson by the Texas Real
> Estate Commission; (C) substantially all remuneration for the service, whether in cash or other
> form of payment, is directly related to sales or other output, including the performance of the
> service, and not to the number of hours worked; and (D) the service is performed under a written
> contract between the individual and the person for whom the service is performed, and the
> contract provides that the individual is not treated as an employee with respect to the service
> for federal tax purposes; or (2) service performed by an individual as an instructor of a person
> licensed or seeking a license as a real estate broker or salesperson if: (A) the individual
> instructs in an educational program or course approved by the Texas Real Estate Commission; and
> (B) the service is performed under a written contract between the individual and the person for
> whom the service is performed and the contract provides that the individual is not treated as an
> employee with respect to the service for federal tax purposes.

The stray space inside the quotation marks around "broker" in paragraph (1)(A) is in the statute's
published text; it is reproduced here rather than silently corrected, because this course quotes
what the source says.

Before working through the conditions, fix the boundary, because the rest of this module depends
on it. Section 201.072 answers one question: whether the service is "employment" for the purposes
of the subtitle that contains it, which is the unemployment compensation system administered by
the Texas Workforce Commission. It does not decide whether a sales agent is an employee for
wage-and-hour purposes, for workers' compensation, for discrimination law, for tort or vicarious
liability, or at common law. Those questions belong to other bodies of law, and the authorities
that would answer them — the Fair Labor Standards Act, the Texas Payday Law, Texas workers'
compensation law, and Texas case law on common-law employment status and vicarious liability —
**were not held** for this course. This module therefore does not tell you whether a Texas sales
agent is an employee for any of those purposes. It tells you what § 201.072 decides, which is
narrower and far more reliable.

Take the four conditions in paragraph (1) one at a time, because each one is a place where a real
brokerage can go wrong. Condition (A) ties the exclusion to the *work*: the individual must be
engaged in activity described by the definition of "broker" in Occupations Code § 1101.002. That
definition is the familiar list — a person who, for a commission or other valuable consideration
or with the expectation of it, "sells, exchanges, purchases, or leases real estate," "negotiates
or attempts to negotiate the listing, sale, exchange, purchase, or lease of real estate," "lists
or offers, attempts, or agrees to list real estate for sale, lease, or exchange," and the rest of
the twelve acts listed there. Condition (A) is doing real work: it means the exclusion covers the
licensed *brokerage* activity, not everything a person might do for a brokerage. A sales agent who
also runs the office's bookkeeping for an hourly wage is not performing activity described by the
broker definition when doing the bookkeeping.

Condition (B) requires that the individual be licensed as a broker or salesperson by the Texas
Real Estate Commission. Note the statute's own vocabulary here. Chapter 201 says "salesperson,"
which is the word the Legislature used in 1993; TRELA and the Commission's rules now say "sales
agent." They mean the same licence. Note also what condition (B) implies about the lapsed or
inactive licensee: an unlicensed person performing services for a brokerage cannot use § 201.072
at all, because condition (B) fails on its face.

Condition (C) is the pay condition, and it is the one brokerages most often break by accident.
"[S]ubstantially all remuneration for the service, whether in cash or other form of payment, is
directly related to sales or other output, including the performance of the service, and not to
the number of hours worked." Commission on closings is output-based pay and satisfies this easily.
A referral bonus tied to a transaction is output-based. A per-transaction administrative fee
credit is output-based. What is *not* output-based is anything measured by time: an hourly rate
for sitting floor duty, a monthly guaranteed salary that does not depend on production, a stipend
for attending Tuesday sales meetings. A brokerage in Sugar Land that pays its new agents $15 an
hour to staff the front desk on rotation, and commission on closings, has created a
mixed-remuneration problem. Whether the arrangement still satisfies "substantially all" depends on
what proportion of remuneration the hourly pay represents, and here is the honest answer: neither
§ 201.072 nor 26 U.S.C. § 3508 defines "substantially all," and no Treasury regulation, Internal
Revenue Service ruling, or Texas Workforce Commission rule construing the phrase was held for this
course. The threshold is therefore not taught here, and you should not accept a number for it from
anyone who cannot show you the source.

Condition (D) is the written contract, and it has two parts that must both be present. First, "the
service is performed under a written contract between the individual and the person for whom the
service is performed." Second — and this is the sentence people leave out — "the contract provides
that the individual is not treated as an employee with respect to the service for federal tax
purposes." A written agreement that says the agent is an independent contractor, sets a commission
split, allocates expenses and says nothing about federal tax treatment does not satisfy condition
(D) as written. The statute does not ask whether the parties intended non-employee treatment; it
asks whether the contract *provides* it. When you read your own agreement, find that clause. If it
is not there, that is a question for your broker before you sign, not after a Texas Workforce
Commission audit.

**The second clause, about instructors.** Paragraph (2) of § 201.072 excludes service by "an
individual as an instructor of a person licensed or seeking a license as a real estate broker or
salesperson" on two conditions: the individual "instructs in an educational program or course
approved by the Texas Real Estate Commission," and the service is performed under a written
contract containing the same federal-tax-purposes provision. This matters to more agents than you
would guess, because experienced licensees routinely teach qualifying and continuing education for
a school on the side. Notice the difference in structure: paragraph (2) has no output-based pay
condition. An instructor may be paid by the classroom hour and still fall outside "employment,"
provided the course is Commission-approved and the written contract carries the federal-tax
clause. The Legislature dropped condition (C) for instructors on purpose, because teaching is
inherently time-measured.

**What § 201.072 conspicuously does not require.** Read the four conditions again and list what is
missing: there is no requirement that the agent be free from the broker's control, no requirement
that the agent set their own hours, no requirement that the agent supply their own vehicle or pay
their own expenses, no requirement that the agent be free to accept or reject assignments. That
absence is not an oversight, and you can prove it by looking two sections earlier. Section
201.070, the product demonstrator exclusion, conditions the exclusion on nine control factors,
including that the individual "determines the method of performing the service," "is free from
control by the principal business as to where the individual works," and "controls solely
opportunity for profit or loss." Section 201.073, the delivery service exclusion, has a nearly
identical control list, including that the individual "may accept or reject a job from the
delivery service" and "is free from control by the delivery service as to when the individual
works." The Legislature plainly knew how to make freedom from control a condition of an exclusion.
In § 201.072 it chose not to. That is the single most important structural point in this unit, and
it is the point that resolves the tension you are about to meet in Unit 5: a Texas broker can
supervise a sales agent as closely as the Commission's rules require without endangering the
§ 201.072 exclusion, because control is not one of the four conditions.

**The federal side.** For federal tax purposes the governing provision is 26 U.S.C. § 3508,
"Treatment of real estate agents and direct sellers." Subsection (a) states the general rule: "For
purposes of this title, in the case of services performed as a qualified real estate agent or as a
direct seller-" then "(1) the individual performing such services shall not be treated as an
employee, and (2) the person for whom such services are performed shall not be treated as an
employer." Read subsection (a) carefully in two directions. First, it cuts both ways: it is not
merely a shelter for the agent, it also removes employer status from the brokerage, which is what
removes the federal employment tax withholding and matching obligations from the broker's side of
the ledger. Second, read its opening words as a limit. "For purposes of this title" confines the
entire section to Title 26, the Internal Revenue Code. Section 3508 decides federal tax treatment
and nothing else. It resolves no question of Texas law — not your broker's supervision duty, not
your status under Labor Code chapter 21, not anyone's liability to anyone.

Subsection (b)(1) supplies the definition that the general rule turns on. "The term "qualified
real estate agent" means any individual who is a sales person if-" and then three conditions: "(A)
such individual is a licensed real estate agent," "(B) substantially all of the remuneration
(whether or not paid in cash) for the services performed by such individual as a real estate agent
is directly related to sales or other output (including the performance of services) rather than
to the number of hours worked, and" "(C) the services performed by the individual are performed
pursuant to a written contract between such individual and the person for whom the services are
performed and such contract provides that the individual will not be treated as an employee with
respect to such services for Federal tax purposes." Subsection (b)(3) adds a coordination rule:
"This section shall not apply for purposes of subtitle A to the extent that the individual is
treated as an employee under section 401(c)(1) (relating to self-employed individuals)."

Put the two statutes side by side and the design becomes obvious. Texas condition (B) and federal
condition (A) both require a licence. Texas condition (C) and federal condition (B) are the same
output-based-pay test in slightly different words. Texas condition (D) and federal condition (C)
are the same written-contract-plus-federal-tax-clause requirement. Texas adds condition (A), tying
the exclusion to brokerage activity as § 1101.002 defines it. This is not two independent regimes
you must satisfy separately by luck; it is one drafting pattern, and a single well-written
agreement satisfies both. That is why the same document does duty for the Texas Workforce
Commission and the Internal Revenue Service.

**What the agreement should actually contain.** The statutory core is short: the parties by name;
a statement that the individual holds a Texas real estate licence and will perform activity
described by Occupations Code § 1101.002; compensation that is measured by production rather than
by hours; and an express provision that the individual will not be treated as an employee with
respect to the services for federal tax purposes. Everything beyond that core is there because
other law makes it necessary or because the brokerage wants it. The provisions that belong there
because other law requires them include: the written statement of the scope of your authorized
activities, which 22 TAC § 535.2 obliges the broker to give you in writing and which many
brokerages fold into the same document; a clause acknowledging that all compensation for acts
subject to the Act is paid by, through, or with the written consent of the sponsoring broker,
which is the § 535.3 rule taken up in Unit 6; a clause addressing what happens to compensation on
a transaction that has not yet closed when the relationship ends, which is governed by
§ 1101.651(b) and which the parties can allocate but cannot re-route around the broker; a clause
on notice of termination, which § 1101.367(a) makes non-optional in any event; and a clause on
records, because § 535.2 requires the *broker* to maintain a defined list of transaction records
for at least four years and the broker needs your file to do it.

**The non-interaction you must not get wrong.** This is the most useful proposition in the unit,
so state it flatly: your status as an independent contractor for unemployment compensation
purposes under § 201.072, and for federal tax purposes under § 3508, has **no effect whatever** on
your broker's obligations under the licensing law. Those obligations come from a different body of
law serving a different purpose — brokerage supervision and licensing responsibility — and they
are not conditioned on employment. Rule 22 TAC § 535.2 says so in its closing sentence: "This
section is not meant to create or require an employer/employee relationship between a broker and a
sponsored sales agent." Read that in the direction it actually points. It does not say the
broker's obligations are lighter for a contractor. It says the broker's obligations exist
independently of employment status, and that a licensee should not read § 535.2's supervision
requirements as converting the relationship into employment. Alongside it sits Occupations Code
§ 1101.803, a licensing-responsibility provision: "A licensed broker is liable to the commission,
the public, and the broker's clients for any conduct engaged in under this chapter by the broker
or by a sales agent associated with or acting for the broker." The word "associated" is doing
deliberate work. The Legislature attached that liability to association, not to employment. A
clause in your contractor agreement stating that the broker is not responsible for your acts would
be worth precisely nothing against § 1101.803, and a broker who supervises less because the agents
are contractors has confused two statutes that answer different questions.

**Stated plainly: what was not held.** The Internal Revenue Service's administrative guidance and
the Treasury regulations under 26 U.S.C. § 3508, and the Texas Workforce Commission's own rules
and audit practice under chapter 201, were **not held** for this course, alongside the
wage-and-hour, workers' compensation and common-law authorities already named. Where you have
heard that the Internal Revenue Service applies a twenty-factor control test, or that a brokerage
must or must not carry workers' compensation on its agents, this module states no position,
because the sources that would settle it are not in front of us. What is in front of us —
§ 201.072 for unemployment compensation and § 3508 for federal tax — is enough to tell you what
your written agreement must say and why.

### Statute reading exercise: Reading § 201.072 against its neighbours

Open Texas Labor Code § 201.072, then § 201.070 and § 201.073 in the same subchapter.

1. List the four conditions in § 201.072(1) in your own words. Which of them refers to an activity
   definition found in a different code entirely, and which code and section is it?
2. Sections 201.070 and 201.073 each condition their exclusion on a list of control factors.
   Identify three such factors from either section. Then state whether any equivalent factor
   appears in § 201.072, and explain what you conclude from the difference.
3. Section 201.072(2) covers real estate instructors. Which of the four conditions in paragraph
   (1) has no counterpart in paragraph (2)? Why would the Legislature have left it out for
   instructors?
4. A broker tells a new agent, "We don't need a written agreement, everyone here knows you're a
   contractor." Point to the specific language in § 201.072 that this defeats, and say what the
   consequence is under the section.
5. Section 201.072 opens with the phrase "In this subtitle." What does that phrase limit the
   exclusion to, and what does it therefore tell you about whether § 201.072 answers questions
   arising under Labor Code chapter 21?

## Unit: Employment Law

Now turn the problem around. Unit 1 established that a licensed Texas sales agent working under a
proper written agreement is, for unemployment-tax and federal-tax purposes, not an employee. A
student who stops there draws the wrong conclusion — that employment law is simply irrelevant to a
real estate brokerage. It is not. Brokerages hire receptionists, transaction coordinators,
marketing staff, bookkeepers, listing coordinators, unpaid interns and property management staff.
Team leaders make decisions about who gets leads, who gets desk space and who gets a place on the
team. And the state's principal employment discrimination statute contains one subchapter whose
coverage threshold is a single employee. The right conclusion is narrower and more useful:
employment law applies to a brokerage in proportion to how much genuine employment it contains,
and you need to know where that line falls. Note the change of subject matter as you cross from
Unit 1 into this one. Section 201.072 serves unemployment compensation and § 3508 serves federal
tax; the statute in this unit serves **discrimination law**, and it carries its own definitions of
"employer" and "employee". Neither of the tests you learned in Unit 1 is imported into it.

**The statute.** Texas Labor Code chapter 21 is the Texas Commission on Human Rights Act as it now
stands. Section 21.001 states its purposes, and the first and fourth are the ones to remember: to
"provide for the execution of the policies of Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and its
subsequent amendments (42 U.S.C. Section 2000e et seq.)," and to "secure for persons in this
state, including persons with disabilities, freedom from discrimination in certain employment
transactions, in order to protect their personal dignity." Paragraph (3) adds the policies of
"Title I of the Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990 and its subsequent amendments (42 U.S.C.
Section 12101 et seq.)." That opening tells you how to read the whole chapter: it is Texas's
state-law counterpart to the federal employment discrimination scheme, deliberately built to track
it. The texts of Title VII and the ADA themselves were **not held** for this course, so this
module teaches the Texas chapter and does not state what the federal statutes say. Section 21.0015
tells you who administers it: "The powers and duties exercised by the Commission on Human Rights
under this chapter are transferred to the Texas Workforce Commission," and throughout the chapter
"commission" means the Texas Workforce Commission.

**The core prohibition.** Section 21.051 is the operative section for employers, and it is short
enough to hold in your head:

> Sec. 21.051. DISCRIMINATION BY EMPLOYER. An employer commits an unlawful employment practice if
> because of race, color, disability, religion, sex, national origin, or age the employer: (1)
> fails or refuses to hire an individual, discharges an individual, or discriminates in any other
> manner against an individual in connection with compensation or the terms, conditions, or
> privileges of employment; or (2) limits, segregates, or classifies an employee or applicant for
> employment in a manner that would deprive or tend to deprive an individual of any employment
> opportunity or adversely affect in any other manner the status of an employee.

Seven protected characteristics: race, color, disability, religion, sex, national origin, age. Two
prohibited modes: adverse treatment in hiring, discharge, compensation or terms and conditions;
and limiting, segregating or classifying in a way that deprives someone of opportunity. Several
other sections extend the same logic. Section 21.055 makes retaliation an unlawful employment
practice where an employer "retaliates or discriminates against a person who, under this chapter"
opposes a discriminatory practice, makes or files a charge, files a complaint, or "testifies,
assists, or participates in any manner in an investigation, proceeding, or hearing." Section
21.056 reaches an employer who "aids, abets, incites, or coerces a person to engage in a
discriminatory practice."

Chapter 21 also defines the edges of those seven characteristics. Section 21.101 limits age
discrimination under the chapter to "discrimination against an individual 40 years of age or
older." Section 21.106(a) provides that discrimination "because of sex or on the basis of sex
includes discrimination because of or on the basis of pregnancy, childbirth, or a related medical
condition," and (b) requires that a woman so affected "be treated for all purposes related to
employment, including receipt of a benefit under a fringe benefit program, in the same manner as
another individual not affected but similar in the individual's ability or inability to work."
Section 21.002(6) defines disability as "a mental or physical impairment that substantially limits
at least one major life activity of that individual, a record of such an impairment, or being
regarded as having such an impairment," and § 21.0021(a)(1) directs that the term "shall be
construed in favor of broad coverage of individuals," with § 21.0021(b) requiring that substantial
limitation be assessed "without regard to the ameliorative effects of mitigating measures" such as
medication, hearing aids and assistive technology. Section 21.128(a) makes it an unlawful
employment practice to "fail or refuse to make a reasonable workplace accommodation to a known
physical or mental limitation of an otherwise qualified individual with a disability who is an
employee or applicant for employment, unless the respondent demonstrates that the accommodation
would impose an undue hardship on the operation of the business of the respondent." Against those
obligations the chapter sets defences: § 21.002(2) defines a "bona fide occupational
qualification" as a qualification "reasonably related to the satisfactory performance of the
duties of a job" and "for which a factual basis exists for the belief that no person of an
excluded group would be able to satisfactorily perform the duties of the job with safety or
efficiency," and § 21.102(a) protects differences applied under "a bona fide seniority system,
merit system, or an employee benefit plan … that is not a subterfuge to evade this chapter" or "a
system that measures earnings by quantity or quality of production."

**Who is an "employer," and this is where it matters for you.** Section 21.002(8) defines
"Employer" as "(A) a person who is engaged in an industry affecting commerce and who has 15 or
more employees for each working day in each of 20 or more calendar weeks in the current or
preceding calendar year; (B) an agent of a person described by Paragraph (A); (C) an individual
elected to public office in this state or a political subdivision of this state; or (D) a county,
municipality, state agency, or state instrumentality, regardless of the number of individuals
employed." Fifteen employees, on each working day, in each of twenty or more calendar weeks, in
this year or last year. Most Texas brokerages never reach that threshold in *employees*; a Waco
firm with eighty sponsored sales agents and three salaried staff has three employees for this
purpose, not eighty-three, provided the agents are genuine independent contractors. Note paragraph
(B), though: "an agent of a person described by Paragraph (A)" is itself an employer under the
chapter, which is how a supervising individual who makes personnel decisions for a covered firm
becomes exposed in their own right.

Section 21.002(7) defines "Employee" as "an individual employed by an employer, including an
individual subject to the civil service laws of this state or a political subdivision of this
state, except that the term does not include an individual elected to public office in this state
or a political subdivision of this state." That definition is circular on purpose: an employee is
someone employed by an employer. Chapter 21 supplies no test for distinguishing an employee from
an independent contractor.

**The honest answer about sales agents.** Here is the point at which a careless course would
overstate. Chapter 21 protects an "employee," meaning "an individual employed by an employer." A
sales agent whose service is genuinely excluded from "employment" under Labor Code § 201.072 —
output-based pay, written contract, the federal-tax clause — and whose relationship with the
broker is, in substance as well as on paper, a contractor relationship, is not on the face of
these definitions an employee of the broker, and 22 TAC § 535.2 closes with the statement that the
section "is not meant to create or require an employer/employee relationship between a broker and
a sponsored sales agent." But notice the limits of what that establishes. Section 201.072 excludes
real estate service from "employment" **"In this subtitle"** — that is, for unemployment
compensation purposes only. It does not by its own terms decide anything under chapter 21, which
sits in a different subtitle and defines its own terms; and 26 U.S.C. § 3508, confined by its own
words to Title 26, decides nothing under Texas law at all. Do not import either test into a
chapter 21 question. Whether a Texas court applying chapter 21 would treat a particular sales
agent as an employee turns on judicial decisions applying that chapter's definitions to real
facts, and **no case law was held** for this course. This module therefore does not tell you that
a sales agent can never bring a chapter 21 claim against a broker, and it does not tell you that
one always can. It tells you what the statutes say and where the sources run out. A broker who
assumes the answer is "never" — and who therefore runs the brokerage as though discrimination law
simply does not exist inside the office — is making a bet on an unread authority.

**The one-employee subchapter.** There is a part of chapter 21 where the fifteen-employee
threshold does not apply at all, and every broker in Texas should know it. Subchapter C-1 was
added in 2021. Section 21.141 gives that subchapter its own definition: "'Employer' means a person
who: (A) employs one or more employees; or (B) acts directly in the interests of an employer in
relation to an employee." One employee. The same section defines "sexual harassment" as "an
unwelcome sexual advance, a request for a sexual favor, or any other verbal or physical conduct of
a sexual nature" where submission is made a term or condition of employment explicitly or
implicitly, where submission or rejection is used as the basis for an employment decision, or
where the conduct "has the purpose or effect of unreasonably interfering with an individual's work
performance" or "of creating an intimidating, hostile, or offensive working environment." Section
21.142 then provides that "[a]n employer commits an unlawful employment practice if sexual
harassment of an employee occurs and the employer or the employer's agents or supervisors: (1)
know or should have known that the conduct constituting sexual harassment was occurring; and (2)
fail to take immediate and appropriate corrective action." Two elements: knowledge or constructive
knowledge, and failure to take immediate and appropriate corrective action. A separate section,
§ 21.1065, extends parallel sexual harassment protection to unpaid interns, which is worth knowing
in an industry that uses them. The deadlines differ too: § 21.202(a) requires a complaint to be
filed "not later than the 180th day after the date the alleged unlawful employment practice
occurred," while § 21.202(a-1) gives a sexual harassment complaint until "the 300th day after the
date the alleged sexual harassment occurred," and § 21.202(b) directs that "[t]he commission shall
dismiss an untimely complaint." A complainant who receives a notice of the right to file a civil
action has, under § 21.254, sixty days to bring it, and under § 21.256 no action may be brought
"later than the second anniversary of the date the complaint relating to the action is filed."

**Two more provisions with a real-estate flavour.** Section 21.0022 excludes franchisors: "For
purposes of this chapter, a franchisor is not considered to be an employer of: (1) a franchisee;
or (2) a franchisee's employees." Given how much Texas brokerage operates under franchise brands,
that is a live point — the national brand is not the employer of the local office's staff.
Subsection (c) preserves an exception where a franchisor "has been found by a court of competent
jurisdiction in this state to have exercised a type or degree of control over the franchisee or
the franchisee's employees not customarily exercised by a franchisor for the purpose of protecting
the franchisor's trademarks and brand." And § 21.2585(d) caps the sum of compensatory damages for
non-pecuniary losses and punitive damages by employer size, at "$50,000 in the case of a
respondent that has fewer than 101 employees" rising in steps to "$300,000 in the case of a
respondent that has more than 500 employees." Subsection (c) excludes back pay and interest on
back pay from those capped compensatory damages, so back pay is recoverable outside the cap.

**What reaches you regardless of employment status.** Do not confuse chapter 21 with the law that
governs how you treat the public. Occupations Code § 1101.652(b)(32) makes it a disciplinary
ground for a license holder engaged in real estate brokerage to discriminate against an owner,
potential buyer, landlord or potential tenant on the basis of race, color, religion, sex,
disability, familial status, national origin or ancestry, including steering a prospect interested
in equivalent properties to a different area on those bases. Section 1101.652 is taught in full in
Module 10, and fair housing law proper — the federal Fair Housing Act and Texas Property Code
chapter 301 — is taught there too. The point for this topic is only that the ground attaches to
the licence and does not care whether you are an employee or a contractor.

### Applied exercise: A Corpus Christi brokerage's employment questions

Delta Bay Realty in Corpus Christi has a designated broker, four salaried staff (an office
manager, two transaction coordinators and a bookkeeper), one unpaid summer intern from Del Mar
College, and fifty-two sponsored sales agents, each of whom signed a written independent
contractor agreement containing a clause that the agent will not be treated as an employee for
federal tax purposes and each of whom is paid only on closed transactions. Three situations arise
in the same month.

1. A transaction coordinator alleges that the office manager repeatedly made unwelcome comments of
   a sexual nature and that she reported it to the designated broker, who did nothing. Apply
   § 21.002(8)(A) and then § 21.141(1). Does the fifteen-employee threshold defeat her claim?
   State the two elements § 21.142 requires her to establish.
2. The same coordinator's colleague, aged 44, is passed over for a promotion in favour of a
   29-year-old. Using § 21.002(8)(A) and § 21.101, work out whether chapter 21's employer
   definition is satisfied on these facts, counting the firm's employees, and whether age is
   within the chapter's protection at 44.
3. A sponsored sales agent claims she was denied leads because of her national origin and wants to
   file a chapter 21 complaint against the brokerage. Identify the definitional obstacle in
   § 21.002(7) and (8), identify the limiting phrase in § 201.072 that prevents that section from
   resolving the question, and state what this course can and cannot tell her.
4. The alleged harassment in question 1 occurred 240 days before the coordinator consults a
   lawyer. Apply § 21.202(a) and (a-1) and say whether her complaint is timely, showing which
   deadline governs.
5. Delta Bay operates under a national franchise brand. The coordinator wants to name the
   franchisor as a respondent. Apply § 21.0022(b) and (c) and state what she would have to

## Unit: Employment Relationships between Brokers and Principals

This unit exists because of a word. Real estate law uses "employ" and "employment" in a sense that
predates and differs from labour law, and students who have just spent an hour on Labor Code
chapters 201 and 21 walk straight into the confusion. In the law of agency, a principal *employs*
an agent when the principal engages the agent to act on the principal's behalf. That is what a
listing agreement does and what a buyer representation agreement does. The seller employs the
broker. It is a contract of employment in the agency sense — an engagement — and it has nothing to
do with payroll, withholding, unemployment tax or Labor Code chapter 21. If you carry only one
sentence out of this unit, carry this one: the broker is employed by the client, and the sales
agent is not employed by anybody.

You can see TRELA itself using the word in the agency sense. Occupations Code § 1101.002(1)(B)(i)
provides that "Broker" includes a person who "is employed by or for an owner of real estate to
sell any portion of the real estate." That is the engagement sense. Elsewhere in the same chapter
the word is used in the labour sense — § 1101.005(6) exempts from the chapter "a person employed
by an owner in the sale of structures and land on which structures are located if the structures
are erected by the owner in the course of the owner's business," and § 1101.005(8) exempts "an
owner or the owner's employee who leases the owner's improved or unimproved real estate." The
Commission's rules draw the labour-law sense sharply where it matters: 22 TAC § 535.5(c) provides
that a real estate license is not required "for an employee employed by a business entity for the
purpose of buying, selling, or leasing real property for the entity," and § 535.5(d)(2) then
defines the term for that purpose: "An employee of a business entity means a person employed and
directly compensated by the business entity. An independent contractor is not an employee." So the
same statute and rules use both senses, a few pages apart, and the reader is expected to tell them
apart from context. Learn to do it.

**Who the client's agent is.** Occupations Code § 1101.557(a) settles it: a broker who represents
a party in a real estate transaction, or who lists real estate for sale under an exclusive
agreement for a party, "is that party's agent." Not the sales agent. The broker. Section 1101.557,
including the information and offer-presentation obligations subsection (b) attaches to that
broker, is taught in full in Module 02. What matters here is only the identity it fixes: the
person the client employs, in the agency sense, is the broker.

The Commission's own rule states the fiduciary standard for that relationship. Under 22 TAC
§ 535.2, "A broker owes the highest fiduciary obligation to the principal and is obliged to convey
to the principal all information known to the agent which may affect the principal's decision
unless prohibited by other law." Read the internal structure of that sentence. The obligation runs
to the principal and is owed by the *broker*, but the information that must be conveyed is "all
information known to the agent." The rule is written on the assumption that the agent's knowledge
is the broker's problem. That assumption is precisely why Unit 5 exists.

**Why the paperwork is in the broker's name.** Section 535.2 provides that "Listings and other
agreements for real estate brokerage services must be solicited and accepted in a broker's name."
Not in the sales agent's name, not in the team's name. The corresponding statute for the agent is
Occupations Code § 1101.351(c): "A licensed sales agent may not engage or attempt to engage in
real estate brokerage unless the sales agent is sponsored by a licensed broker and is acting for
that broker." Between them, those two provisions define the entire architecture of Texas
brokerage. The client contracts with the broker; the sales agent performs the work as the person
acting for the broker; and § 1101.803, quoted in Unit 1, supplies the answerability by making the
broker liable to the Commission, the public and the broker's clients for conduct by a sales agent
associated with or acting for the broker.

The practical consequence catches new agents by surprise every year. You spend six weeks
cultivating a seller in Frisco, you take the listing, and then you decide to move to a different
brokerage. The listing does not go with you, because the listing was never yours; it is an
agreement between the seller and your former broker. Whether the seller may terminate that
agreement and re-list with you at your new firm is a question of the listing agreement's own terms
and of the seller's rights under it, not a question you can answer by declaring that you brought
the client in. Meanwhile, your entitlement to be paid on transactions you worked before you left
is governed by Occupations Code § 1101.651(b), which is set out in full in Unit 6 and which
expressly contemplates payment by the broker that "was sponsoring the sales agent when the sales
agent earned the compensation."

**The contract of employment between client and broker, and its statutory constraints.** Because
the listing or representation agreement is a contract, TRELA regulates its form and content in
several places, and you should know them as the boundaries of the broker–principal engagement.

First, the writing requirement for suing on it. Occupations Code § 1101.806 gates any action for a
commission: subsection (c) requires the promise or agreement, or a memorandum, to be in writing and
signed by the party against whom the action is brought, and subsection (b) requires the plaintiff
to allege and prove licence status "at the time the act was commenced." Section 1101.806 is taught
in full in Module 04.

Second, the termination date. Section 1101.652(b)(12) makes it a disciplinary ground to fail to
specify a definite termination date not subject to prior notice in a contract, other than a
property management contract, in which the license holder agrees to perform services requiring a
licence. An open-ended listing that runs until one side gives thirty days' notice engages it, and
so does a listing with a blank in the expiration field. The subdivision, and § 1101.652 generally,
are taught in Module 10.

Third, the shape of the compensation the client agrees to. A net listing — an agreement in which
the broker's commission is the difference between the sales proceeds and the amount the owner
wants — is permitted only on the conditions 22 TAC § 535.16 imposes on both entry into it and its
content. Rule 535.16 is taught in full in Module 04.

Fourth, on the buyer side, § 1101.652(b)(34) makes it a disciplinary ground to fail to enter into
the written agreement with a prospective buyer that Occupations Code § 1101.563 requires.
Section 1101.563 is taught in Module 05 and § 1101.652 in Module 10.

**Two traps at the boundary of the two senses of "employment."** The first is § 1101.652(b)(26),
taught in Module 10, which makes it a disciplinary ground to establish an association "by
employment or otherwise" with a person other than a license holder if that person is expected or
required to act as a license holder. Those four words — "by employment or otherwise" — are why the
label on the relationship does not matter. Whether you call the person an employee, a contractor,
a partner or an assistant, if the person is expected or required to act as a license holder and is
not one, the association itself is the violation. That is the hinge between this unit and the next
one.

The second trap is the reverse: assuming that because a broker is "employed" by the seller, the
broker owes the seller the obedience of a servant. The broker's duty is fiduciary, not servile,
and the rule that defines it — § 535.2's highest-fiduciary-obligation sentence — pairs the duty
with a limit, "unless prohibited by other law." A seller who instructs a broker to conceal a known
material defect is instructing the broker to do something other law prohibits. The engagement does
not authorise it.

## Unit: Employment and Compensation of Personal Assistants

An assistant is the first hire most producing agents make, and it is where more licence trouble
starts than in any other staffing decision, because the temptation is always the same: the
assistant is competent, willing and cheaper than a licensed partner, and there is one more task
you would like them to take on. The rules governing what an unlicensed assistant may do are not a
matter of degree or of intention. They are a list of acts for which a licence is required, and the
assistant may do everything else and none of those.

**Where the line is drawn.** The primary source is 22 TAC § 535.4, headed "License Required," read
alongside § 535.5, "License Not Required." Work through the acts § 535.4 places on the licensed
side.

Showing property. Section 535.4(c) provides that "[u]nless otherwise exempted by the Act, a person
must be licensed as a broker or sales agent to show a property," and — this is the operative half
— "[f]or purposes of this section, to 'show' a property includes causing or permitting the
property to be viewed by a prospective buyer or tenant, unlocking or providing access onto or into
a property for a prospective buyer or tenant, and hosting an open house at the property." Read
that definition slowly, because it is broader than the word "show" sounds. *Unlocking the door*
for a prospective buyer is showing. *Providing access* is showing. *Hosting an open house* is
showing. An unlicensed assistant cannot meet a buyer at a property in Pflugerville to let them in
"just to look around while I finish another appointment," and cannot sit an open house on Sunday
afternoon even if instructed to answer no questions. The rule reaches the act of providing access,
not the quality of the conversation that follows.

Section 535.4(d) provides the narrow unescorted-access mechanism, and its conditions must all be
met: a license holder may permit a prospective tenant unescorted access to view a property
available for rent or lease only if "the property is vacant, meaning no person lives at, and no
personal property except property intended to remain or convey is stored at, the property," "the
license holder employs a method to control access and verify the identity of the prospective
tenant," and "the property owner has signed a written consent that sets out in bold print in at
least 12-point font that: the property owner is aware that unescorted access may occur; and
specifies whether the broker enabling unescorted access or the property owner will be responsible
for any damage that results from such unescorted access." Note that this is a licence-holder
mechanism for prospective *tenants* viewing *vacant* rental property, with a bold-print, 12-point
owner consent. It is not a workaround for sending an unlicensed assistant to open a listed house
for a buyer.

Soliciting listings and negotiating. Section 535.4(f) provides that "[a] real estate license is
required for a person to solicit listings or to negotiate in Texas for listings." An unlicensed
assistant may not call expired listings or for-sale-by-owner sellers to solicit the listing. The
assistant may perform clerical work on a mailing campaign the broker designs and sends in the
broker's name, but the moment the assistant is on the phone soliciting the listing, § 535.4(f) is
engaged.

Supervising. Section 535.4(e) provides that "[t]he employees, agents, or associates of a licensed
broker must be licensed as brokers or sales agents if they direct or supervise other persons who
perform acts for which a license is required." An unlicensed office administrator who assigns
leads to agents, reviews their contract work and directs how they handle clients is directing
persons who perform acts for which a licence is required, and by the terms of this subsection must
be licensed.

Finding property for people, for a fee. Section 535.4(m) provides that "[a] real estate license is
required for a person to receive a fee or other consideration for assisting another person to
locate real property for sale, purchase, rent, or lease, including the operation of a service
which finds apartments or homes." Section 535.4(l) requires a licence of "[a] person who arranges
for a tenant to occupy a residential property" if the person "does not own the property or lease
the property from its owner," "receives valuable consideration," and "is not exempt under the
Act." Section 535.4(n) reaches the compilation and distribution of information about vacancies or
property for sale "if payment of any fee or other consideration received by the person who
compiles and distributes the information is contingent upon the sale, purchase, rental, or lease
of the property," and adds that "[a]n advance fee is a contingent fee if the fee must be returned
if the property is not sold, purchased, rented, or leased." Section 535.4(o) requires a licence
where a person, for compensation, "advertises for others regarding the sale, purchase, rent, or
lease of real property," "accepts inquiries received in response to such advertisements," and
"refers the inquiry to the owner of the property" — all three together.

**What the unlicensed assistant may do.** Section 535.5(g) is the clearest permission in the
rules: "An answering service or clerical or administrative employees identified to callers as such
to confirm information concerning the size, price, and terms of property advertised are not
required to be licensed under the Act." Every element of that sentence is load-bearing. The person
must be *identified to callers* as an answering service or as clerical or administrative staff.
The information must be *confirmation* of size, price and terms. And it must be about property
that has been *advertised*. Confirming that the Katy listing is 2,410 square feet, listed at
$389,000, with the seller asking for a 30-day close, to a caller who has been told they are
speaking to an administrative assistant, sits inside this rule. Telling that caller the seller
will probably take $370,000 does not; that is negotiating, and it is also a disclosure problem.

Beyond § 535.5(g), the safe territory for an unlicensed assistant is defined by subtraction:
anything that is not an act described in § 535.4 or in the § 1101.002 definition of broker. In
practice — and this is practice, not a citation, because no held rule enumerates permitted
assistant tasks — that includes scheduling, calendar and file management; assembling and
delivering documents at the license holder's direction; ordering signs, photography, staging and
repairs; data entry into the brokerage's systems; bookkeeping; managing the broker's advertising
placements at the broker's direction; and greeting people at the office. Note carefully that the
absence of a held enumeration is exactly why the analysis has to run the other way: you do not
start from a list of permitted chores, you start from § 535.4 and § 1101.002 and ask whether the
task is on the licensed side.

**Referrals: the trap with a dollar figure in it.** Rule 22 TAC § 535.20 provides that
"[r]eferring a prospective buyer, seller, landlord, or tenant to another person in connection with
a proposed real estate transaction is an act requiring the person making the referral to be
licensed if the referral is made with the expectation of receiving valuable consideration," and
then defines the term: "For the purposes of this section, the term 'valuable consideration'
includes but is not limited to: money; gifts of merchandise having a retail value greater than
$50; rent bonuses; and discounts." A $75 gift card to the neighbour who sent you a buyer is
valuable consideration under this rule and makes that neighbour an unlicensed person making a
referral with the expectation of consideration. Section 535.20(b) sets out a narrow exemption, and
all four of its conditions must be met: the person "is engaged in the business of selling goods or
services to the public"; the person "sells goods or services to a real estate license holder who
intends to offer the goods or services as an inducement to potential buyers, sellers, landlords or
tenants"; "[a]fter selling the goods or services to the real estate license holder, the person
refers the person's customers to the real estate license holder"; and "[t]he payment to the person
for the goods or services is not contingent upon the consummation of a real estate transaction by
the person's customers." That last condition is the heart of it. The moment the vendor's payment
depends on whether the referred customer closes, the exemption is gone.

**Who may pay the assistant, and how.** Three provisions govern the money, and they must be read
together.

Occupations Code § 1101.651(a) constrains the broker: "A licensed broker may not pay a commission
to or otherwise compensate a person directly or indirectly for performing an act of a broker
unless the person is: (1) a license holder; or (2) a real estate broker licensed in another state
who does not conduct in this state any of the negotiations for which the commission or other
compensation is paid." Note "or otherwise compensate" and "directly or indirectly." The
prohibition is not limited to commissions and is not defeated by routing the payment through
someone else. Section 1101.652(b)(11) makes the same conduct a disciplinary ground; § 1101.652 is
taught in Module 10.

Rule 22 TAC § 535.147(a) restates the prohibition at rule level with its qualifier intact: "Except
as otherwise provided by the Act or Commission rules, a broker or sales agent may not share a
commission or fees with any person who engages in acts for which a license is required and is not
actively licensed as a broker or sales agent." Read the conjunction. The prohibited payee is a
person who *engages in acts for which a license is required* **and** *is not actively licensed*.
That is why an unlicensed assistant who does only clerical work is not caught by § 535.147(a): the
assistant is not engaging in acts for which a licence is required. Section 535.147(b) confirms the
same logic from the other side: "An unlicensed person may share in the income earned by a business
entity licensed as a broker or exempted from the licensing requirements under the Act if the
person engages in no acts for which a license is required and does not lead the public to believe
that the person is in the real estate brokerage business."

Rule 22 TAC § 535.3 constrains the sales agent, and this is the provision agents forget when they
hire their own assistant. Its second sentence: "A sales agent may not pay a commission or other
valuable consideration to another person except with the written consent of the sales agent's
sponsoring broker." Note the breadth — "a commission or other valuable consideration," not merely
a commission. Occupations Code § 1101.651(c) is narrower in its terms: "A sales agent may not pay
a commission to a person except through the broker that is sponsoring the sales agent at that
time." The rule reaches "other valuable consideration"; the statute speaks of a commission.
Whether an hourly wage paid by a sales agent to a purely clerical personal assistant is "other
valuable consideration" within § 535.3 was not resolved in any source held for this course, and
this module does not assert an answer. What it does tell you is the safe course, which is not a
legal conclusion but a consequence of the rule's breadth: obtain your sponsoring broker's written
consent before you pay anyone anything out of your own pocket in connection with your brokerage
practice, and keep the consent.

**Putting it together for a working arrangement.** There are three lawful structures for an
assistant, and they differ in who pays. The assistant may be the broker's employee, paid by the
brokerage, doing only unlicensed tasks; the money never touches § 535.147(a) because the assistant
engages in no acts requiring a licence. The assistant may be the sales agent's own employee or
contractor, paid by the agent — in which case § 535.3's second sentence puts the sponsoring
broker's written consent squarely in the path. Or the assistant may be licensed. A licensed
assistant is not a hybrid: a licensed assistant is a sales agent, must be sponsored by a broker
under § 1101.351(c), may receive compensation only as § 535.3 and § 1101.651(b) permit, and comes
with the full weight of § 535.2 supervision on the sponsoring broker. Many producing agents
discover that the third option is administratively simpler than policing the boundary of the
second, and some discover it after a complaint.

One last point about titles. Nothing in the held rules regulates what you print on an unlicensed
assistant's business card, but recall that § 535.147(b), quoted above, allows an unlicensed person
to share a licensed entity's income only where the person engages in no acts requiring a licence
and "does not lead the public to believe that the person is in the real estate brokerage
business." Alongside it, § 1101.652(b)(23)(C) — Module 10 — makes it a disciplinary ground to
publish an advertisement implying that a sales agent is responsible for the operation of the
broker's brokerage business. An unlicensed assistant styled a buyer specialist on a team's
marketing is a problem waiting to be found.

### Applied exercise: Staffing a team in Round Rock

You are a sales agent sponsored by Hill Country Brokers. Your production has grown to the point
that you hire Marisol, who is not licensed, at $22 an hour, paid by you personally. Over one week
you ask her to do the following: (i) key your new listing into the MLS from the data sheet you
prepared; (ii) meet a buyer at your Round Rock listing and unlock the door so the buyer can walk
through while you finish a closing; (iii) answer your business line and, when a caller asks about
the advertised Georgetown listing, confirm the square footage, the list price and the seller's
requested closing date, after telling the caller she is your administrative assistant; (iv) call
the eleven expired listings in Hutto and ask the owners whether they would like to re-list; and
(v) accept a $100 restaurant gift card from your preferred lender for each buyer she refers to
him.

1. Take tasks (i) through (v) one at a time and state, with the specific rule subsection, whether
   each is an act for which a licence is required. Name the subsection of § 535.4 or § 535.5 that
   governs each.
2. You are paying Marisol yourself. Identify the sentence in 22 TAC § 535.3 that this implicates
   and state what you must obtain from Hill Country Brokers. Then state honestly what this course
   does and does not resolve about whether an hourly wage is caught by that sentence.
3. Suppose you had instead offered Marisol $500 for every transaction that closes from a lead she
   brings in. Apply § 535.147(a) and § 1101.651(a) and explain why this arrangement fails,
   identifying the two elements § 535.147(a) requires.
4. Marisol's lender gift card: apply § 535.20(a). Is a $100 gift card within the rule's definition
   of "valuable consideration"? Then test the arrangement against all four conditions of
   § 535.20(b) and state which one it fails.
5. Your broker suggests solving the problem by having the brokerage pay Marisol directly for the
   same five tasks. Does that change the answer to any of tasks (i) through (v)? Explain, using
   § 1101.651(a).

## Unit: Broker Responsibility (Relationships between Brokers and Agents, §535.2)

This is the unit that pays for the whole module. Rule 22 TAC § 535.2 is the operational statement
of what a Texas broker must do about the people the broker sponsors, and the Commission enforces
it. It is also, read in the wrong frame of mind, the source of the apparent contradiction this
module opened with: how can a broker be legally responsible for a sales agent who is not the
broker's employee? The answer, established in Unit 1, is that Texas law never made the broker's
responsibility depend on employment. Rule 535.2 and Occupations Code §§ 1101.351 and 1101.803
serve brokerage supervision and licensing responsibility; they are not employment law and they do
not ask an employment question. **A sales agent's independent contractor status for federal tax or
unemployment compensation purposes does not reduce the broker's supervision duty by one line, and
does not reduce the broker's responsibility for the agent's authorized acts.** Section 1101.803
attaches liability to a sales agent "associated with or acting for the broker." Section 201.072's
exclusion from "employment" contains no control condition, so nothing the broker does by way of
supervision endangers it. And § 535.2 itself closes with the sentence: "This section is not meant
to create or require an employer/employee relationship between a broker and a sponsored sales
agent." Supervision and employment are separate ideas in Texas real estate law. Hold them apart
and the rule reads cleanly.

Work through it subsection by subsection.

**(a) The written scope notice, and the trap in its third sentence.** "A broker is required to
notify a sponsored sales agent in writing of the scope of the sales agent's authorized activities
under the Act. Unless such scope is limited or revoked in writing, a broker is responsible for the
authorized acts of the broker's sales agents, but the broker is not required to supervise the
sales agents directly. If a broker permits a sponsored sales agent to conduct activities beyond
the scope explicitly authorized by the broker, those are acts for which the broker is
responsible."

Three sentences, three separate rules. First, the notice must be *in writing*, and it must state
the *scope* of the agent's authorized activities. Second, the broker is responsible for authorized
acts unless scope is limited or revoked in writing — and the rule expressly says the broker "is
not required to supervise the sales agents directly." That phrase matters: § 535.2 does not
require the broker to look over your shoulder, and it does not require personal review of every
act. Third, and this is the trap: if the broker *permits* the agent to work beyond the explicitly
authorized scope, the broker is responsible for those acts anyway. So a broker who writes a narrow
scope — "residential resale only, no property management, no commercial" — and then knowingly lets
an agent manage four rent houses has not limited the broker's exposure. The written scope protects
the broker only for as long as the broker enforces it. A scope document that the brokerage does
not police is worse than useless, because it documents that the broker knew where the boundary
was.

**(b) The fiduciary standard.** "A broker owes the highest fiduciary obligation to the principal
and is obliged to convey to the principal all information known to the agent which may affect the
principal's decision unless prohibited by other law." This is the subsection discussed in Unit 3.
Note again that the standard is the *highest* fiduciary obligation, that the trigger is
information that "may affect the principal's decision" rather than information the broker judges
important, and that the qualifier "unless prohibited by other law" is part of the rule and must be
quoted with it.

**(c) Trust funds.** "A broker is responsible for the proper handling of trust funds placed with
the broker and must comply with §535.146 of this chapter (relating to Maintaining Trust Money)."
Rule 535.146 defines "[t]rust money" as "client's money, earnest money, rent, unearned fees,
security deposits, or any money held on behalf of another person" and provides that "[a]ny trust
money accepted by a broker is held in a fiduciary capacity and must be maintained in a designated
trust account maintained by the broker or delivered to an escrow agent authorized in Texas in
accordance with the agreement of the principals of the transaction." Two sentences from § 535.146
bear directly on the broker–agent relationship: "A sales agent shall not maintain a trust account.
Any trust money received by a sales agent must be immediately delivered to the sales agent's
sponsoring broker." If a buyer hands you an earnest money cheque in Amarillo, you do not hold it,
you do not deposit it, and you do not decide when it should go to title. You deliver it
immediately to your broker. That is the handling rule, and it is taught here because § 535.2(c)
makes it a broker responsibility. What happens to the § 535.146 accounting duty when the agency
relationship itself ends — its survival past termination — is taught in Module 07.

**(d) Property management.** "A broker is responsible for any property management activity by the
broker's sponsored sales agent that requires a real estate license." Property management is where
sponsored agents most often operate semi-independently, collecting rent, signing leases and
handling security deposits, and this subsection removes any doubt that the broker owns the
consequences. Read it with § 535.4(h), which provides that a person "controls the acceptance or
deposit of rent from a resident of a single-family residential real property unit and must be
licensed under the Act if the person has the authority to: use the rent to pay for services
related to management of the property; determine where to deposit the rent; or sign checks or
withdraw money from a trust account," and with § 535.4(j): "A person must be licensed as a broker
to operate a rental agency."

**(e) Delegation — the mechanics you will be examined on and audited on.** "A broker may delegate
to another license holder the responsibility to assist in administering compliance with the Act
and Rules, but the broker may not relinquish overall responsibility for the supervision of license
holders sponsored by the broker. Any license holder who leads, supervises, directs, or manages a
team must be delegated as a supervisor. Any such delegation must be in writing. A broker shall
provide the name of each delegated supervisor to the Commission using a process acceptable to the
Commission within 30 days of any such delegation that has lasted or is anticipated to last more
than three consecutive months. The broker shall notify the Commission in the same manner within 30
days after the delegation of a supervisor has ended. In the event the delegated supervisor is a
broker at the time of delegation or later becomes a broker, that broker may, in lieu of the
sponsoring broker, notify the Commission in writing when the delegation ends."

Extract the operative facts. Delegation is of the responsibility "to assist in administering
compliance" — the broker may not relinquish *overall* responsibility, ever. Any license holder who
"leads, supervises, directs, or manages a team" must be delegated as a supervisor; team leadership
is not an informal marketing arrangement under this rule, it is a delegation that must be
documented. Every delegation must be in writing. The Commission must be told the delegated
supervisor's name within **30 days** of a delegation that "has lasted or is anticipated to last
more than three consecutive months" — note both limbs, because a delegation that was expected to
be short but has run past three months triggers the same 30-day clock. The Commission must be told
within **30 days** after the delegation ends. And where the delegated supervisor is or becomes a
broker, that broker may give the end-of-delegation notice instead of the sponsoring broker.

Work the dates. A Lubbock broker delegates supervision of the residential team to a sales agent on
1 March, expecting the arrangement to last through the spring market and end in May. In May the
broker extends it. The delegation has now lasted more than three consecutive months as of 1 June,
and the broker has until 1 July — 30 days — to provide the delegated supervisor's name to the
Commission. If instead the broker had known on 1 March that the arrangement would run to the end
of the year, the delegation was "anticipated to last more than three consecutive months" on day
one, and the 30-day clock ran from 1 March, expiring 31 March.

**(f) Listings in the broker's name.** "Listings and other agreements for real estate brokerage
services must be solicited and accepted in a broker's name." Discussed in Unit 3; note here that
it is a broker responsibility, not merely an agent obligation, which means the broker who
tolerates listings taken in a team's name is the one the Commission looks to.

**(g) Advertising.** "A broker is responsible to ensure that a sponsored sales agent's advertising
complies with §535.154 of this chapter (relating to Registration and Use of Alternate, Team and
Assumed Business Names Used in Advertisements) and §535.155 of this chapter (relating to
Advertisements)." Rules 535.154 and 535.155 — the alternate, team and assumed business name
vocabulary and the advertising rules themselves — are taught in Module 12. What subsection (g)
adds is the allocation of responsibility: the sponsored agent's advertising is the broker's
compliance problem.

**(h) Records: four years, eight categories.** The rule opens with an exception and then a floor:
"Except for records destroyed by an 'Act of God' such as a natural disaster or fire not
intentionally caused by the broker, the broker must, at a minimum, maintain the following records
in a format that is readily available to the Commission for at least four years from the date of
closing, termination of the contract, or end of a real estate transaction." The eight categories
are: "disclosures"; "commission agreements such as listing agreements, buyer representation
agreements, or other written agreements relied upon to claim compensation"; "substantive
communications with parties to the transaction"; "offers, contracts, and related addenda";
"receipts and disbursements of compensation for services subject to the Act"; "property management
contracts"; "appraisals, broker price opinions, and comparative market analyses"; and "sponsorship
agreements between the broker and sponsored sales agents."

Three details students lose. First, the period runs from the *later*-occurring of three triggers
as they apply — closing, termination of the contract, or end of the transaction — not from the
date the file was opened. A listing that expires unsold in September 2026 starts its four years in
September 2026. Second, "substantive communications with parties to the transaction" includes text
messages and emails; the medium is irrelevant, the substance is what the rule names. Third, the
last category — "sponsorship agreements between the broker and sponsored sales agents" — means
your independent contractor and sponsorship paperwork is itself a required broker record. That is
the link back to Unit 1: the written agreement that § 201.072(1)(D) requires is a document the
broker must be able to produce to the Commission for four years.

**(i) Written policies and procedures.** "A broker who sponsors sales agents or is a designated
broker for a business entity shall maintain, on a current basis, written policies and procedures
to ensure that" eight things are true. In order: that each sponsored sales agent "is advised of
the scope of the sales agent's authorized activities subject to the Act and is competent to
conduct such activities, including having competence in the geographic market area where the sales
agent represents clients"; that each agent "maintains their license in active status at all times
while they are engaging in activities subject to the Act"; that "[a]ny and all compensation paid
to a sponsored sales agent for acts or services subject to the Act is paid by, through, or with
the written consent of the sponsoring broker"; that each agent is given, "on a timely basis,
before the effective date of the change, notice of any change to the Act, Commission rules, or
Commission promulgated contract forms"; that beyond statutory minimum continuing education each
agent "receives such additional educational instruction the broker may deem necessary" to maintain
competency, with the specific floor that "when a sales agent performs a type of real estate
brokerage activity for the first three times, the broker must require that the sales agent receive
coaching and assistance from an experienced license holder competent for that activity"; that each
agent "complies with the Commission's advertising rules"; that all trust accounts "including but
not limited to property management trust accounts, and other funds received from consumers are
maintained by the broker with appropriate controls in compliance with §535.146"; and that
"[r]ecords are properly maintained pursuant to subsection (h) of this section."

Two of those deserve a second look. The geographic competence requirement means a broker's policy
cannot be indifferent to an agent sponsored in San Antonio taking a listing in Marfa; competence
"in the geographic market area where the sales agent represents clients" is written into the rule.
And the first-three-times coaching requirement is the most concrete supervision obligation in the
whole rule: not "encourage," not "make available," but "the broker must require that the sales
agent receive coaching and assistance from an experienced license holder competent for that
activity," for each *type* of activity, for the first three times. A new agent's first three
listings, first three buyer representations, first three leases and first three property
management engagements each carry the requirement separately.

**(j) and (k) The response clocks.** "In addition to the requirements of §535.157 of this chapter
(relating to Obligation to Respond Timely), a broker or supervisor delegated under subsection (e)
of this section must respond to sponsored sales agents within two calendar days." And: "A
sponsoring broker or supervisor delegated under subsection (e) of this section shall deliver mail
and other correspondence from the Commission to their sponsored sales agents within three calendar
days after receipt." Rule 535.157, taught in Module 12, sets the outward-facing clock: two
calendar days for a broker or sales agent to respond to a principal, to a license holder
representing another party, or to an unrepresented party. So there are three clocks to keep
straight: two calendar days for a license holder to
respond to a principal, the other side's license holder, or an unrepresented party (§ 535.157);
two calendar days for the broker or delegated supervisor to respond to a sponsored sales agent
(§ 535.2(j)); and three calendar days for the broker or delegated supervisor to pass on Commission
correspondence (§ 535.2(k)). Calendar days, not business days. A Friday email is not answered in
time on the following Wednesday.

Subsection (k) has a mechanical reason for existing, and it is in a different rule. Under 22 TAC
§ 535.21(b) — taught in Module 12 — the Commission sends notices to an active sales agent at the
address of that agent's *sponsoring broker*, not to the agent. That is why the broker has three
calendar days to forward it under § 535.2(k), and why an agent who never receives a Commission
notice has a problem that starts in the broker's inbox.

**(l) and (m) Entities, and the closing sentence.** "When the broker is a business entity, the
designated broker is the person responsible for the broker responsibilities under this section."
That connects to Occupations Code § 1101.355(a), under which a business entity seeking a licence
must "designate one of its managing officers as its broker for purposes of this chapter" and must
provide proof of errors and omissions insurance "with a minimum annual limit of $1 million for
each occurrence if the designated broker owns less than 10 percent of the business entity," and
§ 1101.355(b), under which a business entity "may not act as a broker unless the entity's
designated broker is a licensed individual broker in active status and good standing." The
corporate form does not diffuse the responsibility; § 535.2(l) puts it on a named human being. And
then the final subsection, which this unit began with: "This section is not meant to create or
require an employer/employee relationship between a broker and a sponsored sales agent."

**Why this design is coherent.** That is a substantial supervisory apparatus: a written scope
notice, a fiduciary standard, trust fund and property management responsibility, written
delegation with Commission notice, name control over listings, advertising compliance, four years
of eight categories of records, eight written policies, response clocks, and personal
responsibility on the designated broker of an entity. It coexists with independent contractor
status because the two regimes ask different questions. The broker supervises because the
licensing law requires it; the brokerage pays by production and papers the file because the
unemployment and tax statutes condition their exclusions on that, and on nothing about control.

## Unit: Compensation Issues

Everything in this module converges here, because money is where the relationships in the previous
five units become visible. There is one organising idea: **compensation for acts subject to the
Act flows through the broker.** Every rule below is a variation on that theme, and every violation
in this area is a variation on someone trying to route money around it.

**The core rule.** Rule 22 TAC § 535.3, "Compensation to or Paid by a Sales Agent," is two
sentences long and you should be able to recite both:

> A sales agent may not receive a commission or other valuable consideration except with the
> written consent of the sales agent's sponsoring broker or the broker who sponsored the sales
> agent when the sales agent became entitled to the commission or other valuable consideration. A
> sales agent may not pay a commission or other valuable consideration to another person except
> with the written consent of the sales agent's sponsoring broker.

Both directions are covered — receiving and paying — and both are conditioned on *written*
consent. Note also that the receiving sentence contemplates two possible consenting brokers: the
current sponsoring broker, or "the broker who sponsored the sales agent when the sales agent
became entitled to the commission or other valuable consideration." The rule anticipates that you
may change brokers between earning and being paid.

**The statute behind it.** Occupations Code § 1101.651(b) is the provision to quote whole, because
the clause people drop is the one that solves the change-of-sponsorship problem:

> (b) A sales agent may not accept compensation for a real estate transaction from a person other
> than the broker that is sponsoring the sales agent or was sponsoring the sales agent when the
> sales agent earned the compensation.

"[O]r was sponsoring the sales agent when the sales agent earned the compensation." Any version of
§ 1101.651(b) that stops after "the broker that is sponsoring the sales agent" states a different
and wrong rule, because it would make it impossible for an agent who changes brokerages to be paid
on a transaction earned at the old firm. Read it with the surrounding subsections. Section
1101.651(a) provides that "A licensed broker may not pay a commission to or otherwise compensate a
person directly or indirectly for performing an act of a broker" unless that person is "a license
holder" or "a real estate broker licensed in another state who does not conduct in this state any
of the negotiations for which the commission or other compensation is paid." And § 1101.651(c): "A
sales agent may not pay a commission to a person except through the broker that is sponsoring the
sales agent at that time."

**Working the change-of-sponsorship problem.** You are sponsored by Broker A in Tyler. On 12 March
you put a $410,000 house under contract with a buyer client; the co-operating fee offered is 2.5
percent, and your split with Broker A is 70/30 in your favour. On 1 April you move to Broker B.
The transaction closes on 20 April. Who pays you, and how much?

The co-operating fee is $410,000 × 0.025 = $10,250, and it is paid to the broker, not to you. Your
70 percent share is $7,175, and Broker A's 30 percent share is $3,075. Section 1101.651(b) permits
you to accept that compensation from the broker that "was sponsoring the sales agent when the
sales agent earned the compensation" — Broker A. It does not permit you to accept it from the
title company directly, and it does not permit Broker B to collect it and pass it through, because
Broker B was not the sponsoring broker when you earned it. Rule 535.3's first sentence requires
written consent, and the consenting broker here is Broker A. Note what has to have happened by 20
April: Occupations Code § 1101.367(a) requires the terminating party, on the end of a sponsorship
relationship, to notify both the other party and the Commission immediately in writing, and the
Commission then places the sales agent licence on inactive status; § 1101.367(b) returns it to
active status on the new broker's request assuming sponsorship. Section 1101.367 and the mechanics
of terminating a sponsorship are taught in Module 07. What belongs here is the compensation
consequence, plus one supervision link: § 535.2(i) requires the broker's written policies to
ensure that each sponsored agent
"maintains their license in active status at all times while they are engaging in activities
subject to the Act" — which is why a gap between brokerages is not merely administrative. If you
show a property on 28 March while your licence sits inactive, you have a licensing problem that no
later cure repairs.

**Receiving compensation through your own entity.** Many agents want commissions paid to a company
rather than to themselves personally. TRELA permits it on tight conditions. Occupations Code
§ 1101.355(c) states the default: "A business entity that receives compensation on behalf of a
license holder must be licensed as a broker under this chapter." Subsection (d) then supplies the
exception, opening "Notwithstanding Subsection (c) or any other law, a business entity is not
required to be licensed under this chapter if the business entity" — and every one of its five
conditions is required. The entity must receive "compensation on behalf of a broker or sales agent
licensed under this chapter that is earned by the license holder while engaged in real estate
brokerage"; it "performs no other acts of a broker"; it is "a limited liability company as defined
by Section 101.001, Business Organizations Code" or "an S corporation as defined by 26 U.S.C.
Section 1361"; it "is registered with the commission"; and it "is at least 51 percent owned by the
license holder on whose behalf the entity receives compensation." Rule 22 TAC § 535.5(h) states
the same five conditions and points to § 535.35 for the registration process, and § 535.4(g)
confirms the default from the other direction: "Unless otherwise exempted by §535.5 of this
chapter … and §1101.355(d) of the Act, a business entity owned by a broker or sales agent which
receives compensation on behalf of the license holder must be licensed as a broker under the Act."
Rule 535.147(c) closes the loop: "A broker or sales agent may not share a commission or fees with
an unlicensed business entity created by a license holder for the purpose of collecting a
commission or fees on behalf of the license holder, unless the entity is exempted from the
requirements of licensure as provided by §535.5 of this chapter … and §1101.355(d) of the Act." A
general partnership will not do. A C corporation will not do. A 50/50 LLC with your spouse will
not do — the rule says at least 51 percent owned by the license holder. And it must be registered.

**Rebates to a party.** Rule 22 TAC § 535.147(d) permits rebates on conditions: "A license holder
may rebate or pay a portion of the license holder's fee or commission to a party in the
transaction when the sales agent has the written consent of the sales agent's sponsoring broker
and the party represented by the license holder. A commission or fee may not be paid to any party
to the transaction in a manner that misleads a broker, lender, title company, or governmental
agency regarding the real estate transaction or the financial resources or obligations of the
buyer. A license holder who intends to pay a portion of the license holder's fee or commission to
a party the license holder does not represent must obtain the written consent of the party
represented by the license holder before making the payment." Three requirements, all of which
must be satisfied: the sponsoring broker's written consent and the represented party's written
consent; no structure that misleads a broker, lender, title company or governmental agency about
the transaction or about the buyer's financial resources or obligations; and, where the rebate
goes to a party you do *not* represent, the prior written consent of the party you do represent.
The second requirement is the one that ends careers. A rebate paid outside closing and omitted
from the settlement statement, on a loan where the buyer's cash to close was calculated without
it, is a rebate paid "in a manner that misleads a … lender … regarding … the financial resources
or obligations of the buyer."

**Undisclosed compensation.** Rule 22 TAC § 535.148, "Receiving an Undisclosed Commission or
Rebate," is taught in full in Module 05. Two of its subsections bear directly on the flow of money
in this unit. Subsection (a) bars a license holder from receiving a commission, rebate or fee in a
transaction from a person other than the person the license holder represents without first
disclosing to the client the intention to receive it and obtaining the client's consent — disclose
first, then consent, in that order. Subsection (f) requires TREC No. RSC-4, Disclosure of
Relationship with Residential Service Contract Provider or Administrator, for any payment received
for services provided for or on behalf of a residential service contract provider or administrator
licensed under Occupations Code chapter 1304; if a home warranty company pays you for anything,
RSC-4 is not optional. The section's further consent requirements, its bar on referral payments
among settlement service providers, and the carve-outs in subsection (h) belong to Module 05.

**The statutory grounds that sit behind all of this.** Occupations Code § 1101.652(b), taught in
full in Module 10, makes several compensation practices independent grounds for suspension or
revocation: subdivision (8), receiving compensation from more than one party to a transaction
without the full knowledge and consent of all parties; (13), accepting an undisclosed commission,
rebate or direct profit on an expenditure made for a principal; (11), paying or dividing a
commission or fee with a person who is not a license holder or a broker or sales agent licensed in
another state; (9), failing within a reasonable time to properly account for or remit money
belonging to another person; and (10), commingling another person's money with the license
holder's own. Notice how (9) and (10) sit alongside the trust-money duty in § 535.2(c) and
§ 535.146: money that is not yours is not yours even briefly.

**Co-operation across state lines.** Section 1101.651(a)(2) permits a Texas broker to compensate
"a real estate broker licensed in another state who does not conduct in this state any of the
negotiations for which the commission or other compensation is paid." Rule 22 TAC § 535.131
elaborates: "A broker licensed in Texas may cooperate with a foreign broker and share earned
commissions with a foreign broker"; "Only Texas license holders may handle negotiations physically
conducted within Texas"; and a resident of a foreign country or territory that does not license
brokers "is considered to be licensed as a foreign broker for the purposes of §1101.651 of the
Act, if the person practices as a real estate broker in compliance with the law of the foreign
country or territory." Rule 535.4(a) supplies the jurisdictional rule those provisions depend on:
"The Act applies to any person acting as a real estate broker or sales agent while physically
within Texas, regardless of the location of the real estate involved or the residence of the
person's customers or clients," and "a person conducting brokerage business from another state by
mail, telephone, the Internet, email, or other medium is acting within Texas if the real property
concerned is located wholly or partly in Texas." So an Oklahoma broker may be paid a referral fee
on a Sherman transaction, provided the Oklahoma broker conducted none of the Texas negotiations;
and a Texas licensee sitting in Dallas negotiating an Arkansas property is acting under the Act
while physically within Texas.

**Suing for a commission.** Section 1101.806, taught in Module 04, sets the gates: subsection (b)
requires the plaintiff to allege and prove licence status "at the time the act was commenced";
subsection (c) requires a signed writing or memorandum from the party being sued; subsection (d)
forfeits the commission where the license holder failed to advise a buyer as § 1101.555 requires;
and subsection (a)(1) exempts an agreement to share compensation among license holders, so a
co-broke dispute between two licensed brokers is not governed by these gates.

### Applied exercise: Following the money on a Denton closing

Priya is a sales agent sponsored by Redbud Realty. She represents a buyer on a $525,000 purchase
in Denton. The listing broker's offer of compensation to the buyer's broker is 2.75 percent.
Priya's split with Redbud is 65/35 in her favour. Along the way: the seller's home warranty
provider offers Priya $150 in marketing support because she recommended their product to the
buyer; Priya wants to rebate $2,000 of her share to her buyer at closing without showing it on the
settlement statement, because the buyer is short on cash to close; and Priya's newly formed LLC,
of which she owns 60 percent and her brother owns 40 percent, has asked Redbud to make the
commission cheque payable to the LLC. The LLC has not registered with the Commission.

1. Compute the compensation to the buyer's broker and Priya's share under the 65/35 split. State
   which entity receives the funds at closing and cite the provision that requires it.
2. Apply 22 TAC § 535.148(a) and (f) to the $150 from the home warranty provider. What must Priya
   do before receiving it, and which TREC form does § 535.148(f) require?
3. Apply 22 TAC § 535.147(d) to the proposed $2,000 rebate. Identify each of the three
   requirements in that subsection and state precisely which one the plan to keep the rebate off
   the settlement statement violates, quoting the language that governs.
4. Test the LLC against all five conditions of Occupations Code § 1101.355(d). Which conditions
   does it satisfy and which does it fail? What is the consequence under § 1101.355(c) and 22 TAC
   § 535.147(c)?
5. Two weeks before closing, Priya moves to a different brokerage. Using § 1101.651(b) and 22 TAC
   § 535.3, state who may pay her, whose written consent she needs, and what § 1101.367(a)
   required to happen on the day she left Redbud.

### Module recap

Start where the module started, with a single word used in two ways. In Labor Code chapters 201
and 21 the word "employment" carries tax and discrimination consequences. In the law of agency it
means an engagement: the seller employs the broker. Nothing about the first sense follows from the
second, and a student who keeps them separate will get every question in this topic right. The
corollary matters even more: each authority in this module serves one limited purpose. Section
201.072 serves unemployment compensation; 26 U.S.C. § 3508 serves federal tax treatment; Labor
Code chapter 21 serves discrimination law; 22 TAC § 535.2 and Occupations Code §§ 1101.351 and
1101.803 serve brokerage supervision and licensing responsibility; and 22 TAC §§ 535.3, 535.147
and 535.148 with Occupations Code § 1101.651 serve compensation. None of them decides a question
belonging to another.

For unemployment compensation, § 201.072 excludes real estate service from "employment" "In this
subtitle" on four conditions: activity within the § 1101.002 definition of broker; a Texas Real
Estate Commission licence; remuneration that is "substantially all … directly related to sales or
other output … and not to the number of hours worked"; and a written contract providing that the
individual "is not treated as an employee with respect to the service for federal tax purposes."
Paragraph (2) does the same for instructors of Commission-approved courses, without the
output-based pay condition. For federal tax, 26 U.S.C. § 3508(a) says a qualified real estate
agent "shall not be treated as an employee" and the person for whom the services are performed
"shall not be treated as an employer," and § 3508(b)(1) conditions that on a licence, output-based
remuneration, and a written contract with the same federal-tax provision. Neither statute
conditions anything on freedom from control — compare § 201.070 and § 201.073, which are full of
control conditions — and neither defines "substantially all." Wage-and-hour law, workers'
compensation, common-law employment status and vicarious liability **were not held** for this
course, and this module states no position on them.

Chapter 21 is discrimination law with its own definitions. Section 21.051 prohibits an employer
from discriminating because of race, color, disability, religion, sex, national origin or age.
Section 21.002(8)(A) makes an employer a person "engaged in an industry affecting commerce" with
"15 or more employees for each working day in each of 20 or more calendar weeks in the current or
preceding calendar year," and § 21.002(7) defines an employee as "an individual employed by an
employer." Those definitions are the chapter's reach, and neither § 201.072's four conditions nor
§ 3508's three are imported into them; whether a genuine independent contractor sales agent can be
an "employee" under chapter 21 depends on case law that **was not held**. What the
fifteen-employee threshold does not limit is Subchapter C-1: § 21.141(1) defines "employer" there
as a person who "employs one or more employees," and § 21.142 makes an employer liable for sexual
harassment where the employer or its agents or supervisors knew or should have known and failed
"to take immediate and appropriate corrective action." Deadlines: 180 days under § 21.202(a), 300
days for sexual harassment under § 21.202(a-1), 60 days to sue after a right-to-sue notice under
§ 21.254, two years outside limit under § 21.256. Independently of employment status,
§ 1101.652(b)(32) makes discrimination in brokerage a licence matter; § 1101.652 is Module 10's.

The broker–principal relationship is the agency sense of employment. Section 1101.557(a) — Module
02 — says a broker who represents a party or lists under an exclusive agreement "is that party's
agent." Rule 535.2 states that the broker "owes the highest fiduciary obligation to the principal"
and must convey "all information known to the agent which may affect the principal's decision
unless prohibited by other law," and that listings "must be solicited and accepted in a broker's
name." Section 1101.351(c) puts the sales agent in position: sponsored, and "acting for that
broker." Constraints on the engagement come from authorities taught elsewhere and are cited here
only for their effect: the writing and licence-status gates on suing for a commission in
§ 1101.806(b) and (c) and the net listing rule in § 535.16 (Module 04), the definite termination
date required by § 1101.652(b)(12) and the written buyer agreement required by § 1101.652(b)(34)
(Module 10, with § 1101.563 in Module 05).

Personal assistants are a boundary problem with a clean method: start from 22 TAC § 535.4 and the
§ 1101.002 broker definition and ask whether the task is on the licensed side. Showing — which
§ 535.4(c) defines to include "unlocking or providing access onto or into a property for a
prospective buyer or tenant" and "hosting an open house at the property" — is licensed. Soliciting
or negotiating for listings under § 535.4(f) is licensed. Directing or supervising persons who
perform acts requiring a licence under § 535.4(e) is licensed. Confirming size, price and terms of
advertised property, by a person identified to callers as clerical or administrative staff, is
not, under § 535.5(g). Referring with the expectation of valuable consideration — including "gifts
of merchandise having a retail value greater than $50" — requires a licence under § 535.20(a),
subject only to the four-condition vendor exemption in § 535.20(b). Payment is governed by
§ 1101.651(a), § 535.147(a) and (b), and, when the agent pays out of pocket, the second sentence
of § 535.3.

Broker responsibility under § 535.2 is the operational core, and it does not soften because the
agents are contractors: a written scope notice, with responsibility extending to acts the broker
permits beyond that scope; the highest fiduciary obligation; trust funds under § 535.146, which a
sales agent may never hold; property management activity; written delegation, with any team leader
delegated as a supervisor, the Commission notified within 30 days of a delegation lasting or
anticipated to last more than three consecutive months and within 30 days after it ends; listings
in the broker's name; advertising compliance under §§ 535.154 and 535.155; eight categories of
records kept at least four years from closing, contract termination or the end of the transaction;
eight written policies including mandatory coaching for a sales agent's first three times
performing a type of activity; a two-calendar-day response to sponsored agents and a
three-calendar-day forward of Commission correspondence; the designated broker personally
responsible when the broker is an entity; and the closing statement that the section "is not meant
to create or require an employer/employee relationship between a broker and a sponsored sales
agent."

Compensation resolves into one sentence and its exceptions: money for acts subject to the Act
moves through the broker. Rule 535.3 requires written consent both to receive and to pay. Section
1101.651(b) permits a sales agent to accept compensation only from "the broker that is sponsoring
the sales agent or was sponsoring the sales agent when the sales agent earned the compensation" —
quote it whole, always. Section 1101.651(a) limits whom a broker may pay; § 1101.651(c) requires a
sales agent's payments to go through the sponsoring broker. An entity may receive an agent's
commission without a licence only on all five conditions of § 1101.355(d), including at least 51
percent ownership by the license holder and registration with the Commission. Rebates require the
sponsoring broker's and the represented party's written consent and must not mislead a lender or
title company about the buyer's financial resources or obligations, under § 535.147(d).
Third-party compensation must be disclosed and consented to before receipt under § 535.148(a),
with TREC No. RSC-4 required under § 535.148(f); the rest of § 535.148 is taught in Module 05.

Finally, the honest inventory. The Internal Revenue Service's administrative guidance and the
Treasury regulations under § 3508, the Texas Workforce Commission's rules and audit practice under
chapter 201, the Fair Labor Standards Act, the Texas Payday Law, Texas workers' compensation law,
Texas case law on common-law employment status and vicarious liability, Title VII and the
Americans with Disabilities Act themselves, and judicial decisions applying Labor Code chapter 21
to real estate sales agents **were not held** for this course. Where those sources would have
settled a question, this module said so rather than guessing. Occupations Code §§ 1101.555 and
1101.563 are a different matter: they were held, they are cited here only for the effect
§ 1101.806(d) and § 1101.652(b)(34) give them, and they are taught in Modules 12 and 05. That
habit — knowing which of your beliefs rests on an authority you have actually read — is the most
valuable thing you can carry out of a law of agency course into a career where other people's
money is in your hands.
