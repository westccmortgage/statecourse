# Texas Law of Agency — Module 08

## TREC Topic: Clarifying Agency Relationships

**Prescribed time: 45 minutes.** This module carries the two units the Commission's LOA-1 course
approval form places under *Clarifying Agency Relationships*, whose scope note is *"Disclosure of
agency."*

**Learning objectives.** After completing this module you will be able to: distinguish the two separate
disclosure duties Occupations Code § 1101.558 imposes — the oral-or-written representation disclosure
under subsection (b) and the written notice under subsection (b-1) — and state precisely when each is
triggered, how 22 TAC § 531.20 permits the completed Information About Brokerage Services notice to be
delivered, what the rule forbids by way of email placement and altered reproduction, how the website
and social-media link requirements operate, and how the Consumer Protection Notice adopted by 22 TAC
§ 531.18 differs from the IABS in source, audience and method of delivery; and read a brokerage's own
written policy against 22 TAC § 535.2 and Occupations Code §§ 1101.559 through 1101.561 well enough to
identify the scope notice you are owed in writing, the delegation of supervision and the 30-day
Commission notice it triggers, the eight subjects the broker's written policies and procedures must
address, and the questions about intermediary consent, appointments, compensation and record retention
you must be able to answer from your own brokerage's manual before your first transaction.

**Every legal statement in this module comes from a source that was read for this course.** Where a
statement is practice rather than law, the module says so and cites nothing for it.

## Unit: Disclosure Policy

A student hearing the phrase "disclosure policy" for the first time usually pictures a form. That is
the wrong picture, and getting it right at the outset will save you a great deal of confusion. The
Information About Brokerage Services notice — the IABS — is a form. A disclosure policy is the set of
standing decisions a brokerage makes about *who* delivers that form, *when* they deliver it, *by which
method*, *what they do when the first contact arrives in a channel the rule does not accommodate*, and
*how the delivery is proved four years later when a complaint arrives*. The statute and the rule set a
floor. They tell you what must happen. They do not tell you how a firm with forty sales agents working
seven days a week will make it happen the same way every time. That is the policy's job, and the
absence of one is the single most common reason a brokerage's IABS deliveries are inconsistent.

Start with why Texas cares so much about this at all. Two of the shortest rules in the Commission's
canon carry the weight. 22 TAC § 531.2, titled Fidelity, provides that a license holder acting as an
agent for another is a fiduciary and that special obligations follow, among them "that the primary
duty of the license holder is to represent the interests of the client, and the license holder's
position, in this respect, should be clear to all parties concerned in a real estate transaction; that,
however, the license holder, in performing duties to the client, shall treat other parties to a
transaction fairly". Read the phrase *should be clear to all parties concerned*. Fidelity to your
client is not a private arrangement. It is a fact about the transaction that everyone standing in the
room is entitled to know, because everyone standing in the room is calibrating what to say to you based
on whose side you are on. 22 TAC § 531.3, Integrity, adds that a license holder "has a special
obligation to exercise integrity in the discharge of the license holder's responsibilities, including
employment of prudence and caution so as to avoid misrepresentation, in any way, by acts of commission
or omission." A buyer who assumes you are working for her because you were friendly at the door and
you never corrected the assumption has been misled by an act of omission.

The Legislature converted that principle into a disciplinary ground. Under Occupations Code
§ 1101.652(b), the Commission may suspend or revoke a license or take other authorized disciplinary
action if the license holder, while engaged in real estate brokerage, "fails to make clear to all
parties to a real estate transaction the party for whom the license holder is acting." Notice what that
subdivision does not require. It does not require that anyone lost money. It does not require that the
confusion was deliberate. Failing to make your role clear is itself the violation.

**The two duties inside § 1101.558.** Almost every disclosure mistake a new sales agent makes comes
from collapsing two separate obligations into one. Occupations Code § 1101.558 contains both, and
they have different triggers, different audiences, and different formalities.

The first is the **representation disclosure** in subsection (b): "A license holder who represents a
party in a proposed real estate transaction shall disclose, orally or in writing, that representation
at the time of the license holder's first contact with" either "another party to the transaction" or
"another license holder who represents another party to the transaction." Four features of that
sentence matter. It applies only if you *represent* somebody — it is a disclosure of an existing
agency, not a notice given to everyone you meet. Its audience is the other side: the other party, or
the other party's license holder. Its trigger is *first contact*, not first substantive communication.
And it may be made "orally or in writing." There is no prescribed form and no signature. When you call
the listing agent about a property in Alamo Heights and open with "Hi, I'm Marisol Vega with Loop 410
Realty, I represent the buyer," you have discharged § 1101.558(b) in eleven words.

The second is the **written notice** in subsection (b-1): "At the time of a license holder's first
substantive communication with a party relating to a proposed transaction regarding specific real
property, the license holder shall provide to the party written notice in at least a 10-point font"
that does four things. The notice must be one that "describes the ways in which a broker can represent
a party to a real estate transaction, including as an intermediary"; that "describes the basic duties
and obligations a broker has to a party to a real estate transaction that the broker represents"; that
"describes the basic obligations a broker has to a party to a real estate transaction that the broker
does not represent"; and that "provides the name, license number, and contact information for the
license holder and the license holder's supervisor and broker, if applicable."

Compare the two. Subsection (b) is conditional on your representing someone and is aimed at the other
side. Subsection (b-1) is not conditional on representation at all — it is owed to a *party* with whom
you have a first substantive communication about a specific property, whether or not you represent
anyone, and whether or not you ever will. Subsection (b) can be spoken. Subsection (b-1) must be
written and must meet a font floor. The word "party" is defined for this subchapter at § 1101.551(2) as
"a prospective buyer, seller, landlord, or tenant or an authorized representative of a buyer, seller,
landlord, or tenant, including a trustee, guardian, executor, administrator, receiver, or
attorney-in-fact," and the definition adds that "The term does not include a license holder who
represents a party." So you do not owe the (b-1) notice to the agent on the other side. You owe it to
the human being who might buy or sell.

Subsection (b-2) is the hinge into the rules: "The commission by rule shall prescribe the text of the
notice required under Subsections (b-1)(1) and (2) and establish the methods by which a license holder
shall provide the notice." That instruction is why the IABS exists as a Commission form rather than as
prose you draft yourself, and why the delivery methods are enumerated in a rule rather than left to
judgment.

**What 22 TAC § 531.20 actually requires.** The rule opens by adopting the form: "The Commission
adopts by reference the Information About Brokerage Services Notice, TREC No. IABS 1-2 (IABS
Notice)." The text of TREC No. IABS 1-2 itself was not held for this course, so this module teaches
the rule that governs the notice rather than reciting the form. The rule imposes two distinct
obligations on **each license holder** — not merely on the broker. The first is a website
obligation: each license holder shall provide "a link to a completed IABS Notice in a readily
noticeable place on the homepage of each business website, labeled:" either "Texas Real Estate
Commission Information About Brokerage Services", "in at least 10 point font", or "TREC Information
About Brokerage Services", "in at least 12 point font". The shorter label carries the larger font
requirement, which is exactly the sort of detail an audit catches and an agent forgets. The second
obligation is delivery: "the completed IABS Notice at the first substantive communication as
required under §1101.558, Texas Occupations Code."

*Business website* is defined in the rule as a website on the internet that "is accessible to the
public", "contains information about a license holder's real estate brokerage services", and whose
content "is controlled by the license holder." All three elements. Control of content is the one that
decides most close cases: a page you build and edit is yours; a listing display operated by somebody
else is not obviously within your control, and the question is one of fact rather than of branding.

Social media gets an express accommodation. For purposes of providing the required link on a social
media platform, the rule says the link may be located on "the account holder profile" or on "a separate
page or website through a direct link from the social media platform or account holder profile." That
is the answer to the student's perennial question about a business page that has no homepage in the
ordinary sense. You are not required to jam the notice into a caption; a profile link, or a direct link
from the profile to a page carrying the notice, satisfies the rule.

Now the delivery methods, which are the part a disclosure policy exists to operationalise. For purposes
of § 1101.558, the rule provides that the completed IABS Notice can be provided "by personal delivery
by the license holder"; "by first class mail or overnight common carrier delivery service"; "in the
body of an email"; or "as an attachment to an email, or a link within the body of an email, with a
specific reference to the IABS Notice in the body of the email." And then the sentence that catches
experienced agents: "The link to a completed IABS Notice may not be in a footnote or signature block in
an email."

Think about why that last sentence is there. A link permanently welded into your email signature is
decorative. It appears on every message you have ever sent, including the ones that have nothing to do
with a transaction, and precisely because it is always there it communicates nothing at the moment the
statute cares about. The rule requires a deliberate act at a specific point in the relationship, and it
requires a "specific reference to the IABS Notice in the body of the email" when you deliver by
attachment or link. If your brokerage's template puts the IABS link in the signature block and the
office manager tells you that covers it, the office manager is wrong, and 22 TAC § 531.20 is the
sentence you show her.

One more constraint on the form itself. The rule permits reproduction, but tightly: "License holders
may reproduce the IABS Notice published by the Commission, provided that the text of the IABS Notice is
copied verbatim and the spacing, borders and placement of text on the page must appear to be identical
to that in the published version of the IABS Notice, except that the Broker Contact Information section
may be prefilled." So a brokerage may print the notice with its own broker name, licence number, phone
and email already filled in — that is the prefill the rule allows, and it is why your firm hands you a
stack of half-completed notices on your first day. What the brokerage may not do is redesign the page,
add a paragraph of marketing copy, shrink the borders to fit a letterhead, or tidy up the wording.
Verbatim text, identical spacing, identical borders, identical placement.

**The channel problem: texts and online leads.** Here is the practical scenario your policy has to
answer, because the rule does not answer it directly. A consumer sees your sign on a house in Katy
and texts your cell phone at 9:40 on a Saturday night: "Is 2214 Cinco Ranch still available and what
would the payment be at 6.5 percent?" That is a communication with a party relating to a proposed
transaction regarding specific real property, and it is not a bare availability check — the consumer
is asking you to discuss terms.

Notice what the permitted delivery list does *not* include. Text message is not among the four methods
22 TAC § 531.20 enumerates for providing the completed IABS Notice. Personal delivery, first class mail
or overnight common carrier, the body of an email, and an attachment or in-body link with a specific
reference — that is the list. The competent response, and the one a good office policy scripts for you,
is to move the conversation into a channel the rule recognises before you go further: ask for an email
address, send the completed notice in the body of the email or as an attachment with a specific
reference to it in the body, and only then answer the payment question. The same discipline applies to
a portal lead that arrives with a phone number and no email. Your policy should tell you the exact
words to use to get the email address, and it should tell you where the sent copy is filed.

You should also know the honest limit of what the law says here. The phrase "first substantive
communication" is used by both § 1101.558(b-1) and 22 TAC § 531.20, and a definition of "substantive
communication" was not held for this course; neither the statute nor the rule as held defines it. So
the module will not invent a bright line. What it will tell you is the direction of prudence: the cost
of delivering the notice earlier than strictly required is nothing, and the cost of delivering it later
than required is a disciplinary exposure under § 1101.652(a), which reaches a license holder who
"disregards or violates this chapter or Chapter 1102." A disclosure policy that says "send it with the
first reply that discusses anything beyond address and price" is not overcautious. It is cheap
insurance.

**The three exceptions, and what they are not.** Section 1101.558(c) provides that a license holder
is not required to provide the notice required by subsection (b-1) if "the proposed transaction is
for a residential lease for less than one year and a sale is not being considered"; if "the license
holder meets with a party who the license holder knows is represented by another license holder"; or
if "the communication occurs at a property that is held open for any prospective buyer or tenant and
the communication concerns that property."

Take them one at a time. The lease exception has two conditions joined by "and": under one year *and*
no sale being considered. An eleven-month lease on a Denton duplex where the tenant has asked about a
purchase option at the end of the term does not qualify, because a sale is being considered. The second
exception requires actual knowledge that the party is represented by another license holder — a
suspicion is not knowledge, and if the buyer at your open house says "I'm working with someone but
nothing's signed," you have not been given knowledge of representation. The third exception is
property-specific and location-specific: it covers a communication that occurs *at* the property being
held open and that *concerns that property*. When the same visitor calls you on Tuesday about a
different listing, the exception has nothing to say.

These exceptions also interlock with the newer sections of Subchapter L, and your policy must handle
the interlock. Section 1101.562, effective January 1, 2026, permits a broker to show real property to a
party without representing the party under stated conditions, and subsection (b) requires that before
doing so the broker must, as applicable, "disclose to the party as required by Section 1101.558(b) if
the broker represents the owner of the real property" or "provide to the party the written notice
described by Section 1101.558(b-1) if the broker does not represent the owner of the real property."
Section 1101.563(b) requires that a license holder who performs any act of real estate brokerage for a
prospective buyer of residential real property "must enter into a written agreement with the
prospective buyer before" showing any residential real property to the prospective buyer, or, if none
will be shown, before presenting an offer on the buyer's behalf. Section 1101.652(b) makes it a ground
for disciplinary action to fail "to enter into a written agreement with a prospective buyer as required
by Section 1101.563." TREC's own explanatory article on the 2026 changes — guidance, not controlling
law — reads these provisions together to mean that an agent from the listing broker's office hosting an
open house need not provide the IABS or enter a written agreement with prospective buyers, which is
consistent with the open-house exception in § 1101.558(c)(3); the article also notes that where the
agreement and notice are required, they should be handled before the prospective buyer starts viewing
the property. The controlling text is the statute. The point for policy is that a firm cannot leave
each agent to reconstruct this chain at the door on a Sunday afternoon.

**The Consumer Protection Notice is a different animal.** Students conflate the IABS with the
Consumer Protection Notice constantly, and the confusion is understandable because both are TREC
forms adopted by rule and both must be linked from a homepage. They are otherwise unrelated.

The Consumer Protection Notice has a different statutory parent. Occupations Code § 1101.202(a)
directs that the Commission by rule shall "prescribe a notice containing the name, mailing address, and
telephone number of the commission for the purpose of directing a complaint to the commission" and
shall "establish methods by which consumers and service recipients are provided the notice by a person
regulated under this chapter or Chapter 1102." That is a complaint-routing provision sitting in the
subchapter on commission complaints, not in Subchapter L on practice by license holders. Its purpose is
to make sure a wronged consumer can find the regulator.

The rule executing it is 22 TAC § 531.18: "The Commission adopts by reference the Consumer Protection
Notice, TREC No. CN 1-5." The text of TREC No. CN 1-5 was not held for this course. What the rule
requires is two things. Each license holder shall provide the notice by "displaying it in a readily
noticeable location in each place of business the broker maintains" and by "providing a link to it in a
readily noticeable place on the homepage of each business website, labeled:" either "Texas Real Estate
Commission Consumer Protection Notice", "in at least 10 point font", or "TREC Consumer Protection
Notice", "in at least 12 point font". Section 531.18 carries the same three-element definition of
"business website" and the same social-media accommodation as § 531.20.

Now hold the two side by side. The IABS is *delivered to a party* at a specific moment in a specific
relationship, and it explains agency. The Consumer Protection Notice is *displayed and linked* — posted
in each place of business the broker maintains and on the homepage — and it explains how to complain to
TREC. Nothing in § 531.18 requires you to hand the Consumer Protection Notice to a buyer at first
substantive communication, and nothing in § 531.20 requires you to hang the IABS on the office wall.
An agent who thinks the framed notice by the reception desk discharges the IABS obligation has
misunderstood both rules. A brokerage's written disclosure policy should say, in one sentence each,
which notice is posted and which notice is delivered, and should not use the word "disclosure" loosely
enough to blur them.

**Who pays for a policy failure.** Occupations Code § 1101.803 is one sentence and you should
memorise it: "A licensed broker is liable to the commission, the public, and the broker's clients
for any conduct engaged in under this chapter by the broker or by a sales agent associated with or
acting for the broker." That is why brokers write disclosure policies. Your failure to deliver an
IABS to a buyer in El Paso is your problem under § 1101.652(b), and it is simultaneously your
broker's problem under § 1101.803. The brokerage's interest in a written, trained, auditable
disclosure procedure is not bureaucratic tidiness. It is self-defence, and it is also yours.

### Statute reading exercise: § 531.20 and § 531.18 read against each other

Open 22 TAC § 531.20 and 22 TAC § 531.18 and read both in full before answering.

1. § 531.20 lists the methods by which the completed IABS Notice can be provided for purposes of
   § 1101.558. Write out the list. Then state whether text message, fax, or delivery through a
   transaction-management portal appears on it, and explain what that means for an agent whose first
   substantive communication with a buyer is by text.
2. Both rules require a homepage link with a label, and each offers two label options with different
   font floors. Set out all four label-and-font combinations. Which label carries the larger font
   requirement in each rule, and why would a drafter pair a shorter label with a larger font?
3. § 531.20 permits reproduction of the IABS Notice on stated conditions. Quote the condition sentence
   and identify the one section of the notice the rule says may be prefilled. What does the rule
   require about spacing, borders and placement of text, and what would a brokerage be doing wrong if
   it reformatted the notice to fit its letterhead?
4. § 531.18 states two ways the Consumer Protection Notice must be provided. Neither of them is
   delivery to a party at first substantive communication. What does § 531.18 say instead, and where
   must the display occur?
5. Identify something § 531.20 does *not* say: does it define "first substantive communication"? Does
   it say who within a brokerage must deliver the notice when two of the firm's agents are both in
   contact with the same buyer? What follows for a brokerage that wants a defensible practice?

### Applied exercise: the Saturday-night text in Katy

Priya Raghavan is a sales agent sponsored by Bayou Bend Realty, a Houston brokerage. Bayou Bend holds
the listing on 2214 Cinco Ranch Boulevard in Katy. At 9:40 p.m. on a Saturday, Priya receives a text
from a phone number she does not recognise: "Saw your sign at 2214. Is it still available? What would
the payment be if I put 10% down?" Priya has never met the sender and does not know whether he is
represented. She replies at 9:44 p.m.: "Yes! Still available. At 6.5% on $415,000 with 10% down your
principal and interest would be about $2,361. Want to see it tomorrow?"

1. Which subsection of § 1101.558 was triggered by the sender's text, and which was not? Explain by
   reference to who the audience of each subsection is and what each one is conditioned on.
2. Did Priya's 9:44 p.m. reply deliver the notice required by § 1101.558(b-1)? Answer by reference to
   the delivery methods enumerated in 22 TAC § 531.20, and say what Priya should have sent at 9:44
   instead.
3. Assume the sender turns out to be unrepresented and later files a complaint. Identify the
   subdivision of § 1101.652(b) that reaches a license holder who fails to make clear the party for
   whom the license holder is acting, and state, from § 1101.803, who else is exposed.
4. Bayou Bend's email template carries a link labelled "TREC Information About Brokerage Services" in
   14-point font in the signature block of every outgoing message. The broker says this satisfies
   § 531.20 for email delivery. Quote the sentence in § 531.20 that answers him, and explain what the
   rule requires instead when delivery is by link.
5. Change the facts: the sender's text says he is working with an agent at another firm and asks Priya
   to confirm the square footage only. Does § 1101.558(c) relieve Priya of the (b-1) notice? Identify
   which of the three exceptions is in play and state precisely what Priya must know, not merely
   suspect, for it to apply.

## Unit: Understanding a Brokerage Company's Policy

You will sign with a brokerage. On the day you do, someone will hand you a policy manual — perhaps
thirty pages, perhaps two hundred — and you will be tempted to skim it for the commission split and
file the rest. This unit exists to tell you that a substantial part of that manual is not the firm's
preference. It is the firm's discharge of duties that 22 TAC § 535.2 and Occupations Code Subchapter L
place on your broker, and several of those duties are owed *to you*. If the manual does not contain
them, something is wrong, and you are the one who will find out the hard way.

Begin with the structural fact that makes the broker care. Occupations Code § 1101.351(c) provides that
"A licensed sales agent may not engage or attempt to engage in real estate brokerage unless the sales
agent is sponsored by a licensed broker and is acting for that broker." Your authority to practise is
derivative. And § 1101.803 makes the derivation run in both directions: the broker "is liable to the
commission, the public, and the broker's clients for any conduct engaged in under this chapter by the
broker or by a sales agent associated with or acting for the broker." Your broker is on the hook for
what you do. The policy manual is how a rational broker manages that exposure.

**The scope notice you are owed in writing.** The first sentence of 22 TAC § 535.2 is the one to
know cold: "A broker is required to notify a sponsored sales agent in writing of the scope of the
sales agent's authorized activities under the Act." That is a duty running from broker to agent, in
writing, and it is the document you should ask for on day one if it is not handed to you. The rule
continues: "Unless such scope is limited or revoked in writing, a broker is responsible for the
authorized acts of the broker's sales agents, but the broker is not required to supervise the sales
agents directly." Two things there surprise students. First, limiting or revoking your scope
requires a *writing*; a broker who told you in a hallway that you may not take commercial listings
has not limited your scope for purposes of this sentence. Second, the rule expressly does not
require direct supervision. Texas does not make your broker watch you. It makes your broker
responsible for you, which is a different and heavier thing.

The third sentence closes the obvious loophole: "If a broker permits a sponsored sales agent to conduct
activities beyond the scope explicitly authorized by the broker, those are acts for which the broker is
responsible." A broker cannot write a narrow scope, look the other way while an agent exceeds it, and
then point at the paper. Permitting is enough.

**The eight subjects the written policy must cover.** 22 TAC § 535.2 requires more than a scope
letter. "A broker who sponsors sales agents or is a designated broker for a business entity shall
maintain, on a current basis, written policies and procedures to ensure that" eight things are true.
Read the phrase "on a current basis" — a manual last revised in 2019 is not a current manual, and
the rule's own list includes an obligation about notifying agents of rule changes, so staleness is a
compounding failure.

The eight are worth quoting, because each one is a question you may put to a prospective sponsoring
broker. The policies must ensure that "Each sponsored sales agent is advised of the scope of the
sales agent's authorized activities subject to the Act and is competent to conduct such activities,
including having competence in the geographic market area where the sales agent represents clients."
That geographic clause has teeth: it is echoed in § 1101.652(a), which reaches a license holder who
"fails to consider market conditions for the specific geographic area in which the license holder is
providing a service." An agent who lives in Plano and takes a listing in Marfa because a cousin asked
has a competence problem before she has a pricing problem.

The policies must ensure that "Each sponsored sales agent maintains their license in active status at
all times while they are engaging in activities subject to the Act"; and that "Any and all
compensation paid to a sponsored sales agent for acts or services subject to the Act is paid by,
through, or with the written consent of the sponsoring broker." That compensation item is the rule
counterpart of 22 TAC § 535.3, under which a sales agent "may not receive a commission or other
valuable consideration except with the written consent of the sales agent's sponsoring broker or the
broker who sponsored the sales agent when the sales agent became entitled to the commission or other
valuable consideration." The statutory floor is § 1101.651(b): a sales agent "may not accept
compensation for a real estate transaction from a person other than the broker that is sponsoring the
sales agent or was sponsoring the sales agent when the sales agent earned the compensation."

The policies must ensure that "Each sponsored sales agent is provided on a timely basis, before the
effective date of the change, notice of any change to the Act, Commission rules, or Commission
promulgated contract forms." Before the effective date. If your brokerage's first communication about a
new promulgated form arrives the week after it took effect, the policy has failed on its own terms.

The policies must ensure that "In addition to completing statutory minimum continuing education
requirements, each sponsored sales agent receives such additional educational instruction the broker
may deem necessary to obtain and maintain, on a current basis, competency in the scope of the sponsored
sales agent's practice subject to the Act. At a minimum, when a sales agent performs a type of real
estate brokerage activity for the first three times, the broker must require that the sales agent
receive coaching and assistance from an experienced license holder competent for that activity." That
last sentence is a concrete, countable entitlement, and new agents routinely do not know they have it.
Your first three listings, your first three buyer representations, your first three lease transactions,
your first three property management engagements — each of those is a *type* of activity, and for each
of them the broker must require that you receive coaching and assistance from an experienced license
holder competent for that activity. If your brokerage's answer to "who is helping me with my first
listing presentation" is "figure it out," that is not a culture problem. It is a rule problem.

The policies must ensure that "Each sponsored sales agent complies with the Commission's advertising
rules"; that "All trust accounts, including but not limited to property management trust accounts,
and other funds received from consumers are maintained by the broker with appropriate controls in
compliance with §535.146 of this chapter"; and that "Records are properly maintained pursuant to
subsection (h) of this section."

**Delegation of supervision, and the notice the Commission must receive.** The delegation paragraph
is the one that trips up brokerages the moment they grow a team. 22 TAC § 535.2 provides: "A broker
may delegate to another license holder the responsibility to assist in administering compliance with
the Act and Rules, but the broker may not relinquish overall responsibility for the supervision of
license holders sponsored by the broker. Any license holder who leads, supervises, directs, or
manages a team must be delegated as a supervisor. Any such delegation must be in writing."

Three separate propositions. Delegation is permitted, but only of the responsibility "to assist in
administering compliance" — overall supervisory responsibility cannot be handed off. Team leadership is
not optional here: anyone who "leads, supervises, directs, or manages a team" *must* be delegated as a
supervisor. And the delegation must be in writing, which means a team lead operating on an
understanding is a compliance defect regardless of how well the team performs.

Then the notice: "A broker shall provide the name of each delegated supervisor to the Commission using
a process acceptable to the Commission within 30 days of any such delegation that has lasted or is
anticipated to last more than three consecutive months. The broker shall notify the Commission in the
same manner within 30 days after the delegation of a supervisor has ended. In the event the delegated
supervisor is a broker at the time of delegation or later becomes a broker, that broker may, in lieu of
the sponsoring broker, notify the Commission in writing when the delegation ends."

Work the arithmetic, because the trigger has two limbs. Suppose Caprock Realty in Lubbock delegates
Dana Whitfield as supervisor of its four-agent West Texas Land Team on 3 February 2026, and the
delegation is expected to run indefinitely. It is *anticipated to last* more than three consecutive
months, so the 30-day clock starts at the delegation, not three months later: the broker must provide
Dana's name to the Commission by 5 March 2026. Now change the facts. Caprock delegates Dana on 1 June
2026 solely to cover a colleague's leave, with the delegation ending 31 July 2026 — two months, and
neither anticipated nor actually lasting more than three consecutive months. No Commission notice is
triggered by that delegation. The writing requirement, however, is unconditional: even the two-month
delegation must be in writing. Finally, suppose the indefinite delegation ends on 30 September 2027.
The broker must notify the Commission within 30 days after the delegation ended — by 30 October 2027 —
and if Dana holds a broker licence by then, Dana may make that notification in writing in lieu of
Caprock's designated broker.

Two further paragraphs of § 535.2 fix response times, and they are the ones a sales agent feels every
week. "In addition to the requirements of §535.157 of this chapter (relating to Obligation to Respond
Timely), a broker or supervisor delegated under subsection (e) of this section must respond to
sponsored sales agents within two calendar days." And: "A sponsoring broker or supervisor delegated
under subsection (e) of this section shall deliver mail and other correspondence from the Commission to
their sponsored sales agents within three calendar days after receipt." Set those beside 22 TAC
§ 535.157 itself, which provides that "A broker or sales agent must respond to his or her principal,
a broker or sales agent representing another party to a real estate transaction, or an unrepresented
party to a real estate transaction within two calendar days." Calendar days, not business days. A
question sent to your broker on Friday afternoon about whether the firm will act as intermediary is
owed an answer by Sunday. A policy manual that does not name the channel through which you reach your
supervisor — and a backup when that person is unreachable — cannot deliver a two-calendar-day response
in the real world.

**The firm's name, your name, and the listing.** 22 TAC § 535.2 also provides that "Listings and
other agreements for real estate brokerage services must be solicited and accepted in a broker's
name", and that "A broker is responsible to ensure that a sponsored sales agent's advertising
complies with" the Commission's advertising rules — 22 TAC § 535.154 on alternate, team and assumed
business names and 22 TAC § 535.155 on advertisements. That is why the way your firm's identity
appears is a policy question and not a matter of taste.

Section 535.155(a) requires that each advertisement include, in a readily noticeable location, "the
name of the license holder or team placing the advertisement" and "the broker's name in at least half
the size of the largest contact information for any sales agent, associated broker, or team name
contained in the advertisement." That is a measurable ratio, and it is the reason your firm's yard-sign
and business-card templates are not negotiable. "Advertisement" is defined expansively as "any form of
communication by or on behalf of a license holder designed to attract the public to use real estate
brokerage services," reaching email, text messages, social media and the internet, and excluding "a
communication from a license holder to the license holder's current client." Under § 535.154, names are
registered rather than merely adopted: an alternate name must be registered by the license holder
before use in an advertisement, and a team name or an assumed business name must be registered by the
*broker* before an associated broker or sponsored sales agent uses it. A team name "may not include any
terms that could mislead the public to believe that the team is offering brokerage services independent
from its sponsoring broker," and must end with the word "team" or "group". Behind all of it sits
§ 1101.652(b)(23)(D), which reaches an advertisement that "fails to include the name of the broker for
whom the license holder acts, which name may be the licensed name, assumed name, or trade name of the
broker as authorized by a law of this state and registered with the commission." Your social-media
profile is an advertisement. Your policy manual should tell you exactly what goes in it.

**Intermediary policy has to exist before the transaction does.** Here is the discipline that
separates a serious brokerage from a careless one. The moment a firm's buyer client wants to see the
firm's own listing, the brokerage is inside Subchapter L, and there is no time to invent a policy.

Occupations Code § 1101.561(b) states the trap plainly: "A broker must agree to act as an intermediary
under this subchapter if the broker agrees to represent in a transaction" both "a buyer or tenant" and
"a seller or landlord." The obligation attaches at the moment of double representation, not at the
moment somebody writes an offer.

Section 1101.559(a) sets the conditions: a broker may act as an intermediary if "the broker obtains
written consent from each party for the broker to act as an intermediary in the transaction" and "the
written consent of the parties states the source of any expected compensation to the broker."
Subsection (b) tells you how that consent is ordinarily captured: "A written listing agreement to
represent a seller or landlord or a written agreement to represent a buyer or tenant that authorizes a
broker to act as an intermediary in a real estate transaction is sufficient to establish written
consent of the party to the transaction if the written agreement specifies in conspicuous bold or
underlined print the conduct that is prohibited under Section 1101.651(d)." That is the whole reason
your listing agreement and your buyer representation agreement carry a bolded block: it is the mechanism
by which consent is obtained months before anybody knows the transaction will be in-house. A brokerage
whose policy is to leave the intermediary authorisation blank "unless it comes up" has destroyed its
own ability to comply, because by the time it comes up the seller may be unreachable or unwilling.

Section 1101.559(c) fixes the standard of conduct — "An intermediary shall act fairly and impartially"
— and adds that appointment of an associated license holder under § 1101.560 to communicate with, carry
out the instructions of, and provide opinions and advice to the parties to whom that license holder is
appointed "is a fair and impartial act." Section 1101.560(b) permits appointment only if "the written
consent of the parties under Section 1101.559 authorizes the broker to make the appointment" and "the
broker provides written notice of the appointment to all parties involved in the real estate
transaction," and § 1101.560(c) permits an appointed license holder to "provide opinions and advice
during negotiations to the party to whom the license holder is appointed." Section 1101.651(d)
enumerates what the broker and any appointed license holder may not do, including disclosing that the
seller will accept less than the asking price or that the buyer will pay more than the written offer
unless separately instructed in writing, disclosing confidential information or information a party has
instructed in writing not to disclose subject to the stated exceptions, treating a party dishonestly,
and violating the chapter.

Every one of those provisions implies a policy question that has to have been answered in advance. Does
this brokerage appoint at all, or does it operate intermediary transactions without appointments? Who
signs the written notice of appointment, and on what form? What happens when the sponsoring broker is
personally the listing agent — can the broker be appointed to one side, and what does the firm do
instead? Where is the separate written instruction filed if a seller authorises disclosure of a
willingness to take less? A policy that answers those questions on paper is the difference between a
clean in-house transaction and a § 1101.652(b)(7) complaint.

**Records, because someone will ask you to prove it.** 22 TAC § 535.2 requires that, except for
records destroyed by an act of God as the rule describes, "the broker must, at a minimum, maintain
the following records in a format that is readily available to the Commission for at least four
years from the date of closing, termination of the contract, or end of a real estate transaction".
The enumerated categories include "disclosures"; commission agreements such as listing agreements,
buyer representation agreements, or other written agreements relied upon to claim compensation;
"substantive communications with parties to the transaction"; offers, contracts and related addenda;
receipts and disbursements of compensation; property management contracts; appraisals, broker price
opinions and comparative market analyses; and "sponsorship agreements between the broker and
sponsored sales agents."

Connect that to the first unit. Your IABS delivery is a *disclosure*, and the email in which you
delivered it is a *substantive communication with a party*. Four years from closing, a complaint can
still arrive; § 1101.204(c) bars the Commission from investigating a complaint submitted later than the
fourth anniversary of the date of the incident, which is precisely why the retention period is what it
is. The question your brokerage's policy must answer is not "should I keep it" but "where does it go,
who moves it there, and can the broker retrieve it without me." If the answer is "it is in my sent
folder on my personal phone," the brokerage cannot produce it when you leave, and the broker is the one
§ 1101.652(a-1) reaches for failing or refusing "to produce on request, within a reasonable time, for
inspection by the commission or a commission representative, a document, book, or record that is in the
license holder's possession and relates to a real estate transaction conducted by the license holder."

**The day-one questions.** You should be able to answer each of the following from your own
brokerage's written policy, and each one is tied to an authority that makes it matter. What is the
written scope of my authorised activities, and what does it exclude — 22 TAC § 535.2, first
sentence. Who is my delegated supervisor, is that delegation in writing, and has the Commission been
notified — § 535.2 delegation paragraph. How do I reach that supervisor, and what is the backup,
given the two-calendar-day response obligation in § 535.2 and § 535.157. When and how does the firm
deliver the IABS, in which channel, and where is the copy filed — § 1101.558(b-1) and 22 TAC §
531.20. What does the firm's homepage carry, and is the label and font correct for both the IABS
link and the Consumer Protection Notice link — § 531.20 and § 531.18. Is the Consumer Protection
Notice displayed in each place of business the broker maintains — § 531.18. Does this firm act as
intermediary, does it appoint, and which paragraph of the listing and buyer representation
agreements captures the written consent and the conspicuous bold or underlined print required by §
1101.559(b). Who signs the written notice of appointment required by § 1101.560(b). In whose name
are listings solicited and accepted — § 535.2. What is my registered team name, does it end in
"team" or "group", and who registered it — § 535.154. What is the required proportion of the
broker's name in my advertising — § 535.155(a). Who consents in writing to any compensation I
receive, and from whom may I not accept it — 22 TAC § 535.3 and § 1101.651(b). Who provides my
first-three-times coaching for each type of activity, as § 535.2 requires. Where do transaction
records go, and for how long — § 535.2 retention paragraph.

A brokerage that cannot answer those questions from its manual has not written a policy; it has written
a recruiting brochure. And you should know that the choice of which brokerage to join is, in the
Commission's framing, not a matter of amenities. 22 TAC § 535.2 closes with the reminder that "When
the broker is a business entity, the designated broker is the person responsible for the broker
responsibilities under this section," and that "This section is not meant to create or require an
employer/employee relationship between a broker and a sponsored sales agent." The firm may treat you as
an independent contractor for every commercial purpose and still owe you every one of these duties.
Independence from employment is not independence from supervision.

Finally, a caution about what is practice rather than law. The particular structure of a policy manual
— its chapters, its acknowledgement page, its onboarding checklist, its rule that all offers route
through a transaction coordinator — is a business decision, not a legal requirement, and no source read
for this course prescribes one. What the law prescribes is the *content* listed above. Read your firm's
manual for that content, and do not be reassured by length.

### Statute reading exercise: reading § 535.2 as a list of your entitlements

Open 22 TAC § 535.2 and read it in full, then answer.

1. The rule opens with a duty owed by the broker *to the sales agent*. State it, and state what the
   rule says about limiting or revoking that scope. What form must the limitation take?
2. The rule says the broker "is not required to supervise the sales agents directly." Reconcile that
   sentence with the sentence about acts beyond the explicitly authorised scope, and with Occupations
   Code § 1101.803. What is the broker actually responsible for?
3. Find the delegation paragraph. List every separate obligation it imposes: who must be delegated,
   what form the delegation takes, what must be provided to the Commission and within what period,
   what triggers that period, what must happen when the delegation ends, and who may give that ending
   notice besides the sponsoring broker.
4. The written policies and procedures paragraph lists the subjects a broker's policies must address.
   Which of those items gives a new sales agent a countable entitlement to coaching, and how many
   times does it apply for each type of activity?
5. Identify what § 535.2 does *not* do. Does it require a particular format for the policy manual? Does
   it require the broker to review each contract before it is signed? Does it create an employment
   relationship? Quote the sentence that answers the last question.

### Applied exercise: Caprock Realty grows a team

Caprock Realty is a Lubbock brokerage. Its designated broker is Ruth Alvarado. On 3 February 2026 Ruth
delegates Dana Whitfield, a sales agent, to lead the four-agent West Texas Land Team; the arrangement is
expected to be permanent. Dana's written delegation is signed the same day. Caprock's policy manual, last
revised in 2019, contains a commission schedule, a dress code, and a page headed "Agency" that reads in
full: "Caprock Realty complies with all TREC disclosure requirements." In March, Caprock's buyer client
Hector Salinas asks to see a Caprock listing on a 240-acre tract near Idalou; the seller signed a listing
agreement in January and the buyer signed a buyer representation agreement in February.

1. Under 22 TAC § 535.2, was Dana required to be delegated as a supervisor, and in what form? By what
   date must Ruth have provided Dana's name to the Commission, and what fact about the delegation
   triggers that deadline? Show the date arithmetic.
2. Suppose instead the delegation had been for a defined ten-week coverage period ending in April.
   Would the Commission notice have been triggered? Would the writing requirement still apply? Cite the
   language of the rule that decides each answer.
3. Caprock's manual is seven years old. Identify the items from the written policies and
   procedures paragraph of § 535.2 that a one-line "Agency" page cannot satisfy, and say for each what
   the rule requires.
4. When Hector asks to see the Caprock listing, what does Occupations Code § 1101.561(b) require of the
   broker? Where should the written consent required by § 1101.559(a)(1) already be found, and what
   does § 1101.559(b) require that written agreement to contain in conspicuous bold or underlined
   print? What additional fact must the written consent state under § 1101.559(a)(2)?
5. Ruth wants Dana appointed to Hector and another agent appointed to the seller. State the two
   conditions § 1101.560(b) imposes before any appointment may be made, and identify from § 1101.651(d)
   two specific things Dana may not do once appointed.
6. Four years after closing, a complaint arrives. Which categories in the record-retention paragraph of
   § 535.2 cover the IABS Priya-style delivery email, the buyer representation agreement, and the
   written notice of appointment? From § 1101.204(c), how long after the incident may a complaint be
   submitted before the Commission is barred from investigating it?

### Module recap

This module had two jobs, and they fit together as floor and building. The floor is what Texas law
requires of every license holder about disclosing agency. The building is what a particular brokerage
decides, in writing, so that the floor is met the same way by every agent every time.

The floor lives in Occupations Code § 1101.558, and it has two storeys. Subsection (b) requires a
license holder who represents a party to disclose that representation "orally or in writing" at first
contact with another party to the transaction or with another license holder who represents another
party. Subsection (b-1) requires, at first substantive communication with a party about a proposed
transaction regarding specific real property, a written notice "in at least a 10-point font" that
describes how a broker can represent a party including as an intermediary, the basic duties owed to a
represented party, the basic obligations owed to an unrepresented party, and the name, licence number
and contact information for the license holder and the license holder's supervisor and broker.
Subsection (b-2) sends the text and the delivery methods to Commission rule, and § 1101.558(c) supplies
three exceptions to the (b-1) notice: a residential lease for less than one year where a sale is not
being considered, a meeting with a party the license holder knows is represented by another license
holder, and a communication at a property held open that concerns that property.

22 TAC § 531.20 executes that instruction. It adopts TREC No. IABS 1-2. It requires each license holder
to place a link to a completed IABS Notice in a readily noticeable place on the homepage of each
business website, labelled "Texas Real Estate Commission Information About Brokerage Services" in at
least 10 point font or "TREC Information About Brokerage Services" in at least 12 point font — the
shorter label carrying the larger font. It defines a business website by three cumulative elements,
including that the content "is controlled by the license holder," and it permits the social-media link
to live on the account holder profile or on a separate page reached by a direct link from the platform
or profile. It enumerates four delivery methods — personal delivery, first class mail or overnight
common carrier, the body of an email, and an attachment or in-body link with a specific reference to
the IABS Notice in the body — and it forbids the link from sitting "in a footnote or signature block in
an email." Text message is not on the list. And reproduction is permitted only with verbatim text and
identical spacing, borders and placement, "except that the Broker Contact Information section may be
prefilled."

Do not confuse that with 22 TAC § 531.18. The Consumer Protection Notice, TREC No. CN 1-5, descends
from § 1101.202(a), which is about routing complaints to the Commission, not about explaining agency.
It is *displayed* in a readily noticeable location in each place of business the broker maintains and
*linked* from each business homepage under labels of "Texas Real Estate Commission Consumer Protection
Notice" in at least 10 point font or "TREC Consumer Protection Notice" in at least 12 point font. It is
not handed to a buyer at first substantive communication, and the IABS is not hung on a wall.

The second unit turned to the brokerage. 22 TAC § 535.2 requires a broker to notify a sponsored sales
agent in writing of the scope of the sales agent's authorised activities; makes the broker responsible
for authorised acts unless scope is limited or revoked in writing, while expressly not requiring direct
supervision; and makes the broker responsible for acts beyond the explicit scope the broker permits. It
requires written policies and procedures maintained "on a current basis" covering scope and competence
including geographic competence, active licence status, compensation paid by or through or with the
written consent of the sponsoring broker, timely notice of changes to the Act, rules and promulgated
forms before their effective date, additional education with mandatory coaching for a sales agent's
first three times performing a type of activity, advertising compliance, trust account controls, and
record maintenance. Delegation of the responsibility to assist in administering compliance is permitted
but overall supervisory responsibility cannot be relinquished; anyone who leads, supervises, directs or
manages a team must be delegated as a supervisor; the delegation must be in writing; the broker must
provide the supervisor's name to the Commission within 30 days of a delegation that has lasted or is
anticipated to last more than three consecutive months, and must notify the Commission within 30 days
after the delegation ends, though a delegated supervisor who is a broker may give that ending notice in
lieu of the sponsoring broker. Response obligations run in calendar days: two for a broker or delegated
supervisor to a sponsored sales agent under § 535.2 and two under § 535.157 for responses to a
principal, another party's license holder, or an unrepresented party; three calendar days to deliver
Commission correspondence. Records must be kept at least four years from closing, termination of the
contract, or end of the transaction, and the enumerated categories expressly include disclosures and
substantive communications with parties.

Intermediary practice is where a missing policy becomes an emergency. Section 1101.561(b) obliges a
broker who agrees to represent both a buyer or tenant and a seller or landlord in a transaction to
agree to act as intermediary. Section 1101.559(a) requires written consent from each party and requires
that the consent state the source of any expected compensation; § 1101.559(b) makes a listing or
representation agreement sufficient consent only if it specifies the conduct prohibited by § 1101.651(d)
in conspicuous bold or underlined print; § 1101.559(c) requires the intermediary to act fairly and
impartially; and § 1101.560(b) permits appointments only where the written consent authorises them and
the broker gives written notice of the appointment to all parties. None of that can be assembled after
the buyer asks to see the in-house listing.

Carry three numbers and three sentences out of this module. The numbers: 10 point and 12 point for the
two label options in both § 531.20 and § 531.18; 30 days for the Commission notice of a delegation that
has lasted or is anticipated to last more than three consecutive months; and four years of record
retention under § 535.2, matched by the four-year investigation bar in § 1101.204(c). The sentences:
§ 1101.652(b) reaches a license holder who "fails to make clear to all parties to a real estate
transaction the party for whom the license holder is acting"; § 1101.803 makes a licensed broker
"liable to the commission, the public, and the broker's clients for any conduct engaged in under this
chapter by the broker or by a sales agent associated with or acting for the broker"; and § 535.2 begins
by requiring the broker "to notify a sponsored sales agent in writing of the scope of the sales agent's
authorized activities under the Act." Ask for that writing on your first day.
