# Texas Law of Agency — Module 02

## TREC Topic: Basic Agency Relationships, Disclosure and Duties to the Client

**Prescribed time: 125 minutes.** This module carries the five units the Commission's LOA-1 course
approval form places under *Basic Agency Relationships, Disclosure and Duties to the Client*, whose
scope note is *"Relationship between principal & agent; authority of agent."*

**Learning objectives.** After completing this module you will be able to: state, from § 1101.557(a)
of the Texas Occupations Code, what makes a broker a party's agent in Texas and identify which acts
do and do not create that relationship; describe where a Texas sales agent's authority actually
comes from, quote the written-scope rule in 22 TAC § 535.2(a), and explain what a license holder may
and may not do without further authorization from the principal; classify a residential listing or
buyer representation agreement within the classical universal/general/special taxonomy and
distinguish single agency, the statutory intermediary under §§ 1101.559–1101.561, subagency after
its removal from TRELA, and the no-representation showing that § 1101.562 now recognises; state the
two-calendar-day response obligation in 22 TAC § 535.157 exactly as the rule states it and to
exactly the persons the rule names, and ground each of the six fiduciary duties in the Texas text
that imposes it; and explain what 22 TAC § 531.20 and § 1101.558 require of you by way of
representation disclosure and the Information About Brokerage Services notice, including when the
notice must be delivered, how it may be delivered, when it is not required, and the constraint the
rule places on reproducing it.

**Every legal statement in this module comes from a source that was read for this course.** Where a
statement is practice rather than law, the module says so and cites nothing for it.

## Unit: Agency Defined

You are about to spend a career being called an agent. Before you accept the word, you need to
understand what it costs you, because in Texas the word is not a job title. It is a legal status
that attaches duties to you the moment it arises, whether or not you meant it to arise, whether or
not you were paid, and whether or not anyone signed anything. Most of the discipline cases you will
read about over the next thirty years come down to a license holder who did not understand — or did
not stop to think about — whose agent he was at the moment he opened his mouth.

Start with the ordinary meaning and then let the statute correct it. In ordinary speech, an "agent"
is someone who does things for someone else: a travel agent, an insurance agent, a talent agent.
That intuition is roughly right and legally useless, because it tells you nothing about the
consequences. The legal idea of agency is that one person, the principal, authorises another person,
the agent, to act on the principal's behalf in dealings with third persons — and that because the
agent is acting for the principal rather than for himself, the law loads the agent with obligations
far heavier than the ordinary obligations of one stranger to another. That is the whole engine of
this course. Everything else is detail.

**Texas does not define "agency." It defines when a broker *is* a party's agent.** Chapter 1101 of
the Occupations Code — the Real Estate License Act, which this course will call TRELA — contains no
general definition of the word "agency" in the sections held for this course, and no definition of
"fiduciary" either. What it contains instead is an operative rule that tells you when the status
exists. Section 1101.557(a) provides: "A broker who represents a party in a real estate transaction
or who lists real estate for sale under an exclusive agreement for a party is that party's agent."
Read that sentence three times. It is the closest thing Texas statutory law gives you to a
definition of the agency relationship in brokerage, and almost every word in it is load-bearing.

First, notice the subject. The sentence is about a **broker**. It is not about you, if you are
studying for a sales agent licence. TRELA's architecture is that the broker is the agent of the
party and the sales agent is the agent of the broker. Section 1101.002(7) defines a sales agent as
"a person who is sponsored by a licensed broker for the purpose of performing an act described by
Subdivision (1)" — Subdivision (1) being the long list of brokerage acts in the definition of
"broker." Section 1101.351(c) drives it home: "A licensed sales agent may not engage or attempt to
engage in real estate brokerage unless the sales agent is sponsored by a licensed broker and is
acting for that broker." And § 1101.002(4) defines a license holder as "a broker or sales agent
licensed under this chapter," which is why the Commission's rules so often speak to "license
holders" generally when a duty falls on both. When a seller in Amarillo signs a listing agreement,
the client is the broker's client. You service that client, you owe that client the duties this
module will spend forty minutes on, and you can be disciplined personally for breaching them — but
the representation relationship the statute recognises is between the seller and the broker.

Second, notice the two triggers, joined by "or." A broker is a party's agent if the broker
**represents** a party in a real estate transaction, *or* if the broker **lists real estate for sale
under an exclusive agreement** for a party. Those are not the same thing. The second trigger is
mechanical: sign an exclusive listing, and the agency status follows from the document without
further inquiry. The first trigger is behavioural. "Represents" is not defined in § 1101.557, and
the statute does not say the representation must be in writing. It can arise from what you actually
do.

That reading is not a guess; the Legislature confirmed it in 2026 when it wrote § 1101.562, which
lets a broker show property without representing a party only if the broker, among other things,
"has not agreed with the party, either orally or in writing, to represent the party" and "is not
otherwise acting as the party's agent at the time of showing the real property." A statute that must
exclude *oral* agreement and *otherwise acting as agent* in order to create a safe harbour is a
statute that assumes both of those can create agency in the first place. (Section 1101.562 itself
is taught in Module 05; what it is doing here is telling you how wide § 1101.557's word "represents"
is.) Undertake to advise a buyer
at an open house in Cedar Park about what she should offer, and you have not merely been friendly.
You have arguably begun to represent her, with everything that follows.

**What agency is not.** Three confusions are worth killing now.

It is not the same as being paid. Compensation and representation are separate questions in Texas,
and the 2026 amendments make the separation explicit: § 1101.563(c) requires a written agreement
with a residential buyer to state the license holder's representation status and the broker's
compensation as two separate items, and to disclose in conspicuous language that compensation is not
set by law and is fully negotiable. **Module 05 teaches § 1101.563 in full.** If who pays determined
who was represented, the statute would not need two separate line items. It does. A seller may agree
to contribute to a buyer's broker's fee without that broker becoming the seller's agent, and a
listing broker does not become the buyer's agent by sharing a commission.

It is not the same as having a licence. A licence is permission from the State to perform the acts
listed in § 1101.002(1) for compensation. Agency is a relationship with a particular person. You can
hold a licence for a year and never once be anyone's agent; you can become someone's agent in a
single afternoon conversation.

And it is not the same as friendliness, cooperation, or access. Section 1101.551(2) defines "party"
for the practice subchapter as "a prospective buyer, seller, landlord, or tenant or an authorized
representative of a buyer, seller, landlord, or tenant, including a trustee, guardian, executor,
administrator, receiver, or attorney-in-fact. The term does not include a license holder who
represents a party." That last sentence matters more than students expect. The agent on the other
side of your deal is not a "party." You do not owe the other broker the duties you owe your
principal, and you cannot discharge a duty owed to a party by telling that party's broker something
and assuming it landed.

**The consequence of the status: you become a fiduciary.** The moment agency exists, Texas loads it.
The Commission's rule on fidelity, 22 TAC § 531.2, opens: "A license holder, while acting as an
agent for another, is a fiduciary. Special obligations are imposed when such fiduciary relationships
are created." The Commission's rule on dishonesty says the same thing from the other end: "A license
holder's relationship with the license holder's principal is that of a fiduciary" (22 TAC §
535.156). The broker responsibility rule states the same standard at its highest register and
attaches a qualifier — the duty to convey information to the principal runs "unless prohibited by
other law" (22 TAC § 535.2(b)) — because there are things you learn that other law forbids you to
pass on. Unit 4 returns to it; Module 09 teaches 22 TAC § 535.2.

Chapter 1101 does not itself use the word "client"; it uses "party" and "principal." The industry
vocabulary of *client* (the person you represent) versus *customer* (the person you deal with but do
not represent) is practice terminology, not statutory terminology, though the Commission's own rules
do use "client": § 531.2 speaks of representing "the interests of the client." Use the words
carefully in front of consumers, and understand that the legal question is never what you called
someone. It is whether you represented them.

**Formation, and the limits of what this course can tell you.** The common-law elements of forming
an agency relationship — the doctrines of express appointment, implied appointment by conduct,
estoppel and ratification — are matters of Texas case law. **Texas case law was not held for this
course**, so this module will not state those elements or their tests. What is held, and therefore
what you may rely on, is this: an exclusive listing creates the status by its terms under §
1101.557(a); representation can exist without a signed writing, as the structure of § 1101.562(a)(1)
presupposes; and as of January 1, 2026, a license holder performing brokerage acts for a prospective
*residential buyer* must have a written agreement before showing property, under § 1101.563(b),
which is discussed in Unit 3.

Two more held provisions shape the relationship's edges. On duration, § 1101.652(b)(12) makes it a
ground for discipline to fail to specify a definite termination date not subject to prior notice in
a contract for services requiring a licence, other than a contract to perform property management
services: Texas does not permit open-ended representation agreements outside property management.
Module 10 teaches § 1101.652. On enforceability of your fee, § 1101.806(c) bars an action to recover
a commission for the sale or purchase of real estate unless the promise or agreement, or a
memorandum of it, is in writing and signed by the party against whom the action is brought or by a
person that party authorised to sign; students routinely misattribute that requirement to the
general statute of frauds, and **Module 04 teaches § 1101.806 and Business & Commerce Code § 26.01.**

Finally, understand who answers for what you do. Section 1101.803 makes a licensed broker liable to
the Commission, the public and the broker's clients for any conduct engaged in under the chapter by
the broker or by a sales agent associated with or acting for the broker; Module 09 teaches it. Your
broker is on the hook for your conduct. That is not a reason to relax. It is the reason your broker will
supervise you, and it is the reason the next unit — where your authority actually comes from — is
not an abstraction.

### Applied exercise: The Cedar Park open house

Renata is a sales agent sponsored by Hill Country Realty, a brokerage that holds the listing on a
house on Little Elm Trail in Cedar Park. Renata is hosting the seller's open house on Saturday. A
couple, the Okonkwos, walk in. They are not represented by anyone. Over twenty minutes Renata
answers their questions about the neighbourhood, tells them the seller would probably take less,
suggests they offer $12,000 under list, and offers to write the offer for them that afternoon. She
is not paid anything by the Okonkwos and nothing is signed at the open house.

1. Under § 1101.557(a), whose agent is Hill Country Realty with respect to this property, and on
   which of the section's two triggers does that rest?
2. Renata told the Okonkwos that the seller would probably take less. Identify the subdivision of §
   1101.651(d) that makes that disclosure prohibited for a broker or appointed sales agent acting as
   an intermediary, and then explain why Renata's problem in this fact pattern is worse than an
   intermediary's would be, given § 531.2's statement about whose interests the license holder's
   primary duty is to represent.
3. Section 1101.562(a) lists four conditions a broker must satisfy to show property to a party
   without representing that party. Which of the four has Renata's conduct defeated, and quote the
   language that defeats it?
4. Nothing was signed and Renata was paid nothing by the Okonkwos. Explain, using § 1101.563(c)'s
   separate treatment of representation status and compensation, why the absence of payment does not
   answer the question of whom Renata was representing.
5. Section 1101.803 tells you who else now has exposure. State the provision's effect in your own
   words and name the person it reaches besides Renata.

## Unit: Authority of Agent

An agent acts for a principal. That is only tolerable if the agent's actions stay inside what the
principal actually authorised — which is why the second half of your topic's scope note is
"authority of agent." A student's instinct is that authority is whatever the client is willing to
let you do. The Texas instinct is different and sharper: your authority is a chain, and every link
is written down somewhere.

**A caution about vocabulary before you start.** The phrases you will hear in national textbooks —
*express authority*, *implied authority*, *apparent authority*, *authority by estoppel*,
*ratification* — are common-law categories developed in case law. **They do not appear in Chapter
1101 or in the Commission rules held for this course.** Texas case law was not held for this course,
so this module does not state the Texas tests for those doctrines and you should not attribute a
Texas holding to them on the strength of anything written here. What this module can do — and what
will actually keep you out of trouble in Texas — is teach the categories as the working concepts
they are, and then show you exactly where the held Texas text puts the boundaries.

**Actual authority: express.** Express actual authority is what the principal has told the agent, in
words, that the agent may do. In Texas brokerage practice that means the listing agreement, the
buyer or tenant representation agreement, the property management agreement, and any written
instruction that follows. Read them. The single most common source of an authority dispute is a
license holder who never read the paragraph limiting what he could do.

The statute polices the edges of express authority hard. Section 1101.652(b)(19) makes it a ground
for discipline to offer real property for sale or lease without the owner's knowledge and consent,
(b)(20) to offer it on terms other than those the owner authorised, and (b)(18) to place a sign on
property offering it for sale or lease without the owner's written consent. **Module 10 teaches §
1101.652.** Notice what these three have in common: each is a licence-threatening
offence built out of the simple idea that you may not act beyond what you were authorised to do. In
Texas, exceeding your authority is not merely a contract problem with your client. It is a
disciplinary matter with the Commission.

**Actual authority: implied.** Implied actual authority is the authority reasonably necessary to
carry out what was expressly authorised. A seller who signs a listing has, in the ordinary course,
authorised you to advertise the property, to hold showings, to receive and present offers — because
a listing that authorised none of those would authorise nothing at all. The boundary is worth
stating carefully, though: implied authority fills gaps, it does not override instructions. If the
seller instructs you in writing that no lockbox may be installed, no amount of *but that's how
listings normally work* creates authority to install one.

Two held provisions show you where the implied-authority boundary is drawn in Texas. First, on
money: trust money a broker accepts is held in a fiduciary capacity and must go to the broker's
designated trust account or to an authorised escrow agent, a sales agent may not maintain a trust
account, and trust money a sales agent receives must be delivered immediately to the sponsoring
broker (22 TAC § 535.146(b); Modules 07 and 09 teach § 535.146). Being authorised to sell the house
does not carry with it authority to hold the earnest money. Second, on binding the principal: §
1101.557(c)(1) says that for purposes of that section, "a license holder who has the authority to
bind a party to a lease or sale under a power of attorney or
a property management agreement is also a party to the lease or sale." The Legislature had to name
the two instruments that confer binding authority. That tells you an ordinary listing or buyer
representation agreement does not confer it. You negotiate; your client signs.

**Apparent authority, and the Texas answer to it.** Apparent authority is the classical name for the
situation where a third person reasonably believes the agent has authority because of something the
*principal* did or allowed, even though the agent in fact lacks it. Texas real estate law's most
concrete engagement with that idea is not in the Occupations Code at all. It is in the broker
responsibility rule, and you should learn its first paragraph close to word-perfect. 22 TAC §
535.2(a):

> A broker is required to notify a sponsored sales agent in writing of the scope of the sales
> agent's authorized activities under the Act. Unless such scope is limited or revoked in writing,
> a broker is responsible for the authorized acts of the broker's sales agents, but the broker is
> not required to supervise the sales agents directly. If a broker permits a sponsored sales agent
> to conduct activities beyond the scope explicitly authorized by the broker, those are acts for
> which the broker is responsible.

Three separate rules live in those three sentences. The scope of your activities must be given to
you **in writing**. Limiting or revoking that scope must also be **in writing** — an oral
instruction from your broker does not shrink your written scope for purposes of this rule. And if
the broker *permits* you to work outside the scope he explicitly authorised, the broker owns those
acts anyway. That last sentence is the Texas version of the apparent-authority problem: a broker
cannot hand you a narrow written scope, watch you exceed it for six months, and then disclaim your
conduct when the complaint arrives.

**Where a Texas sales agent's authority actually comes from.** Assemble the chain. The client
authorises the broker (§ 1101.557(a) makes the broker the party's agent). The broker authorises the
sales agent, in writing, and is responsible for those authorised acts (22 TAC § 535.2(a)). The sales
agent may not act at all except for that broker (§ 1101.351(c): "A licensed sales agent may not
engage or attempt to engage in real estate brokerage unless the sales agent is sponsored by a
licensed broker and is acting for that broker."). You do not derive authority directly from the
client. You derive it from your sponsoring broker, whose own authority comes from the client.

Two further links keep the chain intact at the points where students most want to shortcut it. 22
TAC § 535.2(f): "Listings and other agreements for real estate brokerage services must be solicited
and accepted in a broker's name." The listing is not yours; you cannot take it with you when you
change sponsorship, because it was never yours to take. And compensation travels the same route: §
1101.651(b) bars a sales agent from accepting compensation for a real estate transaction from anyone
other than the sponsoring broker (or the broker who was sponsoring her when she earned it), §
1101.651(c) bars a sales agent from paying a commission except through the sponsoring broker, and 22
TAC § 535.3 states the same requirement as one of the sponsoring broker's written consent. **Module
09 teaches § 1101.651(a)–(c) and § 535.3.** A buyer who wants to hand you a cheque at closing is
asking you to break the chain.

The chain can be extended but not cut. 22 TAC § 535.2(e) lets a broker delegate responsibility to
assist in administering compliance with the Act and Rules without relinquishing overall
responsibility for supervising sponsored license holders, requires any license holder who leads,
supervises, directs or manages a team to be delegated as a supervisor in writing, and requires
notice of the delegation to the Commission; **Module 09 teaches § 535.2, including the delegation
and notice mechanics.** If you join a team, the person leading it holds delegated authority, in
writing — not authority that materialised because everyone calls her the team lead.

**The limits of authority, seen from the third party's side.** Authority protects the principal from
the agent. Texas also has a provision that protects each of them from the other's misstatements:
under § 1101.805(d) and (e), neither a party nor a license holder is liable for the other's
misrepresentation or concealment of a material fact unless that person both knew of the falsity and
failed to disclose that knowledge. Read the two conditions and the conjunction that joins them. You
are not an insurer of your seller's honesty. You are, however, finished the moment you know he is
lying and stay quiet. Subsection (c) preserves the broker's responsibility for the acts and
omissions of a sales agent acting for the broker, and subsection (f) was repealed by Acts 2025, 89th
Leg., R.S., Ch. 1172 (S.B. 1968), Sec. 13(2), effective January 1, 2026 — a point Unit 3 returns to
when it takes up subagency. **Module 03 teaches §§ 1101.802, 1101.804 and 1101.805**, and Module 10
teaches § 1101.801, which provides that discipline under § 1101.652 does not relieve a person from
civil or criminal liability. A Commission order is not a settlement of the lawsuit.

### Statute reading exercise: Tracing the chain of authority

Open § 1101.351 and § 1101.557 of the Occupations Code and 22 TAC § 535.2 and § 535.3. Using only
the text in front of you, answer:

1. Section 1101.557(a) names two ways a broker becomes a party's agent. Write both out. Does either
   of them require a signed writing on its face?
2. Section 535.2(a) requires the broker to notify the sales agent of the scope of authorised
   activities in a particular form, and requires any limitation or revocation of that scope to be in

   if the broker permits the agent to work outside the explicit scope?
3. Section 535.2(a) contains a sentence about direct supervision. Quote it, and say what it means
   for the argument that a broker who never watched his sales agent cannot be responsible for what
   the agent did.
4. Read § 1101.351(c) and § 535.2(f) together. If a sales agent personally negotiates and signs a

   the other one tell you about who the listing belongs to?
5. Section 535.3 and § 1101.651(b) both address a sales agent receiving compensation. Identify the
   one word § 535.3 adds that § 1101.651(b) does not use, and explain what a sales agent must obtain

## Unit: Classifications of Agency

Classifying an agency relationship is not vocabulary drill. It answers a practical question: how
much of the principal's life has this agent been handed? A client who signs a listing has handed
over one house for a fixed period. A client who signs a general power of attorney has handed over
far more. Texas law treats those two situations completely differently, and if you cannot tell which
one you are in, you will not know whether you may sign for your client.

**The classical taxonomy, and its status in Texas.** The traditional three-part classification runs
universal agent, general agent, special agent, ordered from broadest to narrowest authority. This
taxonomy is classical agency vocabulary used throughout the industry and in national texts; **a
Texas statute or Commission rule defining any of the three terms was not held for this course**, and
you
should not go looking for them in Chapter 1101, because they are not there. Learn them as the
working shorthand they are, and then anchor them in Texas text.

A *universal* agent is one authorised to do anything the principal may lawfully delegate — the
broadest possible grant. A *general* agent is authorised to act for the principal in a continuing
series of transactions of a particular kind, typically within an ongoing business or management
relationship. A *special* agent is authorised for a single transaction or a narrow set of acts, with
no power to bind the principal to anything.

**A residential listing or buyer representation agreement is a special agency.** Do not take that on
the strength of the taxonomy; take it on the text. Section 1101.557(c)(1) provides that "a license
holder who has the authority to bind a party to a lease or sale under a power of attorney or a
property management agreement is also a party to the lease or sale." The Legislature identified the
two instruments that confer authority to *bind* — a power of attorney and a property management
agreement. A listing agreement is not on that list. Sections 1101.652(b)(19) and (20) confirm the
same limit from the disciplinary side: you may not offer property for sale without the owner's
knowledge and consent, and you may not offer it on terms other than those the owner authorised. An
agent who could bind the seller would not need the seller's authorisation for the terms. So: you
market, you solicit, you negotiate, you present, you advise. Your client signs. That is special
agency, and it is what almost every relationship you will ever have as a residential license holder
is.

Property management is the place where the line moves. A property management agreement can confer
authority to bind — § 1101.557(c)(1) says so — and it typically runs over an indefinite series of
leases, renewals, repairs and rent collections rather than one transaction. That is the shape of
general agency. Texas signals its distinctiveness elsewhere too: § 1101.652(b)(12) requires a
definite termination date in contracts for services requiring a licence but carves out "a contract
to perform property management services," and 22 TAC § 535.2(d) makes the broker responsible for a
sponsored sales agent's property management activity that requires a real estate licence (Module
09). Treat a property management engagement as a materially larger grant of authority
than a listing, because the statute does.

Universal agency has essentially no place in ordinary brokerage. The nearest thing you will
encounter is a client acting through an attorney-in-fact under a power of attorney — and note that §
1101.551(2) treats an "attorney-in-fact" as an authorized representative who counts as a *party*,
not as your co-agent, and that § 1101.005(2) exempts from the chapter "an attorney-in-fact
authorized under a power of attorney to conduct not more than three real estate transactions
annually." When a power of attorney appears in your transaction, your obligation is to get it to the
title company and your broker, not to interpret its scope yourself.

**Single agency.** In a single agency, the broker represents one side of the transaction and only
one side. The listing broker represents the seller; a different brokerage represents the buyer; each
owes undivided fidelity to its own client and, under 22 TAC § 531.2, must nonetheless "treat other
parties to a transaction fairly." This is the cleanest arrangement in Texas and the easiest to
explain to a consumer. The duties in Unit 4 apply at full strength.

**Dual agency and the Texas answer: the intermediary.** The moment one broker is asked to represent
both sides, the fiduciary duties collide — you cannot owe undivided loyalty in two directions. Texas
does not solve this by permitting a broker to be a disclosed dual agent under the common law. It
solves it by statute, through the intermediary in Subchapter L. Section 1101.551(1) defines the
role: an intermediary "means a broker who is employed to negotiate a transaction between the parties
to a transaction and for that purpose may act as an agent of the parties." Section 1101.561(b) makes
the status mandatory rather than optional: "A broker must agree to act as an intermediary under this
subchapter if the broker agrees to represent in a transaction" both "a buyer or tenant" and "a
seller or landlord." There is no election. If your brokerage's client is buying your brokerage's
listing, the broker is an intermediary.

For a unit on classifications that is the whole of it: intermediary is a classification of the
agency relationship, and it is the one Texas substitutes for dual agency. **The machinery belongs to
Module 06, which teaches it in full** — the written-consent conditions in § 1101.559, the
appointment of associated license holders under § 1101.560, the five prohibitions in § 1101.651(d)
with the qualifiers attached to them, and the rule in § 1101.561(a) that an intermediary's duties
supersede a license holder's duties under any other law, including common law. Do not attempt to run
an intermediary transaction on what you have read in this module.

**Subagency, and what changed on January 1, 2026.** Subagency is the classical arrangement in which
a broker cooperating with the listing broker works for the seller through the listing broker rather
than for the buyer. It used to be visible in TRELA. It is not any more. A word search of Chapter
1101 as held for this course returns **no occurrence of "subagent" or "subagency" anywhere in the
chapter**. Two provisions were repealed by the same 2025 act: § 1101.002(8), a definition, was
repealed by Acts 2025, 89th Leg., R.S., Ch. 1172 (S.B. 1968), Sec. 13(1), effective January 1, 2026,
and § 1101.805(f) was repealed by Sec. 13(2) of the same act, effective the same day. The statute as
held records that those provisions were repealed but does not preserve their former text, so this
module does not state what they said.

TREC's own explanatory article on the 2026 changes — **guidance, not controlling authority** —
describes the two repeals under the heading "Subagency Removed From TRELA" and states: "This means
the circumstances in which subagency could arise in your real estate practice are limited. However,
subagency has not been eliminated generally as a legal concept." Take that at exactly its weight:
the statutory scaffolding is gone, the general legal concept is not, and TREC's advice when the
situation seems to arise is to contact your broker or an attorney. The Seller Agency module of this
course carries a dedicated unit on sub agency and takes the subject further.

**No representation: the newest classification.** Before 2026 a Texas license holder dealing with an
unrepresented buyer occupied an awkward space — not the buyer's agent, but doing things for the
buyer. Section 1101.562, added by Acts 2025, 89th Leg., R.S., Ch. 1172 (S.B. 1968), Sec. 10,
effective January 1, 2026, gives that posture a statutory shape. A broker may show real property
available for sale or lease to a party without representing the party only if all four of the
section's conditions hold: the broker has not agreed with the party, orally or in writing, to
represent the party; is not otherwise acting as the party's agent at the time of the showing;
provides the party no opinions or advice regarding the real property or real estate transactions in
general; and performs no other act of real estate brokerage for the party. Subsection (b) routes the
showing through a disclosure step, which Unit 5 takes apart because the disclosures are this
module's. Section 1101.563, added by the same act and effective the same day, requires a written
agreement with a prospective buyer of residential real property before showing, sets the agreement's
required contents, and caps a showing-only agreement at non-exclusive with a termination date no
more than fourteen days out. **Module 05 teaches §§ 1101.562 and 1101.563 in full, including the
2026 changes.** What this unit needs from them is only the classification: since January 1, 2026,
Texas recognises a posture in which a license holder deals with a party and represents nobody.

### Applied exercise: Intermediary at a Lubbock brokerage

Caprock Properties is a brokerage in Lubbock. Its broker, Yolanda, holds an exclusive listing on a
house on 21st Street at $415,000; the listing agreement authorises Caprock to act as an intermediary
and sets out the § 1101.651(d) prohibitions in conspicuous bold print. A buyer, Dev, signs a buyer
representation agreement with Caprock that contains the same intermediary authorisation and bold
prohibitions. Dev wants to make an offer on the 21st Street house. The total commission stated in
the listing is 5.5% of the sales price, and Caprock's written policy pays 55% of the total to the
sales agents who worked the transaction, split evenly between them, with the balance retained by the
brokerage.

1. Section 1101.561(b) says something about whether Yolanda had a choice here. State it, and say
   which of the two things she must have been agreeing to represent for it to apply.
2. Classify the relationship Caprock now stands in, and say what it is not: name the arrangement
   Texas does *not* use to answer a broker representing both sides. Module 06 is where the consent,
   appointment and prohibition machinery is taught; do not answer it from this module.
3. The house sells for $408,000. Compute the total commission at 5.5%, the amount allocated to the
   sales agents under Caprock's 55% policy, each agent's share, and the amount retained by the
   brokerage.

## Unit: Duty to Respond, Fiduciary Duties and Responsibilities

This is the longest unit in the module and the one you will be tested on hardest, because it is
where the abstraction of "agency" turns into a list of things you must actually do. Work through it
slowly. Start with the duty that has a number attached, because numbers are what get quoted back at
you in a complaint.

**The duty to respond: 22 TAC § 535.157, in full.** The rule is one sentence, and it is titled
"Obligation to Respond Timely":

> A broker or sales agent must respond to his or her principal, a broker or sales agent
> representing another party to a real estate transaction, or an unrepresented party to a real
> estate transaction within two calendar days.

Take it apart with care, because it is easy to overstate and easy to understate.

*Who is bound.* "A broker or sales agent." Both. There is no sponsorship-based exemption; a sales
agent owes the response duty personally.

*To whom the duty runs.* Exactly three categories, and no more: (1) "his or her principal" — the
client; (2) "a broker or sales agent representing another party to a real estate transaction" — the
license holder on the other side; and (3) "an unrepresented party to a real estate transaction" —
the for-sale-by-owner, the unrepresented buyer, the unrepresented tenant. Notice who is *not* on
that list. The rule does not, by its terms, run to a member of the public who is not a party to a
real estate transaction, to a party who is represented by another broker (you deal with that party's
license holder, which is category (2)), to a title company, to an inspector, or to a lender. It also
does not run from a sales agent *to the sales agent's own broker* — that duty runs the other
direction and lives elsewhere, as you will see in a moment.

*How long.* The rule gives you "within two calendar days." Calendar, not business. The rule does not
exclude Saturdays, Sundays or holidays, and it does not say anything about office hours. Compare it
with the trust-money deposit deadline in 22 TAC § 535.146(b), which the Commission set at the close
of business of the second *working* day after receipt. The Commission knows how to write "working
day" when it means working day. In § 535.157 it wrote "calendar days." Do not import a business-day
rule that is not there.

*What the rule does not say.* It does not define the word "respond." It does not require the
response to be in writing, and it does not require the response to be substantive or complete — the
text says respond, not resolve and not answer fully. It does not state when the two days begin to
run; it does not say that they run from receipt, or from the day after receipt. Because the rule
leaves the starting point unstated, the defensible practice — this is practice, not law — is to
treat the clock as starting when the communication arrives and to reply the same day or the next, in
writing, even if the reply says only that you have the message, that you are getting the answer from
your client, and that you will come back on Thursday.

*The mirror rule inside the brokerage.* The duty that § 535.157 does not impose — a sales agent's
duty to respond to her own broker — has a counterpart running the other way. 22 TAC § 535.2(j)
requires a broker, or a supervisor delegated under § 535.2(e), to respond to sponsored sales agents
within two calendar days, and says expressly that this is in addition to the requirements of §
535.157, not a substitute for them. **Module 09 teaches § 535.2.**

*A worked example.* You represent a seller in Pflugerville. On Friday at 4:40 p.m. the buyer's agent
emails you an offer and asks for confirmation of receipt. Saturday and Sunday are calendar days; the
rule does not pause for the weekend. Two calendar days from Friday runs into Sunday, so a Monday
morning reply is already outside the window the rule describes on the most natural reading of it,
and telling the Commission that your office was closed will not help you, because § 535.157 does not
speak in working days. Separately, § 1101.557(b)(3) obliges the broker to "at a minimum, answer the
party's questions and present any offer to or from the party," and § 1101.557(b)(2) obliges the
broker to "inform the party if the broker receives material information related to a transaction to
list, buy, sell, or lease the party's real estate, including the receipt of an offer by the broker."
A received offer is the paradigm case of both duties firing at once: respond to the other agent
within two calendar days, and get the offer in front of your seller.

**Now the fiduciary duties.** The six-duty framework — loyalty, obedience, disclosure,
confidentiality, accounting, and reasonable care and diligence — is the standard industry teaching
frame, and this course uses it because you will meet it everywhere. **It is not a statutory list**;
a Texas source enumerating six duties under those names was not held for this course. What Texas has
is a set of rules and statutes that, taken together, impose every one of them. What follows gives
you the frame and then the Texas text that actually does the work. Learn the text. The frame is
only a filing system.

**Loyalty.** The rule of fidelity, 22 TAC § 531.2, says a license holder acting as an agent for
another is a fiduciary, that special obligations are imposed when such fiduciary relationships are
created, and that they demand — among other things — "that the primary duty of the license holder is
to represent the interests of the client, and the license holder's position, in this respect, should
be clear to all parties concerned in a real estate transaction," "that the license holder be
faithful and observant to trust placed in the license holder, and be scrupulous and meticulous in
performing the license holder's functions," and "that the license holder place no personal interest
above that of the client." The same rule carries the balancing clause you must never quote without:
"that, however, the license holder, in performing duties to the client, shall treat other parties to
a transaction fairly."

22 TAC § 535.156 states loyalty in the plainest available words: "The license holder must put the
interest of the license holder's principal above the license holder's own interest. A license holder
must deal honestly and fairly with all parties; however, the license holder represents only the
principal and owes a duty of fidelity to such principal." Two sentences, two duties, and the
semicolon in the middle is the whole ethic of Texas brokerage: honesty and fairness to everyone,
fidelity to one.

Loyalty has teeth outside the canons. Section 1101.652(b)(16) reaches a license holder who acts in a
dual capacity as broker and undisclosed principal in a real estate transaction (Module 10). 22 TAC §
535.144 extends the same self-dealing rule to a license holder acting for a spouse, parent or child,
for a business entity the license holder owns more than ten per cent of, or for a trust in the
relationships the rule names, and requires written disclosure of licensed status before any contract
of sale or rental agreement; **Module 12 teaches § 535.144.** And 22 TAC § 535.16, taught in Module
04, carries the net listing rule: a broker may not take a net listing unless the principal requires
one and appears familiar with current market values, and a net listing used on that footing must
assure the principal of not less than the principal's desired price and limit the broker to a
specified maximum commission. That is not a flat prohibition — do not tell a client net listings are
illegal in Texas — but it is a narrow, conditional permission with two mandatory contract terms
attached, and the reason for it is pure loyalty: a net listing places the broker's interest in the
spread above the principal's interest in the best possible price.

**Obedience.** The principal instructs; the agent follows lawful instructions. 22 TAC § 535.16(a)
puts the affirmative side of it: "A broker is obligated under a listing contract to negotiate the
best possible transaction for the principal, the broker has agreed to represent." The prohibitive
side is §§ 1101.652(b)(19) and (20), already met in Unit 2: no offering without the owner's
knowledge and consent, no offering on terms other than those authorised. And the "unless otherwise
instructed in a separate writing" qualifiers in § 1101.651(d)(1)–(3), taught in Module 06, show the
shape of obedience in the intermediary setting: the client's instruction, in a separate writing,
changes what you may do.

Obedience is not unlimited. It never reaches an unlawful instruction. A seller who instructs you not
to show the house to a particular category of buyer is instructing you to violate §
1101.652**(b)**(32) — note the subsection — which reaches a license holder who discriminates against
an owner, potential buyer, landlord or potential tenant on the basis of race, color, religion, sex,
disability, familial status, national origin or ancestry, including steering a prospective buyer or
tenant to a different area on any of those bases. 22 TAC § 531.19 covers the same ground in the
Commission's canons. **Module 10 teaches both.** There is no client instruction that makes any of
that lawful, and *my seller told me to* is a confession, not a defence.

**Disclosure.** This is the duty that generates the most complaints, because it is affirmative: you
must tell your principal things without being asked. The broad statement is 22 TAC § 535.2(b) — the
broker is obliged to convey to the principal all information known to the agent that may affect the
principal's decision, unless prohibited by other law. 22 TAC § 535.156 states the offer-specific
version and its exception, and you must learn both halves together:

> A license holder shall convey to the principal all known information which would affect the
> principal's decision on whether or not to make, accept or reject offers; however, if the
> principal has agreed in writing that offers are not to be submitted after the principal has
> entered into a contract to buy, sell, rent, or lease a property, the license holder shall have
> no duty to submit offers to the principal after the principal has accepted an offer.

Quote the first clause alone and you will teach a falsehood. The duty to submit offers after the
client is under contract can be switched off — but only if the principal has agreed **in writing**
that offers are not to be submitted after the principal has entered into a contract. No writing, no
exception; you keep presenting offers.

The same rule adds a continuing obligation: "A license holder has an affirmative duty to keep the
principal informed at all times of significant information applicable to the transaction or
transactions in which the license holder is acting as agent for the principal." The words "at all
times" and "affirmative" are the operative ones. Silence is a breach even if nobody asked.

The statute reinforces this at § 1101.557(b): a broker who represents a party or lists under an
exclusive agreement "must inform the party if the broker receives material information related to a
transaction to list, buy, sell, or lease the party's real estate, including the receipt of an offer
by the broker" and "shall, at a minimum, answer the party's questions and present any offer to or
from the party."

Disclosure also runs outward, to people who are not your client, in specific respects. Section
1101.652(b)(3) reaches a material misrepresentation to a potential buyer concerning a significant
defect, including a latent structural defect, known to the license holder that would be a
significant factor to a reasonable and prudent buyer, and (b)(4) reaches failing to disclose such a
defect to a potential buyer. 22 TAC § 535.156 puts it as a duty "to convey accurate information to
members of the public with whom the license holder deals." Your seller's confidences do not cover
the foundation.

There is a boundary on the other side, and it is statutory. Section 1101.556 provides that a license
holder is not required to inquire about, disclose or release information about whether an occupant
had or may have had AIDS, an HIV-related illness or an HIV infection, or whether a death occurred on
the property by natural causes, suicide or accident unrelated to the condition of the property, and
§ 1101.802 backs that with immunity. **Module 03 teaches §§ 1101.556 and 1101.802.** They are the
"unless prohibited by other law" clause in § 535.2(b) doing real work: some information the law
positively protects.

**Confidentiality.** The sharpest held statement of the confidentiality duty in Texas is §
1101.651(d)(3), and it is worth noticing that it is written for the intermediary context. It forbids
disclosure of confidential information, or of information a party has instructed the broker or sales
agent in writing not to disclose, subject to three exceptions — a separate writing from the party, a
disclosure required by the chapter or by court order, and information that materially relates to the
condition of the property. **Module 06 teaches § 1101.651(d).** Combine it with § 531.2's demand
that the license holder "be faithful and observant to trust placed in the license holder" and §
535.156's statement that the license
holder "represents only the principal and owes a duty of fidelity to such principal," and the
working rule for any agency, not only the intermediary, is: your client's negotiating position,
motivation, financial capacity and timetable are not yours to give away; property condition never
becomes confidential; and a court order or a disclosure duty imposed by TRELA beats an instruction
to stay quiet.

Confidentiality does not expire when the deal does. **A Texas source stating a duration for the
confidentiality obligation was not held for this course**, so this course does not state one; the
prudent assumption, as practice
rather than law, is that it continues after closing and after termination, and your listing and
representation agreements may say so expressly.

**Accounting.** Accounting is the duty to keep the principal's money and property separate from your
own, to be able to say at any moment where it is, and to hand it over when it is due. Four
subdivisions of § 1101.652(b) carry it: (b)(9), failing within a reasonable time properly to account
for or remit money received that belongs to another; (b)(10), commingling another's money with your
own; (b)(30), failing to deposit escrow or trust funds within a reasonable time with an authorised
title company or in a custodial, trust or escrow account at an authorised banking institution; and
(b)(31), disbursing from such an account before the transaction is completed or terminated.

The mechanics are in 22 TAC § 535.146 — the definition of trust money, the broker's fiduciary
holding of it, the bar on a sales agent maintaining a trust account, the deposit deadline set at the
close of business of the second working day after receipt, and what counts as prima facie evidence
of commingling — and 22
TAC § 535.2(c) makes the broker responsible for the proper handling of trust funds placed with the
broker. **Modules 07 and 09 teach § 535.146; Module 09 teaches § 535.2.** For your purposes as a
sales agent the operative sentence is the one you already met in Unit 2: you do not hold trust
money, you deliver it to your broker immediately.

Accounting also covers money that comes to you *because* of the transaction. Section 1101.652(b)(13)
reaches an undisclosed commission, rebate or direct profit on an expenditure made for a principal,
and (b)(8) reaches receiving compensation from more than one party without the full knowledge and
consent of all parties. 22 TAC § 535.148, taught in Module 05, makes the consent requirement
concrete — disclosure to the client and the client's consent before receiving a commission, rebate
or fee from someone other than the person you represent — and separately prohibits paying or
receiving a fee or other valuable consideration to or from another settlement service provider for
referrals or for inclusion on preferred-provider lists. The referral fee from the inspector you
always recommend is not a grey area.

**Reasonable care and diligence.** Competence is a fiduciary duty in Texas, not an aspiration.
Section 1101.652(b)(1) makes it a ground for discipline to act negligently or incompetently while
engaged in real estate brokerage. Note the subsection carefully, because this next one is different:
§ 1101.652**(a)**(6) — subsection (a), not (b) — reaches a license holder who fails to consider
market conditions for the specific geographic area in which the license holder is providing a
service. Students misfile that one constantly.

The Commission's canon on competency, 22 TAC § 531.4, states the standard: "It is the obligation of
a license holder to be knowledgeable and competent as a real estate brokerage practitioner." The
license holder must "be informed on local market issues and conditions affecting real estate in the
geographic area where a license holder provides services to a client"; "be informed on national,
state, and local issues and developments in the real estate industry"; "exercise judgment and skill
in the performance of brokerage activities"; and "be educated in the characteristics involved in the
specific type of real estate being brokered for others." The canon on integrity, 22 TAC § 531.3,
adds: "A license holder has a special obligation to exercise integrity in the discharge of the
license holder's responsibilities, including employment of prudence and caution so as to avoid
misrepresentation, in any way, by acts of commission or omission." Read "acts of commission or
omission" as the Commission telling you that saying nothing can be a misrepresentation.

The broker's written policy obligations under 22 TAC § 535.2(i) push competence down into the
brokerage: the policies must ensure each sponsored sales agent is advised of the scope of authorised
activities and is competent to conduct them, including in the geographic market area where the agent
represents clients, and they carry a floor — the first three times a sales agent performs a type of
brokerage activity, the broker must require coaching and assistance from an experienced license
holder competent for that activity. **Module 09 teaches § 535.2.** First three times: if you have
never done a farm and ranch transaction, your broker is required by rule to put an experienced
license holder alongside you for the first three.

**The catch-all, and what a breach costs.** Two subdivisions matter to this topic above the rest. §
1101.652(b)(2) makes dishonest conduct, bad faith or conduct demonstrating untrustworthiness a
ground for suspension or revocation, and it appears in more Commission orders than any other. §
1101.652(b)(7) is the agency-specific one: failing to make clear to all parties the party for whom
the license holder is acting. **Module 10 teaches § 1101.652 in full, including the false-promise
subdivisions (b)(5) and (b)(6), the sweep-up in (b)(33), and the disciplinary process.** Section
1101.801 adds that discipline does not relieve a person of civil or criminal liability, and §
1101.803 puts your broker in the room with you for everything you do.

## Unit: Information About Brokerage Services - Disclosure of Representation

Everything in this module so far has been about what agency *is* and what it *requires*. This unit
is about telling people. Texas consumers cannot protect themselves against a relationship they do
not know exists, and so TRELA imposes two entirely separate disclosure duties, at two different
moments, in two different forms, with two different sets of exceptions. Students merge them
constantly. Do not.

**Duty one: the representation disclosure, § 1101.558(b).** "A license holder who represents a party
in a proposed real estate transaction shall disclose, orally or in writing, that representation at
the time of the license holder's first contact with" either "another party to the transaction" or
"another license holder who represents another party to the transaction."

Four things to hold. It applies only if you *represent* someone. It may be **oral or in writing** —
this is the one disclosure Texas lets you make by speaking. It is due at **first contact**, not
first substantive communication. And its audience is the other side: another party, or the license
holder representing another party. Section 1101.558(b) has no exception list attached to it. The
exceptions in subsection (c), which you will meet in a moment, are addressed to the notice required
by Subsection (b-1), not to the (b) disclosure.

The disciplinary backstop is § 1101.652(b)(7): failing "to make clear to all parties to a real
estate transaction the party for whom the license holder is acting."

**Duty two: the written notice, § 1101.558(b-1).** This is the Information About Brokerage Services
notice — the IABS. The statute provides: "At the time of a license holder's first substantive
communication with a party relating to a proposed transaction regarding specific real property, the
license holder shall provide to the party written notice in at least a 10-point font that:" Four
contents follow.

> (1) describes the ways in which a broker can represent a party to a real estate transaction,
> including
> as an intermediary;
> (2) describes the basic duties and obligations a broker has to a party to a real estate
> transaction
> that the broker represents;
> (3) describes the basic obligations a broker has to a party to a real estate transaction that the
> broker does not represent; and
> (4) provides the name, license number, and contact information for the license holder and the
> license
> holder's supervisor and broker, if applicable.

Contrast it with duty one at every point. It is owed whether or not you represent anyone — the
trigger is a substantive communication with a party, not a representation you hold. It must be
**written**, in at least a 10-point font. It is due at the **first substantive communication with a
party relating to a proposed transaction regarding specific real property** — all of those
qualifiers matter; a general conversation about the market, with no specific property in view, is
not the trigger the statute describes. Its audience is the party you are communicating with, not the
other side's agent. And note what subdivision (4) requires: the name, license number and contact
information for **you**, for **your supervisor** and for **your broker**, "if applicable." That is
why a completed IABS carries several licence numbers.

Section 1101.558(b-2) hands the drafting to the Commission: "The commission by rule shall prescribe
the text of the notice required under Subsections (b-1)(1) and (2) and establish the methods by
which a license holder shall provide the notice." You do not write this notice. TREC does.

**A note on the section's history, because it is a trap.** Subsections (a), (d) and (e) of §
1101.558 were repealed by Acts 2015, 84th Leg., R.S., Ch. 1158, Sec. 92, effective January 1, 2016;
the statute as held records the repeals without preserving the repealed text, so this module does
not state what those subsections said. The section was most recently amended by Acts 2025, 89th
Leg., R.S., Ch. 1172 (S.B. 1968), Sec. 9, effective January 1, 2026. If you have an older study
guide that cites § 1101.558(c) for the notice requirement or quotes subsection (d), throw it away.
The notice duty is now in subsection (b-1) and subsection (c) is the exception list.

**When the notice is NOT required: § 1101.558(c).** The section opens: "A license holder is not
required to provide the notice required by Subsection (b-1) if:" Three exceptions follow.

> (1) the proposed transaction is for a residential lease for less than one year and a sale is not
> being
> considered;
> (2) the license holder meets with a party who the license holder knows is represented by another
> license holder; or
> (3) the communication occurs at a property that is held open for any prospective buyer or tenant
> and
> the communication concerns that property.

Read each carefully. Exception (1) has two elements joined by "and": a residential lease of **less
than one year** *and* no sale being considered. A fourteen-month lease is outside it. A twelve-month
lease with a purchase option under discussion is outside it. Exception (2) turns on your
**knowledge** that the party is represented by another license holder — not on your assumption, and
not on the party's vague reference to "my realtor." Exception (3) is the open-house exception, and
it is narrow in two ways: the property must be "held open for any prospective buyer or tenant," and
"the communication concerns that property." Turn the conversation at the open house to the buyer's
own house down the road, and the exception by its terms no longer covers that communication.

These three exceptions relieve you of the **(b-1) notice**. They do not touch the § 1101.558(b)
representation disclosure, they do not touch § 1101.563's written-agreement requirement, and — as
you will see — they do not touch the website-link duty in the Commission's rule.

**The rule: 22 TAC § 531.20.** The Commission's IABS rule opens by adopting the form: "The
Commission adopts by reference the Information About Brokerage Services Notice, TREC No. IABS 1-2
(IABS Notice). The IABS Notice is published by and available from the Texas Real Estate Commission,
P.O. Box 12188, Austin, Texas 78711-2188, www.trec.texas.gov." **The IABS Notice form itself was not
held for this course**, so this module does not reproduce or paraphrase the language printed on the
form; what it teaches is the rule and the statute that govern its use.

Subsection (b) imposes two duties. Under (b)(1), each license holder shall provide "a link to a
completed IABS Notice in a readily noticeable place on the homepage of each business website,
labeled:" — and then one of two labels, either "Texas Real Estate Commission Information About
Brokerage Services", in at least 10 point font, or "TREC Information About Brokerage Services", in
at least 12 point font. Two label options, two different minimum font sizes; the longer label may be
smaller. Under (b)(2), each license holder shall provide "the completed IABS Notice at the first
substantive communication as required under §1101.558, Texas Occupations Code." Note that the rule
as held cross-references § 1101.558 as a whole; the statutory duty it is implementing now sits in §
1101.558(b-1).

The term business website is defined in the rule as "a website on the internet that: is accessible
to the public; contains information about a license holder's real estate brokerage services; and the
content of the website is controlled by the license holder." All three elements. A personal
Instagram account that never mentions your brokerage services is not a business website under that
definition; one that advertises your listings is. For social media, the rule says the link required
under subsection (b)(1) "may be located on: the account holder profile; or a separate page or
website through a direct link from the social media platform or account holder profile." You may
satisfy it from the profile bio, provided the link is there and labelled as the rule requires.

**How the notice may be delivered.** The rule states the permitted methods: "For purposes of
§1101.558, Texas Occupations Code, the completed IABS Notice can be provided: by personal delivery
by the license holder; by first class mail or overnight common carrier delivery service; in the body
of an email; or as an attachment to an email, or a link within the body of an email, with a specific
reference to the IABS Notice in the body of the email." Four routes: hand it over; mail it first
class or send it by overnight common carrier; paste it into the body of an email; or attach it or
link it, **with a specific reference to the IABS Notice in the body of the email**. That last
qualifier is the one people fail. An attachment named *iabs.pdf* with an email body that says only
that it was great talking today does not comply, because the body contains no specific reference to
the IABS Notice.

And the rule adds a flat prohibition: "The link to a completed IABS Notice may not be in a footnote
or signature block in an email." Every year, license holders decide the tidy solution is to drop the
IABS link into the email signature so it goes out on everything. The rule forecloses exactly that.

**Reproducing the form.** The last requirement in § 531.20 is one students never expect: "License
holders may reproduce the IABS Notice published by the Commission, provided that the text of the
IABS Notice is copied verbatim and the spacing, borders and placement of text on the page must
appear to be identical to that in the published version of the IABS Notice, except that the Broker
Contact Information section may be prefilled." You may reproduce it — many brokerages build it into
their transaction software — but the text must be **verbatim** and the spacing, borders and
placement of text must **appear to be identical** to the published version. The single permitted
deviation is that the Broker Contact Information section may be prefilled. You may not add your logo
into the body, reformat it to fit a page, trim a paragraph you find confusing, or otherwise tidy it
up. If your brokerage's template does any of that, it is out of compliance with § 531.20.

**A companion notice that is not the IABS.** 22 TAC § 531.18 adopts a different document by
reference, the Consumer Protection Notice, TREC No. CN 1-5, and requires it to be displayed in each
place of business and linked from the homepage of each business website under the same two-label,
two-font-size structure as the IABS link. **Module 08 teaches § 531.18.** For this unit the point is
only that it is a different form: it carries a display-in-the-office requirement the IABS rule does
not, it is not delivered at first substantive communication, and it means two links on your
homepage, not one. The Consumer Protection Notice form itself was not held for this course either.

**How the 2026 changes wire into all of this.** Section 1101.562(b), which you met in Unit 3, routes
a non-representation showing through this unit's two duties: before showing property without
representing the party, the broker must, as applicable, "disclose to the party as required by
Section 1101.558(b) if the broker represents the owner of the real property" or "provide to the
party the written notice described by Section 1101.558(b-1) if the broker does not represent the
owner of the real property." So the disclosure you owe depends on which side, if any, you are on.
Represent the owner: make the (b) representation disclosure, orally or in writing. Represent nobody
in the transaction: deliver the (b-1) written notice — the IABS.

TREC's explanatory article on the 2026 changes — again, **guidance, not controlling authority** —
works the open-house case through this structure. Where the agent hosting an open house "is a member
of a listing broker's office (e.g., a sales agent at the brokerage holding the listing), the agent
does not need to provide the IABS or enter into a written agreement with prospective buyers," but
"will need to disclose he or she represents the owner either orally or in writing as required by
1101.558(b)." Where the hosting agent "is not a member of the listing broker's office and does not
represent the seller (e.g., a showing agent)," the article states the agent must provide the IABS
and enter into a written agreement as described by § 1101.563(c) with prospective buyers, and that
this is so "even when a prospective buyer has a written representation agreement with another
broker." That last point surprises people; test it against the statute yourself, because the
statutory text is what governs. Section 1101.563(b) conditions the written-agreement duty on
performing "any act of real estate brokerage for a prospective buyer of residential real property,"
not on whether that buyer is represented elsewhere.

**The habit to build.** Nothing in this unit is difficult. It fails in practice for one reason:
license holders treat the IABS as paperwork rather than as the first thing they do. Build the
sequence into muscle memory. Before you talk about a specific property with a party, deliver the
notice by one of the four permitted routes and keep the proof — and remember that 22 TAC § 535.2(h),
taught in Module 09, requires your broker to keep disclosures among the brokerage's records,
available to the Commission, for at least four years. If you cannot produce the IABS you say you
sent, your broker has a records problem as well as a disclosure problem.

### Applied exercise: Four conversations in Corpus Christi

You are a sales agent with Padre Coast Realty. Consider each situation separately and answer from §
1101.558 and 22 TAC § 531.20.

1. You are hosting an open house at a Padre Coast listing on Ocean Drive. A visitor asks about the
   house's flood history and the neighbourhood. Is the (b-1) written notice required? Identify the
   subdivision of § 1101.558(c) you rely on and both of its elements. Separately, what does §
   1101.558(b) require of you here, and in what form or forms may you satisfy it?
2. That same visitor then asks you to help her lease out her own condo on Alameda Street for eight
   months. Does § 1101.558(c)(1) excuse the notice for that conversation? Work through both elements
   of the exception.
3. A prospective buyer emails you asking about a specific listing. You reply and attach the
   completed IABS Notice as a PDF; the body of your email reads only *Hi Marcus — here's the info
   you asked for, call me anytime.* Does that satisfy 22 TAC § 531.20? Quote the words in the rule
   that decide it.
4. Your team lead suggests putting the IABS link into everyone's email signature block so it goes
   out automatically. Quote the sentence in § 531.20 that answers her, and then state what the rule
   requires on the brokerage's homepage instead, including both permitted labels and their minimum
   font sizes.
5. Your brokerage's software prints an IABS with the paragraphs re-flowed into two columns and the

### Module recap

The through-line of this module is a single chain, and if you can draw it from memory you understand
the topic. A client authorises a broker; § 1101.557(a) makes that broker the client's agent — "A
broker who represents a party in a real estate transaction or who lists real estate for sale under
an exclusive agreement for a party is that party's agent." The broker authorises the sales agent in
writing and answers for the agent's acts; 22 TAC § 535.2(a) requires the written scope, requires any
limitation or revocation to be written too, and makes the broker responsible even for acts beyond
the explicit scope if the broker permits them. The sales agent may not act except for that broker; §
1101.351(c) says so, § 535.2(f) says listings are solicited and accepted in the broker's name, and §
1101.651(b) and (c) with 22 TAC § 535.3 keep compensation flowing through the broker — those last
three, and the rest of § 535.2, are Module 09's.

Agency in Texas can arise without a signature. Section 1101.562(a)(1) presupposes it by excluding a
party the broker has agreed to represent "either orally or in writing," and (a)(2) by excluding a
broker "otherwise acting as the party's agent." What follows the status is fiduciary: 22 TAC § 531.2
opens "A license holder, while acting as an agent for another, is a fiduciary"; § 535.156 opens "A
license holder's relationship with the license holder's principal is that of a fiduciary"; and §
531.2 carries the balancing clause you must never quote without — the license holder, in performing
duties to the client, "shall treat other parties to a transaction fairly."

On classification, remember that universal, general and special agency are classical vocabulary that
no held Texas source defines, and that a residential listing or buyer representation agreement is a
special agency because § 1101.557(c)(1) identifies only a power of attorney and a property
management agreement as instruments conferring authority to bind a party. Single agency is one
broker, one side. Dual agency is answered in Texas by the intermediary: § 1101.561(b) makes the
status mandatory where a broker agrees to represent both a buyer or tenant and a seller or landlord,
and everything else about it — consent under § 1101.559, appointments under § 1101.560, the
prohibitions in § 1101.651(d) — is Module 06's. Subagency's two references were repealed from TRELA
effective January 1, 2026 — § 1101.002(8) by Sec. 13(1) and § 1101.805(f) by Sec. 13(2) of Acts
2025, 89th Leg., R.S., Ch. 1172 (S.B. 1968) — and the word appears nowhere in the chapter as held.
The no-representation showing is § 1101.562 and the residential buyer's written agreement is §
1101.563; both are Module 05's, and what you take from them here is the classification.

The number to retain from the duties unit is **two calendar days** under 22 TAC § 535.157 — "A
broker or sales agent must respond to his or her principal, a broker or sales agent representing
another party to a real estate transaction, or an unrepresented party to a real estate transaction
within two calendar days" — running to exactly those three categories and no others, in calendar and
not working days, with no stated form, no completeness requirement and no stated starting point. The
same two days run the other way, from a broker or delegated supervisor to sponsored sales agents,
under 22 TAC § 535.2(j), expressly in addition to § 535.157.

The six duties are a teaching frame, not a statute; no held Texas source enumerates six fiduciary
duties by name. Loyalty lives in § 531.2 ("place no personal interest above that of the client") and
§ 535.156 ("must put the interest of the license holder's principal above the license holder's own
interest"). Obedience lives in 22 TAC § 535.16(a) and stops at unlawful instructions. Disclosure
lives in § 535.156, whose offer-submission duty is switched off only "if the principal has agreed in
writing that offers are not to be submitted after the principal has entered into a contract to buy,
sell, rent, or lease a property," and in § 1101.557(b)(2) and (3), which oblige the broker to inform
the party of material information including the receipt of an offer and, at a minimum, to answer the
party's questions and present any offer to or from the party. Confidentiality's sharpest held
statement is § 1101.651(d)(3), Module 06's. Reasonable care lives in the Commission's canons, 22 TAC
§§ 531.3 and 531.4. The disciplinary consequences of breaching any of them sit in § 1101.652, which
is Module 10's.

Finally, the two disclosures, which are this module's to hold. Section 1101.558(b) is the
representation disclosure: made only if you represent a party, orally or in writing, at first
contact, to another party or to the license holder representing another party, with no exception
list of its own. Section 1101.558(b-1) is the IABS: a written notice in at least 10-point font, at
first substantive communication with a party relating to a proposed transaction regarding specific
real property, containing the four items in (b-1)(1)–(4), with the Commission prescribing the text
under (b-2) and the three exceptions in (c) — the under-one-year residential lease with no sale
being considered, the party you know is represented by another license holder, and the communication
at a property held open that concerns that property. 22 TAC § 531.20 adopts TREC No. IABS 1-2,
requires the labelled homepage link in one of two labels at 10-point or 12-point, permits four
delivery methods, forbids the link in an email footnote or signature block, and permits reproduction
only if the text "is copied verbatim and the spacing, borders and placement of text on the page must
appear to be identical to that in the published version of the IABS Notice, except that the Broker
Contact Information section may be prefilled." Keep the proof.
