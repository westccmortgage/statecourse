# Texas Law of Agency — Module 11

## TREC Topic: Deceptive Trade Practices and Consumer Protection Act

**Prescribed time: 140 minutes.** This module carries the five units the Commission's LOA-1 course
approval form places under *Deceptive Trade Practices and Consumer Protection Act*, whose scope note
is *"Deceptive trade practices."*

**Learning objectives.** After completing this module you will be able to: distinguish common-law
fraud from negligent misrepresentation and from innocent misrepresentation by reference to the
states of mind the Texas statutes actually define, and explain why the Deceptive Trade
Practices-Consumer Protection Act displaces both in practice; identify who qualifies as a "consumer"
under Business & Commerce Code § 17.45(4), apply the § 17.46(b) laundry-list subdivisions that
realistically expose a Texas license holder, and recognise an unconscionable action under
§ 17.45(5); compute a DTPA damages award under § 17.50(b), including economic damages, mental anguish,
the additional-damages multiplier for conduct committed knowingly and for conduct committed
intentionally, and the mandatory award of attorneys' fees under § 17.50(d); assert and evaluate
every defense the subchapter provides, including the § 17.506(a) written-reliance defense, the
§ 17.506(d) tender defense, the § 17.49 exemptions with their exact dollar thresholds, the § 17.505
notice prerequisite, and the § 17.565 limitations period; and connect civil DTPA exposure to the
parallel disciplinary grounds in Occupations Code § 1101.652 and to the Real Estate Recovery Trust
Account created by Subchapter M of Chapter 1101, stating its statutory payment caps exactly.

**Every legal statement in this module comes from a source that was read for this course.** Where a
statement is practice rather than law, the module says so and cites nothing for it.

## Unit: Fraud versus Misrepresentation

A buyer stands in the driveway of a house in Pflugerville and asks you a question you do not know
the answer to. "Has this ever flooded?" "The seller said the roof is four years old — is that
right?" What you say next is the entire case, and its legal character — whether it is a lie, a
guess, a careless repetition, or an honest mistake — determines which body of law the buyer's lawyer
will reach for and how much it will cost you.

Most students arrive at this material with three words jumbled together: fraud, misrepresentation,
and deception. They are not synonyms. They describe three different levels of culpability, and Texas
law treats them very differently in terms of who must prove what, what damages are available, and
how long the claimant has to sue.

**Start with what this course holds and what it does not.** The elements of common-law fraud in
Texas are stated in judicial opinions, not in a statute. No case reporter and no judicial opinion
were held for this course, and so **the common-law elements of fraud were not held and are not
stated here as law.** The same is true of the common-law elements of negligent misrepresentation and
of innocent misrepresentation: those formulations were not held. That is not a gap you should paper
over with something you half-remember from a study guide. It is the honest position, and it turns
out to matter very little in practice, for a reason you are about to see: the Texas Legislature has
written the distinction between these states of mind directly into the statutes that will actually
be used against you, and those statutes *were* held. Learn the statutory version. It is the one that
governs your license and your wallet.

**The statutory ladder of culpability.** Texas statutes describe at least four rungs of mental state
relevant to a false statement in a real estate transaction, and each rung carries a different
consequence.

The lowest rung is *no mental state at all* — a statement that is simply false, made by someone who
believed it was true and had no particular reason to doubt it. Under the Deceptive Trade
Practices-Consumer Protection Act, Business & Commerce Code § 17.46(b) declares a long list of acts
to be "false, misleading, or deceptive acts or practices," and most of the entries on that list
contain no scienter requirement whatever. Section 17.46(b)(5), to take the one you are most likely
to trip over, makes it a deceptive act to be "representing that goods or services have sponsorship,
approval, characteristics, ingredients, uses, benefits, or quantities which they do not have or that
a person has a sponsorship, approval, status, affiliation, or connection which the person does not."
Read that subdivision again and notice what is missing. It does not say "knowingly representing." It
does not say "falsely representing with intent to deceive." It says *representing*. If the
characteristics are not there, the representation violates the subdivision, and your sincerity is
irrelevant to liability. That is the single most important sentence in this module.

The next rung up is *negligence* — a statement made without the care a reasonable license holder
would use. Texas has not, in any source held for this course, defined negligent misrepresentation by
statute. Carelessness is nonetheless a disciplinary offence in its own right: Occupations Code
§ 1101.652(b)(1) reaches a license holder who "acts negligently or incompetently," a ground Module 10
teaches. You need not have lied; you need only have been sloppy.

The third rung is *actual awareness*. The DTPA defines it at § 17.45(9): "Knowingly" means "actual
awareness, at the time of the act or practice complained of, of the falsity, deception, or
unfairness of the act or practice giving rise to the consumer's claim or, in an action brought under
Subdivision (2) of Subsection (a) of Section 17.50, actual awareness of the act, practice,
condition, defect, or failure constituting the breach of warranty, but actual awareness may be
inferred where objective manifestations indicate that a person acted with actual awareness." Two
things deserve your attention in that definition. The first is the time reference: awareness "at the
time of the act or practice complained of," not awareness acquired later. The second is the last
clause, which is the one defendants always wish were not there — "actual awareness may be inferred
where objective manifestations indicate that a person acted with actual awareness." You cannot
defeat a knowing finding simply by taking the stand and saying you did not know. A jury may infer
your awareness from what you did, what you had in your file, what you wrote in an e-mail, and what
any competent license holder in your position would have understood.

The top rung is *intent*. Section 17.45(13) defines it, and this definition must be read whole,
because the last clause reverses the meaning of everything before it if you drop it. "Intentionally"
means "actual awareness of the falsity, deception, or unfairness of the act or practice, or the
condition, defect, or failure constituting a breach of warranty giving rise to the consumer's claim,
coupled with the specific intent that the consumer act in detrimental reliance on the falsity or
deception or in detrimental ignorance of the unfairness. Intention may be inferred from objective
manifestations that indicate that the person acted intentionally or from facts showing that a
defendant acted with flagrant disregard of prudent and fair business practices to the extent that
the defendant should be treated as having acted intentionally."

Sit with that last sentence. It says two separate things. First, intention may be inferred from
objective manifestations, the same evidentiary move the statute allows for knowledge. Second — and
this is the clause that catches license holders who thought "intentionally" meant a conscious plan
to cheat — intention may also be inferred "from facts showing that a defendant acted with flagrant
disregard of prudent and fair business practices to the extent that the defendant should be treated
as having acted intentionally." Flagrant disregard of prudent and fair business practices, if
flagrant enough, is treated as intent. A license holder who never formed a plan to deceive anyone,
but who behaved so far outside ordinary professional care that a jury describes the conduct as
flagrant, may be found to have acted intentionally. The words "to the extent that the defendant
should be treated as having acted intentionally" are not a limitation that saves you; they are the
bridge that carries recklessness across into intent. Quote the definition without that final clause
and you will teach yourself the opposite of the law.

**Where the Occupations Code draws its own lines.** TRELA grades fault along the same ladder, and
Module 10 teaches those grounds in full. Name them and move on: § 1101.652(b)(3) reaches a material
misrepresentation to a potential buyer concerning a significant defect known to the license holder;
(b)(4) reaches a failure to disclose such a defect; (b)(5) reaches a false promise made when the
license holder "is unable or does not intend to keep the promise"; (b)(6) reaches a continued and
flagrant course of misrepresentation; and § 1101.652(a-1)(1) reaches misrepresentation, dishonesty,
or fraud when the license holder is buying or selling in the license holder's own name or a close
relative's.

What this module does teach in full is the Commission rule that gives § 1101.652(b)(5) its reach.
Rule 22 TAC § 535.145 provides that for purposes of that subdivision the term false promise
"includes both oral and written promises." The rule continues: "The fact that a written agreement
between the parties to a real estate transaction does not recite a promise made by a real estate
license holder to one of the parties or that a person did not detrimentally rely on the false
promise will not prevent the Commission from determining that a false promise was made." So the
Commission does not need reliance. And the rule closes the last escape hatch: "In determining
whether this section has been violated, neither a written contractual provision disclaiming oral
representations nor the Texas Rules of Evidence Rule 1004, the parol evidence rule, shall prevent
the Commission from considering oral promises made by a license holder." What you said in the car on
the way to the property is evidence.

**Who bears a misrepresentation, § 1101.805.** Occupations Code § 1101.805 allocates responsibility
for a misrepresentation or concealment between a party and a license holder; Module 03 teaches it.
The half that operates here is subsection (e): a license holder "is not liable for a
misrepresentation or a concealment of a material fact made by a party to a real estate transaction
unless the license holder: (1) knew of the falsity of the misrepresentation or concealment; and (2)
failed to disclose the license holder's knowledge of the falsity of the misrepresentation or
concealment." Both conditions must be met before you are exposed, and the second is in your hands:
the moment you learn the seller's answer is wrong, disclose it and you are outside the exception.
Subsection (b) provides that the section "prevails over any other law, including common law."

**Why the DTPA swallows all of this in practice.** In the abstract, fraud, negligent
misrepresentation and innocent misrepresentation are three different lawsuits with three different
burdens. In a Texas residential transaction they usually collapse into one, and it is the DTPA.

First, the DTPA does not require the plaintiff to prove your state of mind to establish liability.
As you saw, the laundry-list subdivisions are mostly written without scienter, and § 17.50(a)(1)
makes a consumer's action available where the defendant used "a false, misleading, or deceptive act
or practice" that is "specifically enumerated in a subdivision of Subsection (b) of Section 17.46 of
this subchapter" and "relied on by a consumer to the consumer's detriment," provided that act was "a
producing cause of economic damages or damages for mental anguish." Producing cause, not proximate
cause; enumerated act, not proven lie. Your innocence goes to the size of the award, not to whether
you lost.

Second, the DTPA pays the plaintiff's lawyer. Section 17.50(d) is a single unconditional sentence:
"Each consumer who prevails shall be awarded court costs and reasonable and necessary attorneys'
fees." Not *may*. A common-law fraud claim carries no such guarantee in any source held for this
course. This single sentence is why a $9,000 repair dispute becomes a filed lawsuit: the fee award
makes small cases economically rational to bring.

Third, the DTPA multiplies: § 17.50(b)(1) permits mental anguish damages and additional damages when
conduct is knowing or intentional, which the Damages unit works through. Fourth, the DTPA cannot be
waived except in the narrow circumstances of § 17.42. And fifth, § 17.44(a) directs that the
subchapter "shall be liberally construed and applied to promote its underlying purposes" — set out
in full in the next unit.

**One place where the older causes of action still matter: time.** Civil Practice & Remedies Code
§ 16.004(a) provides that "[a] person must bring suit on the following actions not later than four
years after the day the cause of action accrues," and subdivision (4) of that list is "fraud."
Subdivision (5) is "breach of fiduciary duty." The DTPA, by contrast, carries its own two-year
period at § 17.565, which you will meet in the Defenses unit. So a claimant who sat on a claim for
three years has lost the DTPA route and may still have a fraud route. Chapter 16 does not name
negligent misrepresentation anywhere in the sections read for this course, and **which limitations
period governs a negligent misrepresentation claim was not held.** Do not guess at it, and do not
advise a client about it; that is a question for their lawyer.

### Statute reading exercise: The two states of mind in § 17.45

Open Business & Commerce Code § 17.45 and read subdivisions (9) and (13) side by side, in full,
without skipping the final sentence of either.

1. Both definitions permit a fact-finder to infer the defendant's state of mind. Write out the exact
   phrase each subdivision uses to authorise that inference, and explain what evidence a plaintiff's
   lawyer would gather to satisfy each one in a case about a license holder who advertised a house
   as having a "new roof."
2. Subdivision (13) permits intention to be inferred from a second, additional source that
   subdivision (9) does not mention. Quote that clause in full, including the words that follow
   "flagrant disregard of prudent and fair business practices." Explain, in your own words, what it
   means for a defendant to be "treated as having acted intentionally" when the defendant formed no
   plan to deceive anybody.
3. Subdivision (9) contains a time limitation on the awareness it requires. Identify it, and explain
   what it means for a license holder who genuinely did not know a fact at the time of a showing but
   learned it the following week.
4. Neither subdivision says anything about whether the consumer must prove the defendant's motive or
   profit. Confirm this by reading the text, and say why that silence matters to a defendant who
   argues "I gained nothing from the statement."
5. Read § 17.46(b)(5) and § 17.46(b)(13). One of them contains a scienter word and the other does
   not. Identify which is which and explain what a plaintiff must prove under each.

## Unit: Deceptive Trade Practices and Consumer Protection

Business & Commerce Code § 17.41 states the short title: "This subchapter may be cited as the
Deceptive Trade Practices-Consumer Protection Act." Everyone in Texas real estate calls it the DTPA.
It occupies Subchapter E of Chapter 17, running from § 17.41 through § 17.63.

**The interpretive instruction comes first, because it colours everything else.** Section 17.44(a)
directs that the subchapter "shall be liberally construed and applied to promote its underlying
purposes, which are to protect consumers against false, misleading, and deceptive business
practices, unconscionable actions, and breaches of warranty and to provide efficient and economical
procedures to secure such protection." When a court has to choose between two readings of a DTPA
provision, the statute itself tells the court which way to lean. Subsection (b) adds a single
cross-reference: "Chapter 27, Property Code, prevails over this subchapter to the extent of any
conflict." Chapter 27 of the Property Code is the residential construction liability chapter.
**Property Code Chapter 27 was not held for this course**, and so this module does not tell you what
it says or where a conflict would arise; it tells you only that § 17.44(b) gives Chapter 27
priority, so a claim involving construction defects in a residence is a matter on which a lawyer
must be consulted rather than a matter you should reason about from the DTPA alone.

**Who is a "consumer," and why you can be sued by someone who never paid you a dime.** Standing
under the DTPA runs through one definition. Section 17.45(4): "Consumer" means "an individual,
partnership, corporation, this state, or a subdivision or agency of this state who seeks or acquires
by purchase or lease, any goods or services, except that the term does not include a business
consumer that has assets of $25 million or more, or that is owned or controlled by a corporation or
entity with assets of $25 million or more."

Take that definition apart clause by clause, because each clause does work.

*"Seeks or acquires."* The person does not have to complete a purchase. A buyer who "seeks" a house
and walks away from the deal because of what you said is still a consumer. The word "seeks" is why a
failed transaction is not a safe harbour.

*"By purchase or lease."* Both are covered. Your leasing and property-management practice sits
squarely inside the Act, not outside it.

*"Any goods or services."* Section 17.45(1) defines "Goods" as "tangible chattels or real property
purchased or leased for use." Real property is expressly a good under the DTPA. Section 17.45(2)
defines "Services" as "work, labor, or service purchased or leased for use, including services
furnished in connection with the sale or repair of goods." Brokerage is a service. So both the house
and your work on it are inside the Act's subject matter.

*The $25 million exclusion.* A "business consumer," defined at § 17.45(10) as "an individual,
partnership, or corporation who seeks or acquires by purchase or lease, any goods or services for
commercial or business use," loses consumer status once its assets reach $25 million, or once it is
"owned or controlled by a corporation or entity with assets of $25 million or more." That number is
$25 million exactly. It is an asset test, not a revenue test, and it is the only size-based limit
inside the definition of consumer itself.

Now the point students most often miss. **Read the definition again and find the word "privity." It
is not there.** Section 17.45(4) asks who sought or acquired goods or services. It does not ask from
whom. It does not require that the consumer bought anything from the defendant, or paid the
defendant, or had a contract with the defendant. This is why a buyer who was represented by a
completely different brokerage, who never signed anything with you, who never met you until the
option period, can sue *you*, the listing agent, under the DTPA. The buyer sought to acquire real
property; you engaged in a practice enumerated in § 17.46(b) that was a producing cause of the
buyer's economic damages; the statute asks nothing further about the relationship between you.

The subchapter confirms this reading in its own text. Section 17.506(c) — which you will study in
detail under Defenses — provides that where a defendant asserts reliance on written information from
another source, "suit may be asserted against the third party supplying the written information
without regard to privity where the third party knew or should have reasonably foreseen that the
information would be provided to a consumer." The Legislature knew how to say "without regard to
privity" and said it.

**The four doors into a DTPA action.** Section 17.50(a) provides that "[a] consumer may maintain an
action where any of the following constitute a producing cause of economic damages or damages for
mental anguish," and then lists four. Subdivision (1) is the laundry-list door: "the use or
employment by any person of a false, misleading, or deceptive act or practice that is: (A)
specifically enumerated in a subdivision of Subsection (b) of Section 17.46 of this subchapter; and
(B) relied on by a consumer to the consumer's detriment." Subdivision (2) is breach of an express or
implied warranty. Subdivision (3) is "any unconscionable action or course of action by any person."
Subdivision (4) is a violation of Chapter 541 of the Insurance Code, which is not your subject.

Notice three features of subdivision (1). It requires an act "specifically enumerated" in a
subdivision of § 17.46(b) — a general sense that you behaved badly is not enough. It requires
detrimental reliance by the consumer. And it applies to the act of "any person," a word § 17.45(3)
defines as "an individual, partnership, corporation, association, or other group, however
organized." Section 17.46(d) locks the exclusivity down from the other direction: for the purposes
of the relief authorized in Subdivision (1) of Subsection (a) of Section 17.50, the term false,
misleading, or deceptive acts or practices "is limited to the acts enumerated in specific
subdivisions of Subsection (b) of this section." So for a private consumer suit, the laundry list is
a closed list. The open-ended prohibition in § 17.46(a) — "False, misleading, or deceptive acts or
practices in the conduct of any trade or commerce are hereby declared unlawful" — is, by its own
terms, "subject to action by the consumer protection division under Sections 17.47, 17.58, 17.60,
and 17.61 of this code." That is the Attorney General's consumer protection division, not a private
plaintiff.

This is genuinely good news for you: to know your civil exposure under subdivision (1), you need to
know a finite list.

**The laundry list, applied to real estate.** Section 17.46(b) opens by providing that, "Except as
provided in Subsection (d) of this section," the term false, misleading, or deceptive acts or
practices "includes, but is not limited to, the following acts". Thirty-four subdivisions follow.
Many of them — odometer tampering, kosher labelling, pyramid schemes, synthetic cannabinoids — will
never touch your practice. The ones below will.

*§ 17.46(b)(2) — "causing confusion or misunderstanding as to the source, sponsorship, approval, or
certification of goods or services."* A sales agent's website branded "Frisco Premier Realty Group"
with no reference to her sponsoring broker, carrying a badge reading "Certified Relocation
Specialist" that corresponds to no certification she holds, is confusion as to source and as to
certification — and the subdivision requires no proof that she meant to confuse anyone.

*§ 17.46(b)(3) — "causing confusion or misunderstanding as to affiliation, connection, or
association with, or certification by, another."* The same facts reach subdivision (3) when the
confusion concerns affiliation — marketing materials that imply the agent is affiliated with a
national franchise she has no relationship with, or with the builder whose inventory she is
reselling.

*§ 17.46(b)(5) — "representing that goods or services have sponsorship, approval, characteristics,
ingredients, uses, benefits, or quantities which they do not have or that a person has a
sponsorship, approval, status, affiliation, or connection which the person does not."* This is the
workhorse subdivision in Texas real estate litigation and the one to memorise. "Characteristics" and
"quantities" are the operative words. A listing in Katy states "2,860 sq. ft." when the appraisal
district record and the actual measurement say 2,455; a listing in Bastrop County says "not in a
flood zone" when the tract sits partly in a mapped floodplain. It does not matter that you copied
the square footage from the seller's old appraisal. The representation is either accurate or it is
not.

*§ 17.46(b)(7) — "representing that goods or services are of a particular standard, quality, or
grade, or that goods are of a particular style or model, if they are of another."* The Amarillo
listing that advertises "new HVAC system" when the compressor was replaced but the air handler and
ductwork are twenty-two years old. Standard, quality, and grade claims are checkable.

*§ 17.46(b)(9) — "advertising goods or services with intent not to sell them as advertised."* This
one does contain a scienter element — "with intent not to sell them as advertised" — and it is the
bait listing: a property advertised below market that the brokerage knows is already under contract,
in order to harvest buyer leads.

*§ 17.46(b)(11) — "making false or misleading statements of fact concerning the reasons for,
existence of, or amount of price reductions."* "Reduced $40,000 — motivated seller" when the list
price was never $40,000 higher is inside this subdivision.

*§ 17.46(b)(12) — "representing that an agreement confers or involves rights, remedies, or
obligations which it does not have or involve, or which are prohibited by law."* You are handing
consumers contracts constantly. Tell a buyer that the option period "lets you get out any time
before closing," or tell a seller that a listing agreement "can be cancelled by either side on a
week's notice" when it cannot, and you have represented that an agreement confers rights it does not
involve. This is where careless explanations of contract forms become a DTPA claim.

*§ 17.46(b)(13) — "knowingly making false or misleading statements of fact concerning the need for
parts, replacement, or repair service."* Note the "knowingly." A license holder who steers a seller
into an unnecessary $14,000 foundation job with a preferred contractor, knowing the statements about
the need for the work are false, is inside this subdivision.

*§ 17.46(b)(14) — "misrepresenting the authority of a salesman, representative or agent to negotiate
the final terms of a consumer transaction."* Telling a buyer "I can accept that counter for the
seller right now" when you have no such authority is a subdivision (14) violation. This is a
distinctively agency-law deceptive practice and it belongs in a Law of Agency course.

*§ 17.46(b)(20) — "representing that a guaranty or warranty confers or involves rights or remedies
which it does not have or involve, provided, however, that nothing in this subchapter shall be
construed to expand the implied warranty of merchantability as defined in Sections 2.314 through
2.318 and Sections 2A.212 through 2A.216 to involve obligations in excess of those which are
appropriate to the goods."* Residential service contracts are sold in a very large share of Texas
transactions. "The warranty covers the whole HVAC system, so you don't need to worry about the age"
is a representation about what a warranty confers; if the contract excludes pre-existing conditions
or caps the payout, subdivision (20) is engaged.

*§ 17.46(b)(24) — "failing to disclose information concerning goods or services which was known at
the time of the transaction if such failure to disclose such information was intended to induce the
consumer into a transaction into which the consumer would not have entered had the information been
disclosed."* This is the silence subdivision, and it is the one to think about hardest, for three
reasons. First, it is the only laundry-list entry that makes *not speaking* actionable, and real
estate is a business of things left unsaid. Second, it has two express conditions and both must be
met: the information must have been "known at the time of the transaction," and the failure to
disclose must have been "intended to induce the consumer into a transaction into which the consumer
would not have entered had the information been disclosed." A genuinely forgotten fact is not within
it; a fact you did not know is not within it. Third — and this is why it dominates the analysis —
subdivision (24) is carved out of *both* statutory exemptions that would otherwise protect you.

Consider a Texas fact pattern. You list a house in Friendswood. In the file, from a prior listing
you handled on the same house two years earlier, sits an inspection report noting active seepage at
the north foundation wall. The seller's disclosure notice this time says no known water penetration;
that notice is the seller's own statement of the seller's knowledge and belief, so the seller's
answer may be perfectly proper as to the seller. That does not answer the question about you. You
knew, at the time of the transaction, of information concerning the property. If a fact-finder
concludes you kept quiet in order to get the deal closed, subdivision (24) is satisfied. Add
§ 1101.652(b)(4), which independently makes that silence a disciplinary ground, and one omission has
produced both civil and administrative liability.

**Unconscionable action or course of action.** The second major door, § 17.50(a)(3), does not depend
on the laundry list at all. It reaches "any unconscionable action or course of action by any
person." Section 17.45(5) defines an unconscionable action or course of action as "an act or
practice which, to a consumer's detriment, takes advantage of the lack of knowledge, ability,
experience, or capacity of the consumer to a grossly unfair degree."

Every word of that definition is doing work. The conduct must be "to a consumer's detriment" — there
must be harm. It must be a practice that "takes advantage of" one of four specific consumer
vulnerabilities: lack of knowledge, ability, experience, or capacity. And it must do so "to a
grossly unfair degree," which is a deliberately high bar; ordinary sharp dealing is not enough. But
notice what the definition does *not* require: it does not require a false statement, it does not
require any state of mind, and it does not require reliance. A course of conduct — the phrase
"course of action" is right there — can be unconscionable even where no individual statement in it
was false.

The Texas fact patterns that fit are the ones involving an information gap the license holder
exploits. An elderly widow in Del Rio, recently bereaved, is persuaded to list her homestead for a
price the license holder knows is well under market so that the license holder's investor client can
buy it, with no comparative market analysis provided and no explanation of what a listing price
means. The plaintiff's theory is not that a specific sentence was false; it is that the license
holder took advantage of the consumer's lack of knowledge and experience to a grossly unfair degree.

**Waivers are void, with three narrow exceptions.** New license holders sometimes ask whether a
clause in the contract can waive DTPA claims. Section 17.42(a) answers: "Any waiver by a consumer of
the provisions of this subchapter is contrary to public policy and is unenforceable and void;
provided, however, that a waiver is valid and enforceable if: (1) the waiver is in writing and is
signed by the consumer; (2) the consumer is not in a significantly disparate bargaining position;
and (3) the consumer is represented by legal counsel in seeking or acquiring the goods or services."
All three conditions must be met. Subsection (b) adds that a waiver "is not effective if the
consumer's legal counsel was directly or indirectly identified, suggested, or selected by a
defendant or an agent of the defendant" — so you may not hand your consumer the name of a lawyer and
then rely on the waiver that lawyer supervises. A typical residential transaction, where the buyer
has no lawyer, cannot produce a valid waiver.

**The DTPA does not replace other law; it stacks on it.** Section 17.43 provides that "[t]he
provisions of this subchapter are not exclusive" and that its remedies "are in addition to any other
procedures or remedies provided for in any other law," with one important limit: "no recovery shall
be permitted under both this subchapter and another law of both damages and penalties for the same
act or practice." The section then draws a line students routinely blur: "A violation of a provision
of law other than this subchapter is not in and of itself a violation of this subchapter." Breaking
a TREC rule is not automatically a DTPA violation. But an act that violates another law "may be made
the basis of an action under this subchapter if the act or practice is proscribed by a provision of
this subchapter or is declared by such other law to be actionable under this subchapter." So the
same conduct can violate TRELA and the DTPA simultaneously, and usually does — but through two
independent routes.

**The notice letter, and what you do the day it arrives.** Section 17.505(a) sets a condition
precedent: "As a prerequisite to filing a suit seeking damages under Subdivision (1) of Subsection
(b) of Section 17.50 of this subchapter against any person, a consumer shall give written notice to
the person at least 60 days before filing the suit advising the person in reasonable detail of the
consumer's specific complaint and the amount of economic damages, damages for mental anguish, and
expenses, including attorneys' fees, if any, reasonably incurred by the consumer in asserting the
claim against the defendant. During the 60-day period a written request to inspect, in a reasonable
manner and at a reasonable time and place, the goods that are the subject of the consumer's action
or claim may be presented to the consumer."

Sixty days. Written. In reasonable detail. Itemised as to economic damages, mental anguish, and
expenses including attorneys' fees. That letter is your early-warning system, and it is also the
starting gun on the two clocks that matter most, both of which run from receipt: the sixty-day
period in § 17.505(a), and the thirty-day tender window in § 17.506(d) that you will study under
Defenses.

Here is what a competent license holder does on the day the letter lands, and none of this is law —
it is practice, and this course states no authority for it. Note on the letter the date it was
received. Do not telephone the consumer, do not answer the allegations in any form, and do not
apologise, because everything you say now is evidence. Notify your sponsoring broker the same day
and notify your errors and omissions carrier, because late notice can forfeit coverage. Preserve the
entire file — listing agreement, every version of the disclosure notice, the MLS edit history, every
text and e-mail, showing feedback, inspection reports and the CMA — and do not "tidy up" anything.
Then get the letter to counsel inside the first few days, because the two statutory windows are
short and one of them, the § 17.506(d) tender, may be the cheapest exit you will ever be offered.

There are two situations in which the consumer need not give the sixty-day notice at all. Section
17.505(b): "If the giving of 60 days' written notice is rendered impracticable by reason of the
necessity of filing suit in order to prevent the expiration of the statute of limitations or if the
consumer's claim is asserted by way of counterclaim, the notice provided for in Subsection (a) of
this section is not required, but the tender provided for by Subsection (d), Section 17.506 of this
subchapter may be made within 60 days after service of the suit or counterclaim." So a consumer
racing the limitations period, or a consumer who is a defendant asserting a counterclaim, may skip
the letter — and in exchange you get sixty days from service to make the tender.

## Unit: Damages

A great deal of what makes the DTPA frightening is arithmetic. Work through the numbers slowly,
because the difference between an ordinary finding and a "knowingly" finding, and between
"knowingly" and "intentionally," is the difference between a claim your carrier absorbs and a claim
that ends a career.

**Start with the base: economic damages.** Section 17.45(11) defines economic damages as
"compensatory damages for pecuniary loss, including costs of repair and replacement. The term does
not include exemplary damages or damages for physical pain and mental anguish, loss of consortium,
disfigurement, physical impairment, or loss of companionship and society." Two halves. The inclusive
half is broad. The exclusive half is a list of things that are *not* economic damages, and the item
you must notice is mental anguish. Mental anguish is not economic damages. It is a separate
category, and § 17.50(b)(1) makes it available only in a specific circumstance.

**Section 17.50(b)(1) is the damages engine.** It provides that in a suit filed under the section,
"each consumer who prevails may obtain: (1) the amount of economic damages found by the trier of
fact. If the trier of fact finds that the conduct of the defendant was committed knowingly, the
consumer may also recover damages for mental anguish, as found by the trier of fact, and the trier
of fact may award not more than three times the amount of economic damages; or if the trier of fact
finds the conduct was committed intentionally, the consumer may recover damages for mental anguish,
as found by the trier of fact, and the trier of fact may award not more than three times the amount
of damages for mental anguish and economic damages".

Break that one sentence into its three tiers.

*Tier one — an ordinary violation.* The consumer recovers "the amount of economic damages found by
the trier of fact." No mental anguish. No multiplier. Economic damages, full stop — plus the costs
and fees that § 17.50(d) makes mandatory.

*Tier two — conduct committed knowingly*, as § 17.45(9) defines "knowingly." Two things unlock.
First, "the consumer may also recover damages for mental anguish, as found by the trier of fact."
Second, "the trier of fact may award not more than three times the amount of economic damages." Read
the multiplier carefully: at this tier the base of the multiplier is *economic damages only*. Mental
anguish is recoverable but is not part of the base that gets multiplied. And the words are "may
award not more than" — this is a ceiling on the fact-finder's discretion, not an automatic tripling.

*Tier three — conduct committed intentionally*, as § 17.45(13) defines "intentionally." Mental
anguish is again recoverable, and now the multiplier's base expands: "the trier of fact may award
not more than three times the amount of damages for mental anguish and economic damages." Mental
anguish joins the base. That is the entire difference between tier two and tier three, and in a case
with substantial mental anguish findings it is an enormous difference.

**Worked example one: the Round Rock square footage claim.** Return to the facts from the last unit.
The buyers paid $505,000 for a house advertised at 2,900 square feet that measures 2,640. Assume the
trier of fact finds economic damages of $22,000, representing the difference in value the buyers
proved. Assume the buyers' reasonable and necessary attorneys' fees are found to be $48,000 and
court costs $3,200.

At tier one, with no culpability finding, the judgment is economic damages of $22,000, plus $48,000
in fees and $3,200 in costs, for a total of **$73,200**. The fee award is nearly twice the damages.
That is not an anomaly; it is the ordinary shape of a DTPA judgment in a modest case, and it is why
these cases get filed.

Now assume the jury also finds that you acted knowingly — you had the appraisal district record in
your file and entered 2,900 anyway. The jury finds mental anguish of $15,000. Tier two allows
economic damages of $22,000, mental anguish of $15,000, and additional damages of "not more than
three times the amount of economic damages." Three times $22,000 is $66,000. If the jury awards the
maximum, the damages total $22,000 + $15,000 + $66,000 = **$103,000**, and fees and costs of $51,200
are added on top, for **$154,200**.

Now assume instead a finding that the conduct was committed intentionally. Tier three allows
additional damages of "not more than three times the amount of damages for mental anguish and
economic damages." The base is now $22,000 + $15,000 = $37,000, and three times that is $111,000.
Maximum damages become $22,000 + $15,000 + $111,000 = **$148,000**, plus $51,200 in fees and costs,
for **$199,200**. The same false number in the same MLS field produced a $73,200 exposure, a
$154,200 exposure, or a $199,200 exposure, depending entirely on what a jury concluded about your
state of mind — and remember from Unit 1 that both § 17.45(9) and § 17.45(13) permit the jury to
infer that state of mind from objective manifestations.

**Do not include fees in the multiplier base.** Section 17.50(e) is explicit: "In computing
additional damages under Subsection (b), attorneys' fees, costs, and prejudgment interest may not be
considered." In the tier three calculation above, that is why the base was $37,000 and not $37,000
plus $51,200. A student who trebles the whole judgment has misread the statute.

**Prejudgment interest.** Section 17.50(f) provides that a court "may not award prejudgment interest
applicable to: (1) damages for future loss under this subchapter; or (2) additional damages under
Subsection (b)." Interest does not run on the multiplier.

**Attorneys' fees, in both directions.** Section 17.50(d) is unconditional for the consumer: "Each
consumer who prevails shall be awarded court costs and reasonable and necessary attorneys' fees."
But § 17.50(c) points the other way: "On a finding by the court that an action under this section
was groundless in fact or law or brought in bad faith, or brought for the purpose of harassment, the
court shall award to the defendant reasonable and necessary attorneys' fees and court costs." That
is also a "shall," and it requires a finding by the court — not the jury.

**The other relief the statute authorises.** Section 17.50(b)(2) permits "an order enjoining such
acts or failure to act." Section 17.50(b)(3) permits "orders necessary to restore to any party to
the suit any money or property, real or personal, which may have been acquired in violation of this
subchapter" — this is the provision under which a commission you were paid can be ordered returned.
Section 17.50(b)(4) permits "any other relief which the court deems proper, including the
appointment of a receiver or the revocation of a license or certificate authorizing a person to
engage in business in this state if the judgment has not been satisfied within three months of the
date of the final judgment." Read the limitation that immediately follows, because it protects you
specifically: "The court may not revoke or suspend a license to do business in this state or appoint
a receiver to take over the affairs of a person who has failed to satisfy a judgment if the person
is a licensee of or regulated by a state agency which has statutory authority to revoke or suspend a
license or to appoint a receiver or trustee." You are regulated by TREC, which holds revocation
authority under Occupations Code § 1101.652, so the civil court will not revoke your real estate
license under § 17.50(b)(4). TREC may still do so under its own statute.

**Chapter 41 does not apply.** Section 17.50(g) states flatly: "Chapter 41, Civil Practice and
Remedies Code, does not apply to a cause of action brought under this subchapter." The general Texas
exemplary damages caps are not available to a DTPA defendant. The only ceiling on additional damages
is the one inside § 17.50(b)(1) itself.

**A limit on mental anguish that students miss.** Section 17.49(e) provides: "Except as specifically
provided by Subsections (b) and (h), Section 17.50, nothing in this subchapter shall apply to a
cause of action for bodily injury or death or for the infliction of mental anguish." So the DTPA is
not a vehicle for a stand-alone emotional distress claim. Mental anguish enters a DTPA case only
through the door § 17.50(b) opens — a finding that the conduct was committed knowingly or
intentionally — and not as an independent cause of action.

## Unit: Defenses

A statute that liberally construes itself in the consumer's favour, dispenses with privity, and pays
the consumer's lawyer nonetheless contains a real set of defenses. They are taken in the order a
case actually unfolds: exemption, procedural prerequisite, limitations, and then the substantive
defenses to damages.

**The exemptions in § 17.49, beginning with the one written for you.** Section 17.49(i) provides:
"Nothing in this subchapter shall apply to a claim against a person licensed as a broker or
salesperson under Chapter 1101, Occupations Code, arising from an act or omission by the person
while acting as a broker or salesperson." Then, immediately: "This exemption does not apply to: (1)
an express misrepresentation of a material fact that cannot be characterized as advice, judgment, or
opinion; (2) a failure to disclose information in violation of Section 17.46(b)(24); or (3) an
unconscionable action or course of action that cannot be characterized as advice, judgment, or
opinion."

Read the exemption and its three carve-outs together and you will see the shape of your real
exposure. The general rule genuinely does exempt you. Note the statute's own wording: it says
"broker or salesperson," using the older term that TRELA itself has replaced with "sales agent," and
it ties the exemption to the act being done "while acting as" one. An act outside your brokerage
capacity — flipping a house in your own name, running an unlicensed side business — is not within
the exemption at all.

But now the carve-outs, because they are where every real case lives.

*Carve-out (1): "an express misrepresentation of a material fact that cannot be characterized as
advice, judgment, or opinion."* Four conditions in a single clause. It must be *express*, so a claim
built on implication rather than a statement is outside the carve-out. It must be a
*misrepresentation*, so a true statement is outside. It must concern a *material fact*. And it must
be a statement that *cannot be characterized as advice, judgment, or opinion*. That last condition
is the reason experienced Texas license holders speak the way they do. "In my opinion this
neighbourhood has held its value well" is opinion; "This house is 2,900 square feet" is a fact. The
exemption survives the first and dies on the second. A court reads the substance, and dressing a
hard factual claim in the words "I think" will not convert it into an opinion.

*Carve-out (2): "a failure to disclose information in violation of Section 17.46(b)(24)."* Silence.
This is why subdivision (24) was singled out for close reading in Unit 2. Every other laundry-list
subdivision must fight its way through carve-out (1) or (3); subdivision (24) is named by number and
walks straight through the exemption. If a Texas consumer's lawyer can plead a knowing failure to
disclose that was intended to induce the transaction, the broker exemption is simply gone.

*Carve-out (3): "an unconscionable action or course of action that cannot be characterized as
advice, judgment, or opinion."* This is why § 17.45(5) matters so much to license holders.
Unconscionability requires no false statement at all, and it survives the exemption whenever the
course of conduct cannot be recharacterised as advice or opinion.

**The professional services exemption, § 17.49(c).** Alongside the broker-specific exemption sits a
broader one: "Nothing in this subchapter shall apply to a claim for damages based on the rendering
of a professional service, the essence of which is the providing of advice, judgment, opinion, or
similar professional skill. This exemption does not apply to: (1) an express misrepresentation of a
material fact that cannot be characterized as advice, judgment, or opinion; (2) a failure to
disclose information in violation of Section 17.46(b)(24); (3) an unconscionable action or course of
action that cannot be characterized as advice, judgment, or opinion; (4) breach of an express
warranty that cannot be characterized as advice, judgment, or opinion; or (5) a violation of Section
17.46(b)(26)." The first three carve-outs match § 17.49(i) word for word; § 17.49(c) adds two more.
Section 17.49(d) extends its reach: "Subsection (c) applies to a cause of action brought against the
person who provided the professional service and a cause of action brought against any entity that
could be found to be vicariously liable for the person's conduct." That is how a sponsoring broker
sued for a sales agent's conduct comes within the same analysis.

**The two transaction-size exemptions, with their exact thresholds.** Section 17.49(f): "Nothing in
the subchapter shall apply to a claim arising out of a written contract if: (1) the contract relates
to a transaction, a project, or a set of transactions related to the same project involving total
consideration by the consumer of more than $100,000; (2) in negotiating the contract the consumer is
represented by legal counsel who is not directly or indirectly identified, suggested, or selected by
the defendant or an agent of the defendant; and (3) the contract does not involve the consumer's
residence."

All three conditions, joined by "and." More than $100,000 — not $100,000 or more. Counsel who was
not identified, suggested, or selected by the defendant, directly or indirectly. And a contract that
"does not involve the consumer's residence." Section 17.45(12) defines "Residence" as a building
"(A) that is a single-family house, duplex, triplex, or quadruplex or a unit in a multiunit
residential structure in which title to the individual units is transferred to the owners under a
condominium or cooperative system; and (B) that is occupied or to be occupied as the consumer's
residence." Both elements: the building type *and* actual or intended occupancy. A $640,000 house in
Southlake bought by a family who will live in it is a transaction over $100,000, but it involves the
consumer's residence, so condition (3) fails; most residential buyers also have no lawyer, so
condition (2) fails as well. **The § 17.49(f) exemption almost never protects a residential license
holder.** It was written for commercial deals between represented parties.

Section 17.49(g) is the larger and simpler one: "Nothing in this subchapter shall apply to a cause
of action arising from a transaction, a project, or a set of transactions relating to the same
project, involving total consideration by the consumer of more than $500,000, other than a cause of
action involving a consumer's residence." More than $500,000, and no written-contract or counsel
requirement — but again with the residence exception carved out of it. A $2.4 million industrial
building sale in Laredo is outside the DTPA under § 17.49(g). A $2.4 million house in Highland Park
that the buyer will occupy is not.

Commit both numbers exactly: **$100,000** with the three conditions at § 17.49(f), **$500,000** with
the single condition and the residence exception at § 17.49(g).

**The notice prerequisite as a defensive tool, § 17.505.** You already know the consumer must give
sixty days' written notice under § 17.505(a). What happens if the consumer does not? Section
17.505(c): "A person against whom a suit is pending who does not receive written notice, as required
by Subsection (a), may file a plea in abatement not later than the 30th day after the date the
person files an original answer in the court in which the suit is pending. This subsection does not
apply if Subsection (b) applies." Thirty days from your original answer — miss that window and the
tool is gone. An abatement is not a dismissal; the consumer cures it by sending the letter and
waiting sixty days. But it is real leverage and a real delay.

**The § 17.506(a) reliance-on-written-information defense.** This is the defense most directly aimed
at what a license holder actually does all day: repeating information gathered from records and from
other people. Section 17.506(a): "In an action brought under Section 17.50 of this subchapter, it is
a defense to the award of any damages or attorneys' fees if the defendant proves that before
consummation of the transaction he gave reasonable and timely written notice to the plaintiff of the
defendant's reliance on: (1) written information relating to the particular goods or service in
question obtained from official government records if the written information was false or
inaccurate and the defendant did not know and could not reasonably have known of the falsity or
inaccuracy of the information; (2) written information relating to the particular goods or service
in question obtained from another source if the information was false or inaccurate and the
defendant did not know and could not reasonably have known of the falsity or inaccuracy of the
information; or (3) written information concerning a test required or prescribed by a government
agency if the information from the test was false or inaccurate and the defendant did not know and
could not reasonably have known of the falsity or inaccuracy of the information."

Now count the conditions, because students remember this defense as "I can rely on public records"
and that is not what it says. First, the information must have been **written**: an oral statement
from the seller or a contractor's verbal assurance will not support it. Second, you must have given
**reasonable and timely written notice to the plaintiff of your reliance**, and you must have given
it **before consummation of the transaction**. That is the condition that defeats almost every
license holder who tries to invoke § 17.506(a) after the fact; the defense is not available to
someone who quietly relied on a document and produced it at trial. Third, the information must in
fact have been **false or inaccurate**, and fourth, you must prove you "did not know and could not
reasonably have known" of the falsity — both a subjective and an objective test, and the objective
half is where a defendant with a contrary document in the same file loses.

Section 17.506(b) adds a causation requirement and a limitation on its effect: "In asserting a
defense under Subdivision (1), (2), or (3) of Subsection (a) of Section 17.506 above, the defendant
shall prove the written information was a producing cause of the alleged damage. A finding of one
producing cause does not bar recovery if other conduct of the defendant not the subject of a
defensive finding under Subdivision (1), (2), or (3) of Subsection (a) of Section 17.506 above was a
producing cause of damages of the plaintiff." So the defense is claim-specific, not case-wide.
Successfully defending the square footage representation does not immunise you on the separate
representation about the roof.

Section 17.506(c) then opens the door to the real defendant: "In a suit where a defense is asserted
under Subdivision (2) of Subsection (a) of Section 17.506 above, suit may be asserted against the
third party supplying the written information without regard to privity where the third party knew
or should have reasonably foreseen that the information would be provided to a consumer; provided no
double recovery may result." The measuring service, the surveyor, the contractor who wrote the
report — each can be brought in, without privity, subject to the bar on double recovery.

**The § 17.506(d) tender defense — the cheapest exit in the statute.** Subsection (d) provides: "In
an action brought under Section 17.50 of this subchapter, it is a defense to a cause of action if
the defendant proves that he received notice from the consumer advising the defendant of the nature
of the consumer's specific complaint and of the amount of economic damages, damages for mental
anguish, and expenses, including attorneys' fees, if any, reasonably incurred by the consumer in
asserting the claim against the defendant, and that within 30 days after the day on which the
defendant received the notice the defendant tendered to the consumer: (1) the amount of economic
damages and damages for mental anguish claimed; and (2) the expenses, including attorneys' fees, if
any, reasonably incurred by the consumer in asserting the claim against the defendant."

Thirty days from receipt of the notice. Tender of the full amount claimed, in both categories — the
damages and the fees. Do that and you have a defense to the cause of action itself, which means no
mental anguish, no multiplier, and no growing fee award. Compare the Round Rock arithmetic: a
$22,000 demand tendered inside thirty days against a potential $199,200 judgment. That is why the
thirty-day clock is the one you write on your calendar first. Remember that under § 17.505(b), where
the notice is excused, "the tender provided for by Subsection (d), Section 17.506 of this subchapter
may be made within 60 days after service of the suit or counterclaim."

**Limitations, § 17.565.** The section reads in full: "All actions brought under this subchapter
must be commenced within two years after the date on which the false, misleading, or deceptive act
or practice occurred or within two years after the consumer discovered or in the exercise of
reasonable diligence should have discovered the occurrence of the false, misleading, or deceptive
act or practice. The period of limitation provided in this section may be extended for a period of
180 days if the plaintiff proves that failure timely to commence the action was caused by the
defendant's knowingly engaging in conduct solely calculated to induce the plaintiff to refrain from
or postpone the commencement of the action."

Three components. The period is **two years**. It runs from the later of two triggers: the date the
act or practice occurred, or the date the consumer "discovered or in the exercise of reasonable
diligence should have discovered" the occurrence — a discovery rule written into the statute itself,
with a constructive-discovery half that prevents a consumer from simply not looking. And there is a
**180-day** extension, available only where the plaintiff proves that the late filing "was caused by
the defendant's knowingly engaging in conduct solely calculated to induce the plaintiff to refrain
from or postpone the commencement of the action." Note the word "solely." Conduct with a mixed
purpose does not earn the extension. Because § 16.004(a)(4) gives a fraud claim four years, a
claimant who is out of time under § 17.565 may still have a fraud claim alive: when you receive a
demand letter about a transaction from three years ago, do not conclude that you are safe.

**And one defense that comes from TRELA, not the DTPA.** Occupations Code § 1101.805(e), quoted in
Unit 1, protects a license holder from a party's misrepresentation or concealment unless the license
holder both knew of the falsity and failed to disclose that knowledge. Read alongside § 17.506(a) it
yields one defensive posture — document in writing, before consummation, the source of every fact
you pass along, and the moment you learn a fact you passed along is false, say so.

## Unit: Ethical and Legal Concerns

Everything in the first four units concerned money moving from you to a consumer through a
courthouse. This unit concerns the other two tracks that run alongside it: the administrative track,
where TREC can take the license itself, and the fund the Legislature created so that a consumer who
wins a judgment against a broke license holder is not left with a worthless piece of paper.

**Two proceedings, two standards, one set of facts.** A DTPA judgment does not revoke your license:
§ 17.50(b)(4), under Damages above, bars a civil court from revoking the license of a judgment
debtor regulated by a state agency that holds revocation authority, and TREC holds it. So the civil
court takes money and TREC takes licenses, and each does its own work on its own record. A license
holder who settles a DTPA claim quietly for $18,000 has resolved nothing with the Commission. A
license holder whom TREC declines to discipline is not thereby cleared in a civil suit. The two
proceedings do not bind each other.

**The disciplinary grounds that track DTPA exposure.** Occupations Code § 1101.652 is taught in
Module 10, which walks the grounds and the disciplinary process. What belongs here is the overlap:
the grounds already named in Unit 1 — (b)(1) negligence or incompetence, (b)(3) and (b)(4) on known
material defects spoken and unspoken, (b)(5) the false promise, (b)(6) a continued and flagrant
course of misrepresentation — reach the same conduct the DTPA reaches, on an administrative record
and with no consumer, no reliance and no dollar of loss required. One ground goes further and adopts
the DTPA outright: § 1101.652(b)(15) reaches a license holder who "solicits, sells, or offers for
sale real property by means of a deceptive practice."

The Commission's rule at 22 TAC § 535.149 supplies the content of that ground, and its last sentence
is the one that welds this entire module together: it defines deceptive practices to "include, but
are not limited to the acts described in the Texas Business and Commerce Code §17.46, done in a
manner defined in that section." The Commission has adopted the DTPA's own list of deceptive acts as
the content of a disciplinary ground. So a § 17.46(b)(5) misrepresentation about square footage is
not only a civil claim; it is, by rule, a "deceptive practice" for purposes of § 1101.652(b)(15), and
the Commission may suspend or revoke on that basis. The two tracks are not merely parallel — they
share their definitions.

Three neighbouring subdivisions reach related conduct administratively and are taught in Module 10:
§ 1101.652(b)(23) reaches an advertisement that "tends to create a misleading impression" — a
markedly lower bar than any civil claim, since no consumer need have been deceived, relied, or lost
a dollar; (b)(24) reaches a knowingly inaccurate statement of account or invoice; and (b)(25)
reaches an unjustified or unwarranted threat of legal proceedings, worth remembering on the day you
are angry about a demand letter.

**The rule that states the ethical duty directly.** Rule 22 TAC § 535.156 sets out the license
holder's fiduciary obligations to a principal, and Module 02 teaches it. One sentence of it resolves
most DTPA questions before they become questions: "A license holder has a duty to convey accurate
information to members of the public with whom the license holder deals." Not to clients. To members
of the public. The buyer across the table from you, represented by another brokerage, owing you
nothing and owed by you no fiduciary duty, is still owed accurate information under § 535.156. That
rule and § 17.45(4)'s privity-free definition of consumer are the same idea expressed in two
regulatory languages.

**The Real Estate Recovery Trust Account: the consumer's backstop.** Suppose a consumer wins.
Suppose the license holder has no assets, no insurance, and no intention of paying. Subchapter M of
Chapter 1101 answers that problem. Section 1101.601(a) provides that "[t]he commission shall
maintain a real estate recovery trust account to reimburse aggrieved persons who suffer actual
damages caused by an act described by Section 1101.602 committed by: (1) a license holder; (2) a
certificate holder; or (3) a person who does not hold a license or certificate and who is an
employee or agent of a license or certificate holder." Subsection (b) adds the timing requirement:
"The license or certificate holder must have held the license or certificate at the time the act was
committed."

Section 1101.602 defines what conduct qualifies: "An aggrieved person is entitled to reimbursement
from the trust account if a person described by Section 1101.601 engages in conduct that requires a
license or certificate of registration under this chapter and is described by Section
1101.652(a-1)(1) or (b), if the person is a license holder, or Section 1101.653(1), (2), (3), or
(4), if the person is a certificate holder." For a license holder, the qualifying conduct is
§ 1101.652(a-1)(1) — misrepresentation, dishonesty, or fraud in a transaction in the license
holder's own name or a spouse's or close relative's — or *any* of the grounds in § 1101.652(b). The
entire subsection (b) list, including negligence and incompetence at (b)(1), is trust-account
conduct.

**The deadline, and the order of events.** Section 1101.605(a): "An action for a judgment that may
result in an order for payment from the trust account may not be brought after the second
anniversary of the date the cause of action accrues." Two years from accrual — the same period as
the DTPA's § 17.565, though measured by its own accrual rule and without § 17.565's express
discovery language. The account is also not a first resort: § 1101.606(a) requires the aggrieved
person to obtain a court judgment first and then, "after final judgment is entered, execution
returned nulla bona, and a judgment lien perfected, file a verified claim in the court that entered
the judgment." *Nulla bona* is the sheriff's return meaning no property was found to seize; the
consumer must have actually tried to collect and failed. And § 1101.607(2)(B) bars a license or
certificate holder who is seeking to recover compensation, including a commission, in the
transaction that is the subject of the application. The fund is a consumer remedy, not a
fee-collection device.

**The caps — state them exactly.** Section 1101.610(a): "Payments from the trust account for claims,
including attorney's fees, interest, and court costs, arising out of a single transaction may not
exceed a total of $125,000, regardless of the number of claimants." Section 1101.610(b): "Payments
from the trust account for claims based on judgments against a single license or certificate holder
may not exceed a total of $250,000 until the license or certificate holder has reimbursed the trust
account for all amounts paid."

**$125,000 per transaction, regardless of the number of claimants. $250,000 per license holder**,
and the per-license-holder ceiling lifts only when the license holder has reimbursed the account in
full. Both figures are inclusive of attorney's fees, interest, and court costs — the $125,000 is not
damages plus fees, it is everything. Section 1101.610(c) handles oversubscription: "If the court
finds that the total amount of claims against a license or certificate holder exceeds the
limitations in this section, the court shall proportionately reduce the amount payable on each
claim." And § 1101.610(e): "For purposes of this section, a business entity and the broker who is
the business entity's designated broker are considered a single license holder." The entity and its
designated broker share one $250,000 ceiling.

Return to the Round Rock arithmetic for a moment. A maximum tier-three judgment there came to
$199,200. If that judgment arose out of a single transaction and the license holder were
judgment-proof, the trust account would pay at most $125,000 of it, inclusive of the fees and costs.
The consumer would be $74,200 short. The fund is a backstop, not insurance.

**What happens to you afterwards.** Section 1101.612(a): "The commission is subrogated to all rights
of a judgment creditor to the extent of an amount paid from the trust account, and the judgment
creditor shall assign to the commission all right, title, and interest in the judgment up to that
amount." The debt does not disappear; it changes creditors, and the new creditor is the agency that
licenses you. Section 1101.655(a) then makes the consequence automatic: the Commission "shall revoke
a license, approval, or registration" if the Commission "makes a payment from the real estate
recovery trust account under Subchapter M on behalf of a license or registration holder" and the
holder "does not repay the real estate recovery trust account the full amount of a payment made on
the license or registration holder's behalf before the 31st day after the date the commission
provides notice to the license or registration holder." Thirty-one days. Section 1101.655(c): "A
person is not eligible for a license or certificate until the person has repaid in full the amount
paid from the account for the person, plus interest at the legal rate." Section 1101.655(d) applies
the same machinery to "both the business entity and designated broker" where the payment was made on
behalf of either.

Section 1101.613 confirms the independence of the two tracks: "This subchapter does not limit the
commission's authority to take disciplinary action against a license or certificate holder for a
violation of this chapter or a commission rule," and "[a] license or certificate holder's repayment
of all amounts owed to the trust account does not affect another disciplinary proceeding brought
under this chapter." Paying the fund back does not buy your way out of discipline. And § 1101.615(a)
requires that "[t]he commission by rule shall prescribe a notice regarding the availability of
payment from the trust account for aggrieved persons and establish methods by which each license and
certificate holder shall provide the notice to consumers and service recipients." The consumer's
backstop is one you are statutorily required to help the Commission tell them about.

**The ethical proposition, stated plainly.** Neither track requires dishonesty before it bites, and
the trust account exists on the premise that some license holders will cause real damage and be
unable to pay for it.

So the practical ethic is not "do not lie." It is narrower and harder: **say only what you know, say
where you learned it, and correct it in writing the moment you learn it is wrong.** Every defense in
this module rewards that habit — the § 17.506(a) written notice of reliance before consummation, the
§ 1101.805(e) protection that fails only when you knew of a falsity and stayed silent, and the
§ 17.49(i) exemption that survives for advice, judgment and opinion and dies on express
misrepresentations of material fact.

### Module recap

This module began with three words that students arrive confusing — fraud, misrepresentation,
deception — and ended with a fund the Legislature maintains because some license holders cause
damage they cannot pay for. The line running through it is a single proposition: in Texas, the legal
risk of what you say about a property is not calibrated to how honest you were. It is calibrated to
whether the statement was accurate, and your honesty enters the case only later, when the jury
decides what to multiply. Understand that inversion and the rest of the module follows.

The common-law elements of fraud, of negligent misrepresentation, and of innocent misrepresentation
come from judicial opinions, and no judicial opinion was held for this course, so those element
lists were not held and were not taught. What was held is the statutory ladder that replaces them in
practice. Business & Commerce Code § 17.45(9) defines "knowingly" as "actual awareness, at the time
of the act or practice complained of," of the falsity, deception, or unfairness — with the closing
clause that "actual awareness may be inferred where objective manifestations indicate that a person
acted with actual awareness." Section 17.45(13) defines "intentionally" as actual awareness "coupled
with the specific intent that the consumer act in detrimental reliance," and then adds that
intention may be inferred "from facts showing that a defendant acted with flagrant disregard of
prudent and fair business practices to the extent that the defendant should be treated as having
acted intentionally." That final clause is the whole point of the definition; drop it and you have
taught yourself the opposite of Texas law. Alongside those sit the Occupations Code's own grades of
fault: § 1101.652(b)(1) for negligence and incompetence, § 1101.652(b)(3) and (b)(4) for known
material defects spoken and unspoken, § 1101.652(b)(5) for a false promise made when the license
holder "is unable or does not intend to keep the promise," and 22 TAC § 535.145, which tells you
that oral promises count, that a merger clause and the parol evidence rule will not exclude them,
and that the Commission needs no proof of detrimental reliance.

The DTPA — Business & Commerce Code §§ 17.41 through 17.63, "the Deceptive Trade Practices-Consumer
Protection Act" — is what a Texas consumer actually files. Section 17.44(a) tells courts to construe
it liberally. Section 17.45(4) defines "consumer" without any word about privity, excluding only a
business consumer with assets of $25 million or more or owned or controlled by an entity with assets
of $25 million or more, and § 17.45(1) makes real property a "good." Section 17.50(a) opens four
doors, of which two matter to you: subdivision (1), requiring an act "specifically enumerated in a
subdivision of Subsection (b) of Section 17.46" and "relied on by a consumer to the consumer's
detriment," and subdivision (3), "any unconscionable action or course of action by any person,"
defined at § 17.45(5) as conduct that "takes advantage of the lack of knowledge, ability,
experience, or capacity of the consumer to a grossly unfair degree." Section 17.46(d) makes the
laundry list a closed list for private suits. Within it, the subdivisions that bite in real estate
are (2) and (3) on source and affiliation confusion, (5) on characteristics and quantities, (7) on
standard, quality and grade, (9) on bait advertising, (11) on price reductions, (12) on the rights
an agreement confers, (13) on the need for repairs, (14) on misrepresenting an agent's authority to
negotiate final terms, (20) on warranties, and above all (24) on failing to disclose known
information where the silence "was intended to induce the consumer into a transaction into which the
consumer would not have entered had the information been disclosed."

The numbers are the part you must carry exactly. Damages run from § 17.45(11)'s definition of
economic damages, which expressly excludes mental anguish. Section 17.50(b)(1) then sets three
tiers: economic damages alone; economic damages plus mental anguish plus "not more than three times
the amount of economic damages" where the conduct was committed knowingly; and economic damages plus
mental anguish plus "not more than three times the amount of damages for mental anguish and economic
damages" where it was committed intentionally. On $22,000 in economic damages and $15,000 in mental
anguish, that is a maximum of $22,000, then $103,000, then $148,000 in damages before fees — and
§ 17.50(d) makes "court costs and reasonable and necessary attorneys' fees" mandatory for a prevailing
consumer, while § 17.50(e) keeps fees, costs and prejudgment interest out of the multiplier base and
§ 17.50(g) removes the Chapter 41 caps entirely. Section 17.505(a) requires sixty days' written
notice as a prerequisite, and § 17.506(d) gives you thirty days from receipt of that notice to tender
the full amount claimed plus the consumer's reasonable fees and thereby obtain "a defense to a cause
of action".

The defenses have exact contours. Section 17.49(i) exempts a claim against a Chapter 1101 license
holder arising from an act or omission "while acting as a broker or salesperson," except for an
express misrepresentation of material fact that cannot be characterised as advice, judgment or
opinion, a § 17.46(b)(24) failure to disclose, and an unconscionable action or course of action that
cannot be so characterised. Section 17.49(c) does the same for professional services with five
carve-outs rather than three, and § 17.49(d) extends it to those vicariously liable. Section
17.49(f) exempts written-contract claims where consideration exceeds **$100,000**, the consumer was
represented by independently chosen counsel, and the contract "does not involve the consumer's
residence" as § 17.45(12) defines that term; § 17.49(g) exempts transactions of more than
**$500,000** other than a cause of action involving a consumer's residence. Both are commercial
exemptions and neither ordinarily saves a residential license holder. Section 17.506(a) gives a
defense based on written information from official government records or another source or a
required test, but only where you gave "reasonable and timely written notice to the plaintiff of the
defendant's reliance" *before consummation of the transaction* and did not know and could not
reasonably have known of the falsity — and § 17.506(c) then lets the consumer sue the information's
source "without regard to privity." Section 17.505(c) supplies the plea in abatement, filed within
thirty days of your original answer. Section 17.565 gives two years, running from the act or from
when the consumer "discovered or in the exercise of reasonable diligence should have discovered" it,
extendable by 180 days only on proof of conduct "solely calculated" to induce delay — against a
backdrop in which Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 16.004(a)(4) gives fraud four years. Waivers are
void under § 17.42 unless all three of its conditions are met.

The ethical track runs in parallel and shares the DTPA's definitions. Occupations Code
§ 1101.652(b)(15) makes it a ground to solicit, sell, or offer real property "by means of a deceptive
practice," and 22 TAC § 535.149 defines deceptive practices to "include, but are not limited to the
acts described in the Texas Business and Commerce Code §17.46, done in a manner defined in that
section." Section 1101.652(b)(23) reaches an advertisement that merely "tends to create a misleading
impression," with no consumer, no reliance and no loss required. Rule 22 TAC § 535.156 states the
affirmative duty in one sentence you should be able to recite: "A license holder has a duty to
convey accurate information to members of the public with whom the license holder deals."

Behind all of it stands the Real Estate Recovery Trust Account. Section 1101.601 requires the
Commission to maintain it; § 1101.602 makes the qualifying conduct any ground under
§ 1101.652(a-1)(1) or (b); § 1101.605(a) bars an action for a qualifying judgment "after the second
anniversary of the date the cause of action accrues"; § 1101.606 requires a final judgment,
execution returned nulla bona, a perfected lien and a verified claim; and § 1101.607 bars a license
holder seeking a commission from the same transaction. The caps are **$125,000** for claims "arising
out of a single transaction ... regardless of the number of claimants," inclusive of attorney's
fees, interest and court costs, and **$250,000** for claims based on judgments against a single
license holder until that holder reimburses the account — with a business entity and its designated
broker treated as one license holder. Section 1101.612 subrogates the Commission to the judgment,
and § 1101.655 requires revocation if the license holder does not repay in full before the
thirty-first day after notice, with no new license until repayment "plus interest at the legal rate."

Two things were looked for and not found in the sources held for this course, and you should know
both. Property Code Chapter 27, which § 17.44(b) says "prevails over this subchapter to the extent
of any conflict," was not held, so this module states nothing about its content. And the limitations
period governing a negligent misrepresentation claim was not held; Chapter 16 names fraud expressly
at § 16.004(a)(4) but does not name negligent misrepresentation in any section read here. Where you
need either answer in practice, the answer comes from a lawyer, not from a course.

## Optional reference: Indemnity between defendants, and the trust-account claim worked through

This section is **optional**. Nothing in it is required to complete this topic, and no quiz,
scenario or examination item is drawn from it. It is here because the law it covers is real and a
license holder may need it, not because the course requires you to read it. Skip it and you have
missed nothing the module or its assessments depend on.

**Indemnity: the sentence that can move the loss.** Section 17.555 provides that "[a] person against
whom an action has been brought under this subchapter may seek contribution or indemnity from one
who, under the statute law or at common law, may have liability for the damaging event of which the
consumer complains. A person seeking indemnity as provided by this section may recover all sums that
he is required to pay as a result of the action, his attorney's fees reasonable in relation to the
amount of work performed in maintaining his action for indemnity, and his costs." If the seller lied
to you and you innocently passed the lie along, § 17.555 is the provision under which your lawyer
brings the seller into the case and seeks to shift the loss — together with your own attorney's fees
for prosecuting the indemnity action. Notice that the fee language here differs from the consumer's
fee award under § 17.50(d): it is fees "reasonable in relation to the amount of work performed in
maintaining his action for indemnity." This is worth knowing because it is the only route in the
subchapter by which the loss can end up on the party who actually caused it, and because it reaches
an oral lie by a seller, which the § 17.506(a) written-reliance defense does not.

### Applied exercise: The unpaid judgment in Waco

A buyer obtains a final judgment of $164,000 against a sales agent arising from a single transaction
in Waco — $58,000 in economic damages, $26,000 in additional damages, $71,000 in attorney's fees and
$9,000 in court costs. The sales agent was licensed at the time of the acts, has since let the
license lapse, has no attachable assets, and execution has been returned nulla bona. The buyer's
judgment lien is perfected.

1. Using § 1101.601 and § 1101.602, state whether this buyer is an "aggrieved person" who may reach
   the trust account, and identify the specific statutory route through which the conduct qualifies.
   Explain what § 1101.601(b) requires about the timing of the license.
2. Apply § 1101.610(a) and compute the maximum the trust account may pay on this judgment. State
   what categories of the judgment that ceiling is inclusive of, quoting the statutory words, and
   compute the shortfall the buyer bears.
3. A second, unrelated consumer holds a $190,000 judgment against the same former sales agent from a
   different transaction. Apply § 1101.610(a) and § 1101.610(b) and state the maximum total the
   account may pay across both claims, and what § 1101.610(c) directs the court to do if the claims
   exceed the limit.
4. The cause of action accrued on 14 June. Using § 1101.605(a), state the last date on which an
   action for a judgment that may result in an order for payment from the trust account could be
   brought, and compare that period with the one in Business & Commerce Code § 17.565.
5. Assume the Commission pays $125,000 on the buyer's claim and notifies the former sales agent.
   Using § 1101.612 and § 1101.655, state what the Commission acquires, the exact number of days the
   former sales agent has to repay, what the Commission "shall" do if repayment is not made, and
   what § 1101.655(c) requires before that person could ever hold a license again.
