What is Agency? The License Act - Statute & Rules

Start with the idea itself, before any statute. Agency is the legal relationship in which one

person is authorised to act for another, in dealings with third persons, in such a way that what the

first person does is treated by the law as though the second person had done it. The person who

authorises is the principal. The person who acts is the agent. The whole point of the

arrangement is substitution: the principal cannot be in two places at once, does not know the

market, does not want to negotiate, and so hands the job to somebody else and accepts the

consequences of that person's conduct.

That substitution is what creates the legal problem. If your acts bind me, then your judgment is

being exercised over my money, my property and my legal position, in circumstances where I usually

cannot watch you. Texas law responds by doing two things at once: it gives the agent power, and it

loads the agent with duties designed to make that power safe. When you hear that a Texas license

holder is a fiduciary, that is not a compliment about character. It is a description of a legal

position in which the law has decided that ordinary arm's-length behaviour is not good enough,

because the other side of the relationship cannot protect itself.

Texas law does not begin its treatment of real estate agency with an abstract definition of that

kind. It begins with a licensing statute, and it approaches agency through the back door — by

describing the acts that, if you do them for somebody else and expect to be paid, make you a

broker and put you inside the statute. That is a very important structural point and most students

miss it. You will not find a section of Texas law headed Definition of Agency. What you will find

is a chapter of the Occupations Code that says: do these things for another person, and you are in

our world; and once you are in our world, here is what you owe. Understanding that the statute is

built around acts rather than around titles will save you from a whole family of errors later,

including the very common student belief that you are only an agent if somebody calls you one.

The statute. Chapter 1101 of the Texas Occupations Code opens at § 1101.001 with a short title:

"This chapter may be cited as The Real Estate License Act." That is the source of the abbreviation

you will see everywhere in Texas practice — TRELA. When a broker in Amarillo says *that is a

TRELA violation*, the broker means Chapter 1101 of the Occupations Code. It is an act of the Texas

Legislature. Nobody at the Commission wrote it; the Commission administers it.

Who is a broker. Section 1101.002(1) is the section on which the whole chapter balances. Under

subdivision (1)(A), "broker" means a person who, "in exchange for a commission or other valuable

consideration or with the expectation of receiving a commission or other valuable consideration,

performs for another person one of the following acts". Read that opening clause slowly, because

three of its elements are doing real work.

First, "for another person." This is the agency hinge. Everything you are about to study in this

course flows from those three words. If you buy a duplex in Killeen for your own account, you are

acting for yourself, and you are outside § 1101.002(1) no matter how sophisticated the transaction

is. The moment you do the same work for somebody else, the statute engages.

Second, **"in exchange for a commission or other valuable consideration or with the expectation of

receiving a commission or other valuable consideration."** Note that the expectation is enough. You

do not have to be paid, and you do not have to have a signed fee agreement, for the statute to reach

you. A person who helps a neighbour list a house hoping that a commission or a referral fee will

follow is inside the definition; a person who genuinely does it for nothing is not. Students

routinely assume that I never got paid is a defence. Under the plain words of the definition, it

usually is not.

Third, "one of the following acts." The definition then enumerates twelve acts, and it takes

only one. Subparagraph (A)(i) reaches a person who "sells, exchanges, purchases, or leases real

estate"; (ii) reaches one who "offers to sell, exchange, purchase, or lease real estate"; (iii), one

who "negotiates or attempts to negotiate the listing, sale, exchange, purchase, or lease of real

estate"; (iv), one who "lists or offers, attempts, or agrees to list real estate for sale, lease, or

exchange"; and (v), one who "auctions or offers, attempts, or agrees to auction real estate." Notice

how much of that language is about attempting and offering. The statute is not waiting for a

closing. An attempted negotiation is an act of a broker.

The list continues into activity that students often do not recognise as brokerage at all.

Subparagraph (A)(vi) covers a person who "deals in options on real estate, including a lease to

purchase or buying, selling, or offering to buy or sell options on real estate." Subparagraph

(A)(vii) covers one who "aids or offers or attempts to aid in locating or obtaining real estate for

purchase or lease" — that is the subparagraph that catches the friend who runs around Corpus Christi

finding rent houses for people and takes a small fee. Subparagraphs (A)(viii) and (A)(ix) are a

matched pair: one who "procures or assists in procuring a prospect to effect the sale, exchange, or

lease of real estate," and one who "procures or assists in procuring property to effect the sale,

exchange, or lease of real estate." Between them they cover finding the buyer and finding the house.

Subparagraph (A)(x) covers a person who "controls the acceptance or deposit of rent from a resident

of a single-family residential real property unit" — property management, reached through the money

rather than through the leasing.

Subparagraph (A)(xi) is the one that concerns your pricing work, and it is written with three

cumulative conditions. It covers a person who "provides a written analysis, opinion, or conclusion

relating to the estimated price of real property if the analysis, opinion, or conclusion" "(a) is

not referred to as an appraisal;" "(b) is provided in the ordinary course of the person's business;

and" "(c) is related to the actual or potential management, acquisition, disposition, or encumbrance

of an interest in real property". All three have to be true. That drafting is what makes a

comparative market analysis an act of a broker rather than an act of an appraiser. Finally,

subparagraph (A)(xii) covers a person who "advises or offers advice to an owner of real estate

concerning the negotiation or completion of a short sale."

Subdivision (1)(B) then adds two categories: a person who "is employed by or for an owner of real

estate to sell any portion of the real estate," and a person who "engages in the business of

charging an advance fee or contracting to collect a fee under a contract that requires the person

primarily to promote the sale of real estate by" listing it in a publication primarily used for

listing real estate or referring information about it to brokers.

The other definitions you need immediately. Section 1101.002(4) provides that a **license

holder** "means a broker or sales agent licensed under this chapter." That is the statute's own

umbrella term, and this course uses it rather than agent or Realtor because it is the term the

law uses. Section 1101.002(7) provides that a sales agent "means a person who is sponsored by a

licensed broker for the purpose of performing an act described by Subdivision (1)." Read that

definition again. A sales agent is defined by sponsorship. There is no such thing under Texas law

as a sales agent who is not sponsored by a broker and who is nonetheless out doing brokerage.

Section 1101.002(3) provides that the Commission "means the Texas Real Estate Commission" — that

is TREC, the nine-member state agency that administers this chapter. And § 1101.002(5) provides

that real estate "means any interest in real property, including a leasehold, located in or

outside this state. The term does not include an interest given as security for the performance of

an obligation." Two consequences: a lease is real estate for this chapter's purposes, and a lien or

deed of trust — an interest given as security — is not.

Brokerage as an activity. Section 1101.004(a) restates the same idea as a status: "A person is

engaged in real estate brokerage if the person, with the expectation of receiving valuable

consideration, directly or indirectly performs or offers, attempts, or agrees to perform for another

person any act described by Section 1101.002(1), as a part of a transaction or as an entire

transaction." The final clause matters. You cannot escape by doing only one slice of the work. If

you perform one enumerated act as part of somebody else's transaction, you are engaged in real

estate brokerage.

Subsection (b) then draws a boundary that keeps ordinary commerce out. A person is not engaged in

real estate brokerage, "regardless of whether the person is licensed under this chapter," based

solely on "constructing, remodeling, or repairing a home or other building"; "sponsoring, promoting,

or managing, or otherwise participating as a principal, partner, or financial manager of, an

investment in real estate"; or "entering into an obligation to pay another person that is secured by

an interest in real property." A general contractor in Lubbock does not become a broker by building

houses. An investor does not become a broker by putting money into a syndication. A borrower does

not become a broker by signing a note secured by a lien.

The wholesaling section. Section 1101.0045 deserves attention because it is where unlicensed

activity most often becomes licensed activity by accident. Subsection (a) allows a person to

"acquire an option or an interest in a contract to purchase real property and then sell or offer to

sell the option or assign or offer to assign the contract without holding a license issued under

this chapter" — but only if the person "does not use the option or contract to purchase to engage in

real estate brokerage" and "discloses in writing the nature of the equitable interest to any seller

or potential buyer." Subsection (b) supplies the consequence in one sentence: "A person selling or

offering to sell an option or assigning or offering to assign an interest in a contract to purchase

real property without disclosing the nature of that interest as provided by Subsection (a)(2) is

engaging in real estate brokerage." Notice the structure. The exemption is conditional, and the

condition is a written disclosure. Drop the disclosure and the same conduct is brokerage — which

means, for an unlicensed wholesaler, brokerage without a licence.

Who is outside the chapter altogether. Section 1101.005 lists the persons and transactions to

which the chapter "does not apply." Among them: "an attorney licensed in this state"; "an

attorney-in-fact authorized under a power of attorney to conduct not more than three real estate

transactions annually"; "a public official while engaged in official duties"; "an auctioneer

licensed under Chapter 1802 while conducting the sale of real estate by auction if the auctioneer

does not perform another act of a broker"; "a person conducting a real estate transaction under a

court order or the authority of a will or written trust instrument"; "a person employed by an owner

in the sale of structures and land on which structures are located if the structures are erected by

the owner in the course of the owner's business"; "an on-site manager of an apartment complex"; and

"an owner or the owner's employee who leases the owner's improved or unimproved real estate."

Read those exemptions the way a regulator reads them — narrowly, and with the qualifying clauses

attached. The attorney-in-fact exemption has a numerical cap of "not more than three real estate

transactions annually." The auctioneer exemption applies only "if the auctioneer does not perform

another act of a broker" — the auctioneer who starts negotiating terms has stepped outside it. The

builder's-employee exemption in subdivision (6) applies only where "the structures are erected by

the owner in the course of the owner's business." A new-home sales representative in a Georgetown

subdivision built by her employer is inside that exemption; the same person selling resale homes on

the side is not.

The section also lists transactions rather than people. Subdivision (9) removes from the chapter a

transaction involving the sale, lease or transfer of a real property interest in "minerals, mining,

or a quarry," "standing timber," or "other energy sources"; the sale, lease or transfer of "a

cemetery lot"; "the lease or management of a hotel or motel"; and "the sale of real property under a

power of sale conferred by a deed of trust or other contract lien." Subdivisions (10) and (11) carve

out certain transfers involving limited partnerships and limited liability companies by their

general partners, managers, managing members, members and employees who negotiate in the course of

employment, and subdivision (12) covers "a person conducting a real estate transaction on behalf of

a commercial signage entity."

The licence requirement. Section 1101.351(a) provides that unless a person holds a license

issued under the chapter, the person may not "act as or represent that the person is a broker or

sales agent" or "act as a residential rental locator." Subsection (a-1) applies the same rule to

business entities: "Unless a business entity holds a license issued under this chapter, the business

entity may not act as a broker." Subsection (b) closes the gap between passing the exam and holding

the licence: "An applicant for a broker or sales agent license may not act as a broker or sales

agent until the person receives the license evidencing that authority."

Then comes the sentence that shapes your working life. Section 1101.351(c): "A licensed sales agent

may not engage or attempt to engage in real estate brokerage unless the sales agent is sponsored by

a licensed broker and is acting for that broker." Two conditions, both required, joined by "and."

You must be sponsored, and you must be acting for that broker. A sales agent is not a small

broker. A sales agent is a licence held inside somebody else's licence.

The statute follows that structure through to liability. Section 1101.803 makes a licensed broker

"liable to the commission, the public, and the broker's clients for any conduct engaged in under

this chapter by the broker or by a sales agent associated with or acting for the broker." Read what

that sentence does not do — it adds the broker; it does not subtract you. Your conduct is, by

statute, the broker's exposure. Module 09 teaches broker responsibility.

The Commission builds the operating system on top of that statute in 22 TAC § 535.2, which requires

a broker to notify each sponsored sales agent in writing of the scope of the sales agent's

authorised activities, makes the broker responsible for those authorised acts and for acts the

broker permits beyond that scope, and requires at subsection (f) that "Listings and other agreements

for real estate brokerage services must be solicited and accepted in a broker's name." That last

requirement tells you who the client's contract is actually with: when you take a listing in Plano,

the seller's agreement is with your broker, and you are the person through whom the broker performs.

Module 09 teaches § 535.2 in full.

The rules, and why they bind you as hard as the statute. New license holders often treat the

TREC rules as a kind of internal handbook, softer than real law. That is wrong, and it is an

expensive mistake. Section 1101.151(b) provides that the commission may "adopt and enforce rules

necessary to administer this chapter and Chapter 1102" and may "establish standards of conduct and

ethics for persons licensed under this chapter and Chapter 1102" to "fulfill the purposes of this

chapter and Chapter 1102" and "ensure compliance with this chapter and Chapter 1102." The

Legislature delegated; the Commission legislated within the delegation. The product of that

delegation is codified in Title 22 of the Texas Administrative Code — Chapter 531 (a short chapter

of conduct standards) and Chapter 535 (a long chapter of operational rules).

The enforcement provisions name the rules explicitly. Section 1101.701(a) allows the commission to

impose an administrative penalty on "a person who violates this chapter or a rule adopted or order

issued by the commission under this chapter"; § 1101.751(a) allows the commission to bring an action

to enjoin a violation of the chapter or a commission rule; and § 1101.759(a) authorises a cease and

desist order on the same footing. A rule violation is an enforceable violation. Learn both bodies of

authority, and learn which is which, because a well-trained license holder can say *the statute

requires X and the rule requires Y* and an untrained one says TREC says about everything and is

wrong half the time. Module 10 teaches the penalty and enforcement provisions.

The rules also keep their own definitions, and they are not identical to the statute's. Rule 22 TAC

§ 531.1 defines only two terms for Chapter 531: "Commission–The Texas Real Estate Commission" and

"License Holder–A real estate broker or sales agent licensed under Chapter 1101, Texas Occupations

Code." Rule 22 TAC § 535.1 defines rather more for Chapter 535, including "Act–Chapter 1101, Texas

Occupations Code," and — importantly — a broader licence-holder definition: "License holder–A person

licensed or registered by the Commission under Chapter 1101 or 1102, Texas Occupations Code."

Chapter 1102 governs real estate inspectors. So when you read a Chapter 535 rule that speaks of a

"license holder," check whether the drafting reaches inspectors too. Section 535.1 also defines

"Compensation–A commission, fee, or other valuable consideration for real estate brokerage services

provided by a license holder under the Act" and "Place of business–A place where the license holder

meets with clients and customers to transact business." That last definition is the only place in

the sources read for this course where the words "clients" and "customers" appear side by side in a

definition — and note that it defines the place, not either kind of person. We will come back to

that in Unit 3.

How far the Act reaches. Rule 22 TAC § 535.4 answers the geography question that trips up

license holders working remotely: "The Act applies to any person acting as a real estate broker or

sales agent while physically within Texas, regardless of the location of the real estate involved or

the residence of the person's customers or clients. For the purposes of the Act, a person conducting

brokerage business from another state by mail, telephone, the Internet, email, or other medium is

acting within Texas if the real property concerned is located wholly or partly in Texas." Both

halves are traps in different directions. Sitting in El Paso brokering a New Mexico ranch, you are

within the Act because you are physically in Texas. Sitting in Santa Fe brokering an El Paso ranch,

you are within the Act because the property is in Texas.

The same rule tells you that showing is brokerage: "Unless otherwise exempted by the Act, a person

must be licensed as a broker or sales agent to show a property." And it defines showing broadly —

for purposes of that section, to show a property "includes causing or permitting the property to be

viewed by a prospective buyer or tenant, unlocking or providing access onto or into a property for a

prospective buyer or tenant, and hosting an open house at the property." Unlicensed assistants who

unlock doors are performing an act for which a licence is required. Section 535.4 further provides

that "A real estate license is required for a person to solicit listings or to negotiate in Texas

for listings," and that "A real estate license is required for a person to receive a fee or other

consideration for assisting another person to locate real property for sale, purchase, rent, or

lease, including the operation of a service which finds apartments or homes."

Rule 22 TAC § 535.5 supplies the mirror image. "Acting as a principal, a person may purchase, sell,

lease, or sublease real estate for profit without being licensed as a broker or sales agent." Owners

may deal for themselves. But the rule immediately narrows the point for co-owners: "A person who

owns property jointly may sell and convey title to his or her interest in the property, but to act

for compensation or with the expectation of compensation as an agent for the other owner, the person

must be licensed unless otherwise exempted by the Act." Selling your own half interest is not

brokerage; selling your co-owner's half for a fee is. Section 535.5 also provides that "An answering

service or clerical or administrative employees identified to callers as such to confirm information

concerning the size, price, and terms of property advertised are not required to be licensed under

the Act" — which tells your unlicensed assistant precisely where the line is, and it is a narrow

line: size, price, terms, and identification as clerical staff.

The common-law rules governing how agency relationships are created outside a licensing statute —

formation by ratification, by estoppel, by apparent authority — were not held for this course and

are not taught here. What you have, and what Texas will hold you to, is the statute and the rules.

Topic quiz

Every question must be answered correctly before the next topic opens. An incorrect answer shows the reasoning and then serves a different, related question on the same rule.

Question 1 of 4

Over one weekend an unlicensed man in Tyler introduces an investor to the owner of a twelve-unit building, sits in on two negotiating sessions, and tells friends he expects the investor will look after him if it closes. The sale falls through and he is paid nothing. He says the Real Estate License Act cannot reach him because he never received a commission and never listed anything. Evaluate that under § 1101.002(1)(A).

Section 1101.002(1)(A) opens by reaching a person who acts for another “in exchange for a commission or other valuable consideration or with the expectation of receiving a commission or other valuable consideration,” so the expectation alone engages the statute and “I was never paid” is not the defence students assume. The subdivision then requires only “one of the following acts,” and clause (iii) names one who “negotiates or attempts to negotiate” a sale — an attempt, not a closing. Section 1101.004(a) restates the same idea as a status and reaches an act performed “as a part of a transaction or as an entire transaction.”

Question 2 of 4

An unlicensed person collects a $9,000 fee for finding a buyer for a house in Tyler. State the range of civil penalty exposure created by § 1101.753(a) and § 1101.754(a) together, and say how the two provisions relate to each other.

Section 1101.753(a) makes a person who receives a commission or other consideration for acting as a broker or sales agent without a licence liable to the state for a civil penalty of “not less than the amount of money received or more than three times the amount of money received,” and § 1101.754(a) gives an aggrieved person the same range privately. They are separate provisions with separate claimants, so on a $9,000 fee the arithmetic is $9,000–$27,000 twice, $54,000 at the top, and § 1101.801 confirms that a disciplinary action does not relieve a person of civil or criminal liability besides.

Question 3 of 4

A Fort Worth transaction involves an unrepresented seller, an executor selling a second property out of an estate, a buyer represented by another brokerage, and that buyer's sales agent. Applying the definition of “party” in § 1101.551(2), which of them are parties?

Section 1101.551(2) defines a party as “a prospective buyer, seller, landlord, or tenant or an authorized representative of a buyer, seller, landlord, or tenant, including a trustee, guardian, executor, administrator, receiver, or attorney-in-fact,” and then adds that the term “does not include a license holder who represents a party.” An executor is named in the definition, so the estate's representative is inside it; being represented does not remove a buyer from it; and the exclusion of the counterpart's license holder is why a duty owed to a party is not discharged by telling that party's agent something and assuming it landed.

Question 4 of 4

A Round Rock license holder drives a couple to showings for three months, advises them on price, and writes their offer. Nothing was ever signed. The couple close with someone else and the license holder wants to sue for a fee. Reading § 1101.557(a) against § 1101.806(c), what is the position?

Section 1101.557(a) fixes agency on what the broker is doing — representing a party, or listing under an exclusive agreement — and contains no signature requirement, while § 1101.806(c) conditions any action to recover a commission on a writing “signed by the party against whom the action is brought or by a person authorized by that party to sign the document.” Your own signature proves nothing. That asymmetry is the whole hazard: behaving as an agent can create obligations, and only a signed writing creates a claim to be paid for them.

Roles People Play in Agency Relationships - Client or Customer?

A buyer asks you to show a listed house. You show it, the buyer makes an offer through you, and at closing the listing broker's office pays your broker a share of the commission. No representation agreement was ever signed with the buyer. Who did you represent?

Section 1101.557 sets out what a licence holder representing a party must do and mentions compensation nowhere. Representation arises from an agreement, and for a prospective buyer of residential real property § 1101.563(b) now requires that agreement to be in writing before the property is shown. Payment is a separate question, governed by § 1101.651 and § 535.3 — which is why a broker can be paid by one side while representing the other, and why being paid by nobody does not mean you represented nobody.
