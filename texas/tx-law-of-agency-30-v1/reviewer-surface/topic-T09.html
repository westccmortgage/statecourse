Independent Contract Agreements

You will be handed an Independent Contractor Agreement with your sponsorship paperwork, and most

new agents sign it unread. That is backwards. Two statutes — one Texas, one federal — make a

signed written contract a condition of the status each of them confers, and each confers it for

one narrow purpose only: the Texas statute decides whether your service counts as "employment" for

unemployment compensation purposes, and the federal statute decides federal tax treatment.

Neither decides whether you are an employee for any other purpose, and neither touches your

broker's duty to supervise you.

Labor Code chapter 201 is the state's unemployment compensation statute, and "employment" is the

word that carries the tax: a service that is not "employment" bears no unemployment tax and builds

no benefit entitlement. Because that one word decides who pays and who collects, the Legislature

carved specific trades out of it, and real estate has a section of its own. Section 201.072,

headed SERVICE AS REAL ESTATE BROKER, opens with the words "In this subtitle", so everything it

does is confined to the unemployment compensation subtitle. It reads in full:

Sec. 201.072. SERVICE AS REAL ESTATE BROKER. In this subtitle, "employment" does not include:

(1) service performed by an individual as a real estate broker or salesperson if: (A) the

individual engages in activity described by the definition of " broker" in Section 1101.002,

Occupations Code; (B) the individual is licensed as a broker or salesperson by the Texas Real

Estate Commission; (C) substantially all remuneration for the service, whether in cash or other

form of payment, is directly related to sales or other output, including the performance of the

service, and not to the number of hours worked; and (D) the service is performed under a written

contract between the individual and the person for whom the service is performed, and the

contract provides that the individual is not treated as an employee with respect to the service

for federal tax purposes; or (2) service performed by an individual as an instructor of a person

licensed or seeking a license as a real estate broker or salesperson if: (A) the individual

instructs in an educational program or course approved by the Texas Real Estate Commission; and

(B) the service is performed under a written contract between the individual and the person for

whom the service is performed and the contract provides that the individual is not treated as an

employee with respect to the service for federal tax purposes.

The stray space inside the quotation marks around "broker" in paragraph (1)(A) is in the statute's

published text; it is reproduced here rather than silently corrected, because this course quotes

what the source says.

Fix the boundary before working through the conditions. Section 201.072 answers one question:

whether the service is "employment" for the purposes of the subtitle that contains it, the

unemployment compensation system administered by the Texas Workforce Commission. It does not

decide whether a sales agent is an employee for wage-and-hour purposes, for workers' compensation,

for discrimination law, for tort or vicarious liability, or at common law. The authorities that

would answer those questions were not held for this course; they are named at the end of this

unit.

Condition (A) ties the exclusion to the work: activity described by the definition of "broker"

in Occupations Code § 1101.002. It covers licensed brokerage activity, not everything a person

might do for a brokerage — a sales agent who also keeps the office books for an hourly wage is

not, in doing that, performing activity described by the definition. Condition (B) requires a

Texas Real Estate Commission licence; chapter 201 says "salesperson," the word the Legislature

used in 1993, and TRELA and the Commission's rules now say "sales agent," meaning the same

licence. An unlicensed or lapsed worker cannot use § 201.072, because condition (B) fails on its

face.

Condition (C) is the pay condition, and the one brokerages most often break by accident:

"[S]ubstantially all remuneration for the service, whether in cash or other form of payment, is

directly related to sales or other output, including the performance of the service, and not to

the number of hours worked." Commission on closings is output-based pay; an hourly rate for floor

duty or a guaranteed monthly salary is not, and a brokerage paying both has a mixed-remuneration

problem. The honest answer to it is that neither § 201.072 nor 26 U.S.C. § 3508 defines

"substantially all," and no Treasury regulation, Internal Revenue Service ruling, or Texas

Workforce Commission rule construing the phrase was held for this course. The threshold is not

taught here.

Condition (D) has two parts that must both be present: "the service is performed under a written

contract between the individual and the person for whom the service is performed," and — the

sentence people leave out — "the contract provides that the individual is not treated as an

employee with respect to the service for federal tax purposes." An agreement that calls the agent

an independent contractor, sets a split and allocates expenses but says nothing about federal tax

treatment does not satisfy condition (D): the statute asks not what the parties intended but

whether the contract provides it.

Paragraph (2) does the same for an instructor of a person licensed or seeking a licence as a

broker or salesperson, on two conditions: the individual "instructs in an educational program or

course approved by the Texas Real Estate Commission," and the written contract carries the same

federal-tax-purposes provision. It has no output-based pay condition, so an instructor may be paid

by the classroom hour and still fall outside "employment."

What § 201.072 conspicuously does not require. Nothing in the four conditions requires that

the agent be free from the broker's control, set their own hours, or be free to accept or reject

assignments. That absence is not an oversight: neighbouring exclusions in the same subchapter do

condition the exclusion on control. Section 201.070 requires, among other factors, that the

individual "determines the method of performing the service," and § 201.073 that the individual

"may accept or reject a job from the delivery service." The Legislature knew how to make freedom

from control a condition of an exclusion; in § 201.072 it chose not to. That resolves the tension you meet in Unit 5: a broker may supervise as closely as

the Commission's rules require without endangering the exclusion.

The federal side. For federal tax purposes the governing provision is 26 U.S.C. § 3508,

"Treatment of real estate agents and direct sellers." Subsection (a) states the general rule: "For

purposes of this title, in the case of services performed as a qualified real estate agent or as a

direct seller-" then "(1) the individual performing such services shall not be treated as an

employee, and (2) the person for whom such services are performed shall not be treated as an

employer." It cuts both ways, removing employer status from the brokerage and with it the federal

employment tax withholding and matching obligations. And its opening words are a limit: "For

purposes of this title" confines the section to Title 26, so § 3508 decides federal tax treatment

and nothing else — not your broker's supervision duty, not your status under Labor Code chapter

21, not anyone's liability to anyone.

Subsection (b)(1) supplies the definition the general rule turns on. "The term "qualified real

estate agent" means any individual who is a sales person if-" and then three conditions: "(A) such

individual is a licensed real estate agent," "(B) substantially all of the remuneration (whether

or not paid in cash) for the services performed by such individual as a real estate agent is

directly related to sales or other output (including the performance of services) rather than to

the number of hours worked, and" "(C) the services performed by the individual are performed

pursuant to a written contract between such individual and the person for whom the services are

performed and such contract provides that the individual will not be treated as an employee with

respect to such services for Federal tax purposes."

One well-drafted agreement satisfies both statutes, because they are one drafting pattern rather

than two regimes. Its statutory core is short: the parties by name; a statement that the

individual holds a Texas licence and will perform activity described by Occupations Code

§ 1101.002; compensation measured by production rather than hours; and an express provision that the

individual will not be treated as an employee with respect to the services for federal tax

purposes.

The non-interaction you must not get wrong. Your status as an independent contractor for

unemployment compensation purposes under § 201.072, and for federal tax purposes under § 3508, has

no effect whatever on your broker's obligations under the licensing law. Those obligations

serve a different purpose — brokerage supervision and licensing responsibility — and are not

conditioned on employment. Rule 22 TAC § 535.2 says so in its closing sentence: "This section is

not meant to create or require an employer/employee relationship between a broker and a sponsored

sales agent." It does not make the broker's obligations lighter for a contractor; it says they

exist independently of employment status. Alongside it sits Occupations Code § 1101.803: "A

licensed broker is liable to the commission, the public, and the broker's clients for any conduct

engaged in under this chapter by the broker or by a sales agent associated with or acting for the

broker." The Legislature attached that liability to association, not to employment, so a clause in

your contractor agreement stating that the broker is not responsible for your acts is worth

nothing against § 1101.803.

Stated plainly: what was not held. The Internal Revenue Service's administrative guidance and

the Treasury regulations under 26 U.S.C. § 3508, the Texas Workforce Commission's own rules and

audit practice under chapter 201, the Fair Labor Standards Act, the Texas Payday Law, Texas

workers' compensation law, and Texas case law on common-law employment status and vicarious

liability were not held for this course. So where you have heard that the Internal Revenue

Service applies a twenty-factor control test, or that a brokerage must or must not carry workers'

compensation on its agents, this module states no position.

Topic quiz

Every question must be answered correctly before the next topic opens. An incorrect answer shows the reasoning and then serves a different, related question on the same rule.

Question 1 of 6

A brokerage's independent contractor agreement names the parties, calls the agent an independent contractor, sets a commission split and allocates marketing expenses. It says nothing about federal tax treatment. Test it against Labor Code § 201.072(1)(D).

Section 201.072(1)(D) requires that “the service is performed under a written contract between the individual and the person for whom the service is performed, and the contract provides that the individual is not treated as an employee with respect to the service for federal tax purposes.” The statute asks not what the parties intended but whether the contract provides it, so an agreement that omits the sentence fails the condition on its face. Output-based pay is a different condition, (C), and the instructor exclusion in paragraph (2) carries the same federal-tax term. Note also the boundary: the section opens “In this subtitle,” so it decides whether the service is employment for unemployment compensation and nothing else.

Question 2 of 6

A Waco firm has eighty sponsored sales agents who are genuine independent contractors and three salaried staff. Which subchapters of Labor Code chapter 21 reach it?

Section 21.002(8) defines an employer as a person engaged in an industry affecting commerce “who has 15 or more employees for each working day in each of 20 or more calendar weeks in the current or preceding calendar year,” plus an agent of such a person, and § 21.002(7) defines an employee circularly as “an individual employed by an employer” — the chapter supplies no test for separating an employee from a contractor. Subchapter C-1, added in 2021, has its own definition at § 21.141: an employer is a person who “employs one or more employees.” A two-person brokerage is inside that subchapter the day it hires its first assistant, and § 21.142 makes an employer liable where it or its agents or supervisors knew or should have known of sexual harassment and failed to take immediate and appropriate corrective action.

Question 3 of 6

Two provisions use the word employ. Occupations Code § 1101.002(1)(B)(i) includes within “broker” a person “employed by or for an owner of real estate to sell any portion of the real estate,” and 22 TAC § 535.5(d)(2) says “[a]n employee of a business entity means a person employed and directly compensated by the business entity. An independent contractor is not an employee.” How should a student read them?

Real estate law uses “employ” in a sense that predates labour law: the seller employs the broker, which is what a listing agreement does, and it has nothing to do with payroll, withholding or unemployment tax. The Commission's rules use the labour-law sense where it matters — 22 TAC § 535.5(c) exempts from licensure “an employee employed by a business entity for the purpose of buying, selling, or leasing real property for the entity,” and § 535.5(d)(2) then defines that term and excludes an independent contractor. Section 1101.557(a) settles the agency side: the broker who represents a party or lists under an exclusive agreement “is that party's agent,” and the sales agent performs the work as the person acting for that broker.

Question 4 of 6

Your unlicensed assistant offers to meet a buyer at your Round Rock listing and unlock the door so the buyer can walk through alone while you finish a closing, and to sit the open house on Sunday under instructions to answer no questions. Apply 22 TAC § 535.4(c).

22 TAC § 535.4(c) provides that unless otherwise exempted by the Act “a person must be licensed as a broker or sales agent to show a property,” and then defines showing far more broadly than the word sounds — causing or permitting the property to be viewed, unlocking or providing access onto or into it, and hosting an open house. Instructions to stay silent do not help, because the rule reaches the access rather than the talk. Section 535.5(g) is a different and much narrower permission: an answering service or clerical or administrative employee identified to callers as such may confirm the size, price and terms of advertised property, which is not a permission to be at the property with a buyer.

Question 5 of 6

A sponsored sales agent has led a four-person team at her brokerage for the last eight months. Nothing was ever put in writing and the Commission has never been told. What does 22 TAC § 535.2(e) require?

22 TAC § 535.2(e) lets a broker delegate the responsibility “to assist in administering compliance with the Act and Rules” without relinquishing overall responsibility for supervision, and then adds three operative sentences: any license holder who leads, supervises, directs or manages a team must be delegated as a supervisor; any such delegation must be in writing; and the broker shall provide the name of each delegated supervisor to the Commission within 30 days of any delegation “that has lasted or is anticipated to last more than three consecutive months,” with notice in the same manner within 30 days after the delegation ends. Note both limbs of the reporting trigger — has lasted, or is anticipated to last.

Question 6 of 6

A sales agent forms an LLC owned 60 percent by her and 40 percent by her brother, does no brokerage through it, has not registered it with the Commission, and asks her broker to make the commission cheque payable to it. Apply § 1101.355(c) and (d).

Section 1101.355(c) states the default: “A business entity that receives compensation on behalf of a license holder must be licensed as a broker under this chapter.” Subsection (d) supplies the exception and all five of its conditions are required — the entity receives compensation on behalf of a license holder earned while engaged in real estate brokerage; performs no other acts of a broker; is a limited liability company or an S corporation as defined by the cited provisions; is registered with the Commission; and is at least 51 percent owned by the license holder on whose behalf it receives the compensation. Sixty percent satisfies the ownership condition and the missing registration defeats the arrangement; 22 TAC § 535.147(c) bars sharing a commission with an unlicensed entity created to collect on the license holder's behalf unless it is exempt.

Optional practice — reading § 201.072 against its neighbours. This is document practice on the

primary sources rather than a brokerage fact pattern, and it is the only exercise in this module

that works directly with the Labor Code. Open Texas Labor Code § 201.072, then § 201.070 and

§ 201.073 in the same subchapter.

1. List the four conditions in § 201.072(1) in your own words. Which of them refers to an activity

definition found in a different code entirely, and which code and section is it?

2. Sections 201.070 and 201.073 each condition their exclusion on a list of control factors.

Identify three such factors from either section. Then state whether any equivalent factor

appears in § 201.072, and explain what you conclude from the difference.

3. Section 201.072(2) covers real estate instructors. Which of the four conditions in paragraph

(1) has no counterpart in paragraph (2)? Why would the Legislature have left it out for

instructors?

4. A broker tells a new agent, "We don't need a written agreement, everyone here knows you're a

contractor." Point to the specific language in § 201.072 that this defeats, and say what the

consequence is under the section.

5. Section 201.072 opens with the phrase "In this subtitle." What does that phrase limit the

exclusion to, and what does it therefore tell you about whether § 201.072 answers questions

arising under Labor Code chapter 21?
