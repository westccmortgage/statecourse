Agency, Ethics and the Law

Topic 10 of 12 · 155 prescribed minutes

Distinctions between Law, Ethics, and Morals

The distinction this unit draws is not philosophical. It is jurisdictional. Three different

enforcement systems can reach your conduct in a Texas real estate transaction, each with its own

decision-maker, its own procedure, its own sanction, and its own vocabulary. If you cannot sort a

complaint into the right box, you will either panic over something that carries no consequence or

shrug at something that will cost you your license.

Law is the set of rules the state itself enforces. A criminal statute is enforced by a

prosecutor, in a court, against a defendant, with the state's coercive power at the end of it. A

civil statute or a common-law duty is enforced by a private party who sues, in a court, and collects

a judgment. Both are law. Their common feature is that the enforcer, ultimately, is a government

body with the power to take money, property or liberty. Texas fair housing law supplies an example

of a statute that reaches all three of those levels at once: Property Code § 301.171 makes

intimidating or interfering with a protected person by force or threat of force an offense, and

provides that "An offense under this section is a Class A misdemeanor"; § 301.151(a) lets an

aggrieved person file a civil action in district court; and § 301.112 lets a state agency assess

civil penalties administratively. One underlying prohibition, three enforcement machines.

**Ethics, in a licensed profession, is the set of rules a licensing body or a membership

organization enforces against people who have accepted its authority.** The word carries a soft

connotation in ordinary speech — a matter of conscience, or of taste. In Texas real estate it is

nothing of the kind. The Commission's Canons of Professional Ethics and Client Relations are

administrative rules published at 22 TAC Chapter 531, and they are enforceable. Occupations Code § 1101.656(a) provides that "In addition to any other

authority under this chapter, the commission may suspend or revoke a license, place on probation a

person whose license has been suspended, or reprimand a license holder if the license holder

violates this chapter or a commission rule." Section 1101.701(a) adds the money: "The commission may

impose an administrative penalty on a person who violates this chapter or a rule adopted or order

issued by the commission under this chapter." Read those two sentences together and the point is

unmistakable. The phrase "or a commission rule" and the phrase "or a rule adopted" are what convert

an ethical canon into an enforceable obligation with a license and a bank balance behind it. An

ethical breach in this field is not a matter of taste. It is a rule violation, and the state agency

that issued your license is the enforcer.

The second sort of ethics enforcement is private, and Unit 4 deals with it in detail. Hold on to the

structural difference for now: the Commission enforces against everyone who holds a license, whether

they like it or not; a trade association enforces only against its own members, and only because

they agreed to be bound when they joined and paid dues.

Morals are the convictions a person holds about right conduct, enforced by nobody. No procedure,

no sanction, no forum. That is not a criticism of morals — a license holder's private sense of

decency is usually the thing that keeps the other two systems from ever having to be invoked — but

it is a real limitation, and the limitation runs both directions. A moral conviction does not by

itself create a legal duty. It also does not excuse you from one.

Texas gives a sharp example of morals running against law. Suppose a buyer's agent in San Antonio

learns that a previous occupant of a listed house died of an AIDS-related illness. Many people's

moral intuition is that a buyer would want to know, and that concealing it is a species of lying.

The law says otherwise, in two places, and both were written deliberately. Occupations Code § 1101.802 provides that

"Notwithstanding Section 1101.801, a person is not civilly or criminally liable because the person

failed to inquire about, make a disclosure relating to, or release information relating to whether a

previous or current occupant of real property had, may have had, has, or may have AIDS, an

HIV-related illness, or HIV infection as defined by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention

of the United States Public Health Service." And 22 TAC § 531.19, the Commission's discriminatory

practices canon, closes with the sentence: "For the purpose of this section, disability includes

AIDS, HIV-related illnesses, or HIV infection as defined by the Centers for Disease Control of the

United States Public Health Service." Put those together and the agent who volunteers the

information has not merely done something the law did not require. The agent has made a disclosure

about a previous occupant's disability, which is exactly what § 531.19 forbids. The moral intuition,

followed uncritically, walks you into a rule violation.

Why a license holder can be disciplined for conduct that breaks no criminal law. Discipline does

not require wrongdoing of the sort a jury would recognize — theft, fraud, a falsified document.

Title 22 TAC § 535.157 requires a broker or sales agent to respond

to a principal, to a license holder representing another party, or to an unrepresented party within

two calendar days; that rule is taught in Module 02. Failing to return a cooperating agent's call

for four days is not a crime, and very likely not a tort — the cooperating agent has no contract

with you and probably no damages. But it is a violation of a Commission rule, § 1101.656(a)

authorizes discipline for violating a commission rule, and 22 TAC § 535.191 places "22 TAC

§535.157" in the band for which "An administrative penalty range of $100 - $1,500 per violation per

day may be assessed." Per violation. Per day. That is the whole distance between ethics as a feeling

and ethics as an enforceable standard.

The three systems also run in parallel rather than in sequence, and TRELA says so expressly. Section

1101.801 provides: "Disciplinary action taken against a person under Section 1101.652 does not

relieve the person from civil or criminal liability." A single act — say, spending a buyer's earnest

money out of the operating account — can produce a criminal charge brought by a district attorney, a

civil suit brought by the buyer, and a Commission proceeding, and none of the three waits for the

others. Nor does winning one win the others. An acquittal in a criminal case does not bar the

Commission, whose burden and whose rules are different. A Commission order that assesses a penalty

does not compensate the buyer; § 1101.659(b) limits what a refund order may do, providing that the

amount "may not exceed the amount the consumer paid to the person for a service or accommodation

regulated by this commission" and that "The commission may not require payment of other damages or

estimate harm in a refund order." The Commission is a regulator, not a small-claims court. If the

consumer wants damages, the consumer sues.

It is worth noticing what forum the ethics system actually uses, because it is neither a court nor a

private tribunal. Under § 1101.657(a), "If the commission proposes to deny, suspend, or revoke a

person's license or certificate of registration, the person is entitled to a hearing conducted by

the State Office of Administrative Hearings," and subsection (e) provides that "A hearing under this

section is governed by the contested case procedures under Chapter 2001, Government Code." From

there, § 1101.658(a) gives "A person aggrieved by a ruling, order, or decision under this

subchapter" the right "to appeal to a district court in the county in which the administrative

hearing was held." So the ethics system does eventually touch the courts — but on review, and on an

administrative record, not as a fresh lawsuit. The record the Commission builds during its

investigation is the record you will argue from later.

A last distinction that matters in daily practice. Law tends to be specific and bounded; ethics

rules in this field are frequently open-textured, and that is intentional. Compare Occupations Code

§ 1101.652(b)(30), which tells you precisely where escrow money goes — "in trust with a title

company authorized to do business in this state" or "in a custodial, trust, or escrow account

maintained for that purpose in a banking institution authorized to do business in this state" — with

22 TAC § 531.3, which requires a license holder to exercise integrity "including employment of

prudence and caution so as to avoid misrepresentation, in any way, by acts of commission or

omission." The first is a rule you can comply with by following an instruction. The second is a

standard you comply with by exercising judgment, and it is written broadly precisely so that it can

reach conduct nobody anticipated. Your protection against an open-textured standard is not to hunt

for the loophole. It is to build habits that keep you well inside the line.

Topic quiz

Every question must be answered correctly before the next topic opens. An incorrect answer shows the reasoning and then serves a different, related question on the same rule.

Question 1 of 5

A sales agent at a family funeral does not return a cooperating agent's call for six days. The offer stays open, the seller accepts it, and nobody loses a dollar. Can that reach her, and through which system?

The three enforcement systems are jurisdictional rather than graded by seriousness. Law is enforced by the state through courts; morals are enforced by nobody; and ethics, for a Texas license holder, is enforced by the Commission — which is what the phrases “or a commission rule” in § 1101.656(a) and “or a rule adopted” in § 1101.701(a) accomplish. Failing to return a call for six days is very likely neither a crime nor a tort, and it is squarely a violation of 22 TAC § 535.157's two-calendar-day obligation. Section 1101.756 is narrower than the last option suggests: it reaches a person who wilfully violates or fails to comply with the chapter or a Commission order.

Question 2 of 5

A landlord refuses to rent to an applicant because the applicant uses a wheelchair. Which federal provision states the prohibition, and why is it not § 3604(a)?

Read the three lists slowly, because the difference is deliberate rather than a drafting slip. Sections 3604(a) and (b) name six characteristics; § 3604(c), on notices, statements and advertisements, names seven by adding handicap; and § 3604(f)(1) carries the substantive prohibition on discriminating in the sale or rental of, or making unavailable or denying, a dwelling because of a handicap of the buyer or renter, of a person who will reside there, or of “any person associated with that buyer or renter.” That last phrase catches a common error: refusing an applicant because a visiting adult child uses a wheelchair violates the Act although the applicant has no disability.

Question 3 of 5

A Corpus Christi license holder sells a duplex he owns personally and lies to the buyer about a roof leak. Under the architecture of § 1101.652, where does that conduct sit?

Section 1101.652 has three parts and the part you are in changes what the Commission must prove. Subsection (a) lists general grounds that apply without regard to brokerage; subsection (a-1) contains three grounds concerning the license holder's own transactions and records; and subsection (b) contains thirty-four grounds gated by six words — “if the license holder, while engaged in real estate brokerage.” That gate is exactly why subsection (a-1) exists separately. 22 TAC § 535.144 then extends “license holder” for (a-1)(1) purposes to a license holder acting for a spouse, parent or child, a business entity more than ten per cent owned, or certain trusts.

Question 4 of 5

A trade association's hearing panel considers a member's conduct and declines to act. The member tells a colleague this clears him. Evaluate that, using the held sources on associations.

Everything about an association proceeding follows from its contractual character: you agreed to be bound when you joined and paid dues, so the body reaches only members, applies its own code by its own procedure, and can take membership and the privileges that come with it — commonly including participation in a multiple listing service. It cannot touch the licence. The Commission, by contrast, reaches every license holder in the state; 22 TAC § 531.1 defines License Holder as a broker or sales agent licensed under Chapter 1101 and the Canons apply on that basis alone. Neither proceeding is a prerequisite to the other and neither decides the other; § 1101.801 confirms that discipline does not relieve civil or criminal liability.

Question 5 of 5

A Plano agent takes a listing in Marfa as a favour, prices it from county-wide averages, and never studies the local market. Two grounds in § 1101.652 are candidates. Where do they sit?

Students misfile the market-conditions ground constantly, and the subsection matters because subsection (b) is gated by “while engaged in real estate brokerage” while subsection (a) is not. Section 1101.652(a)(6) reaches a license holder who fails to consider market conditions for the specific geographic area in which the license holder is providing a service; § 1101.652(b)(1) reaches one who acts negligently or incompetently in brokerage. Behind both sits 22 TAC § 531.4, which requires a license holder to be informed on local market issues and conditions “in the geographic area where a license holder provides services to a client” and to be educated in the characteristics of the specific type of real estate being brokered.

Federal and State Law Relating to Conduct

A buyer asks you which neighbourhoods have 'families like ours' and names a protected characteristic. You say nothing in reply and simply send listings in two areas. Where is the risk?

22 TAC § 531.19 reaches a licence holder who inquires about, responds to, facilitates inquiries about, or discloses a preference based on the listed characteristics. Responding and facilitating are named alongside disclosing, so silence about the characteristic while acting on it is within the rule. 42 U.S.C. § 3604(a)-(c) and Property Code §§ 301.021-301.027 reach the underlying conduct, and Occ. Code § 1101.652(b)(32) makes it a ground for discipline.

The bands, section by section. That schedule is 22 TAC § 535.191, and it opens by confirming the

per violation per day," and it covers, among others, § 1101.652(a)(3), § 1101.652(a)(8),

§ 1101.652(b)(23), § 1101.652(b)(29), § 1101.652(b)(33), § 1101.652(b)(34), 22 TAC § 535.21, 22 TAC

§ 535.154, 22 TAC § 535.155 and 22 TAC § 535.157. The middle band is "An administrative penalty

Optional practice: Pricing a Galveston enforcement file

Optional. This is arithmetic practice against the schedule above — no required exercise in this

module asks you to compute an exposure — and no assessment is drawn from it.

An agent sponsored by a Galveston brokerage takes a listing on a beach house. Over the course of the

file the Commission ultimately finds four things. (i) On eleven consecutive days, the agent failed

to respond to the buyer's agent, in violation of 22 TAC § 535.157. (ii) The agent never provided the

IABS notice to the unrepresented buyer at first substantive communication, in violation of 22 TAC

§ 531.20. (iii) The agent deposited the buyer's $9,000 earnest money into her personal checking

account for six days before moving it to the title company, implicating § 1101.652(b)(10). (iv) The

agent told the buyer the seller would definitely replace the seawall before closing, when the

seller had refused, implicating § 1101.652(b)(5). The agent has one prior Commission order against

her from three years ago.

1. Using 22 TAC § 535.191, state the penalty band that applies to each of the four findings, and

quote the band language for each.

2. Compute the minimum and maximum administrative penalty for finding (i) alone, showing the

arithmetic and stating the per-violation-per-day rule you relied on.

3. Compute the maximum administrative penalty for findings (ii), (iii) and (iv) treating each as a

single violation on a single day, then apply the history-of-previous-violations provision in

§ 535.191 and recompute. State the ceiling that § 1101.702(a) places on your result and whether

your figure hits it.
