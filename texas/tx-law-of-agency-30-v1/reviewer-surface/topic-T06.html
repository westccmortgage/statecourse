Representing More Than One Party in a Transaction: Intermediary Brokerage

Topic 6 of 12 · 165 prescribed minutes

The Path from Dual Agency to Intermediary Brokerage

What Texas does when one brokerage firm ends up standing between a buyer and a seller who are both its

clients does not look like what most other states do, and the vocabulary Texas uses for it is statutory,

narrow, and unforgiving of approximation. If you learn one thing from this module, learn to stop saying

the words

dual agent about a Texas transaction. There is a correct word. It is intermediary, and it means

something specific.

Start with what agency actually promises. Section 1101.557(a) of the Occupations Code states the

rule that makes everything else in this module necessary: "A broker who represents a party in a real

estate transaction or who lists real estate for sale under an exclusive agreement for a party is that

party's agent." That is a status, not a courtesy. Once you are the party's agent, the Commission's

rules attach obligations that run in one direction only. Rule 531.2 — the Canon the Commission titles

Fidelity — makes the license holder a fiduciary whose "primary duty ... is to represent the interests

of the client," whose position "should be clear to all parties concerned in a real estate transaction,"

and who must "place no personal interest above that of the client." Rule 535.156 adds the information

duty: the license holder must convey to the principal all known information which would affect the

principal's decision on whether or not to make, accept or reject offers — subject to the written

no-further-offers exception the rule itself states — and must put the principal's interest above the

license holder's own. Module 02 teaches the fiduciary Canons and Rule 535.156 in full, with their

qualifiers.

Put those together and you have the promise a Texas agency relationship makes. Everything you learn

about the other side, you tell your client. Everything your client tells you that would hurt him, you

keep. His interest outranks yours and outranks the other side's. You are not neutral; you are his.

Now try to make that promise twice, to two people who want opposite things. Suppose you list a

house on Chisholm Trail in Round Rock at $415,000. The seller, over coffee at the listing appointment,

tells you the truth every listing agent hears eventually: she has already closed on a house in

Georgetown, she is carrying two notes, and she will take $392,000 rather than carry a third month. That

number — $392,000 — is now yours to protect. If you leak it, you have cost her twenty-three thousand

dollars and you have breached the most basic thing a client buys when she signs a listing.

Three weeks later a buyer walks into your open house. He likes the property. He is pre-approved to

$430,000 and he tells you, because he trusts you, that he would go to $421,000 for this house before he

would lose it. Suppose you now purport to represent him too, in the full sense — as his agent, with

the same duties. His interest is that you tell him the seller will take $392,000. Her interest is that

you never say it. His interest is that you keep his $421,000 ceiling secret. Her interest is that you

disclose it. You now have two principals, each entitled to information you are simultaneously obliged to

withhold from the other.

This is not a hard case. It is an impossible case, and the impossibility is structural rather than

ethical: no version of "advise the buyer to offer $395,000" is loyal to the seller, and no version of

"advise the seller to hold at $415,000" is loyal to the buyer. Whatever you do next, you breach a duty

to somebody. The only question is which one.

What dual agency was, as a concept. The concept the unit title names — one licensee, two

principals, full agency duties to each, made lawful by consent — is the arrangement some states permit

and label dual agency. The theory is that the conflict is curable by disclosure: tell both parties

they will get a diminished form of representation, obtain their informed agreement, and proceed. Even

in the states that allow it, the practical result is that the agent's duties to each party are cut back

to something much thinner than ordinary agency, because a duty that cannot be performed for both cannot

be owed to both. The consent does not dissolve the conflict. It just relocates the loss onto the

parties.

What Texas law actually says. Here is the finding you should take from the statute itself. Search

Chapter 1101 of the Occupations Code — the Real Estate License Act — and you will not find the words

dual agent or dual agency anywhere in it. There is no subchapter defining the role, no section

authorizing it, no consent form the Commission prescribes for it, and no set of reduced duties assigned

to it. **A Texas license holder may not act as a dual agent, because Texas law does not create the

capacity.** Representation in Texas is a status conferred by § 1101.557(a) and carrying the full

fiduciary content Rule 531.2 and Rule 535.156 describe; the statute never provides a diluted second

version of it that a licensee could occupy toward two adverse parties at once.

What the Legislature provided instead is a different role with a different name and different rules.

Section 1101.551(1), the definitions section for Subchapter L of the Act, provides that an intermediary

is "a broker who is employed to negotiate a transaction between the parties to a transaction and for

that purpose may act as an agent of the parties." Read that with care, because the drafting is

deliberate. The intermediary is employed to negotiate a transaction between the parties. The object

of the employment is the transaction, not the victory of one side over the other. And the intermediary

"may act as an agent of the parties" — plural, jointly, in the capacity Subchapter L defines, not as a

separate full agent of each.

Then comes the provision that closes the door on any alternative. Section 1101.561(b): "A broker must

agree to act as an intermediary under this subchapter if the broker agrees to represent in a

transaction: (1) a buyer or tenant; and (2) a seller or landlord." The verb is must. If your broker

takes on both sides of a transaction, the intermediary posture is not one option among several. It is

the only lawful posture the Act supplies, and choosing anything else is a violation of a statute that

22 TAC § 535.191 places in the Commission's highest administrative-penalty band — $1,000 to $5,000 per

violation per day.

Do not confuse "dual capacity" with dual agency. Chapter 1101 does use the word "dual" once in a

disciplinary context, and students trip over it every year. Section 1101.652(b)(16) makes it grounds

for suspension or revocation if a license holder, while engaged in real estate brokerage, "acts in a

dual capacity as broker and undisclosed principal in a real estate transaction." That subdivision is

about a licensee who is secretly a party to the deal — buying for herself through a nominee, taking a

hidden interest in the property — not about a licensee representing two sides. The vice there is the

concealed self-interest, which is why Rule 535.144 — taught in Module 12 — makes a license holder who

deals on her own account, or for close family, a business entity she owns more than ten percent of, or

a trust she serves or benefits from, disclose her licence status in writing. Different

problem, different rule. Section 1101.652(b)(16) is not the Texas dual-agency prohibition, because

Texas does not need one: the capacity was never created.

The two disciplinary hooks that do the real work. Two subdivisions of § 1101.652(b) reach a

licensee who muddles the sides: (b)(7), failing to make clear to all parties the party for whom the

license holder is acting, and (b)(8), receiving compensation from more than one party without the full

knowledge and consent of all parties. Notice how general they are. Neither requires proof that anybody

was cheated out of money; subdivision (7) is violated by ambiguity itself, so if a buyer at your open

house cannot tell whose agent you are, you have already supplied the element. Section 1101.652 as a

whole, and the penalty schedule in 22 TAC § 535.191, are taught in Module 10; the last unit of this

module returns to these two subdivisions where the exposure is the point.

When the intermediary provisions entered Texas law. The held text of Chapter 1101 records the

enactment history at the foot of each section. Sections 1101.559, 1101.560 and 1101.561 were each

"Added by Acts 2001, 77th Leg., ch. 1421, Sec. 2, eff. June 1, 2003" — the act that codified the Real

Estate License Act into the Occupations Code. Section 1101.561 was then "Amended by: Acts 2005, 79th

Leg., Ch. 825 (S.B. 810), Sec. 8, eff. September 1, 2005," and § 1101.557 was amended by section 7 of

that same 2005 act. The legislative history of the intermediary concept before that codification — the

amendments to the former Real Estate License Act in the 1990s that first introduced it — was not held

for this course, and this module therefore does not narrate it. What the held statute does establish is

that the framework you are about to study has been settled Texas law in its codified form since June 1,

2003, with the mandatory-intermediary sentence in § 1101.561(b) reaching its current form by

September 1, 2005.

One more piece of vocabulary that just disappeared. You may have heard an experienced licensee

speak of subagency. The definition at § 1101.002(8) now reads only "Repealed by Acts 2025, 89th Leg.,

R.S., Ch. 1172 (S.B. 1968), Sec. 13(1), eff. January 1, 2026," and the words subagent and subagency

appear nowhere in the statutory text or the Commission rules held for this course. Module 01 owns

§ 1101.002. The point for this module is that the menu of relationships you can lawfully name in a Texas

transaction has gotten shorter, not longer, and the intermediary occupies the middle of it.

Why the Legislature's answer is better than consent-cured dual agency. Consider what the

intermediary framework does that a diluted double agency does not. First, it moves the role up to the

broker. Section 1101.551(1) says the intermediary is "a broker," and Subchapter L never says otherwise.

The individual who has been sitting in the seller's kitchen is not the one asked to hold two loyalties

in one head. Second, it converts confidentiality from a judgment call into a rule list: § 1101.651(d)

tells the intermediary exactly which four things may not be said and exactly what it takes to unlock

each of them. Third, it permits the brokerage to restore genuine advice to each party through

appointments under § 1101.560, without pretending that any one human being is advocating for both.

Fourth, it makes the arrangement consensual in advance and in writing, with the compensation source

disclosed, under § 1101.559(a). And fifth, § 1101.561(a) removes the trap that would otherwise destroy

the whole scheme: "The duties of a license holder acting as an intermediary under this subchapter

supersede the duties of a license holder established under any other law, including common law."

Without that sentence, a Texas broker acting as an intermediary would be obeying a statute and breaching

a fiduciary standard at the same moment. With it, the statute governs.

Topic quiz

Every question must be answered correctly before the next topic opens. An incorrect answer shows the reasoning and then serves a different, related question on the same rule.

Question 1 of 5

A student finds § 1101.652(b)(16), which reaches a license holder who “acts in a dual capacity as broker and undisclosed principal in a real estate transaction,” and concludes that this is where Texas prohibits dual agency. What is wrong with that reading?

Subdivision (16) reaches concealed self-interest — buying through a nominee, taking a hidden interest — which is why 22 TAC § 535.144 requires a license holder dealing on her own account or for close family, an entity she owns more than ten per cent of, or certain trusts to disclose her licence status in writing. Different problem, different rule. Texas has no dual-agency prohibition because Chapter 1101 never creates the capacity: representation is the status § 1101.557(a) confers, carrying the full fiduciary content of 22 TAC §§ 531.2 and 535.156, and the Act supplies no diluted second version of it. What it supplies instead is the intermediary, defined at § 1101.551(1) as a broker.

Question 2 of 5

A brokerage is acting as intermediary on a Round Rock listing at $415,000. The seller told her agent at the listing appointment that she would take $392,000. The seller's appointed agent asks whether she may tell the buyer's side “there is room to move.” What does § 1101.651(d)(1) permit?

Section 1101.651(d)(1) forbids a broker, and any broker or sales agent appointed under § 1101.560, to “disclose to the buyer or tenant that the seller or landlord will accept a price less than the asking price, unless otherwise instructed in a separate writing by the seller or landlord.” The prohibition is on communicating the substance rather than on uttering a particular sentence, so “she's motivated” and “I'd come in under list” are disclosures dressed up in words. The qualifier is the release mechanism and it is exact: a separate writing from that party. And the opening words of subsection (d) bind the appointees as well as the broker, so an appointment does not free anyone from subdivisions (1) through (5).

Question 3 of 5

Two sales agents at the same firm agree between themselves that each will “just work our own client” on an in-house transaction. Both parties' representation agreements authorise intermediary status but say nothing about appointments. Apply § 1101.560(b).

Section 1101.560(b) is a closed gate: a license holder may be appointed “only if” the written consent of the parties under § 1101.559 authorises the broker to make the appointment and the broker provides written notice of the appointment to all parties involved in the real estate transaction. The consent must therefore do two distinct jobs — authorise intermediary status and authorise appointments — and the second is not implied by the first. The notice is the broker's to give, must be written, and goes to all parties rather than only to the party whose appointee is named; each side is entitled to know who has been appointed on the other. A sales agent cannot appoint herself or a colleague.

Question 4 of 5

Priya holds a broker licence, works alone in Fredericksburg with no sponsored license holders, and ends up with both the seller of a cottage and a buyer for it, each under a written agreement authorising intermediary status and appointments. What configuration is lawfully available to her?

Section 1101.561(b) applies to a sole practitioner exactly as to a large firm, so on both parties' written consent under § 1101.559(a) she may — and must — act as intermediary. What she cannot do is appoint: § 1101.560(a) is written in terms of a broker appointing a license holder associated with the broker for one party and another such license holder for any other party, and appointing herself to both would reproduce the undivided-loyalty impossibility the subchapter exists to avoid. So the honest configuration is intermediary without appointments: fair and impartial under § 1101.559(c), silent under § 1101.651(d), still bound by § 1101.557(b) and 22 TAC § 535.157, and advising neither party on price or strategy.

Question 5 of 5

Hosting your own brokerage's open house, you confirm the square footage and the HOA dues to an unrepresented visitor, then tell him the sellers “need to be done by the end of the month,” suggest he offer $318,000 because “they'll take it,” and help him fill in an option fee and a closing date. Which of those acts stayed inside what the law permits?

Section 1101.562(c) is a short list — size, price and terms — and the verb is confirm. Telling the other side your seller's timetable hands over leverage and, if your broker were an intermediary or you an appointed agent, § 1101.651(d)(1) and (d)(3) would forbid it outright with only a separate writing, a legal requirement or court order, or property-condition information as exits; a moving date is none of those. Advising on the offer price is advice regarding the real property, and filling in the option fee and closing date is another act of brokerage for him, so both fall outside § 1101.562(a)(3) and (a)(4). The general risk is § 1101.652(b)(7): ambiguity about whom you are acting for is itself the violation.

Intentional versus Unintended Dual Representation

Your broker has the listing and you have brought the buyer. Both parties are willing to sign whatever is needed. What capacity may the broker occupy?

The words dual agent and dual agency appear nowhere in Chapter 1101 or in any Commission rule held for this course. Texas did not permit the capacity and then regulate it; the capacity does not exist here. Section 1101.559 lets a broker act as an intermediary on the parties' written consent, § 1101.560 allows appointment of associated licence holders, and § 1101.561(b) makes the intermediary posture mandatory when the broker acts for both. Withdrawal is not required.
