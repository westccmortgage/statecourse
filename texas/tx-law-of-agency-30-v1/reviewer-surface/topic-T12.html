The Broker Working for/with the Seller

Everything you have studied so far in this course has been law in the abstract: what agency is, whom

you owe duties to, what the Commission may do to you. This module is the same law arranged

differently. It is arranged as a week of work. You will walk a seller from the moment the phone

rings to the moment the funding wire lands, and at every step you will be asked the same question a

Commission investigator asks: *what required you to do that, and where is the paper that proves you

did it?*

Start with a concrete engagement so the sequence has something to attach to. A homeowner in

Pflugerville emails your brokerage's website form: three-bedroom, two-bath, built in 2004, they

think it is worth about 385,000 dollars, they want to know what you would charge. You reply and set

an appointment for Thursday evening. That single email exchange has already put two obligations in

motion, and most new license holders miss both.

Step one, before the appointment: the IABS. Occupations Code § 1101.558(b-1) requires written

notice — the Information About Brokerage Services form, which 22 TAC § 531.20 adopts by reference —

at the license holder's "first substantive communication with a party relating to a proposed

transaction regarding specific real property"; § 1101.558(b) separately requires you to disclose

whom you represent at the time of first contact with another party or another party's license

holder. Module 02 teaches both provisions, the four permitted delivery methods, and the three

situations § 1101.558(c) excepts.

What is yours in this module is when in the appointment the notice is delivered, and the answer is

that it is delivered before the appointment. The statute does not define "first substantive

communication", and this course holds no source that defines it, so the safe habit has to do the

work the definition would: the Pflugerville email named a specific property and discussed a proposed

transaction, so treat your reply as the trigger and attach the completed IABS to it. Two field

habits lose that bet — handing over a blank form on arrival at the appointment, and relying on a

permanent IABS link in an email signature block, a shortcut § 531.20 forecloses by name.

The second thing you owe from the outset is the Consumer Protection Notice: 22 TAC § 531.18 requires

it displayed in each place of business and linked on the homepage of each business website, and

§ 531.20 imposes the parallel homepage-link duty for the IABS; Module 08 teaches both as documents.

In the sequence, the point is that the links go up on the day your page goes live, not on the day

someone asks.

Step two, at the appointment: the price opinion. You will be asked what the house is worth.

Answering that question is regulated, it is regulated in two directions at once, and 22 TAC § 535.17

is this module's own rule — the one to know cold.

The outer boundary: "A real estate license holder may not perform an appraisal of, or provide an

opinion of value for, real property unless the license holder is licensed or certified under Texas

Occupations Code, Chapter 1103." You are not an appraiser. What you may prepare instead is

brokerage — Occ. Code § 1101.002(1)(A)(xi) brings within the definition of a broker the provision of

"a written analysis, opinion, or conclusion relating to the estimated price of real property" that

is not referred to as an appraisal, is provided in the ordinary course of the person's business, and

relates to the actual or potential management, acquisition, disposition or encumbrance of an

interest in real property. A comparative market analysis is brokerage. An appraisal is not.

Because the two products look alike to a consumer, § 535.17 requires you to say so in writing. If

you provide "a broker price opinion, comparative market analysis, or estimated worth or sale price

under the Act," you "shall also provide the person for whom the opinion, analysis, or estimate is

prepared with a written statement containing the following language: 'This represents an estimated

sale price for this property. It is not the same as the opinion of value in an appraisal developed

by a licensed appraiser under the Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice.'" And the

rule tells you how the sentence must appear: it "must be made part of any written opinion, analysis,

or estimate of worth or sale price and must be reproduced verbatim in at least 12-point font."

Three practical consequences follow. First, "verbatim" means verbatim — you may not tidy the

sentence, shorten it, or paraphrase it into your own listing-presentation voice. Second, "made part

of" means it belongs inside the CMA document, not in a separate email. Third, 12-point is a floor,

and a CMA laid out in 9-point body text with the disclaimer matched to the body text violates the

rule even though the words are right.

One more clause of § 535.17 matters enormously to a sales agent: "A sales agent may prepare, sign,

and present a broker price opinion, comparative market analysis, or estimate of worth or sale price

for the sales agent's sponsoring broker, but the sales agent must submit the broker price opinion,

comparative market analysis, or estimate of worth or sale price in the broker's name and the broker

is responsible for it." You may build it and you may present it. It goes out in the broker's name —

the same principle that puts listings themselves in the broker's name under 22 TAC § 535.2, taught

in Module 09.

The CMA is not a courtesy you may skip. 22 TAC § 535.16, taught in Module 04, obliges a license

holder to provide a BPO or CMA "when negotiating a listing or offering to purchase the property for

the license holder's own account as a result of contact made while acting as a real estate agent."

For Thursday evening, the consequence is that the Pflugerville CMA is compulsory and must carry the

§ 535.17 statement inside it. The second half of that rule — the anti-self-dealing half — is taken

up in Unit 4.

Step three: the listing agreement. Four rules taught elsewhere in the course meet on this one

page, and the sequence is what makes them a single act rather than four.

It is solicited and accepted in the broker's name: 22 TAC § 535.2, Module 09. It must specify a

definite termination date that is not subject to prior notice, because § 1101.652(b)(12) makes the

absence of one a ground for discipline (Module 10) — an open-ended listing "until sold" is a

disciplinary problem, not a marketing preference. It is ordinarily where the owner's written consent

to place a sign lives, § 1101.652(b)(18) making a sign without that written consent a ground for

discipline; if you are marketing under an arrangement that is not a signed listing, get the sign

consent in writing separately, at the same appointment. And a listing contract form adopted by the

Commission must under § 1101.155(c) inform the parties that commissions are negotiable and explain

the availability of Texas coastal natural hazards information where that is appropriate.

Net listings are constrained by 22 TAC § 535.16, taught in Module 04, and are available only on the

conditions that rule sets. In everyday residential practice the honest advice is to decline, and to

say why — the arrangement pays you more the worse you do for your client.

Nothing about the writing is a formality. Section 1101.806(c) bars any action to recover a

commission unless the agreement, or a memorandum of it, is in writing and signed by the party

against whom the action is brought, and § 1101.806(b) requires you to allege and prove you held a

licence "at the time the act was commenced"; Module 04 teaches both, with the parallel requirement

Business & Commerce Code § 26.01 puts on the sale contract. In the sequence, the consequence is

blunt. A seller who shook your hand, watched you spend 4,000 dollars on staging and then closed with

a neighbour owes you nothing you can sue for, and a licence that lapses mid-listing is a

compensation catastrophe rather than a renewal problem.

Step four, the same evening: the seller's disclosure notice. Property Code § 5.008 requires a

seller of residential real property comprising not more than one dwelling unit to give the purchaser

the prescribed written notice or one substantially similar to it. Module 03 teaches the notice, its

content and its exemptions. What belongs here is which step of the listing workflow it is.

Get it completed before the photographs and before the sign. Section 5.008(f) requires delivery "on

or before the effective date of an executory contract binding the purchaser to purchase the

property," and provides that where a contract is entered without it, "the purchaser may terminate

the contract for any reason within seven days after receiving the notice." A listing agent who

leaves the notice until after an offer is accepted has handed the buyer a free option on the deal.

Two things you do at the kitchen table. It is the seller's notice: you obtain it, you deliver it,

and you never fill it in for them. And when the seller stalls over a question — the age of the roof,

the date of the last leak — tell them what § 5.008(d) says, that where the information is unknown to

the seller, the seller "shall indicate that fact on the notice, and by that act is in compliance

with this section." "Unknown" is a lawful answer. A guess is not, and a guess that turns out wrong

becomes the buyer's misrepresentation claim and, where you knew better, your § 1101.652(b)(3) and

(b)(4) problem as well.

Step five: marketing the listing. The moment you publish anything about the Pflugerville house,

22 TAC § 535.155 applies. Its scope is deliberately enormous. An "Advertisement" is "any form of

communication by or on behalf of a license holder designed to attract the public to use real estate

brokerage services and includes, but is not limited to, all publications, brochures, radio or

television broadcasts, all electronic media including email, text messages, social media, the

Internet, business stationery, business cards, displays, signs and billboards." The rule carves out

only two things: "a communication from a license holder to the license holder's current client; and

a directional sign that may also contain only the broker's name or logo."

Every advertisement must include, "in a readily noticeable location in the advertisement", two

items: "the name of the license holder or team placing the advertisement" and "the broker's name in

at least half the size of the largest contact information for any sales agent, associated broker, or

team name contained in the advertisement." That is a size ratio, not a mere presence requirement,

and "Contact Information" is broadly defined as "any information that can be used to contact a

license holder featured in the advertisement, including a name, phone number, email address, website

address, social media handle, scan code or other similar information." If your Instagram tile

carries your handle in 60-point type, the broker's name must be at least 30-point. For social media

and text, the rule allows the required information to sit "on a separate page or on the account user

profile page of the license holder," but only if that page is "readily accessible by a direct link

from the social media or text" and "readily noticeable on the separate page or in the account user

profile."

The rule then lists, "For purposes of this section and §1101.652(b)(23) of the Act," the

advertisements that mislead or are likely to deceive. Several of them are ordinary marketing habits.

An advertisement violates the rule if it is "inaccurate in any material fact or representation"; if

it "identifies a sales agent as a broker"; if it "uses a title, such as owner, president, CEO, COO,

or other similar title, email or website address that implies a sales agent is responsible for the

operations of a brokerage"; if it "contains the name of a sales agent that is not the name as shown

on the sales agent's license issued by the Commission or an alternate name registered with the

Commission"; if it "creates confusion regarding the permitted use of a property"; if it is "about

the value of a property, unless it is based on an appraisal that is disclosed and readily available

upon request by a party or it is given in compliance with §535.17"; or if it "implies the person

making the advertisement was involved in a transaction regarding a property when the person had no

such role".

Three of the listed items are pure timing rules, and they catch careful people. An advertisement

violates the rule if it is "about a property that is subject to an exclusive listing agreement

without the permission of the listing broker and without disclosing the name of the listing broker

unless the listing broker has expressly agreed in writing to waive disclosure"; if it is "offering a

listed property that is not discontinued within 10 days after the listing agreement is no longer in

effect"; or if it is "about a property 10 days or more after the closing of a transaction unless the

current status of the property is included in the advertisement". So when your Pflugerville listing

expires, you have ten days to take down the sign, the landing page, the boosted post and the

syndicated feed. When it closes, your "Just sold!" post is fine for ten days, and after that it

needs the current status included.

Step six: presenting offers. 22 TAC § 535.156, taught in Module 02, requires the license holder

to convey to the principal all known information affecting the principal's decision whether to make,

accept or reject offers, subject to one escape: a written agreement by the principal that offers are

not to be submitted after the principal has entered into a contract.

For the working week the default is unconditional — every offer goes to the seller, on the day it

arrives. There is no oral version of the exception. If your Pflugerville seller does not want back-up

offers presented after going under contract, that instruction is signed and in the file before the

first offer arrives, not negotiated afterwards.

Two authority limits govern how you negotiate, both grounds for discipline under § 1101.652 and

taught in Module 10: you may not offer to sell or lease the property without the owner's knowledge

and consent, § 1101.652(b)(19), and you may not offer it on terms the owner has not authorised,

§ 1101.652(b)(20). Between them they dispose of the common temptation to tell a buyer's agent that

"they'd probably take 375."

Step seven: closing. Two items belong to the seller's agent at the end. The first is money: any

earnest money or other trust money that reaches the brokerage is handled under 22 TAC § 535.146, and

Unit 2 walks the handling in sequence because the buyer's side usually delivers it. The second is

compensation, which § 1101.651 confines to license holders and to out-of-state brokers who conduct

none of the negotiations in Texas, and which reaches a sales agent only through the sponsoring

broker; Module 09 teaches § 1101.651 and 22 TAC § 535.3.

Topic quiz

Every question must be answered correctly before the next topic opens. An incorrect answer shows the reasoning and then serves a different, related question on the same rule.

Question 1 of 5

A sales agent prepares a CMA for a Pflugerville listing appointment. She tidies the Commission's prescribed sentence into her own words, sets it in the same 9-point type as the body of the document, and emails it under her own name. Which of her three decisions comply with 22 TAC § 535.17?

22 TAC § 535.17 requires a license holder who provides a broker price opinion, comparative market analysis or estimated worth or sale price to provide the person it is prepared for with a written statement containing the language the rule sets out, and requires that the statement “be made part of any written opinion, analysis, or estimate of worth or sale price and must be reproduced verbatim in at least 12-point font.” Verbatim means verbatim, “made part of” means inside the document rather than in a covering email, and 12-point is a floor. The same rule permits a sales agent to prepare, sign and present the analysis but requires it to be submitted in the broker's name, with the broker responsible for it.

Question 2 of 5

An unrepresented buyer signs an offer on your listing at the kitchen table. You never give him anything in writing about an abstract or a title policy. Apply § 1101.555 and § 1101.806(d).

Section 1101.555 provides that when an offer to purchase real estate in this state is signed, “a license holder shall advise each buyer, in writing,” either to have the abstract examined by an attorney chosen by the buyer or to be provided with or obtain a title insurance policy. Three points: the trigger is signature of the offer, not acceptance; it runs to each buyer by a license holder, without regard to whom the license holder represents; and it must be written. Section 1101.652(b)(29) states the same requirement as a disciplinary ground phrased around the closing, and § 1101.806(d) attaches the money penalty — a one-sentence advisory, given at signature and kept in the file, protects the entire fee.

Question 3 of 5

A listing expires unsold in September 2026 and the sales agent, changing phones, lets a messaging app delete her negotiation texts with the cooperating agent after thirty days. Apply 22 TAC § 535.2(h).

22 TAC § 535.2(h) requires the broker to maintain the listed records “in a format that is readily available to the Commission for at least four years from the date of closing, termination of the contract, or end of a real estate transaction” — three triggers, so a listing that expires unsold starts its own clock that day. One of the eight categories is “substantive communications with parties to the transaction,” which is where the texts and emails live; an app that deletes them is a decision not to keep records the broker is required to hold. The only excuse the rule recognises is destruction by an Act of God such as a natural disaster or fire not intentionally caused by the broker, and the four years line up with the Commission's four-year complaint window in § 1101.204(c).

Question 4 of 5

You want to buy a San Marcos duplex through an LLC in which you hold 35 percent, from an unrepresented owner you met while previewing the property for a client. Apply 22 TAC § 535.144.

22 TAC § 535.144 begins by expanding who counts, providing that for purposes of § 1101.652(a-1)(1) “license holder” includes a license holder acting on behalf of a spouse, parent or child, a business entity in which the license holder is more than a 10% owner, or a trust for which the license holder acts as trustee or of which the license holder or close family is a beneficiary. Thirty-five percent is well past that threshold. The duty is then to “disclose in writing that he or she is a licensed real estate broker or sales agent acting on his or her own behalf or in a capacity described by subsection (a) in any contract of sale or rental agreement or in any other writing given before entering into any contract of sale or rental agreement.” The rule also forbids using your expertise to the disadvantage of a person with whom you deal.

Question 5 of 5

A licensee's violation continued unremedied over several weeks before she corrected it and told the Commission what she had done. What does § 1101.702 say about how that affects the penalty?

Section 1101.702(a) sets the ceiling: “The amount of an administrative penalty may not exceed $5,000 for each violation. Each day a violation continues or occurs may be considered a separate violation for purposes of imposing a penalty.” Per violation, with a per-day multiplier available, which is why a problem left running is not one violation. Subsection (b) then lists the five factors the executive director must weigh — the seriousness of the violation including the nature, circumstances, extent and gravity of the prohibited acts; the history of previous violations; the amount necessary to deter a future violation; efforts to correct the violation; and any other matter that justice may require. Correcting promptly is statutorily relevant to what you pay.

Other Considerations

You and two colleagues start calling yourselves a team and put the name on signs and a website. Your sponsoring broker approves. What else does the rule require?

22 TAC § 535.154 requires an alternate, team or assumed business name used in advertising to be registered with the Commission before use, and § 535.155 requires advertising to include the name of the broker. A broker's approval is necessary and is not sufficient. No separate entity licence is created by using a team name.

Optional practice: A Saturday in Cedar Park

At 9:40 on a Saturday morning a stranger calls your cell. She saw your sign at 2203 Silverstone

Drive in Cedar Park, a single-family house you have listed for the seller at 419,000 dollars. She

asks to see it at 11:00. She is not represented. At 11:00 she asks you three questions: how big is

the house, is 419,000 a fair price, and would the seller take 400,000.

1. Before you open the door, what must you deliver and what must you sign? Cite § 1101.558(b), §

1101.563(b) and, if you were not the listing agent, § 1101.558(b-1) and 22 TAC § 531.20, and say

which apply on these facts and which do not. 2. She does not want a representation agreement. May

you show the house under a showing-only agreement? State the two limits § 1101.563(e) places on such

an agreement. 3. Answer each of her three questions consistently with § 1101.562(c) and §

1101.562(a)(3). Which may you answer, which may you not, and what exactly does subsection (c) permit

you to do? 4. She decides to write an offer. You are the listing agent and your broker also intends

to represent her. State what § 1101.561(b) requires of the broker and what § 1101.559(a) requires

the written consent to contain. 5. She signs the offer at 4:15 that afternoon. State the written

advisory § 1101.555 requires, and state the consequence under § 1101.806(d) if you never give it.
