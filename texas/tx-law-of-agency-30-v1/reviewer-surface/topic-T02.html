Basic Agency Relationships, Disclosure and Duties to the Client

Topic 2 of 12 · 125 prescribed minutes

Agency Defined

You are about to spend a career being called an agent. Before you accept the word, you need to

understand what it costs you, because in Texas the word is not a job title. It is a legal status

that attaches duties to you the moment it arises, whether or not you meant it to arise, whether or

not you were paid, and whether or not anyone signed anything. Most of the discipline cases you will

read about over the next thirty years come down to a license holder who did not understand — or did

not stop to think about — whose agent he was at the moment he opened his mouth.

Start with the ordinary meaning and then let the statute correct it. In ordinary speech, an "agent"

is someone who does things for someone else: a travel agent, an insurance agent, a talent agent.

That intuition is roughly right and legally useless, because it tells you nothing about the

consequences. The legal idea of agency is that one person, the principal, authorises another person,

the agent, to act on the principal's behalf in dealings with third persons — and that because the

agent is acting for the principal rather than for himself, the law loads the agent with obligations

far heavier than the ordinary obligations of one stranger to another. That is the whole engine of

this course. Everything else is detail.

Texas does not define "agency." It defines when a broker is a party's agent. Chapter 1101 of

the Occupations Code — the Real Estate License Act, which this course will call TRELA — contains no

general definition of the word "agency" in the sections held for this course, and no definition of

"fiduciary" either. What it contains instead is an operative rule that tells you when the status

exists. Section 1101.557(a) provides: "A broker who represents a party in a real estate transaction

or who lists real estate for sale under an exclusive agreement for a party is that party's agent."

Read that sentence three times. It is the closest thing Texas statutory law gives you to a

definition of the agency relationship in brokerage, and almost every word in it is load-bearing.

First, notice the subject. The sentence is about a broker. It is not about you, if you are

studying for a sales agent licence. TRELA's architecture is that the broker is the agent of the

party and the sales agent is the agent of the broker. Section 1101.002(7) defines a sales agent as

"a person who is sponsored by a licensed broker for the purpose of performing an act described by

Subdivision (1)" — Subdivision (1) being the long list of brokerage acts in the definition of

"broker." Section 1101.351(c) drives it home: "A licensed sales agent may not engage or attempt to

engage in real estate brokerage unless the sales agent is sponsored by a licensed broker and is

acting for that broker." And § 1101.002(4) defines a license holder as "a broker or sales agent

licensed under this chapter," which is why the Commission's rules so often speak to "license

holders" generally when a duty falls on both. When a seller in Amarillo signs a listing agreement,

the client is the broker's client. You service that client, you owe that client the duties this

module will spend forty minutes on, and you can be disciplined personally for breaching them — but

the representation relationship the statute recognises is between the seller and the broker.

Second, notice the two triggers, joined by "or." A broker is a party's agent if the broker

represents a party in a real estate transaction, or if the broker **lists real estate for sale

under an exclusive agreement** for a party. Those are not the same thing. The second trigger is

mechanical: sign an exclusive listing, and the agency status follows from the document without

further inquiry. The first trigger is behavioural. "Represents" is not defined in § 1101.557, and

the statute does not say the representation must be in writing. It can arise from what you actually

do.

That reading is not a guess; the Legislature confirmed it in 2026 when it wrote § 1101.562, which

lets a broker show property without representing a party only if the broker, among other things,

"has not agreed with the party, either orally or in writing, to represent the party" and "is not

otherwise acting as the party's agent at the time of showing the real property." A statute that must

exclude oral agreement and otherwise acting as agent in order to create a safe harbour is a

statute that assumes both of those can create agency in the first place. (Section 1101.562 itself

is taught in Module 05; what it is doing here is telling you how wide § 1101.557's word "represents"

is.) Undertake to advise a buyer

at an open house in Cedar Park about what she should offer, and you have not merely been friendly.

You have arguably begun to represent her, with everything that follows.

What agency is not. Three confusions are worth killing now.

It is not the same as being paid. Compensation and representation are separate questions in Texas,

and the 2026 amendments make the separation explicit: § 1101.563(c) requires a written agreement

with a residential buyer to state the license holder's representation status and the broker's

compensation as two separate items, and to disclose in conspicuous language that compensation is not

set by law and is fully negotiable. Module 05 teaches § 1101.563 in full. If who pays determined

who was represented, the statute would not need two separate line items. It does. A seller may agree

to contribute to a buyer's broker's fee without that broker becoming the seller's agent, and a

listing broker does not become the buyer's agent by sharing a commission.

It is not the same as having a licence. A licence is permission from the State to perform the acts

listed in § 1101.002(1) for compensation. Agency is a relationship with a particular person. You can

hold a licence for a year and never once be anyone's agent; you can become someone's agent in a

single afternoon conversation.

And it is not the same as friendliness, cooperation, or access. Section 1101.551(2) defines "party"

for the practice subchapter as "a prospective buyer, seller, landlord, or tenant or an authorized

representative of a buyer, seller, landlord, or tenant, including a trustee, guardian, executor,

administrator, receiver, or attorney-in-fact. The term does not include a license holder who

represents a party." That last sentence matters more than students expect. The agent on the other

side of your deal is not a "party." You do not owe the other broker the duties you owe your

principal, and you cannot discharge a duty owed to a party by telling that party's broker something

and assuming it landed.

The consequence of the status: you become a fiduciary. The moment agency exists, Texas loads it.

The Commission's rule on fidelity, 22 TAC § 531.2, opens: "A license holder, while acting as an

agent for another, is a fiduciary. Special obligations are imposed when such fiduciary relationships

are created." The Commission's rule on dishonesty says the same thing from the other end: "A license

holder's relationship with the license holder's principal is that of a fiduciary" (22 TAC §

535.156). The broker responsibility rule states the same standard at its highest register and

attaches a qualifier — the duty to convey information to the principal runs "unless prohibited by

other law" (22 TAC § 535.2(b)) — because there are things you learn that other law forbids you to

pass on. Unit 4 returns to it; Module 09 teaches 22 TAC § 535.2.

Chapter 1101 does not itself use the word "client"; it uses "party" and "principal." The industry

vocabulary of client (the person you represent) versus customer (the person you deal with but do

not represent) is practice terminology, not statutory terminology, though the Commission's own rules

do use "client": § 531.2 speaks of representing "the interests of the client." Use the words

carefully in front of consumers, and understand that the legal question is never what you called

someone. It is whether you represented them.

Formation, and the limits of what this course can tell you. The common-law elements of forming

an agency relationship — the doctrines of express appointment, implied appointment by conduct,

estoppel and ratification — are matters of Texas case law. **Texas case law was not held for this

course**, so this module will not state those elements or their tests. What is held, and therefore

what you may rely on, is this: an exclusive listing creates the status by its terms under §

1101.557(a); representation can exist without a signed writing, as the structure of § 1101.562(a)(1)

presupposes; and as of January 1, 2026, a license holder performing brokerage acts for a prospective

residential buyer must have a written agreement before showing property, under § 1101.563(b),

which is discussed in Unit 3.

Finally, understand who answers for what you do. Section 1101.803 makes a licensed broker liable to

the Commission, the public and the broker's clients for any conduct engaged in under the chapter by

the broker or by a sales agent associated with or acting for the broker; Module 09 teaches it. Your

broker is on the hook for your conduct. That is not a reason to relax. It is the reason your broker will

supervise you, and it is the reason the next unit — where your authority actually comes from — is

not an abstraction.

Question 1 of 5

An Amarillo seller signs an exclusive listing negotiated at her kitchen table by a sales agent she has known for years. She tells a neighbour that the sales agent is “my agent.” Under § 1101.557(a) and the definitions that surround it, whose agent is the seller's agent?

Section 1101.557(a) is written about a broker: “A broker who represents a party in a real estate transaction or who lists real estate for sale under an exclusive agreement for a party is that party's agent.” Section 1101.002(7) then defines a sales agent as a person “sponsored by a licensed broker for the purpose of performing an act described by Subdivision (1),” and § 1101.351(c) requires the sales agent to be sponsored and to be acting for that broker. You service the client and can be disciplined personally, but the representation the statute recognises is between the seller and the broker — which is also why § 1101.803 puts your conduct on the broker's licence.

Question 2 of 5

A broker's written notice to a sponsored sales agent limits her authorised activities to residential resale. The broker later tells her by telephone not to touch commercial leases, then watches her run three of them over six months without objecting. A complaint arrives out of the third. Apply the three sentences of 22 TAC § 535.2(a).

22 TAC § 535.2(a) carries three rules in three sentences. The broker must notify a sponsored sales agent in writing of the scope of authorised activities. The broker is responsible for the authorised acts “[u]nless such scope is limited or revoked in writing” — so an oral instruction does not narrow the written scope for this rule's purposes. And “[i]f a broker permits a sponsored sales agent to conduct activities beyond the scope explicitly authorized by the broker, those are acts for which the broker is responsible.” The middle sentence's statement that the broker “is not required to supervise the sales agents directly” answers how, not whether, the broker answers.

Question 3 of 5

A buyer client is on a flight and texts her sales agent: “Just sign the counter-offer for me, you know what I want.” The agent holds a signed exclusive buyer representation agreement and nothing else. What does the held Texas text say about the authority that agreement confers?

The traditional universal, general and special agency labels are classical vocabulary that no Texas statute or Commission rule held for this course defines, so the answer comes from the text instead. Section 1101.557(c)(1) provides that “a license holder who has the authority to bind a party to a lease or sale under a power of attorney or a property management agreement is also a party to the lease or sale.” The Legislature named the two instruments that confer binding authority, and an ordinary listing or representation agreement is not one of them. Sections 1101.652(b)(19) and (20) confirm the limit from the disciplinary side: an agent who could bind the client would not need the client's authorisation for terms.

Question 4 of 5

In one afternoon a sales agent receives messages from her own seller client, from the buyer's agent at another brokerage, from a for-sale-by-owner asking about a buyer she represents, from the title company, and from a neighbour with no transaction who is curious about the sign. To how many of the five does 22 TAC § 535.157 give a right to a response within two calendar days?

22 TAC § 535.157 is one sentence: “A broker or sales agent must respond to his or her principal, a broker or sales agent representing another party to a real estate transaction, or an unrepresented party to a real estate transaction within two calendar days.” Three categories, stated exhaustively. A title company, an inspector, a lender and a member of the public who is not a party to a transaction are outside the rule's own words, and so is a represented party you deal with through that party's license holder. The rule also says nothing about the form of the response, so an acknowledgement that names when a full answer will follow satisfies it.

Question 5 of 5

You are hosting an open house at your brokerage's own listing on Ocean Drive. A visitor asks about the flood history of that house, and then asks you to help her lease out her own condominium on Alameda Street for eight months. Apply § 1101.558(c)(3) to the two conversations.

Section 1101.558(c)(3) is narrow in two ways at once: the property must be “held open for any prospective buyer or tenant” and “the communication concerns that property.” Turn the conversation to the visitor's own condominium and the exception by its terms no longer covers that communication. Two further limits matter. The three exceptions in subsection (c) relieve only “the notice required by Subsection (b-1)” — they never touch the subsection (b) disclosure of whom you represent, which is owed at first contact and may be made orally. And the eight-month lease is not saved by exception (c)(1) either, which requires both a residential lease of less than one year and that no sale is being considered.

Information About Brokerage Services - Disclosure of Representation

At an open house you give a visitor the IABS notice and your card. The visitor later says you are their agent because you gave them 'the agency form'. What did delivering the notice actually do?

Section 1101.558(b) requires a licence holder to disclose whom the licence holder represents at first contact, and (b-1) requires the written notice adopted by 22 TAC § 531.20. Both are disclosure obligations. Neither creates a relationship, and the notice's own function is to tell a person what the broker's role is — including that the broker may not represent them at all.

Optional practice — the Cedar Park open house. This exercise is optional. It puts you on the

other side of the problem the required exercises test: not what must I disclose, but *when have I

begun representing someone without meaning to*. Its last two questions turn on a point no required

question in this module tests — that being paid nothing by a person does not answer whether you

represented that person, and that your broker's exposure follows yours.

Renata is a sales agent sponsored by Hill Country Realty, a brokerage that holds the listing on a

house on Little Elm Trail in Cedar Park. Renata is hosting the seller's open house on Saturday. A

couple, the Okonkwos, walk in. They are not represented by anyone. Over twenty minutes Renata

answers their questions about the neighbourhood, tells them the seller would probably take less,

suggests they offer $12,000 under list, and offers to write the offer for them that afternoon. She

is not paid anything by the Okonkwos and nothing is signed at the open house.

1. Under § 1101.557(a), whose agent is Hill Country Realty with respect to this property, and on

which of the section's two triggers does that rest?

2. Renata told the Okonkwos that the seller would probably take less. Identify the subdivision of §

1101.651(d) that makes that disclosure prohibited for a broker or appointed sales agent acting as

an intermediary, and then explain why Renata's problem in this fact pattern is worse than an

intermediary's would be, given § 531.2's statement about whose interests the license holder's

primary duty is to represent.

3. Section 1101.562(a) lists four conditions a broker must satisfy to show property to a party

without representing that party. Which of the four has Renata's conduct defeated, and quote the

language that defeats it?

4. Nothing was signed and Renata was paid nothing by the Okonkwos. Explain, using § 1101.563(c)'s

separate treatment of representation status and compensation, why the absence of payment does not

answer the question of whom Renata was representing.

5. Section 1101.803 tells you who else now has exposure. State the provision's effect in your own

words and name the person it reaches besides Renata.
