Duties and Disclosures to Third Parties

Topic 3 of 12 · 125 prescribed minutes

Non-Fiduciary Duties

Fiduciary duty — loyalty, confidentiality, obedience, accounting, disclosure of material information

— runs to the client, and Module 02 teaches it. Now look at the other people in the room: the buyer

on the other side of your listing, the seller across from your buyer, the unrepresented person who

walked into your open house in Round Rock asking about the foundation. You represent none of them

and owe none of them loyalty. Texas law is nonetheless not indifferent to how you treat them.

The label the Commission's course form uses is non-fiduciary duties, and the word does precise

work. A fiduciary duty arises out of a relationship of trust and confidence; a non-fiduciary duty

arises out of statute, out of Commission rule, out of the general law that forbids anyone in

commerce from lying to the people they deal with. It attaches because you hold a Texas licence and

are dealing with a member of the public.

Texas states both duties in a single rule. 22 TAC § 531.2 (Fidelity), taught in Module 02 and

read as an ethical whole in Module 10, states the primary duty and then attaches a proviso that

reaches everyone else: "that the primary duty of the license holder is to represent the interests of

the client, and the license holder's position, in this respect, should be clear to all parties

concerned in a real estate transaction; that, however, the license holder, in performing duties to

the client, shall treat other parties to a transaction fairly." Fairness to the other side sits

inside the definition of fidelity.

The catalogue. Here, in one place, is every non-fiduciary duty a Texas source actually imposes —

students believe the list is either much shorter than it is (nothing but "don't lie") or much longer

(a general duty to look after the other side, which no held source creates). Most of these

authorities are taught in full elsewhere; this module states the rule, cites it, names its home, and

shows how it operates toward someone who is not your client.

Fair treatment and accurate information. 22 TAC § 535.156 requires honest and fair dealing with

all parties and imposes a duty "to convey accurate information to members of the public with whom

the license holder deals." That second duty does not name a client; it names *members of the

public*. Module 02 teaches the rule; Unit 2 takes up what it demands toward the other side.

Integrity, including by omission. 22 TAC § 531.3 requires "prudence and caution so as to avoid

misrepresentation, in any way, by acts of commission or omission" — the phrase that makes silence

capable of being a violation. Module 02 teaches it as a fiduciary duty and Module 10 as a Canon.

Disclosure of known significant defects to a potential buyer. Occupations Code § 1101.652(b)(4)

authorises discipline for failing to disclose to a potential buyer a significant defect described by

subdivision (b)(3) that is known to the license holder. Module 10 teaches § 1101.652 in full. What

matters here is the addressee: a potential buyer is not your client, so that duty runs across the

table.

Disclosure of who you represent, and the IABS. Occupations Code § 1101.558(b) requires disclosure

of your representation, orally or in writing, at first contact with another party or with that

party's license holder; § 1101.558(b-1) requires a written notice at first substantive

communication, and 22 TAC § 531.20 supplies its text by adopting the Information About Brokerage

Services Notice, TREC No. IABS 1-2. Module 02 owns the IABS, including the three exceptions in

§ 1101.558(c), which run to the written notice only and never to the § 1101.558(b) representation

disclosure. The notice belongs here because of one line of its required content: it must describe

"the basic obligations a broker has to a party to a real estate transaction that the broker does not

represent." Before showing property to someone you do not represent. Occupations Code

§ 1101.562(b) requires either the representation disclosure or the written notice, as applicable, and

§ 1101.563(b) requires a written agreement with a prospective buyer of residential real property

before showing any such property. Module 05 teaches both sections and the 2026 changes that produced

them.

Timely response. 22 TAC § 535.157 reaches "an unrepresented party to a real estate transaction."

Unit 3 below is that duty.

The abstract-or-title-policy advice. Occupations Code § 1101.555 requires a license holder, when

an offer to purchase real estate in this state is signed, to advise "each buyer, in writing," either

to have the abstract examined by an attorney of the buyer's choosing or to be provided with or

obtain a title insurance policy. Each buyer, not each client: if the buyer is unrepresented, that

written advice is yours to give, and failure is a ground for discipline under § 1101.652(b)(29).

Module 12 teaches § 1101.555.

The Consumer Protection Notice. 22 TAC § 531.18 requires each license holder to display TREC No.

CN 1-5 in a readily noticeable location in each place of business and to link to it from the

homepage of each business website. It tells strangers how to complain about you, so it is a duty

owed to the public at large. Module 08 teaches it.

Non-discrimination. 22 TAC § 531.19 forbids a license holder to inquire about, respond to or

facilitate inquiries about, or disclose information about an owner, previous or current occupant,

potential purchaser, lessor or potential lessee that indicates or is intended to indicate a

preference, limitation or discrimination on the bases the rule lists, and § 1101.652(b)(32) makes

discrimination against an owner, potential buyer, landlord or potential tenant a ground for

suspension or revocation. Both run to people who are not your clients; Module 10 teaches both.

Disclosure when you are the principal. 22 TAC § 535.144 obliges a license holder dealing on his or

her own behalf — or for a spouse, parent or child, a business entity in which the license holder is

more than a 10% owner, or a trust in the circumstances the rule describes — to disclose that

licensure in writing, and provides that such a license holder "shall not use the license holder's

expertise to the disadvantage of a person with whom the license holder deals." Again: *a person with

whom the license holder deals*, not a client. The companion obligation to furnish a broker price

opinion or comparative market analysis when you buy or list for your own account, 22 TAC § 535.16,

is taught in Module 04; Module 12 teaches § 535.144.

What you do not owe. The absences matter as much as the list. You do not owe a third party

loyalty, and you do not owe a third party confidentiality — that obligation runs the other way, to

your client. You do not owe a third party advocacy, negotiating help, advice about whether the deal

is good for them, or a duty to find problems you do not know about. **You owe the other side

truthfulness, fairness, accuracy, responsiveness, and the specific statutory disclosures — and

nothing that would require you to work against your own client.**

Texas now has a statutory category that makes this concrete. Occupations Code § 1101.562, effective

January 1, 2026 and taught in Module 05, permits a broker, on the four conjunctive conditions Module

05 sets out, to show real property "to a party without representing the party," forbids the broker

while doing so to "provide to the party opinions or advice regarding the real property or real

estate transactions in general," and permits the broker, in subsection (c), to "confirm information

to a party regarding the size, price, and terms of the real property available for sale or lease."

That is a legislative portrait of a non-fiduciary relationship. The license holder is in the house

with the buyer, is answering questions about size, price and terms, is bound to be accurate and fair

— and is expressly forbidden to give advice, because advice is what agents give clients.

Do not read "non-fiduciary" as "no consequences." Occupations Code § 1101.803, taught in Module

09, makes a licensed broker "liable to the commission, the public, and the broker's clients for any

conduct engaged in under this chapter by the broker or by a sales agent associated with or acting

for the broker" — the public before the clients. And § 1101.801: "[d]isciplinary action taken

against a person under Section 1101.652 does not relieve the person from civil or criminal

liability."

Topic quiz

Every question must be answered correctly before the next topic opens. An incorrect answer shows the reasoning and then serves a different, related question on the same rule.

Question 1 of 6

An unrepresented buyer tours your seller client's house in Round Rock, asks whether the foundation has moved, asks what he should offer, and asks you to press the seller for a faster closing date. Which of those three requests engage a duty you owe him?

22 TAC § 531.2 makes the primary duty representation of the client's interests and then adds, in the same sentence, “that, however, the license holder, in performing duties to the client, shall treat other parties to a transaction fairly.” 22 TAC § 535.156 puts honest and fair dealing with all parties alongside a free-standing duty “to convey accurate information to members of the public with whom the license holder deals,” and § 1101.652(b)(4) makes failing to disclose a known significant defect to a “potential buyer” a disciplinary ground — a potential buyer is not your client. What the other side is not owed is loyalty, confidentiality, advocacy, or advice about whether the deal is good for them.

Question 2 of 6

Representing a seller, you want to move a hesitant buyer's agent along. Which of the following is consistent with the duty to deal honestly and fairly with all parties while still representing only your principal?

22 TAC § 535.156 states both halves in one sentence: “A license holder must deal honestly and fairly with all parties; however, the license holder represents only the principal and owes a duty of fidelity to such principal.” Fairness is not neutrality — you may negotiate hard, decline to volunteer your client's motivation, and advocate a repair position. What you may not do is misstate a competing offer or invent a deadline, because 22 TAC § 531.3 requires “prudence and caution so as to avoid misrepresentation, in any way, by acts of commission or omission” and § 1101.652(b)(2) reaches dishonest conduct without proof that anyone lost money. Volunteering that the seller will take less is not required of anyone: § 1101.651(d)(1) forbids even an intermediary to do it.

Question 3 of 6

You tell the appraiser on your listing that the seller's rear addition was permitted, knowing the seller told you it was not. The appraiser is not a buyer and buys nothing. Which analysis is correct?

Section 1101.551(2) defines “party” as a prospective buyer, seller, landlord or tenant or an authorised representative of one, and excludes a license holder who represents a party; TRELA never defines “third party” at all. Inspectors, lenders, escrow officers, appraisers and surveyors are outside that definition, which is exactly why the working definition has to be built from 22 TAC § 535.156, whose duty “to convey accurate information to members of the public with whom the license holder deals” names no client and no party. The vocabulary changes and the exposure does not: § 531.3 reaches the carelessness and § 1101.652(b)(2) reaches the dishonesty.

Question 4 of 6

A seller tells her listing agent the roof is eight years old and the agent repeats it. Two weeks later the agent finds an invoice showing a temporary patch and a recommendation to replace, and says nothing. Apply § 1101.805(e) before and after the discovery.

Section 1101.805(e) provides that a license holder is not liable for a misrepresentation or concealment of a material fact made by a party “unless the license holder: (1) knew of the falsity … and (2) failed to disclose the license holder's knowledge of the falsity.” Both conditions, joined by “and,” and the word is “knew” — not “should have known” and not “was negligent in believing.” A license holder who learns a statement is false and does disclose that knowledge has satisfied the second condition and remains protected. Note the section's limits: it shields the party's statement, not the license holder's own assertion, and it does nothing about discipline, where § 1101.652(b)(1) reaches negligence and 22 TAC § 535.156 requires accurate information regardless.

Question 5 of 6

A contract on a single-family house in Kyle goes effective with no seller's disclosure notice delivered. The seller finally delivers the notice on day twelve. The buyer reads it, finds nothing objectionable, and decides two days later that he no longer likes the neighbourhood. Apply Property Code § 5.008(f).

Section 5.008(f) sets delivery “on or before the effective date of an executory contract binding the purchaser to purchase the property,” and then provides that if a contract is entered without the notice “the purchaser may terminate the contract for any reason within seven days after receiving the notice.” Three points follow: the clock runs from receipt, so a seller who delivers late on day twelve starts a fresh seven days and a seller who never delivers never starts it at all; the right is unconditional, because the statute says “for any reason”; and the subsection states no damages remedy and no exclusivity clause — compare § 5.012(e), which does make termination the purchaser's exclusive remedy for a different notice.

Question 6 of 6

A tenant died on your listing after falling through a section of the rear deck that had rotted. A prospective buyer asks whether anything has happened at the property. What does § 1101.556 do here?

Section 1101.556 says that, notwithstanding other law, a license holder is not required to inquire about, disclose or release information relating to a prior or current occupant's AIDS, HIV-related illness or HIV infection, or to whether “a death occurred on a property by natural causes, suicide, or accident unrelated to the condition of the property.” The last five words are the qualifier students drop, and they are the whole answer here: a death caused by the property's condition is outside the relief, and the condition that caused it is very likely a significant defect that § 1101.652(b)(3) and (b)(4) require you to disclose to a potential buyer. Property Code § 5.008(c) states the same relief on the seller's side and carries the identical qualifier.

The § 5.008 notice form, item by item. Unit 5 teaches what the notice is, who must deliver it,

when, on what knowledge standard, what the exemptions do and what the purchaser's remedy is. This is

the clause-by-clause tour of the form itself — useful the first time you sit at a kitchen table with

a seller who is filling one in, and useful when a buyer asks which line an answer belongs on.

The body runs to eleven numbered items. Item 1 is the inventory of equipment and systems with

Yes/No/Unknown boxes, followed by the question whether the seller is aware of any of those items

"that are not in working condition, that have known defects, or that are in need of repair." Item 2

asks about working smoke detectors installed in accordance with Chapter 766, Health and Safety Code,

with a footnote explaining the buyer's right to require smoke detectors for the hearing impaired if

the buyer or a family member who will reside in the dwelling is hearing impaired, the buyer gives

written evidence of the impairment from a licensed physician, and "within 10 days after the

effective date, the buyer makes a written request for the seller to install smoke detectors for the

hearing impaired and specifies the locations for installation." Item 3 asks about known defects or

malfunctions in interior walls, ceilings, floors, exterior walls, doors, windows, roof, foundation

or slab, basement, walls and fences, driveways, sidewalks, plumbing, electrical, lighting fixtures

and other structural components. Item 4 lists conditions including active termites, previous

structural or roof repair, termite or wood rot damage needing repair, hazardous or toxic waste,

asbestos components, urea formaldehyde insulation, radon gas, improper drainage, lead based paint,

water damage not due to a flood event, aluminum wiring, previous fires, unplatted easements,

landfill or settling or soil movement or fault lines, subsurface structure or pits, a single

blockable main drain in a pool, hot tub or spa, and, in the form's two-column layout, previous use

of the premises for the manufacture of methamphetamine. Item 5 asks generally about any item,

equipment or system in need of repair.

Items 6, 7 and 8 are the flood block, and they are the most heavily amended part of the form. Item 6

asks about present flood insurance coverage, previous flooding due to a failure or breach of a

reservoir or a controlled or emergency release from one, and previous water penetration into a

structure due to a natural flood event, and then asks whether the property is located wholly or

partly in a 100-year floodplain, a 500-year floodplain, a floodway, a flood pool, or a reservoir.

The statute supplies its own definitions for those terms in the notice: a "100-year floodplain" is

an area identified on the flood insurance rate map as a special flood hazard area designated Zone A,

V, A99, AE, AO, AH, VE, or AR, that "has a one percent annual chance of flooding, which is

considered to be a high risk of flooding," and that "may include a regulatory floodway, flood pool,

or reservoir"; a "500-year floodplain" is an area identified as a moderate flood hazard area

designated Zone X (shaded) that "has a two-tenths of one percent annual chance of flooding, which is

considered to be a moderate risk of flooding." Item 7 asks whether the seller has "ever filed a

claim for flood damage to the property with any insurance provider, including the National Flood

Insurance Program (NFIP)." Item 8 asks whether the seller has "ever received assistance from FEMA or

the U.S. Small Business Administration (SBA) for flood damage to the property."

Item 9 asks about unpermitted room additions or alterations, homeowners' association fees or

assessments, common areas, notices of violations of deed restrictions or governmental ordinances,

lawsuits affecting the property, "[a]ny condition on the Property which materially affects the

physical health or safety of an individual," a rainwater harvesting system larger than 500 gallons

using a public water supply as an auxiliary source, and any portion of the property located in a

groundwater conservation district or a subsidence district. Item 10 is the coastal notice for

property seaward of the Gulf Intracoastal Waterway or within 1,000 feet of the mean high tide

bordering the Gulf of Mexico. Item 11 is the military installation noise notice. The form closes

with the seller's dated signature and the purchaser's dated acknowledgement of receipt.

Subsection (g) of § 5.008 supplies two definitions for the section: a "[b]lockable main drain" is "a main drain

of any size and shape that a human body can sufficiently block to create a suction entrapment

hazard," and a "[m]ain drain" is "a submerged suction outlet typically located at the bottom of a

swimming pool or spa to conduct water to a recirculating pump."

Optional practice — reading § 531.2, § 531.3 and § 535.156 for the non-client. This exercise is

optional document practice. It sends you to the text of three Commission rules the module quotes

only in part, and its last question turns on a point no required question tests: none of the three

rules uses the phrase "third party" at all.

Open 22 TAC § 531.2 (Fidelity), 22 TAC § 531.3 (Integrity) and 22 TAC § 535.156 (Dishonesty; Bad

Faith; Untrustworthiness) and read all three in full before answering. Module 02 teaches these three

rules as sources of duty to the client; read them here only for what they say about everyone else.

1. Section 531.2 states the license holder's primary duty and then adds a clause beginning "that,

however." Write out that clause and identify who benefits from it. Does the clause make the

license holder an agent of those people?

2. Section 535.156 imposes a duty to convey accurate information "to members of the public with whom

the license holder deals." Is that a duty to volunteer information, or a duty about the

quality of information volunteered? Why does the difference matter to a buyer at your open

house?

3. Section 531.3 requires "prudence and caution so as to avoid misrepresentation, in any way, by

acts of commission or omission." Identify what the phrase "or omission" adds that "commission"

alone would not cover, and give an example from a listing appointment.

4. None of these three rules uses the phrase "third party." Which words do they use instead, and

does the vocabulary they actually use cover an unrepresented buyer, a cooperating agent, a home

inspector, and a neighbour who calls about the sign in the yard?

Optional practice — three questions about a house in Galveston. This exercise is optional. It

works the stigmatized-property rules through a single property carrying three different facts at

once, which is the fact pattern that produces real complaints; the required statute reading exercise

on § 5.008 already tests the qualifier that decides the death question.

You are the listing agent for a beach-side house on Galveston Island. Your seller tells you, at the

listing appointment, three things: (i) the previous owner died in the house of a heart attack in

2022; (ii) a tenant died in 2023 after falling through a section of the rear deck, and the deck was

rebuilt afterwards without a permit; and (iii) a former occupant in the 1990s had an HIV-related

illness. Three separate unrepresented buyers ask you three separate questions.

1. Buyer A asks: "Has anyone ever died in this house?" Using Occupations Code § 1101.556 and

Property Code § 5.008(c), state which of the two deaths falls inside the relief the statutes give

and which does not. Quote the qualifier that decides it.

2. Buyer B asks nothing about deaths but asks: "Is there anything wrong with the deck?" Apply

§ 1101.652(b)(3) and (b)(4). Does § 1101.556 have anything to say about your answer? Identify

which item of the § 5.008 notice form the unpermitted rebuild belongs in.

3. Buyer C asks: "Did anyone with HIV ever live here?" State what § 1101.556(1) permits you to do,

what § 1101.802 adds, and what 22 TAC § 531.19 prohibits. Draft the substance of your answer and

explain why it must be the same answer you would give every buyer.

4. Your seller instructs you in writing to tell no one about the 2023 deck death. You are acting as

an intermediary in the transaction. Apply Occupations Code § 1101.651(d)(3) and identify the

exception that governs. Does the seller's written instruction control?

5. Suppose Buyer A asks the question in question 1 and you answer "No." Identify two separate

grounds for discipline under Occupations Code § 1101.652(b) and one Commission rule you would

have violated, and explain why § 1101.556 does not protect the answer.
