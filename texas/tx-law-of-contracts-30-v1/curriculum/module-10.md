# Texas Law of Contracts — Module 10

## TREC Topic: Transaction Process and Closing

You will administer the accepted contract through closing, use a documented checklist without treating it as legal authority, coordinate financing, inspections and title, verify prorations, and distinguish signing from funding and possession.

This is student instruction aligned to the LOC-0 topic. Legal propositions identify primary authority; worked examples are hypothetical. ## T10U1 — Transaction Process

The transaction process begins before the contract is accepted and continues after signatures at closing. Early work identifies the parties, representation, property, intended use, financing, disclosures, and required documents. Acceptance creates an operational calendar. Investigations and approvals inform decisions. Closing instruments and funds perform the core exchange. Post-closing obligations, possession, corrections, and record retention can remain.
### Adapted teaching: The sequence from accepted agreement to closing readiness

**Closing is a deadline system.** From effective date to closing date, the contract (Principles I Module 06)
schedules everything; the licence holder's professional contribution is running that calendar so
that no right lapses unnoticed. The standing critical path:

- **Earnest money and option fees delivered** on the contract's clock, receipted by the escrow
  agent.
- **Title work.** The commitment issues; buyer and lender review; objections go out within the
  contract's window; cures are negotiated. Everything Principles I Module 03 taught about liens and Principles I Module 07
  taught about chains of title happens *here*, as tasks with dates: releases ordered, payoffs
  quoted, heirship documented, that pool contractor's lien resolved.
- **Survey** obtained or updated (Principles I Module 04's five-point pass), feeding both title objections and
  the lender's requirements.
- **Inspections and repairs** inside the option period; repair amendments negotiated in writing.
- **Financing.** Application, appraisal, underwriting, and the approach of the financing
  contingency's deadline — with the agent tracking conditions ("clear to close") rather than
  assuming them.
- **Seller's disclosure** delivered (Property Code § 5.008 — Principles I Module 07); **insurance** bound
  (the lender will not fund without it); utilities, HOA documents and resale certificates
  ordered where applicable.
- **The walk-through**: the buyer's final verification that the property's condition and the
  agreed repairs match the contract — the last, cheap moment to catch what would be expensive
  the day after funding.

**"Clear to close," tracked rather than assumed.** Financing is the critical path's longest pole,
and its milestones deserve the same calendar discipline as the contract's: application submitted
(with the § 1024.6 booklet obligations of this module's federal unit starting the clock on the
lender's side); appraisal ordered and received — with the contract's own machinery, not
improvisation, governing what happens if value comes in low; conditional approval, with the
conditions *listed and owned* (whose document, due when); and final underwriting clearance —
"clear to close" — before which no closing date is real, however confidently scheduled. The
licence holder's technique is unglamorous: a standing weekly status call pattern among agent,
lender and escrow officer, and a refusal to let "we're fine" substitute for a conditions list
with names on it.

**The association file, ordered early.** Where a property sits in a mandatory owners'
association, the contract's HOA machinery adds its own pre-closing thread: the association's
documents and account statement are ordered (associations bill for them, and turnaround times
are real), the buyer's review rights under the contract's terms run on their own clock, and
the association's transfer and statement fees take their place on the settlement statement as
the contract allocates them. The dues proration of the statements unit and Principles I Module 03's
assessment-lien discussion both land in this thread — one more place where the file's earliest
phone call prevents its latest crisis.

**Funds at closing, and the fraud that hunts them.** The escrow officer will require the buyer's
cash to close in **collected funds** — a wire or the equivalent the instructions specify, not a
personal cheque that clears next week — and the amount comes from the settlement statement, not
from memory. Around that wire has grown the industry's most damaging crime: intercepted and
spoofed emails delivering altered wiring instructions days before closing. The professional
protocols are simple and non-negotiable, and a licence holder repeats them to every buyer:
wiring instructions come from the title company through a verified channel; any change to
wiring instructions is presumed fraudulent; and every wire is confirmed by a phone call to a
number *independently known*, never one taken from the email that asked for the money. Stating
these protocols is not legal advice; it is the standard of care in a market where a
misdirected down payment is usually unrecoverable.


Assign each step to the person who must perform it. The buyer supplies financial documents and makes decisions; the seller supplies required property information and performs agreed obligations; the lender underwrites; the title and escrow participants handle their authorized functions; inspectors and other professionals evaluate their subjects; the broker coordinates within its role. An agent’s reminder does not replace the party’s performance, but it can prevent a missed step.

The process should expose uncertainty early. A buyer depending on sale proceeds, a seller lacking authority documents, an unavailable survey, an insurance problem, or a repair requiring permits can affect the closing path. Record the issue, responsible participant, next action, due date, and source. Avoid a generic “pending” status that conceals which condition threatens performance.

In a worked file, the closing date is thirty days away, but the title company requests probate documents that are not yet available. The agent should notify the client and broker, identify the actual requirement, and coordinate with counsel. A promise that title will be ready because most transactions close in thirty days is not evidence. The parties may need a realistic negotiated amendment before the existing agreement reaches a crisis.

## T10U2 — Sample Check List

A checklist is an administrative aid. It should be derived from the accepted documents and applicable duties, not used as a substitute for them. An office checklist can omit a new addendum or assume a deadline that differs from the signed contract. Begin each file by reading the actual agreement and then customizing the factual task record within the broker’s procedures.

A useful row contains the task, source paragraph or authority, triggering event, responsible person, recipient, delivery method, due date or time, completion evidence, and unresolved issue. For example, “survey” is too vague. A better row identifies which party must furnish which survey-related documents, the date due, the recipient, and the evidence of delivery. A separate row tracks the buyer’s review or objection process under the contract.

At acceptance, confirm the complete signed agreement and addenda, communication of acceptance, effective date, party and notice information, escrow details, and money-delivery duties. Schedule inspections and other investigations while the relevant rights remain available. Request title documents and survey information. Confirm lender milestones without treating them as amendments of the contract. Track disclosures with their actual receipt dates.

Before a protection expires, confirm the client has the necessary information and understands the status. The checklist should not make the agent’s preferred decision for the client. It should identify available documents, unanswered questions, and the need for timely legal advice where appropriate. A client’s decision to proceed should be documented accurately, without a misleading statement that the agent guaranteed every risk was resolved.

At amendment, retain the signed change, update affected dates and amounts, and notify the appropriate participants. At closing readiness, compare the settlement figures with the contract, amendments, lender disclosures, payoffs, deposits, and invoices. Confirm identity and authority requirements, signing arrangements, funds, insurance, possession, and any final walkthrough. At completion, retain the final documents, confirmations, and remaining duties under the broker’s retention policy and rule.

A worked checklist defect is a row marked “financing complete” because a prequalification letter arrived. The evidence does not support the status. Replace the label with the actual stage: prequalification received; borrower approval pending; property conditions unresolved; funding not authorized. Separate entries give the client and broker a usable picture of what remains.

Another defect is a deadline calculated once and never updated after a signed amendment. The record should identify the original date, the amendment’s new date, and which dependent tasks changed. Do not delete the historical date; mark it superseded with the source. This preserves a reliable chronology if someone later asks why a notice was sent when it was.

### A worked checklist that exposes dependencies

Assume an accepted residential contract contains a third-party financing addendum, a negotiated termination option, an agreement to furnish an existing survey, and a seller temporary lease after closing. The office’s standard checklist contains generic rows for financing, inspection, title, and closing. Customize those rows by reading the actual documents. The purpose is to make dependencies visible before a delay in one workstream affects another.

For financing, record the lender contact, the buyer’s application and document status, the actual buyer-approval provision, property-approval issues, and the evidence needed for closing authorization. Do not place private financial details in a broadly shared checklist. The status can identify that income documentation remains outstanding without circulating the buyer’s tax return to every participant. Assign the next action to the person who controls it.

For inspection, record the buyer’s selected professionals, appointment dates, report delivery, review with the buyer, and any decision required before a contractual right expires. A report received the evening before a deadline creates a different risk from an appointment merely scheduled. The checklist should therefore distinguish scheduling, performance, report receipt, client review, and documented decision. None of those stages by itself means the seller agreed to repairs.

For survey, identify the exact documents promised, the delivery deadline, the actual receipt, and any issue affecting lender or title acceptance. An old drawing without the associated required information may not satisfy the contract. A newly constructed improvement may make the existing depiction inadequate for the buyer’s concern. The agent should coordinate the appropriate review and identify who bears any new-survey obligation under the signed terms.

For the temporary lease, record its signed version, commencement and termination terms, rent or deposit obligations, possession responsibilities, and insurance referrals. Do not let the main closing date overwrite the later possession date. A key-release row should identify the actual agreement and required confirmation. The buyer’s moving-company reservation is not the source of the seller’s surrender duty.

At each checkpoint, use a status supported by evidence: not requested, requested, received, under review, resolved, or superseded. The word complete should require the relevant completion test. If the title company requests an additional authority document after an earlier review, reopen the affected task and explain why. A checklist is useful because it reflects change accurately, not because every row stays green.

## T10U3 — Loan Approval

Loan approval is a process with defined conditions. Prequalification, preapproval, underwriting approval, satisfaction of conditions, closing authorization, and funding are not synonyms. The lender’s terminology should be clarified for the actual file. The agent should ask what conditions remain and what evidence will establish readiness, while the buyer remains responsible for truthful, timely documentation and decisions.

Borrower conditions can involve income, employment, credit, assets, debts, occupancy, source of funds, and other underwriting matters. Property conditions can involve appraisal, condition, insurance, title, and program standards. A change in either can affect approval. A buyer should not assume approval remains unconditional after taking new debt, changing employment, or receiving a property-related requirement.

The purchase contract has an independent calendar. The Third Party Financing Addendum’s buyer and property approval provisions must be read on their actual terms. A lender’s request for more time does not automatically extend a contractual right. If an extension is needed, the parties must agree through the proper process; if a right may be exercised, the notice and supporting requirements must be evaluated promptly.

The appraisal is an opinion prepared for its defined purpose and intended use. It does not replace a home inspection or guarantee future resale value. Current appraisal delivery requirements can change, including the 2026 UAD transition identified by Fannie Mae. The lender determines whether the submitted appraisal meets the applicable loan requirements. The agent can provide accurate property information and comparable-sale information through permitted channels without pressuring an appraiser to reach a desired value.

In a worked file, the loan is conditionally approved subject to proof that a debt was paid and a roof repair was completed. The buyer supplies proof of payment, but the repair is pending. It is inaccurate to tell the seller financing is fully complete. State which condition is resolved and which remains. Coordinate the repair evidence, lender review, and the contract’s deadlines rather than guessing that the remaining condition is a formality.

Finally, closing authorization and actual funding can be separate. A borrower may sign all documents while the lender still requires a final review or confirmation. The agent should obtain the appropriate closing and funding status from authorized participants before acting on possession or key-release instructions. Do not infer funding from the borrower’s signature completion.

### Coordinating the approval path without becoming the underwriter

A buyer’s loan file often contains several communications that sound final but are not equivalent. A preliminary letter may be based on unverified information. A conditional approval may depend on documents or property conditions. A closing authorization may still require final funding checks. Ask the lender to describe the present stage and outstanding conditions in terms the client can understand. Do not translate every positive update into an unconditional assurance.

Consider a buyer who has supplied income and asset records but plans to use a gift for part of the required funds. The lender may need documentation of the gift and transfer. The agent can remind the buyer to communicate accurately with the lender and avoid unexplained last-minute changes. The agent should not coach the buyer to describe borrowed money as a gift or conceal a repayment arrangement. Truthful information is essential to both underwriting and a reliable transaction.

Consider a property approval issue instead. The appraisal meets the price, but the lender requires evidence that a safety-related repair is complete. The price opinion alone has not satisfied that condition. Identify the required repair evidence, who will produce it, who will review it, and when review can occur. If the contract’s schedule is threatened, communicate early and use the proper amendment or legal-advice process. Do not assume that a lender’s verbal optimism extends the buyer’s contractual protections.

The buyer may also change lenders. A different lender can have different processing times, documentation needs, appraisal requirements, and conditions. The parties’ purchase agreement remains the governing contract unless properly changed. Review any applicable obligations concerning financing changes and promptly coordinate the consequences. A lower quoted rate is only one factor in whether the new lender can meet the actual transaction schedule.

Approval communication should be factual and limited. A useful update says that borrower documentation is complete, appraisal review is pending, and the lender expects to provide a further status on a specified day. An unhelpful update says the loan is guaranteed because the buyer has excellent credit. The lender makes the credit decision, and the buyer’s contractual rights still require their own analysis. This division of roles lets the agent coordinate effectively without claiming authority the agent does not possess.

## T10U4 — Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act (RESPA)

RESPA addresses specified residential settlement processes and prohibits particular referral and unearned-fee practices. Coverage and exemptions matter. Integrated mortgage disclosures also involve TILA and Regulation Z; a modern Closing Disclosure should not be described as simply the old HUD-1 with a new title. Identify the actual loan and disclosure regime.
### Adapted teaching: Settlement disclosures and the distinction between older and current forms

**The federal frame.** The Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act governs settlements involving
**federally related mortgage loans** — which, in practice, means most institutionally financed
one-to-four family residential transactions. Its declared purpose, from the text read for this
course (12 U.S.C. § 2601): reforms so that consumers are *"provided with greater and more timely
information on the nature and costs of the settlement process"* and are *"protected from
unnecessarily high settlement charges caused by certain abusive practices."* Two operative pillars
carry that purpose.

**Reading § 2601 as history.** The findings' vocabulary — "abusive practices," charges that
"unnecessarily inflate the costs" — records the pre-1974 settlement market Congress examined:
referral fees moving invisibly between settlement providers, borrowers meeting their costs for
the first time at the table with no ability to compare or refuse. Each pillar answers one
finding: itemised uniform disclosure answers the information failure; § 2607's prohibitions
answer the kickback economy. Knowing the history keeps the compliance from feeling arbitrary —
every disclosure this module walks exists because its absence was once somebody's business
model, and the licence holder's referral relationships are regulated precisely because
referrals are where the old abuses lived.

**Coverage, read from the purpose.** Section 2601's findings name the market the Act polices —
consumers entering **federally related mortgage loans** — and the practical perimeter follows:
the machinery of this unit attaches to the financed one-to-four family residential transaction,
the same territory Texas's own consumer perimeter (Finance Code § 343.001) and the
promulgated forms serve. Transactions outside it — the all-cash purchase, the true commercial
deal — close under the same Texas escrow customs but without RESPA's federal disclosures
attached. A licence holder does not adjudicate coverage; a licence holder notices the pattern:
where a consumer, a dwelling and an institutional loan meet, assume the federal regime is in
the room.


Use the current [CFPB Regulation X](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1024/) and [Regulation Z § 1026.19](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/19/) for applicable requirements. The creditor generally must ensure receipt of the Closing Disclosure no later than three business days before consummation for covered transactions under § 1026.19(f)(1)(ii). A changed disclosure does not always restart that period; § 1026.19(f)(2)(ii) identifies particular changes requiring a new waiting period. The definition of business day and actual receipt rules matter.

In a worked file, the closing figures change because a minor fee is corrected. The agent should not automatically announce that federal law delays closing three more days. The lender or closing professional must evaluate the actual change under the rule. Conversely, a change in APR beyond the permitted accuracy threshold, a loan-product change, or addition of a prepayment penalty can trigger the specified new period. The broker should coordinate promptly and accurately.

Referral compensation must be evaluated separately from the closing statement’s arithmetic. An amount appearing on a settlement statement is not automatically lawful. A payment labelled marketing, administration, or technology can still require analysis under 12 U.S.C. § 2607 and 12 CFR § 1024.14. The brokerage should resolve the arrangement’s legality before closing, not treat disclosure as permission.

## T10U5 — Inspections

Inspections supply information for the buyer’s decisions. The scope, professional qualifications, engagement terms, timing, and limitations matter. A general home inspection is not a warranty and may recommend further evaluation by an engineer, roofer, electrician, pest professional, or other specialist. A lender’s appraisal is a different service with a different purpose.

Schedule investigation with the contract’s actual rights in mind. The report should arrive early enough for meaningful review, follow-up, advice, and a decision before the relevant deadline. An inspection appointment on the final day of an option period creates practical pressure, but the appointment itself does not extend the period. A pending repair request likewise does not automatically suspend it.

Read the report as information, not as a list of automatic seller obligations. The seller owes repairs only as required by the contract, applicable law, or a later proper agreement. The buyer may negotiate a repair, price change, credit, further investigation, or other lawful choice. An agent should not say the seller “must fix everything the inspector marks” unless the actual obligation supports it.

The agent’s own observations should be communicated accurately. A stain can be described as a stain; the agent should not diagnose its cause or assure that it is harmless without appropriate expertise. Known material defects and contradictory reports raise disclosure responsibilities. Preserve the report and relevant communications, and obtain the seller’s or buyer’s authorized decisions through the correct process.

In a worked inspection, the report notes foundation movement and recommends an engineer. The buyer’s option ends tomorrow. The agent should promptly notify the buyer, identify the report’s recommendation, coordinate available professional help, and clarify whether an extension has actually been agreed. The agent should not tell the buyer the condition is normal for the area as a substitute for evaluation or promise that a request for more time is already effective.

A final walkthrough is also distinct from an inspection. It can help confirm the property’s condition, included items, occupancy, and completion of agreed work immediately before closing. It does not necessarily waive or replace other rights or inspections. If a discrepancy appears, involve the broker and appropriate participants before closing and obtain legal advice where needed. Do not resolve a material issue by promising an undocumented post-closing fix.

### Turning an inspection report into an informed decision

An inspection report contains observations within the inspector’s scope, methods, and limitations. It may identify a need for further evaluation rather than a final diagnosis. The buyer should understand what was inspected, what was inaccessible, and what specialized questions remain. The agent can help coordinate the desired follow-up without reinterpreting technical findings beyond the agent’s competence.

Suppose the general inspector notes movement and recommends evaluation by an appropriate foundation professional. The agent should not reduce that recommendation to a cosmetic issue because similar houses have settled. Nor should the agent announce that the property is structurally unsound without a qualified basis. Deliver the report, identify the recommendation, help the buyer arrange the chosen evaluation, and keep the relevant contractual decision period visible.

A repair request should reflect the buyer’s actual objectives and the authorized form process. The buyer may want replacement, professional repair, additional inspection, a price change, or no change. Those are business choices with different consequences. An agent should not write a vague promise that everything will be fixed and assume it resolves the issue. The agreed scope, timing, evidence of completion, and any necessary legal drafting require attention before the parties rely on the amendment.

The final walkthrough addresses the property’s observed condition and agreed performance at that stage. It does not replace the earlier inspection or automatically create a new termination right. Compare observations with the contract, amendments, and known facts. If a problem appears, communicate promptly and obtain the client’s decision and appropriate advice. Avoid both extremes: insisting that closing must proceed regardless of the problem or declaring that any new observation gives an automatic cancellation right.

Record the difference between a report, a request, an agreement, and completed performance. These four documents or events can concern the same appliance but have different legal significance. A report may identify a defect; a request proposes a response; an amendment records the accepted duty; a receipt or professional confirmation supplies evidence of completion. Keeping them separate prevents a negotiation from being mistaken for an obligation or a promise from being mistaken for performance.

## T10U6 — Title Work

Title work identifies the interests, requirements, exceptions, and insurance conditions relevant to the transaction. The title company’s request for documents is not an inconvenience to be postponed until closing day; it is evidence of what must be resolved for the proposed conveyance and coverage. Obtain and deliver materials promptly and keep the buyer’s contractual review period distinct from the title company’s production process.

Review the commitment with the actual exception documents and survey. A short exception description may not reveal the full scope of an easement or restriction. A requirement for probate, entity authority, marital information, payoff, release, or correction may affect the closing schedule. The agent should identify the issue and responsible person, while legal sufficiency and policy coverage are addressed by the proper professionals.

The contract governs delivery, objection, cure, and related choices. A buyer who wants to object should obtain timely advice and follow the actual requirements. The agent should not decide that an exception is acceptable merely because it is common or that an objection is legally effective because an email was sent. The objection’s subject, specificity, timing, recipient, and contractual basis matter.

In a worked file, a survey shows a garage extending into a utility easement. The commitment includes that easement as an exception. The agent should transmit both documents, identify the physical and recorded facts, and encourage legal and appropriate professional evaluation. A title policy may except the risk, and a lender’s willingness to close does not establish that the garage can remain or be rebuilt.

Title work can change late in the transaction. A new lien, updated payoff, revised survey, or changed seller status can produce new requirements or exceptions. The agent should ask for and distribute relevant updates promptly. Do not assume the first commitment remains the complete final picture. Compare the expected policy and conveyance with the latest closing documents before execution.

The title company does not replace every professional. Tax consequences, contract disputes, unusual reservations, and the buyer’s business-use plans can require separate counsel or specialists. The agent’s coordination should help the client reach the correct person while maintaining the contractual calendar. “Title is handling it” is inadequate if nobody can identify the actual issue and next step.

### A title requirement is a task with a legal purpose

Title requirements can seem administrative because they arrive as a list, but each requirement addresses an identified obstacle or condition to the proposed transaction. A payoff request concerns discharge of debt. An authority document concerns the power to convey. A marital-status inquiry can concern rights affecting execution or homestead. A correction instrument can concern a defect in an earlier record. Ask what is required and who is responsible rather than assuming every item is a routine formality.

For example, the seller provides a mortgage statement showing the current monthly balance. The closing officer still requests an official payoff. The two documents serve different purposes. A payoff may account for accrued interest, fees, timing, and the creditor’s release process. The agent should help obtain the requested document through authorized channels and should not substitute the monthly statement simply because it shows a number that looks close.

Another file contains a prior lien that the seller believes was paid years ago. Payment history and a recorded release are related but distinct evidence. The title company may require documentation or a curative process before removing the exception or satisfying the requirement. The agent should not assure the buyer that the lien can be ignored because the seller remembers paying it. Identify the creditor, available records, and appropriate legal or title steps early.

A third file involves an out-of-state seller signing under a power of attorney. Arrange review before the closing appointment. The document’s validity, scope, continued effectiveness, and acceptance for the transaction can require legal analysis. A notary’s availability does not answer those questions. If the authority is insufficient or unavailable, the parties may need different execution arrangements and a realistic schedule.

These examples show why title work belongs throughout the transaction, not only on the last day. The agent’s role is to recognize a pending requirement, preserve the relevant information, coordinate the responsible participant, and keep the client informed. The title professional and counsel make the determinations within their roles. An administrative checklist should expose legal dependencies without pretending to decide them.

## T10U7 — Preparation for Closing

Closing preparation reconciles the agreement, legal documents, financial figures, identity and authority evidence, and practical possession arrangements. It should occur early enough to resolve discrepancies. The parties should receive the appropriate documents and explanations before signing, with counsel available for legal questions. A scheduled appointment does not prove the transaction is ready.
### Adapted teaching: Participants, document review, funds, and the closing event

**The event itself.** In practice Texas "closing" is often two signing appointments — seller's
package (deed, affidavits, payoff authorisations) and buyer's package (note, deed of trust,
lender disclosures, settlement statement) — coordinated by the escrow officer. The sequence that
turns signatures into a conveyance:

1. **Documents executed and acknowledged** — the notarial acts that qualify instruments for
   recording (Property Code § 12.001; Principles I Module 07).
2. **Funding** — the lender wires; the buyer delivers the cash to close (in collected funds, as
   instructed); the escrow officer confirms receipt against the settlement statement.
3. **Disbursement** — payoffs to existing lienholders (Principles I Module 08's payoff quotes, good through a
   date, with per-diem interest), taxing units if delinquencies exist, commissions,
   service providers, seller's proceeds.
4. **Recording** — the deed and deed of trust go to the county clerk. Principles I Module 07's law makes the
   order of operations obvious: record *immediately*, because § 13.001 protects the world against
   the unrecorded, and the gap between funding and recording is the danger zone the industry
   compresses to hours.
5. **Policies issue** — the owner's and loan title policies (Principles I Module 07), converting the
   commitment's promises into insurance.

**Variations on the appointment.** The two-package pattern flexes constantly, and the agent
should be able to narrate each variation without drama. *Mail-out and remote sellers*: a seller
who cannot attend signs a mailed or electronically delivered package before a notary where the
seller is, returned to escrow ahead of funding — the documents, not the people, must be at the
closing. *Powers of attorney*: a party may sign through an attorney-in-fact under a power of
attorney the title company approved **in advance** — surprise powers presented at the table
stop closings, because the instrument's sufficiency and recording needs are title questions,
not courtesies. *Split and sequential closings*: the seller may sign Monday, the buyer
Wednesday, with funding when every condition meets — "closing day" is really "funding day"
wearing a calendar name. *The double contract warning*: what never flexes is the ledger's
honesty — every term of the parties' real agreement appears on the statement the lender sees,
and any proposal to paper one price for the lender and another between the parties is fraud
territory the licence holder refuses in the room, out loud.

**Neutrality under pressure.** The escrow officer's neutrality is easy to state and regularly
tested, and the licence holder should know the standard collisions. *A party demands the earnest
money*: the stakeholder disburses on the parties' signed agreement or prescribed process, never
on one side's insistence, however righteous (Principles I Module 06's collapsed-deal triage arriving at the
closing desk). *A party asks the closer "should I sign this?"*: the neutral explains what a
document is and does procedurally and stops at the legal-advice line — the same line the agent
walks. *Last-minute changes appear on the statement*: the closer implements only what the
contract, amendments and lender instructions authorise, which is why changes are papered as
amendments rather than negotiated orally at the table. Understanding the neutral's constraints
keeps the agent from asking for what cannot be given — and positions the agent as the person
who gets the *paper* right so the neutral can act.

**After funding: the file's afterlife.** Closing ends with a package, and clients call about it
for years, so know what lands where: the buyer receives the recorded deed back from the county
(the original returns to the grantee after recording), the owner's title policy issued after
recording, and the settlement statement — the document their tax preparer will ask for. The
lender holds the note; the deed of trust returns recorded to the lender's file; the seller
keeps the statement and the payoff's release evidence (Principles I Module 07: an unreleased lien is a
future title requirement with the seller's name on it). The brokerage retains its transaction
file under the records practices the later courses detail. The agent's last professional act
is a closing letter telling the parties exactly this — what they will receive, from whom,
roughly when — because the cheapest client service in the industry is preventing the
"where's my deed?" panic call six weeks out.

**The closing documents, mapped to their modules.** The deed (07); the note and deed of trust
(08); the FIRPTA affidavit (08); the settlement statement (this module's last two units); the
seller's affidavits (debts, liens, parties in possession — the title company's defence-in-depth
against Principles I Module 03's off-record claims); tax certificates and prorations (03 and below). A licence
holder who can say what each paper *does* — in one sentence, without giving legal advice — is
worth the commission at the table.

**The neutral's licence and plant, from the statute.** Principles I Module 07 introduced § 2501.003's defined
roles; the closing table is where they work. The **escrow officer's** defined functions —
countersigning title forms, supervising preparation and delivery of forms, signing escrow
cheques, closing the transaction — describe exactly what the parties watch happen at signing;
and behind the closer stands § 2501.004's **abstract plant**, the geographically indexed record
set (complete from not later than January 1, 1979, kept current) from which the commitment's
promises were built. The two sections together explain the Texas industry's shape: the same
regulated company examines title, insures it, holds the escrow and conducts the closing — one
accountable neutral, with promulgated premiums (§ 2501.003(8)) removing price from the
competition among them.


Confirm the buyer’s required funds through the authorized closing process. Wire instructions should be verified through a trusted, independently established channel, particularly when an email announces a last-minute change. This is a practical fraud-prevention step, not a statement that a particular verification method alone guarantees safety. The agent should follow the brokerage’s procedures and avoid forwarding unverified changes as authoritative.

Verification requires attention to the source of the contact information. Calling a telephone number contained in the suspicious message can connect the buyer to the same impersonator who sent it. Use a previously established, independently verified contact channel for the closing office. Confirm the intended recipient and instructions through that channel, following the closing office’s secure process. Avoid placing full account details or sensitive identity documents in a broadly shared transaction message.

If a participant reports that funds were sent to a potentially fraudulent destination, the matter is urgent. Notify the broker and the relevant financial institution and closing participants through verified channels so they can take appropriate action. Preserve the original messages, headers, payment information, and chronology. The agent should not promise recovery or wait for a routine daily update before escalating. This is practical incident coordination; legal advice, financial-institution action, and any reporting obligations belong to the appropriate professionals.

Fraud prevention also includes accurate expectations before closing. Tell the client how legitimate instructions will be communicated and how changes will be verified under the brokerage and closing office procedures. A client who expects a verified process is less likely to treat an unexpected urgent message as routine. The agent should never imply that a familiar logo or the correct property address proves the sender’s identity; information from a transaction can be copied into an impersonation attempt.

Confirm insurance and possession separately. The buyer may need coverage effective at closing, and temporary occupancy by buyer or seller can require specific insurance advice. Utility transfer and moving arrangements should follow the agreed possession date. Do not deliver keys or encourage early occupancy based solely on convenience. Use the actual contract and temporary lease where applicable.

A worked closing draft contains a seller contribution that was never agreed, omits a deposit receipt, and includes a new reservation in the deed. These are separate discrepancies requiring different reviewers. The financial items should be reconciled with signed agreements and receipts; the deed issue requires title and legal review. The agent should not let the parties sign on an assurance that everything can be corrected later.

## T10U8 — Prorations

Proration divides an expense or income item between parties for their respective periods under the agreement. The contract determines who bears which period and whether later adjustment is contemplated. The calculation method must be stated: actual calendar days or another agreed convention, whether the closing day belongs to buyer or seller, the annual or monthly amount, and any estimate or credit already paid. Two people can calculate different correct arithmetic from different assumptions; establish the assumptions first.
### Adapted teaching: Debits, credits, taxes, rents, deposits, and transparent closing arithmetic

**The ledger's logic.** A closing statement is double-entry bookkeeping for the transaction:
every dollar appears as a **debit** (a charge) to one party or a **credit** (a receipt) to a
party, and the columns must reconcile to the penny before the escrow officer may disburse. The
recurring geography:

- **Buyer debits**: purchase price; lender's charges; title premiums per the contract's
  allocation; recording fees for the buyer's instruments; escrow fee share; prepaid insurance;
  reserves.
- **Buyer credits**: earnest money already deposited; the new loan's proceeds; the seller-paid
  prorations below.
- **Seller debits**: existing loan payoff with per-diem interest; releases and their recording;
  commissions; seller's escrow fee share; accrued-but-unpaid items prorated to the buyer.
- **Seller credits**: the purchase price; items the seller prepaid beyond closing, prorated back.

**And a last vocabulary pair.** *Settlement* and *closing* are the same event wearing federal
and Texas names respectively — the statutes of this module say "settlement," the Texas table
says "closing," and the licence holder translates freely between them, as this course has,
without letting either word obscure the two-stage truth underneath: signing, then funding.

**Rounding, stated once.** Statements compute to the penny and state their rounding: per diems
are carried to at least four decimal places before the final multiplication (the 365-day worked
example's $17.7534 shows why — rounding the per diem first shifts the result by whole cents),
and each final line item rounds to the cent. Small rules; real disputes prevented.

**Debit and credit, de-mystified once.** Students trip on the words because banking reversed
them in everyday speech, so fix the statement's usage plainly: on a closing statement a
**debit is a charge against a party** — it increases what that party must bring or reduces
what that party takes away — and a **credit is money in a party's favour**. The buyer's loan
is a buyer *credit* (it arrives on the buyer's behalf); the price is a buyer *debit* and a
seller *credit* (charged to one, owed to the other); the seller's payoff is a seller *debit*
(paid out of the seller's money at the table). Test any entry with one question — *whose
money, which direction?* — and the labels assign themselves.

**Net sheets: the statement's forecast.** The closing statement has a forward-looking cousin the
licence holder personally prepares: the **estimated net sheet**. For a seller at listing — price
scenarios minus payoff, commission, title and escrow charges per local custom, prorations
estimated — it answers "what will I walk away with?"; for a buyer writing an offer — cash to
close built from price, loan, deposits and estimated charges — it answers "what must I bring?"
Three professional rules govern every net sheet: *label it an estimate*, on its face, with its
assumptions stated (payoff unverified, taxes estimated at last year's figure); *source every
number you can* (order the payoff figure early — Principles I Module 08 taught why the recorded lien tells
you nothing about the balance); and *estimate conservatively*, because a seller told $85,000
who receives $89,398 forgives the variance in the pleasant direction only. The net sheet is
also where proration literacy pays daily: the agent who can run the June 15 arithmetic of this
unit fills the sheet's proration lines with computation, not guesswork.

**Prorations: the arithmetic of fairness.** Continuous charges — property taxes above all, plus
rents on tenant-occupied property, HOA dues, sometimes insurance — are divided between the
parties as of the closing date. The standard Texas pattern for taxes follows Principles I Module 03's law:
the year's taxes are a lien from January 1 (§ 32.01) but are billed late in the year, so at a
mid-year closing the *seller's* share — January 1 through the day before closing (or through
closing, as the contract allocates the day) — is **credited to the buyer** and debited to the
seller, and the buyer later pays the whole bill. Two computational conventions coexist, and the
statement states which it used: the **statutory/banker's year** (360 days, twelve 30-day months)
and the **actual/calendar year** (365 or 366 days, actual month lengths).

*Worked proration:* closing June 15; annual taxes estimated at $6,480; seller responsible
through June 14. **360-day method:** per diem = $6,480 ÷ 360 = $18.00; seller's share = 5 months
(Jan–May, 150 days) + 14 days = 164 days × $18.00 = **$2,952.00** — buyer credit, seller debit.
**365-day method:** per diem = $6,480 ÷ 365 = $17.7534…; seller's days = 31+28+31+30+31+14 =
165; 165 × $17.7534 = **$2,929.32**. The methods differ by $22.68 on the same facts — the reason
the method is a stated term, not a rounding choice. *Worked rent proration:* a tenant-occupied
property closes June 15 with June rent of $1,800 collected by the seller on June 1; the buyer
owns June 15–30 (16 days of a 30-day month): buyer credit = 16/30 × $1,800 = **$960.00**. And
the tenant's **security deposit** transfers to the buyer as a credit in full — it is the
tenant's money changing custodians, not income to prorate.

**HOA dues and the day itself.** Two conventions complete the proration toolkit. *Association
dues* prorate exactly like taxes but usually run monthly and prepaid: a $360 monthly assessment
paid by the seller on June 1, with closing June 15 and the buyer owning the 15th onward under
the contract's allocation, returns 16/30 × $360 = **$192.00** to the seller — a seller credit
and buyer debit, the mirror of the tax proration's direction, because here the seller *prepaid*
what the buyer will enjoy. *The day of closing* belongs to whichever party the contract says —
proration clauses state it expressly — and the statement's dates must match that allocation;
"through the day before closing" and "through closing" differ by one per diem on every prorated
line, which on the worked tax figures is a visible $18.00. Neither convention is law; both are
contract terms, which is why the agent's review starts from the contract every time.

**A complete miniature statement, reconciled.** Assemble a whole (simplified) closing to see the
columns prove themselves. *Facts: price $300,000; buyer's new loan $240,000; earnest money
$3,000 held by the title company; seller's payoff $187,400; commission 6% split evenly;
seller's escrow fee share $450, buyer's $450; owner's title premium $1,800 paid by seller per
contract; buyer's lender charges $4,200; recording, buyer's instruments, $180; closing June 15,
annual taxes $6,480 prorated on the 360-day method, seller through June 14 (share $2,952.00).*

*Buyer's column* — debits: price $300,000; lender charges $4,200; escrow fee $450; recording
$180 → **$304,830**. Credits: loan $240,000; earnest money $3,000; tax proration $2,952 →
**$245,952**. **Cash due from buyer = $58,878.00.**

*Seller's column* — credits: price **$300,000**. Debits: payoff $187,400; commission $18,000;
owner's premium $1,800; escrow fee $450; tax proration $2,952 → **$210,602**. **Cash due to
seller = $89,398.00.**

*The escrow officer's proof*: receipts (buyer's cash $58,878 + loan $240,000 + earnest money
$3,000 = $301,878) equal disbursements (seller's proceeds $89,398 + payoff $187,400 +
commission $18,000 + owner's premium $1,800 + both escrow fees $900 + lender charges $4,200 +
recording $180 = $301,878). To the penny — and notice *why* it balances: the price and the
proration each appear once in each column, opposite signs, and every third-party charge is
collected from exactly one party. When a real statement refuses to reconcile, the error is
almost always one of those symmetries broken — a proration entered on one side only, a fee
collected twice — and the reviewer who understands the symmetry finds it in minutes.


Consider a hypothetical calendar-year tax amount of $7,300 in a 365-day year. The daily amount is $20. If the agreement assigns 100 days to the seller, the seller’s share is $2,000. If the buyer will pay the eventual bill, that allocation commonly appears as a seller charge and buyer credit. If the seller already paid the bill, the reimbursement direction can reverse for the buyer’s share. The arithmetic is incomplete until payment status and contractual allocation are known.

Do not confuse a proration with a payoff or a lender escrow deposit. A mortgage payoff clears specified debt. A lender’s initial escrow collection funds future taxes or insurance. A tax proration allocates the buyer-seller burden for the agreed period. The same closing can contain all three without duplicating the same obligation. Reconcile each line to its source before calling it an error.

Deposits and prepaid rent also differ. A tenant’s security deposit may transfer to the purchaser with corresponding obligations; it is not earned rental income. Rent received for a period spanning closing may require allocation. A delinquent rent amount raises a different question from rent already collected. Obtain the lease and ledger and apply the actual agreement rather than assuming every tenant-related amount is prorated identically.

### A complete proration explanation includes the direction of payment

Take a hypothetical property with annual taxes estimated at $10,950 in a 365-day year. The daily amount is $30. The contract’s agreed convention allocates 150 days to the seller and 215 days to the buyer. The seller’s share is $4,500 and the buyer’s share is $6,450. These figures allocate the burden; they do not yet tell the closing officer which party receives a credit.

If the taxes are unpaid and the buyer will pay the full bill, the seller’s $4,500 share commonly becomes a seller debit and buyer credit under the agreement. The buyer receives that credit because the buyer later pays a bill that includes the seller’s period. If the seller already paid the full annual bill, the buyer instead may reimburse the seller for the buyer’s $6,450 period. The same time allocation produces a different settlement direction because the payment history differs.

Now add a lender escrow collection of $1,825. That line is not automatically a duplicate tax charge. The lender may be collecting reserves for future payments under the loan arrangement. Compare the escrow disclosure and requirements with the buyer-seller proration. A payoff line for the seller’s existing loan is different again. Each amount needs its own source, purpose, and recipient.

For rental income, assume monthly rent of $1,500 has been received for a 30-day month and the agreement assigns 12 days to the seller. The daily amount is $50; the seller’s share is $600 and the buyer’s is $900. If the seller holds the entire payment, the buyer’s share requires the agreed credit or transfer. If the tenant has not paid, the issue includes who bears the receivable and collection risk. Do not credit collected rent that does not exist without a contractual basis.

A security deposit should remain distinct from rent. Its transfer concerns custody and obligations under the lease and applicable landlord-tenant law, not allocation of earned income. Obtain the ledger and actual deposit amount. An estimate copied from a rent roll can omit a later increase, deduction, or transfer. Accurate closing arithmetic begins with accurate source records.

Finally, describe every estimate as an estimate. A tax proration based on a prior bill or a provisional current figure can require later adjustment under the agreement. Do not promise that the closing amount is the final tax liability for every purpose. Identify the calculation assumptions, payment status, and any continuing adjustment obligation so the parties understand both the current settlement and the work that may remain.

## T10U9 — Closing and Funding

Closing is the coordinated completion of the transaction under the governing documents. Signing, lender authorization, funding, disbursement, delivery, recording, and possession are related events that can occur at different times. Use precise status language. “Documents signed” is not identical to “funds received and authorized for disbursement.” “Deed recorded” is not a complete statement about possession under a temporary lease.

The agent should confirm status through the authorized closing participants and follow the contract’s possession terms. Do not release keys because the parties have left the signing room if funding or other conditions remain. Do not tell the seller that proceeds have been disbursed merely because the lender sent a wire confirmation to another participant. Obtain the appropriate confirmation and preserve it in the file.

If funding is delayed, identify the cause and next action. A lender condition, document correction, missing authority evidence, wire problem, or federal disclosure issue requires a different response. Notify the broker and client promptly, coordinate with the appropriate participant, and obtain any necessary legal advice or amendment. An agent should not promise that an informal grace period exists or that moving in will solve the funding problem.

At completion, the parties should have their copies of the relevant documents and a clear record of remaining obligations. Temporary possession, agreed repairs or deliverables, tax adjustments, deposit transfers, and recorded releases may require follow-up. The broker’s record-retention duty under 22 TAC § 535.2 is separate from the parties’ legal claims and the lender’s document retention. Preserve the complete transaction file, including substantive communications and compensation records.

A worked final status might read: “Both parties signed the closing documents. The escrow officer confirms buyer funds and lender authorization have been received and disbursement is complete. Seller possession continues under the signed temporary lease through the stated date.” This identifies evidence and the remaining occupancy arrangement. It is more useful than “closed,” which can leave the client unsure about keys, money, or responsibilities.

If the transaction fails to close, the file still needs an orderly conclusion. Preserve the accepted contract, amendments, performance evidence, notices, failed conditions, escrow status, and legal referrals. Do not delete an unsuccessful file or treat the absence of a deed as the absence of obligations. The termination or dispute can generate the very questions that require a reliable record later.

### A delayed funding case and a precise final record

Assume the buyer and seller sign on a Friday morning. The lender requests one corrected document before authorizing disbursement. The buyer’s funds are at the closing office, but lender funds are not yet authorized. The seller’s agent asks whether keys may be released because the buyer has a moving truck outside. The accurate status is that signing occurred and funding remains pending. The possession provision and any temporary lease must govern the next step.

The agent should contact the authorized closing participant, identify the outstanding condition, inform the client and broker, and coordinate the needed correction. The agent should not promise that funding will occur that afternoon or create an informal occupancy arrangement. If the parties wish to change possession or timing, use the appropriate authorized documentation and legal advice. Early occupancy can create risks concerning insurance, damage, liability, and failure to fund.

When authorization arrives, obtain the appropriate confirmation of disbursement and completion rather than inferring it from an email saying the lender is satisfied. Different participants may use the word funded to describe different stages. Ask the closing officer what has actually occurred and what remains. A precise confirmation supports accurate instructions about keys and proceeds.

The final record should include the operative contract and amendments, closing documents, settlement figures, funding and disbursement confirmation as appropriate, possession arrangements, and unresolved follow-up items. If a seller remains under a temporary lease, the file is not simply inactive until move-out. Calendar the agreed surrender and relevant obligations. If a release or correction will be recorded later, identify the responsible participant and expected evidence.

This approach also helps when the transaction does not complete. The absence of funding should be recorded with the actual reason and communications. A failed closing can leave contractual rights, escrow questions, and professional duties unresolved. Preserve the file and refer legal disputes promptly. A reliable final status describes what occurred; it does not convert disappointment into an unsupported legal conclusion about default or entitlement.


### Statute reading exercise: A corrected disclosure is not always a new waiting period

Read 12 CFR § 1026.19(f)(1)(ii) and (f)(2). Identify the initial receipt timing and the specified changes that require a new waiting period. Explain why a minor fee correction and a loan-product change should be referred for different analyses rather than treated alike.

Feedback: The initial covered Closing Disclosure timing and the revised-disclosure rules are distinct. Section 1026.19(f)(2)(ii) identifies the specified APR, loan-product, and prepayment-penalty changes. Other corrections have their own timing treatment. The lender or closing professional should apply the rule to the actual facts and business-day definition.

### Applied exercise: The missing $4,000 deposit

The buyer’s closing draft omits a $4,000 earnest-money deposit. The contract and escrow receipt show it was received. Identify the evidence and participants needed to correct the statement and explain why the agent should not change the buyer’s wire amount unilaterally.

Feedback: Provide the operative contract and escrow receipt to the closing professional and broker, reconcile the draft, and obtain corrected authorized instructions. The agent should not alter required funds independently because other figures or lender conditions may be affected. The buyer needs the correct final statement and verified payment instructions.

### Applied exercise: Signed but not funded

The buyer signs at noon and asks for keys. The lender still needs proof of an agreed repair, and the escrow officer has not confirmed funding. The seller’s movers are waiting. Prepare an accurate status explanation and list the next steps.

Feedback: Signatures are complete, but funding is not confirmed. Obtain the required repair evidence through the appropriate participants, clarify lender and escrow status, follow the contract’s possession terms, and involve the broker if timing creates a dispute or amendment need. Do not release keys or promise an automatic extension based on the signing appointment.


### Module recap

A reliable closing follows the contract’s actual calendar and documents. Track approval stages, inspections, title requirements, amendments, disclosure timing, and financial reconciliation separately. State proration assumptions before calculating. Confirm funding and possession through their proper sources, preserve remaining obligations, and retain the complete file whether the sale closes or terminates.
