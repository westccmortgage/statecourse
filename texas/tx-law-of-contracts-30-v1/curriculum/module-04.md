## T04U1 — Interests in Real Estate

A property can support several interests at once. A fee owner may grant a lease, mortgage the fee, convey minerals, and allow an easement. Each instrument concerns a particular interest. A purchaser needs to know which interests will be acquired and which will remain outstanding. “Full ownership” in a conversation is not a substitute for the actual conveyance and title evidence.
### Adapted teaching: The estate vocabulary and the continuing limits on private ownership

*This unit is history. It asserts no current legal rule, and no statute is cited for it.*

The vocabulary of this module — estate, fee, tenement, tenure — is medieval English, and knowing the
story keeps the terms from being arbitrary. After the Norman Conquest, England organised land under
**feudal tenure**: all land was ultimately the Crown's, and everyone below the Crown *held of*
someone above in exchange for services — military service, labour, produce. What a person owned was
not the land itself but an **estate**: a bundle of rights in land, measured by duration. The word
**fee** (from *fief*) named an estate that could be inherited; **fee simple** was the fief with the
fewest strings — inheritable by anyone, alienable, enduring indefinitely.

Over centuries the services fell away, statutes made estates freely transferable and devisable, and
tenure hollowed out into a formality. The system that crossed the Atlantic was **allodial** in
substance: individuals own land outright, subject not to a lord's services but to the sovereign's
four residual powers — taxation, eminent domain, police power and escheat — which this course meets
in later units and modules. Texas adds its own layer of history: much of the state was originally
granted under Spanish and Mexican civil law, and the traces persist — surveying in **varas** (the
Spanish unit you will meet in Principles I Module 04), community property (Principles I Module 05), and generous homestead
protection (this module's final unit), all of them civil-law inheritances that distinguish Texas from
the purely English-derived states.

**The four residual powers, introduced.** The allodial owner's title is complete against every
private claimant but remains subject to four powers every sovereign keeps, and it is worth meeting
them here as a set because the rest of the course takes them up one at a time. **Taxation** — the
government's charge on ownership itself; this module's Tax Liens unit shows Texas securing it with
the strongest ordinary lien in the state. **Eminent domain** — the power to take private property
for public use on payment of compensation, exercised through condemnation; Principles I Module 07 places it among
the involuntary transfers. **Police power** — the power to regulate use without taking title:
zoning, building codes, subdivision platting (Principles I Module 04 shows the county version), environmental
rules. Regulation under the police power is not compensated; taking under eminent domain is — and
the line between them is one of the most litigated boundaries in American law, which is precisely
why a licence holder describes land-use restrictions and never opines on their validity.
**Escheat** — the state's taking of property left ownerless, the modern echo of land returning to
the lord when a tenant died without heirs; Principles I Module 07 meets it at the end of the intestacy ladder.
Four powers, four later landings: the frame built in this unit carries the whole course.

Why keep teaching the feudal frame at all? Because the *duration* idea survives intact. Modern law
still classifies interests by how long they last — forever, for a life, for a term of years — and
still uses the medieval names. When a Texas statute says a conveyed estate is a "fee simple," it is
speaking this language; the next unit shows Texas speaking it plainly.


Possessory interests concern present or future possession. Security interests support payment or another obligation. Easements concern specified uses of another’s land. Restrictive covenants constrain use through private instruments. These categories can coexist without being interchangeable. An easement holder may have a right to cross a tract while lacking a right to occupy its house; a tenant may possess the house while lacking power to convey the owner’s fee.

The contract review should identify the interest promised, its duration, known third-party rights, and any reserved interest. A seller with a life estate needs a different authority and title inquiry from a fee owner. A tract subject to a grazing lease requires attention to the lease and possession. A buyer purchasing only an undivided share needs to understand the shared nature of ownership. Use appropriate counsel for interpretation and custom drafting before committing the parties to an unsuitable conveyance.

## T04U2 — Governmental Powers

Government can regulate, tax, acquire, and in appropriate circumstances receive ownerless property. These powers are distinct. Zoning regulates use without necessarily acquiring title. Eminent domain involves acquisition under lawful authority and applicable compensation procedures. Taxation supports public revenue and can create liens. Escheat addresses property passing to the state under applicable law when qualifying ownership or succession is absent. A sales contract cannot eliminate these powers.
### Adapted teaching: Acquisition, dedication, and public interests in land and water

The previous unit dealt with government telling an owner what may be done with land the owner keeps. This unit deals with government acquiring, holding, and administering interests in land itself — and with the line between the two, because that line decides whether an owner is paid.

**Regulation versus acquisition.** When a municipality zones a tract, it exercises the police power described in Local Government Code § 211.001, and the owner keeps the land. When a governmental body takes an interest in the tract for public use, it must acquire that interest, and the owner must be compensated. The two elements ordinarily stated for a valid exercise of **eminent domain** — the power of a governmental entity to take private property — are that the taking be for a **public use** and that the owner receive **just compensation**. The Texas statutes read for this course confirm the compensation element without setting out the constitutional standard, and this module does not state constitutional text that was not read. Water Code § 11.035(b) provides that "[t]he party obtaining private property by condemnation shall cause damages to be assessed and paid for as provided by the statutes of this state relating to eminent domain." The word **condemnation** names the *procedure* by which the power of eminent domain is exercised; a common error is to use the two terms interchangeably.

**Who holds the power.** Eminent domain is not held by every public body simply because it is public. Water Code § 11.033 states the delegation for water-related purposes: "All political subdivisions of the state and constitutional governmental agencies exercising delegated legislative powers have the power of eminent domain to be exercised as provided by law for domestic, municipal, and manufacturing uses and for other purposes authorized by this code, including the irrigation of land for all requirements of agricultural employment." The same section declares that "[t]he right to take water necessary for domestic and municipal supply purposes is primary and fundamental." A condemning authority must therefore point to a delegation, and the taking must fall within it.

Texas has also delegated condemnation power to private water appropriators, under supervision, and the sequence shows the shape of the framework. Water Code § 11.035(a) provides that "[a]n appropriator may obtain rights-of-way over private land and may obtain the land necessary for pumping plants, intakes, headgates, and storage reservoirs by condemnation." Section 11.035(d) requires investigation, notice to the landowner, and a hearing; and § 11.035(e) provides that if the commission determines the condemnation is necessary, the executive director may institute condemnation proceedings "in the name of the State of Texas for the use and benefit of the party who applied for the condemnation and all others similarly situated." The closing words matter: the taking is not made for the applicant alone, and § 11.035(g) lets another party later apply to the commission to take the benefits of the proceeding on fees the commission fixes. A delegated power, a statutory public purpose, a determination of necessity, notice and hearing, and assessed damages paid to the owner.

**Dedication.** Not every public interest in land is taken; much of it is given. **Dedication** is the owner's offer of land to public use, and the platting statutes carry the mechanics. Local Government Code § 212.004(a) speaks of laying out "streets, alleys, squares, parks, or other parts of the tract intended by the owner of the tract to be dedicated to public use," and § 212.004(b)(3) requires the plat to state the dimensions of each such part. The offer is not self-executing. Section 212.011(a) provides that "[t]he approval of a plat is not considered an acceptance of any proposed dedication and does not impose on the municipality any duty regarding the maintenance or improvement of any dedicated parts until the appropriate municipal authorities make an actual appropriation of the dedicated parts by entry, use, or improvement," and § 212.011(b) provides that disapproval "is considered a refusal by the municipality of the offered dedication indicated on the plat."

Dedication in Texas platting therefore has two sides: an **offer** shown on the plat and an **acceptance** shown by entry, use, or improvement. A buyer looking at a platted "park" or a platted street stub should not assume the city owns or maintains it. The Legislature has also limited what a city may demand: § 212.010(c) provides that the responsible authority "may not require the dedication of land within a subdivision for a future street or alley that is: (1) not intended by the owner of the tract; and (2) not included, funded, and approved in" an adopted capital improvement plan of the municipality, or a similar plan of the county or the state. And once a public corridor exists, private instruments cannot narrow it: Local Government Code § 212.153(e) provides that a municipality "may not enforce a deed restriction which purports to regulate or restrict the rights granted to public utilities to install, operate, maintain, replace, and remove facilities within easements and private or public rights-of-way."

**State ownership of water, and the surveying rule behind streambeds.** Some public ownership in Texas is very old and very broad. Water Code § 11.021(a) provides that "[t]he water of the ordinary flow, underflow, and tides of every flowing river, natural stream, and lake, and of every bay or arm of the Gulf of Mexico, and the storm water, floodwater, and rainwater of every river, natural stream, canyon, ravine, depression, and watershed in the state is the property of the state." Section 11.0235(a) adds that "[t]he waters of the state are held in trust for the public, and the right to use state water may be appropriated only as expressly authorized by law," and § 11.022 provides that the right to use state water "may be acquired by appropriation in the manner and for the purposes provided in this chapter" — the purposes § 11.023 lists, including domestic and municipal supply, agriculture, industry, mining, navigation, recreation, and "any other beneficial use." The consequence for a transaction is direct: a buyer of riverfront acreage does not buy the flowing water. If the buyer's plan requires diverting or impounding state water, the buyer needs a right acquired under Chapter 11, and a license holder should say so and refer the client rather than guess.

The public interest in streambeds has a surveying origin worth knowing. Natural Resources Code § 21.001(3) defines a **navigable stream** as "a stream which retains an average width of 30 feet from the mouth up." Section 21.012(a) provides that land surveyed for individuals lying on a navigable stream "shall front one-half of the square on the stream with the line running at right angles with the general course of the stream," where prior lines permit, and § 21.012(b) states the rule flatly: "A navigable stream may not be crossed by the lines of a survey." Section 21.011 requires each survey of public land to be made under authority of law by a surveyor duly appointed, elected, or licensed and qualified. The practical takeaway is that a boundary along a qualifying stream is not a matter of the seller's opinion, and a question near a stream of that size belongs to a licensed surveyor.

**The government as landowner.** Government does not merely regulate; it owns, and when it owns the regulatory picture inverts. Local Government Code § 211.013(c) provides that the municipal zoning subchapter "does not apply to a building, other structure, or land under the control, administration, or jurisdiction of a state or federal agency," while § 211.013(d) applies the subchapter to privately owned land and buildings "when leased to a state agency" — ownership, not occupancy, drives the exemption.

**Publicly financed improvements attached to private lots.** A buyer can acquire a private lot and, with it, an obligation to help pay for public improvements. Property Code § 5.014(a) requires a person who proposes to sell or convey real property located in a **public improvement district** to give the purchaser a prescribed written notice first. The notice text in § 5.014(a-1) tells the purchaser, in capital letters, that "AN ASSESSMENT HAS BEEN LEVIED AGAINST YOUR PROPERTY FOR THE AUTHORIZED IMPROVEMENTS, WHICH MAY BE PAID IN FULL AT ANY TIME," that unpaid balances come due in annual installments varying year to year, and that failure to pay "may result in penalties and interest being added to what you owe or in a lien on and the foreclosure of your property." A license holder working a subdivision built with district financing should expect this notice and never treat it as boilerplate.

**Escheat.** Where an owner dies leaving no person entitled to take the property and no valid disposition of it, the doctrine of **escheat** directs the property to the state. That is the general doctrine; no source read for this course sets out the Texas escheat procedure, waiting periods, or filing requirements, and this module therefore states no mechanics and cites nothing for them. A license holder who encounters a property with no locatable owner or heir should treat it as a title matter for the title company and counsel.


For a transaction example, a seller discloses a proposed road-widening project. The agent should obtain the available project notices, identify whether a taking has been proposed or completed, and communicate the facts. The agent should not estimate the legal adequacy of compensation or promise that the project cannot affect the property because no construction equipment is visible. Counsel should evaluate the acquisition and the contract’s risk allocation; the title reviewer should evaluate recorded interests.

Public regulation and private ownership also intersect with remedies. A buyer whose intended use is barred does not automatically have a breach claim against the seller. The agreement, disclosures, representations, investigation rights, and actual facts matter. The agent’s role is to protect an informed decision and preserve the opportunity to use the contractual process, not to promise a later lawsuit will restore the buyer’s position.

## T04U3 — Estates in Land

An estate describes the extent and duration of an ownership or possession interest. It answers what the holder has and how long it can last. A fee simple, a life estate, and a leasehold are not different grades of the same deed; they carry different boundaries. The instrument creating the estate and the governing law must be read together.
### Adapted teaching: Freehold, defeasible, life, and leasehold interests

An **estate** is an interest in real property that is or may become possessory, measured by duration.
Interests that never carry possession — easements, liens — are not estates; they are the subject of
the encumbrance units that follow.

**Freehold estates** last for an indefinite duration.

- **Fee simple absolute** — the greatest estate known to law: indefinite duration, fully inheritable,
  fully transferable, subject only to the sovereign powers and to whatever encumbrances its owner
  created or suffered. Texas states a powerful default in Property Code § 5.001: *an estate in land
  that is conveyed or devised is a fee simple unless the estate is limited by express words or unless
  a lesser estate is conveyed or devised by construction or operation of law* — and the ancient words
  of inheritance ("to A *and his heirs*") are expressly unnecessary. The practical meaning for every
  deed you will ever read: silence means fee simple; a lesser estate must be spelled out.
- **Defeasible fees** — fee estates that can end on a stated event: "to the school district so long
  as the land is used for a school." The holder has a fee that may last forever but is *defeasible* —
  capable of being defeated — with a future interest (a possibility of reverter or right of re-entry)
  waiting in the grantor or the grantor's successors. These appear in Texas practice mostly in old
  grants for schools, churches and railways, and they matter to a licence holder chiefly as a title
  finding that demands a lawyer, not a form.
- **Life estate** — an estate measured by a life: "to my mother for her life." The life tenant
  possesses and uses the property, and owes the future interest holder the duty not to commit
  **waste** — not to consume or degrade what must be handed on. At the measuring life's end the
  property passes to the **remainderman** (if the future interest was granted to a third person) or
  **reverts** to the grantor's successors (a *reversion*). Texas recognises the creation of future
  estates by ordinary deed: Property Code § 5.041 permits an inter vivos conveyance of a freehold
  estate *that commences in the future*, as freely as by will. Life estates arise in Texas both by
  deliberate estate planning and — as Principles I Module 07 shows — by operation of the intestacy statute, which
  gives a surviving spouse a life estate in one-third of certain land.

**Leasehold (non-freehold) estates** last for a definite or terminable period — the tenant's
possessory interest under a lease. The classical four:

- **Estate for years** — a fixed term with a stated end date, however short or long; no notice is
  needed to end it, because the calendar ends it.
- **Periodic tenancy** — repeating periods (month to month) that renew until notice.
- **Estate at will** — possession with the owner's consent, no fixed term, terminable by either
  party.
- **Tenancy at sufferance** — the holdover: a tenant who entered rightfully and stays after the
  right ends, possessing without consent but not (yet) a trespasser.

Two Texas anchors for leaseholds now, both already met: a leasehold is "real estate" in a licence
holder's hands (Occupations Code § 1101.002(5) includes leaseholds expressly), and a conveyance of an
estate *for more than one year* must satisfy the writing requirements of Property Code § 5.021 —
which is why the one-year line runs through leasing practice, and why Principles I Module 06 returns to it through
the statute of frauds.

**Reading an estate.** Every question about an estate is answered by three smaller questions: How
long does it last? What can end it early? Who takes when it ends? Run those three against any
granting language — "to A for life, then to B"; "to the church while used for worship"; "to T for
five years" — and the estate names fall out mechanically. The licence holder's discipline is humbler
and harder: *notice* when granting language is anything other than a plain fee, and route it to
counsel instead of improvising.

**The estate ladder, worked.** Classification is a skill, not a memory feat, and it is learned by
running the three questions against real granting language. Work each of the following before
reading its resolution.

1. *"To the City of Alpine, so long as the land is used as a public park."* — How long? Potentially
   forever. What ends it early? Non-park use. Who takes? The grantor's successors, holding the
   possibility of reverter. A defeasible fee; a title examiner will flag it, and no buyer should be
   told it is "basically a fee simple."
2. *"To my sister Carmen."* — No duration stated, no condition, no words of inheritance. Under
   Property Code § 5.001 the silence itself answers: fee simple absolute, because a conveyed estate
   is a fee simple *unless limited by express words*, and words of inheritance are unnecessary.
3. *"To Dr. Osei for her life, then to the county hospital district."* — A life estate in Dr. Osei,
   who possesses, pays the carrying costs, and must not commit waste; a remainder in the district,
   which waits. The district's interest is a present, transferable property interest even though
   possession is postponed — exactly the future-estate kind that Property Code § 5.041 lets a deed
   create as freely as a will.
4. *"Lease commencing March 1 and ending the last day of February two years later."* — An estate for
   years: fixed term, ends by the calendar, no notice required. Being longer than one year, it lives
   under the writing requirement the statute of frauds materials in Principles I Module 06 develop.
5. *"Tenant may remain month to month after the initial term at the same rent."* — A periodic
   tenancy after the term ends: repeating periods renewing until proper notice.

The pattern to carry out of the drill: the deed's *granting and habendum language* controls, the
statutory default fills silence with fee simple, and every departure from a plain fee is a finding
to disclose and route — never to interpret away.


Suppose a life tenant signs a contract promising an unrestricted fee. The buyer’s contract expectation and the seller’s estate differ. The life tenant cannot convey a greater estate merely by using a general form. The remainderman or other affected holder may need to participate, or a different transaction may be required. The agent should report the title evidence and obtain professional review. Do not erase the life-estate language from an old deed or treat a closing date as extending the measuring life.

A future interest also requires careful communication. A remainderman may hold a valuable interest while another person has current possession. That distinction affects showing access, possession promises, insurance questions, and the ability to deliver the contemplated estate. The physical occupant, record owner, and person negotiating the sale may be different people. Establish each role before writing commitments about vacancy or possession.

## T04U4 — Encumbrances

An encumbrance burdens an interest in property. It need not prevent a sale, but it can affect what the buyer receives, how the property may be used, and what must occur before closing. Monetary encumbrances commonly require payoff, release, subordination, or other treatment; nonmonetary burdens may continue after conveyance. A contract objection process gives the parties a structured way to address identified matters, but the right and timing come from the actual agreement.
### Adapted teaching: Liens and nonmonetary burdens are different problems

An **encumbrance** is any right or interest held by someone other than the owner that burdens the
property — diminishing its value or restricting its use — without preventing transfer. The owner can
sell; the buyer takes subject to the encumbrance. Encumbrances divide cleanly in two.

**Money encumbrances — liens.** A **lien** secures a debt: it gives the creditor the right, on
default, to have the property sold and be paid from the proceeds. Property Code § 51.0001 gives the
Texas vocabulary for the consensual kind: a **security instrument** is *a deed of trust, mortgage, or
other contract lien on an interest in real property*; the **mortgagor** is the grantor of the
security instrument (the borrower), the **mortgagee** its holder; a **trustee** or **substitute
trustee** is the person authorised to exercise the power of sale. Liens are classified on two axes
you should apply to every lien you meet:

- **Voluntary or involuntary** — created by the owner's agreement (a deed of trust) or by operation
  of law against the owner's wishes (tax liens, judgment liens, mechanic's liens).
- **General or specific** — reaching all the debtor's non-exempt property (a judgment) or only the
  particular parcel (a property tax lien, a deed of trust, a mechanic's lien on the improved
  property).

The two units after this one walk the involuntary liens in detail; Principles I Module 08 gives the deed of trust
its own ninety minutes.

**Non-money encumbrances** burden use rather than secure debt.

- **Easements** — non-possessory rights to use another's land for a defined purpose. An **easement
  appurtenant** serves a neighbouring parcel: the burdened land is the *servient* estate, the
  benefited land the *dominant* estate, and the easement passes automatically with the dominant land.
  An **easement in gross** serves a person or company rather than a parcel — the utility line, the
  pipeline — and in commercial form is transferable. Easements arise by express grant or reservation
  in a recorded instrument; by implication from prior use or by necessity when a conveyance leaves a
  parcel landlocked; and by **prescription** — long, open, adverse use, the easement cousin of the
  adverse possession doctrines you will meet in Principles I Module 07. A licence holder's duties are to spot
  easements in the title commitment, walk the land with them in mind (that mowed strip under the
  power line is somebody's right), and never to assure a buyer that an easement is harmless.
- **Restrictive covenants** — private promises recorded against land that bind successive owners:
  subdivision schemes of use, setback, size and architecture. They are enforced by the persons they
  benefit (neighbours, owners' associations), they coexist with zoning, and in practice the stricter
  of the two controls what an owner may actually build.
- **Encroachments** — physical intrusions of a structure across a boundary: the fence two feet over,
  the eave overhanging. An encroachment is discovered by survey, not by the deed records, which is
  one of the two standing reasons every buyer is urged to obtain a new survey (the other is Module
  04's business). Encroachments cloud marketability in both directions — the intruding owner may be
  trespassing; the suffering owner may be losing land toward a prescriptive claim.
- **Licences** — mere permissions to enter, personal and ordinarily revocable; not interests in land
  at all, listed here because buyers routinely mistake a neighbour's long-tolerated permission for an
  easement. The distinction is the difference between a right and a favour.

### Adapted teaching: Voluntary and statutory liens and their consequences for a conveyance

The remaining liens divide by how they arise.

**Voluntary: the deed of trust.** The security instrument of § 51.0001 — the borrower's own grant of
a lien with a power of sale. Named here for completeness; Principles I Module 08 is devoted to it.

**Involuntary liens** arise by operation of law:

- **Judgment liens.** A money judgment, once properly abstracted into a county's records, becomes a
  lien on the debtor's non-exempt real property in that county — a *general* lien, reaching whatever
  non-exempt land the debtor owns or acquires there. Two Texas limits tame it: the homestead is not
  "non-exempt" property (this module's final unit), and enforcement is subject to the limitation
  periods of the Civil Practice & Remedies Code you will meet in Principles I Modules 06 and 07. For practice: a
  judgment against a seller found in the title search must be released, paid or negotiated before
  closing; a judgment against a *similar name* is resolved by the title company's identity
  procedures, not by the licence holder's guess.
- **Mechanic's and materialman's liens.** Texas gives those who improve land a specific, involuntary
  lien on what they improved, and gives it generously. Property Code § 53.021 extends the lien to a
  person who, under a contract with the owner or the owner's agent, contractor or subcontractor,
  *labors or furnishes labor or materials* for construction or repair of an improvement; to one who
  *specially fabricates* material **even if the material is never delivered**; to licensed
  architects, engineers and surveyors for their plans, plats and surveys; to landscapers of an
  improvement; and to demolition contractors. Section 53.001 defines "improvement" just as broadly —
  buildings and structures of every kind, clearing, fencing, machinery, and design work itself. Two
  practice consequences follow. *First*, recent construction on a listing means potential lien
  claimants the deed records do not yet show — the general contractor may be paid while a
  subcontractor or the lumberyard is not, and their claims can surface after closing. *Second*,
  because the lien can secure work contracted by an "agent, trustee, receiver, contractor, or
  subcontractor," the owner's own diligence — retainage practices, lien waivers, affidavits of bills
  paid at closing — is the real protection, and Texas closing practice is built around collecting
  them. The licence holder's reflex, whenever the phrase "we just remodelled" appears: ask what was
  done, when, by whom, and whether everyone has been paid — and route the paper questions to the
  title company early.
- **Other statutory and equitable liens** — federal tax liens, owners'-association assessment liens
  (which § 32.05 mentions precisely because the property tax lien outranks them), landlord's and
  vendor's liens — populate title searches and are handled, like all title matters, by requiring
  their release or payoff at closing.

**Where the mechanic's lien meets the homestead.** The two strongest ideas in this module collide
on residential remodels. Property Code § 41.001(b)(3) allows a work-and-material encumbrance to be
properly fixed on a homestead only *if contracted for in writing as provided by Sections 53.254(a),
(b), and (c)* — the statute conditions the lien's validity against a homestead on formalities the
parties must satisfy up front, in writing. This course has read § 41.001's cross-reference, not
§ 53.254 itself, so the content of those formalities is left to counsel; the licence holder's
operational rule needs no more: a contractor's claim against a *homestead* stands on different, and
stricter, footing than the same claim against investment property, and a handshake remodel of a
family home is a title problem in the making on both sides of the deal.

**Reading a lien situation.** For any lien, ask the same four questions in order: What debt does it
secure? Is it voluntary or involuntary? Is it general or specific? Where does it rank? Rank —
priority — is usually first-in-time by recording date, except where a statute says otherwise; you
have now met the great exception (the tax lien), and Principles I Module 08 completes the picture with
foreclosure, where rank becomes money.


A neighbor’s fence extending across a survey line is evidence of a physical condition; it is not automatically proof of an easement or adverse-possession title. A recorded easement is a legal instrument; the location, permitted use, maintenance obligations, and duration can matter. A restrictive covenant may affect the planned studio even though it does not appear as a visible object on a site visit. Send the relevant instruments and survey to the appropriate reviewers and track the buyer’s deadline.

When a seller says a lien was paid, request release evidence through the title process. Payment and the public record’s status are different facts. When a seller says an easement is “never used,” do not infer that it disappeared. When a buyer says a recorded restriction is unfair, do not assume unenforceability. The professional habit is to identify the burden accurately and obtain the correct resolution mechanism.

## T04U5 — Water Rights

Water questions begin with classification. Surface water in defined watercourses, groundwater beneath land, and diffuse surface runoff are not governed identically. A waterfront photograph does not establish a right to withdraw a stated amount of water. A well does not establish its permitted production, quality, or adequacy for the buyer’s planned use. A lake boundary does not guarantee a constant water level.
### Adapted teaching: State water, groundwater, and drainage questions

Water rights in Texas are a body of law large enough for its own course; the principles a licence
holder must carry are few but firm.

**Surface water in watercourses belongs to the state.** Water Code § 11.021, read in full for this
course, declares that *the water of the ordinary flow, underflow and tides of every flowing river,
natural stream and lake, and of every bay or arm of the Gulf of Mexico, and the storm water,
floodwater and rainwater of every river, natural stream, canyon, ravine, depression and watershed in
the state is the property of the state*. A deed bounded by a river conveys land; the flowing water is
Texas's, used by private persons only under rights granted through the state's permitting system.
Riparian frontage is access and amenity — it is not ownership of the stream.

**Groundwater is different in kind.** Percolating groundwater under a parcel is treated in Texas as
part of the landowner's property, historically governed by the **rule of capture** — an owner may
pump what the owner's wells can produce — with modern management layered on through local groundwater
conservation districts that permit and space wells in much of the state. The licence holder's
takeaways: a working well is not proof of a *right* to pump indefinitely; district rules vary by
county; and groundwater estates, like mineral estates, can be severed and sold apart from the surface.

**Diffuse surface water** — sheet runoff before it reaches a watercourse — follows drainage
doctrines: an owner may not gather and discharge it onto a neighbour to the neighbour's injury.
Grading and drainage disputes are neighbour law, frequent, and firmly in the route-to-counsel
category.

**A vocabulary for water conversations.** Three word-pairs organise nearly every client question.
*Riparian and littoral* describe location: riparian land abuts a flowing watercourse, littoral land
abuts a sea or large lake — and in Texas both words describe frontage, not ownership of the state's
water. *Surface water and groundwater* describe source, and the legal regimes differ in kind: the
state's ownership and permitting under Water Code § 11.021 for the one, the landowner-centred
capture-and-district regime for the other — so "can I use the water?" has no single answer until you
know which water. *Right and permit* describe authority: on working land, what matters is never the
physical presence of water but the documented authority to take it, in whatever amount, for
whatever use, from whatever source. A licence holder who keeps the three pairs straight can frame
any water question accurately — and will notice immediately when a seller's claim ("the place has
water rights") is actually an assumption wearing a deed's clothes.

**For practice:** waterfront and rural listings demand precision. Describe frontage, access and
improvements; never advertise ownership of a flowing stream; treat irrigation rights, well permits
and district rules as documents to obtain, not assumptions to make. Water questions on working land —
who may pump how much, from what, under which permit — decide value, and they are answered from the
permits and the district, not from the seller's memory.


The current primary sources include [Water Code § 11.021](https://tcss.legis.texas.gov/resources/WA/htm/WA.11.htm) for state water and [Water Code § 36.002](https://tcss.legis.texas.gov/resources/WA/htm/WA.36.htm) for recognized groundwater ownership and its statutory qualifications. Groundwater ownership does not promise unlimited production. District authority, rules, permits, well facts, conveyances, and other law must be evaluated for the actual tract. Water-rights information is a distinct part of current TREC’s form system; use the applicable current form and its instructions rather than treating a mineral addendum as a water-rights instrument.

In a hypothetical rural purchase, a buyer plans a small commercial nursery using an existing domestic well. The file should identify well records, any district, actual capacity and testing, applicable permit or exemption questions, and any severed or reserved groundwater interest. The seller’s statement that the well has always served the house does not establish adequacy for commercial irrigation. The agent should help assemble the evidence and advise appropriate water, engineering, and legal review before an investigation deadline.

Drainage presents a different problem. Water Code § 11.086 addresses diverting or impounding natural surface-water flow in a manner that damages another’s property by overflow. A seller’s new berm can therefore have consequences beyond the lot line. Do not characterize the berm as harmless because it is on the seller’s land. Obtain the relevant condition information and qualified evaluation. Distinguish observed water paths from legal responsibility for damage.

## T04U6 — Mineral Rights

Mineral ownership can be separated from surface ownership. The seller of the surface may own all, some, or none of the mineral estate. The mineral estate itself can contain different rights and economic interests, so a royalty check does not necessarily establish ownership of every mineral right. A prospective buyer should know what the seller actually owns, what the contract proposes to convey or reserve, and what existing leases or other instruments remain relevant.

The Commission’s [22 TAC § 537.51](https://www.trec.texas.gov/agency-information/rules-and-laws/trec-rules) adopts the Addendum for Reservation of Oil, Gas, and Other Minerals, Form 44-3. The addendum addresses a seller’s reservation of all or part of the mineral estate. Its definitions and choices should be read from the current published document. The existence of a form does not answer a disputed mineral-title question or authorize an agent to calculate a complex fraction from a chain of deeds.

A reservation keeps a specified interest in the grantor; an exception identifies an interest excluded from the conveyance. The effect of the actual instrument requires legal interpretation. Parties frequently use “reserve” loosely when they mean “disclose an earlier reservation by someone else.” Those are different events. If a prior owner reserved minerals decades ago, the present seller cannot convey them simply by leaving a reservation blank unchecked. Likewise, a seller who does own minerals needs the appropriate documented arrangement if retaining part of them.

Mineral documents may address executive rights, leasing, royalties, bonus payments, development, access, and use of the surface. A buyer’s concern may be physical use rather than income. Explain that these are separate questions requiring review of the actual instruments. Do not promise “no drilling” from the absence of a currently visible well. Existing rights, leases, express surface restrictions, and applicable law may matter even when there is no current operation.

Consider a sale in which the seller receives one quarter of the royalties payable under a lease and wants to “keep half the minerals.” The two fractions may have different denominators and describe different interests. The agent should not translate the request into a form percentage by guesswork. Obtain the relevant deed and lease, refer the ownership and reservation language to counsel, and preserve the negotiated business objective. Accurate fact gathering can prevent a reservation from retaining much more or much less than the seller intended.

Now consider a buyer who wants unrestricted recreational use of a ranch. An existing mineral lease includes access rights, and a surface-use agreement identifies a roadway. Those instruments should be delivered and reviewed under the appropriate contract provisions. The agent can identify the road on the available survey and provide the documents. Whether the operator may expand its use or whether a restriction is enforceable requires legal analysis. A title policy’s treatment of mineral matters also must be read; the buyer should not assume every mineral risk is insured.

Water and minerals should remain distinct in the file. The mineral addendum’s defined estate is not a universal transfer of groundwater rights, building materials, or every substance beneath the surface. Use the correct documents and avoid informal assurances that “all subsurface rights” always travel together. When a proposed reservation does not fit an available form, advise attorney drafting before signing. Section 537.11’s prohibition on drafting legal rights applies to mineral provisions just as it applies to a financing contingency.

Read the current [mineral-reservation addendum](https://www.trec.texas.gov/sites/default/files/pdf-forms/44-3.pdf) as a sequence of distinct decisions. Section B identifies the seller’s reservation, while Section C addresses the seller’s surface-use election. Section C also states the limit on that election concerning rights held by other owners or existing mineral lessees who are not parties. A reservation choice therefore should not be summarized as an assurance that every competing surface-use right has disappeared. The buyer needs the title instruments, existing lease information, and appropriate professional advice to understand the remaining interests. Section D separately supplies an information obligation when the seller does not reserve the entire seller-owned mineral interest. Keeping ownership, reservation, surface use, and information delivery separate prevents a checked box from being described as a broader legal result than the form provides.

At closing, the deed and contract must be coordinated through the title and legal process. The agent should flag a proposed deed reservation that differs from the accepted contract and request review before execution. The goal is to convey the bargained-for estate through the proper instrument. A buyer should not discover the difference only after signing and funding.

## T04U7 — Environmental issues

Environmental conditions can affect health, use, value, financing, insurance, and liability. A residential inspection is not automatically an environmental site assessment; a seller’s disclosure is not a warranty or a substitute for investigation. The appropriate scope depends on the property and intended use. A former service station, a rural tract with buried drums, and a pre-1978 house require different questions.
### Adapted teaching: Environmental vocabulary, lead disclosure, Texas condition notices, and professional investigation

Environmental regulation reaches real estate three ways: it can forbid a use, it can define the parties exposed to cleanup costs in terms of who owns or operates rather than who caused the problem, and it can create a disclosure obligation that sits squarely in the license holder's path. A Texas license holder is not an environmental professional and must not act like one. What the license holder owes is accurate disclosure of what is known, an honest statement of what is not known, and a referral to someone qualified.

**Which federal sections were read, and which were not.** Four federal statutory sections were held and read for this course, and this unit teaches them from their own text: **42 U.S.C. § 4852d**, the disclosure section of the Residential Lead-Based Paint Hazard Reduction Act; **42 U.S.C. § 9601**, the definitions section of CERCLA; **42 U.S.C. § 4321**, the congressional declaration of purpose of the National Environmental Policy Act; and **16 U.S.C. § 1531**, the congressional findings, purposes, and policy of the Endangered Species Act. Every federal proposition stated below with a section number comes from one of those four sections. **What was not held is still not stated here: CERCLA's liability provisions in 42 U.S.C. § 9607, the Clean Water Act's wetlands and dredge-and-fill permitting provisions, the federal asbestos and underground storage tank regimes, the later operative sections of the Endangered Species Act and of the National Environmental Policy Act, and every EPA and HUD regulation promulgated under any of these statutes — including the lead disclosure rule that § 4852d itself directs the agencies to write — were not read for this course. This unit states no threshold, permit trigger, exemption, deadline, or form content from any of them, and those requirements must be verified against the current federal authority before being relied on in a transaction.**

**The federal lead-based paint disclosure: 42 U.S.C. § 4852d.** Of the four, this is the section that binds a residential license holder personally, on ordinary transactions, and it is the one to know cold. Section 4852d(a)(1) directed the Secretary of Housing and Urban Development and the Administrator of the Environmental Protection Agency to promulgate regulations "for the disclosure of lead-based paint hazards in target housing which is offered for sale or lease," and then told them what those regulations must contain. Read that structure honestly: the duties below are stated in the statute as the required content of a rule, and the rule itself was not a held source for this course. What § 4852d(a)(1) says the regulations "shall require" is that, **before the purchaser or lessee is obligated under any contract to purchase or lease the housing**, the seller or lessor shall (A) "provide the purchaser or lessee with a lead hazard information pamphlet, as prescribed by the Administrator of the Environmental Protection Agency under section 406 of the Toxic Substances Control Act"; (B) "disclose to the purchaser or lessee the presence of any known lead-based paint, or any known lead-based paint hazards, in such housing and provide to the purchaser or lessee any lead hazard evaluation report available to the seller or lessor"; and (C) "permit the purchaser a 10-day period (unless the parties mutually agree upon a different period of time) to conduct a risk assessment or inspection for the presence of lead-based paint hazards."

Four precise points sit in that sentence. First, the timing is fixed to a moment, not a date: the pamphlet, the disclosure, and the opportunity all come *before the purchaser or lessee is obligated under any contract* — not before closing, and not at closing. Second, the disclosure is of what is **known**, which is the same posture Texas takes in Property Code § 5.008: the section does not require the seller to test, and does not require a report to be created. Third, the records duty is delivery of what exists: § 4852d(a)(1)(B) requires the seller or lessor to "provide to the purchaser or lessee any lead hazard evaluation report available to the seller or lessor" — to hand over a report that is available, not to commission one. Fourth, note whom subparagraph (C) names. Subparagraphs (A) and (B) say "purchaser or lessee"; subparagraph (C) gives the 10-day risk-assessment-or-inspection period to "the purchaser." The period is 10 days "unless the parties mutually agree upon a different period of time," so it is a default the parties may lengthen or shorten by agreement, not a fixed waiting period.

The contract itself is regulated. Section 4852d(a)(2) provides that the regulations "shall provide that every contract for the purchase and sale of any interest in target housing shall contain a Lead Warning Statement and a statement signed by the purchaser" that the purchaser has "read the Lead Warning Statement and understands its contents," has "received a lead hazard information pamphlet," and has "had a 10-day opportunity (unless the parties mutually agreed upon a different period of time) before becoming obligated under the contract to purchase the housing to conduct a risk assessment or inspection for the presence of lead-based paint hazards." Section 4852d(a)(3) then prints the Lead Warning Statement and specifies how it travels: the text is to be "printed in large type on a separate sheet of paper attached to the contract." Its opening words are the source of the date everyone quotes — "Every purchaser of any interest in residential real property on which a residential dwelling was built prior to 1978 is notified that such property may present exposure to lead from lead-based paint that may place young children at risk of developing lead poisoning" — and its closing words restate the duty in plain language for the buyer: "The seller of any interest in residential real property is required to provide the buyer with any information on lead-based paint hazards from risk assessments or inspections in the seller's possession and notify the buyer of any known lead-based paint hazards. A risk assessment or inspection for possible lead-based paint hazards is recommended prior to purchase."

A caution about the scope word. Section 4852d uses the defined term **target housing** and does not define it; the definition sits in another section of the same chapter that was not held. The pre-1978 line appears in § 4852d only inside the Lead Warning Statement's own text, quoted above. So state the 1978 date the way the warning statement states it — as the notice given to every purchaser of residential real property on which a dwelling was built prior to 1978 — and do not extend, narrow, or improvise the boundaries of "target housing" from memory. The section likewise does not define "lead-based paint hazard," "risk assessment," or "inspection."

**What § 4852d says about the agent.** This is the part of the section a license holder is most often surprised by, and it is short. Section 4852d(a)(4), headed "Compliance assurance," provides: "Whenever a seller or lessor has entered into a contract with an agent for the purpose of selling or leasing a unit of target housing, the regulations promulgated under this section shall require the agent, on behalf of the seller or lessor, to ensure compliance with the requirements of this section." Read the operative words. Not *inform the seller*. Not *if the seller asks*. **Ensure compliance** — an affirmative duty placed on the agent by the federal statute itself, on behalf of the seller or lessor, and triggered simply by having a listing or leasing engagement on a unit of target housing. Nothing in the section makes the duty conditional on the seller's cooperation, and nothing in it excuses the agent because the seller filled out the form. This is why a residential file for a pre-1978 property includes the signed disclosure, the attached Lead Warning Statement, evidence the pamphlet was delivered, any evaluation report the seller had, and either the 10-day opportunity or the parties' written agreement to a different period.

Consequences follow for a knowing violation. Section 4852d(b)(1) subjects "[a]ny person who knowingly violates any provision of this section" to civil money penalties "in accordance with the provisions of section 3545 of this title" — a cross-reference to a section of title 42 that was not read here, so no penalty amount is stated for it. Section 4852d(b)(2) authorizes the Secretary to act to enjoin a violation. Section 4852d(b)(3) is the one that reaches a private plaintiff: a person who knowingly violates the section "shall be jointly and severally liable to the purchaser or lessee in an amount equal to 3 times the amount of damages incurred by such individual," and under § 4852d(b)(4) a prevailing party in such an action may be awarded court costs, reasonable attorney fees, and expert witness fees. Section 4852d(b)(5) makes failure to comply a prohibited act under section 409 of the Toxic Substances Control Act and states that, for enforcement under that Act, "the penalty for each violation applicable under section 16 of that Act" — the section the text cross-references as 15 U.S.C. 2615, which was not read here — "shall not be more than $10,000." Finally, § 4852d(c) draws a line that prevents a common misunderstanding: nothing in the section "shall affect the validity or enforceability of any sale or contract for the purchase and sale or lease of any interest in residential real property or any loan, loan agreement, mortgage, or lien made or arising in connection with a mortgage loan, nor shall anything in this section create a defect in title." A lead-paint failure is a penalty-and-damages problem, not a title problem, and telling a client the contract is void because the addendum was missed would be wrong.

**CERCLA vocabulary: 42 U.S.C. § 9601 — definitions, and only definitions.** Section 9601 opens "For purpose of this subchapter" and then defines terms. It is important to be exact about what that does and does not give us. **The CERCLA liability provisions — who is liable, to whom, for what, and with what defenses — are in 42 U.S.C. § 9607, which was not held and was not read. This course states no CERCLA liability rule, no defense, and no allocation standard.** What § 9601 does supply is the vocabulary those provisions run on, and the vocabulary alone explains why a buyer's lawyer worries.

Start with **"owner or operator."** Section 9601(20)(A) provides that the term means, "in the case of an onshore facility or an offshore facility, any person owning or operating such facility," with a parallel rule for vessels, and clause (iii) reaching back to "any person who owned, operated, or otherwise controlled activities at such facility immediately beforehand" where title or control was conveyed to a unit of State or local government "due to bankruptcy, foreclosure, tax delinquency, abandonment, or similar means." Notice what is absent: the definition says nothing about causing, contributing to, or knowing about contamination. It is a status defined by ownership or operation. The same paragraph carves out the lender: the term "does not include a person, who, without participating in the management of a vessel or facility, holds indicia of ownership primarily to protect his security interest in the vessel or facility" — the secured-creditor exclusion, elaborated in § 9601(20)(F) for lenders that are not participants in management. Subparagraph (D) excludes a unit of State or local government that acquired ownership or control through law-enforcement seizure, bankruptcy, tax delinquency, abandonment, or other circumstances in which government takes title "by virtue of its function as sovereign," except where it caused or contributed to the release.

Then **"facility."** Section 9601(9) defines it as "(A) any building, structure, installation, equipment, pipe or pipeline (including any pipe into a sewer or publicly owned treatment works), well, pit, pond, lagoon, impoundment, ditch, landfill, storage container, motor vehicle, rolling stock, or aircraft, or (B) any site or area where a hazardous substance has been deposited, stored, disposed of, or placed, or otherwise come to be located," excluding a consumer product in consumer use and any vessel. Subparagraph (B) is the one to sit with: an ordinary tract of Texas dirt becomes a "facility" because something came to be located on it — including, by the words used, coming to be located there without anyone putting it there on purpose.

Then **"release."** Section 9601(22) defines it as "any spilling, leaking, pumping, pouring, emitting, emptying, discharging, injecting, escaping, leaching, dumping, or disposing into the environment (including the abandonment or discarding of barrels, containers, and other closed receptacles containing any hazardous substance or pollutant or contaminant)," excluding four things: a release resulting in exposure to persons solely within a workplace, as to a claim those persons may assert against their employer; engine exhaust emissions from a motor vehicle, rolling stock, aircraft, vessel, or pipeline pumping station engine; certain releases of source, byproduct, or special nuclear material governed by the Atomic Energy Act; and "the normal application of fertilizer." An abandoned drum in a fence line is inside the definition by its own words.

Then **"hazardous substance."** Section 9601(14) does not list chemicals; it incorporates six other federal lists — substances designated under section 311(b)(2)(A) of the Federal Water Pollution Control Act, substances designated under 42 U.S.C. § 9602, hazardous wastes identified or listed under section 3001 of the Solid Waste Disposal Act, toxic pollutants listed under section 307(a) of the Federal Water Pollution Control Act, hazardous air pollutants listed under section 112 of the Clean Air Act, and imminently hazardous chemical substances or mixtures under section 7 of the Toxic Substances Control Act. Those six lists were not read for this course and no substance is named from them here. But the paragraph's closing exclusion is directly useful in Texas: the term "does not include petroleum, including crude oil or any fraction thereof which is not otherwise specifically listed or designated as a hazardous substance under subparagraphs (A) through (F) of this paragraph, and the term does not include natural gas, natural gas liquids, liquefied natural gas, or synthetic gas usable for fuel." That exclusion is narrower than it sounds — within § 9601(14) it turns on whether the material is "otherwise specifically listed or designated as a hazardous substance under subparagraphs (A) through (F) of this paragraph" — so it is not a license to tell a buyer that an old fuel tank or an oilfield tract raises no federal issue. It is a reason to route the question to an environmental professional and to counsel. Note also that the Texas notice in Property Code § 5.013 uses the phrase "hazardous substance" for a different purpose and takes its meaning from Health and Safety Code § 361.003, not from § 9601(14); the phrases are not interchangeable.

Finally, § 9601 gives the diligence vocabulary. Section 9601(35)(A) defines "contractual relationship" **"for the purpose of section 9607(b)(3) of this title"** — a section not held — and in doing so sets out what a defendant who acquired property after the disposal must establish, including that "[a]t the time the defendant acquired the facility the defendant did not know and had no reason to know that any hazardous substance which is the subject of the release or threatened release was disposed of on, in, or at the facility." Section 9601(35)(B)(i) explains "reason to know": the person must show that "on or before the date on which the defendant acquired the facility" it "carried out all appropriate inquiries, as provided in clauses (ii) and (iv), into the previous ownership and uses of the facility in accordance with generally accepted good commercial and customary standards and practices" — the inquiries are the ones those clauses define, not whatever inquiry the buyer thought sufficient — and took reasonable steps to stop any continuing release, prevent any threatened future release, and prevent or limit exposure to any previously released hazardous substance. Section 9601(35)(B)(iii) then lists the criteria the Administrator must include in the standards, and the list reads like a due-diligence checklist written by Congress: the results of an inquiry by an environmental professional; interviews with past and present owners, operators, and occupants; reviews of historical sources "such as chain of title documents, aerial photographs, building department records, and land use records"; searches for recorded environmental cleanup liens; reviews of federal, state, and local government records, waste disposal records, underground storage tank records, and spill records; "[v]isual inspections of the facility and of adjoining properties"; the person's specialized knowledge or experience; "[t]he relationship of the purchase price to the value of the property, if the property was not contaminated"; commonly known or reasonably ascertainable information; and the obviousness of contamination. Section 9601(35)(B)(iv)(II) names the interim standard for property purchased on or after May 31, 1997 and until the Administrator's regulations issue: the ASTM procedures, "including the document known as 'Standard E1527–97', entitled 'Standard Practice for Environmental Site Assessment: Phase 1 Environmental Site Assessment Process'", which the clause says shall satisfy its requirements. And § 9601(35)(B)(v) is the residential rule: for property in residential or similar use purchased by a nongovernmental or noncommercial entity, "a facility inspection and title search that reveal no basis for further investigation" satisfies the subparagraph.

That is why commercial and industrial purchasers commission environmental site assessments before closing. It is still true that **no source read for this course requires an environmental site assessment in any transaction** — the practice is driven by a defense located in § 9607, which was not read — but the practice is no longer merely custom in this course's telling: § 9601(35)(B) is where the phrase "all appropriate inquiries" and the Phase I standard come from, and § 9601(40)(B) repeats the same criteria for a **bona fide prospective purchaser**, defined in § 9601(40)(A) as a person who acquires ownership of a facility after January 11, 2002 (or, on the terms stated there, a leasehold interest after that date) and establishes by a preponderance of the evidence each of eight criteria — that all disposal occurred before acquisition, that all appropriate inquiries were made, that all legally required notices are given, that appropriate care is exercised by taking reasonable steps to stop continuing releases and limit exposure, that full cooperation and access are provided, that land use restrictions and institutional controls are respected, that information requests and subpoenas are answered, and that the person is not affiliated with a potentially responsible party. Section 9601(39)(A) supplies the term a redevelopment client will use: a **brownfield site** is "real property, the expansion, redevelopment, or reuse of which may be complicated by the presence or potential presence of a hazardous substance, pollutant, or contaminant," subject to the exclusions in (39)(B). Two honest limits close this out. Section 9601(32) says the terms "liable" and "liability" are "construed to be the standard of liability which obtains under section 311 of the Federal Water Pollution Control Act" — a cross-reference to a statute not held, so this course does not state what that standard is. And § 9601 contains **no** definition of "contiguous property"; that vocabulary lives elsewhere in the chapter, was not read, and is not stated here.

**NEPA: 42 U.S.C. § 4321.** The National Environmental Policy Act's declaration of purpose is a single sentence, and quoting it in full is the whole of what this course can say: "The purposes of this chapter are: To declare a national policy which will encourage productive and enjoyable harmony between man and his environment; to promote efforts which will prevent or eliminate damage to the environment and biosphere and stimulate the health and welfare of man; to enrich the understanding of the ecological systems and natural resources important to the Nation; and to establish a Council on Environmental Quality." That is a statement of national policy and the creation of a federal council. It imposes no duty on a Texas seller, no permit requirement on a private lot, and no disclosure obligation on a license holder. The environmental-review machinery associated with NEPA sits in later sections of chapter 55 that were not read, so this course states nothing about when a review or an impact statement is required, what it must contain, or how long it takes. The practical point is a referral point and is practice, not law: where a client's project depends on a federal permit, a federal approval, or federal money, federal environmental review may become part of that federal process, and the timing question belongs to counsel and to the agency, not to the license holder's memory.

**Endangered species: 16 U.S.C. § 1531.** Section 1531 is likewise a findings-and-purposes section, and it is directed at government. Section 1531(a) records the findings — that species have been rendered extinct "as a consequence of economic growth and development untempered by adequate concern and conservation," that others are depleted to the point of danger, that these species are "of esthetic, ecological, educational, historical, recreational, and scientific value to the Nation and its people," that the United States has pledged itself under named treaties and conventions, and that encouraging States and other interested parties through federal financial assistance and incentives is "a key to meeting the Nation's international commitments and to better safeguarding, for the benefit of all citizens, the Nation's heritage in fish, wildlife, and plants." Section 1531(b) states the purposes: "to provide a means whereby the ecosystems upon which endangered species and threatened species depend may be conserved, to provide a program for the conservation of such endangered species and threatened species, and to take such steps as may be appropriate to achieve the purposes of the treaties and conventions" listed in subsection (a). Section 1531(c)(1) declares it "the policy of Congress that all Federal departments and agencies shall seek to conserve endangered species and threatened species and shall utilize their authorities in furtherance of the purposes of this chapter," and § 1531(c)(2) adds the policy "that Federal agencies shall cooperate with State and local agencies to resolve water resource issues in concert with conservation of endangered species." Read carefully, this section commands *federal agencies*; the provisions that reach private conduct — listing, critical habitat, the prohibitions on taking, and the permit and conservation-plan mechanisms — are in later sections of the chapter that were not held. So a license holder can accurately say that federal law declares a policy of conserving endangered and threatened species and the ecosystems they depend on, and that a species question on a specific tract is one for counsel and a qualified biologist. A license holder cannot say from this course what is prohibited on a particular parcel, what a permit costs, or how long one takes.

**The conditions with no held federal source.** Several environmental conditions a Texas license holder meets constantly have no held federal source in this course, and are described here factually rather than legally, with no section cited and nothing asserted about permits, thresholds, or deadlines. **Wetlands** regulation restricts discharges of dredged or fill material into certain waters, and land that functions as wetland may be regulated even where it looks buildable and is dry much of the year; the Clean Water Act provisions and their implementing rules were not read and must be verified. **Asbestos** is a fibrous mineral used historically in insulation and floor, ceiling, and roofing materials; disturbing it is the hazard, and its presence is an inspection-and-abatement question for qualified professionals. **Radon** is a naturally occurring radioactive soil gas identified only by testing. **Underground storage tanks** create both a leak risk and a closure obligation and are often invisible from the surface; the federal tank rules were not read. Three of the four reach the license holder anyway through Texas law rather than federal law: "Asbestos Components" and "Radon Gas" are named items on the Texas seller's disclosure notice, and a buried tank will usually be reached, if at all, through that notice's "Subsurface Structure or Pits" and "Hazardous or Toxic Waste" items. That is the pattern to hold onto — for most environmental conditions in a Texas residential file, the operative instrument is the state disclosure notice, not a federal rule.

**Texas law: what the seller's disclosure notice actually puts in issue.** Texas does not leave environmental condition to the general law of misrepresentation. Property Code § 5.008(a) requires a seller of residential real property comprising not more than one dwelling unit to give the purchaser a written notice as prescribed by the section, or a substantially similar notice containing at a minimum all of the section's items. The statutory form in § 5.008(b) asks the seller directly whether the seller is aware of a long list of conditions, and the environmental content is unmistakable. Item 4 asks about "Hazardous or Toxic Waste," "Asbestos Components," "Urea formaldehyde Insulation," "Radon Gas," "Lead Based Paint," "Improper Drainage," "Water Damage Not Due to a Flood Event," "Landfill, Settling, Soil Movement, Fault Lines," "Subsurface Structure or Pits," and "Previous Use of Premises for Manufacture of Methamphetamine."

Item 6 asks whether the seller is aware of present flood insurance coverage, previous flooding due to a reservoir failure, breach, or controlled or emergency release, and previous water penetration into a structure from a natural flood event, and whether the property lies wholly or partly in a 100-year floodplain, a 500-year floodplain, a floodway, a flood pool, or a reservoir. The notice defines those terms itself: a "100-year floodplain" is an area identified on the flood insurance rate map as a special flood hazard area with "a one percent annual chance of flooding," and a "500-year floodplain" is a moderate hazard area with "a two-tenths of one percent annual chance of flooding." Items 7 and 8 ask whether the seller has ever filed a flood damage claim, including with the National Flood Insurance Program, and whether the seller has received FEMA or Small Business Administration assistance for flood damage. Item 9 reaches related ground, including "[a]ny notices of violations of deed restrictions or governmental ordinances affecting the condition or use of the Property," "[a]ny condition on the Property which materially affects the physical health or safety of an individual," and whether any portion of the property lies in a groundwater conservation district or a subsidence district. Item 10 tells a buyer of coastal property seaward of the Gulf Intracoastal Waterway or within 1,000 feet of the mean high tide bordering the Gulf of Mexico that the property "may be subject to the Open Beaches Act or the Dune Protection Act" — Chapters 61 and 63 of the Natural Resources Code respectively — "and a beachfront construction certificate or dune protection permit may be required for repairs or improvements."

Two limits belong with the form. Section 5.008(d) provides that the notice "shall be completed to the best of seller's belief and knowledge as of the date the notice is completed and signed by the seller," and that if information is unknown the seller "shall indicate that fact on the notice, and by that act is in compliance with this section." Section 5.008(e) exempts listed transfers, including transfers pursuant to a court order or foreclosure sale, by a trustee in bankruptcy, by a fiduciary administering an estate or trust, between co-owners, and to a spouse or lineal relative. The notice's own heading states that it "IS NOT A SUBSTITUTE FOR ANY INSPECTIONS OR WARRANTIES THE PURCHASER MAY WISH TO OBTAIN." A common error is to treat a completed disclosure notice as a clean bill of health. It is a statement of the seller's knowledge, nothing more.

**How the federal lead duty and the Texas notice fit together.** On a pre-1978 Texas house both instruments are live at once, and neither one does the other's work. Property Code § 5.008 is a Texas statute; it reaches a **sale** of residential real property comprising not more than one dwelling unit; it asks whether the seller is *aware* of "Lead Based Paint" as one item among many in item 4; it is satisfied by the seller's honest "unknown" under § 5.008(d); and it does not apply at all to the transfers listed in § 5.008(e). Section 4852d reaches target housing offered for **sale or lease**; it requires the lead hazard information pamphlet, the disclosure of known lead-based paint and known lead-based paint hazards, delivery of any lead hazard evaluation report available to the seller or lessor, the purchaser's 10-day risk-assessment or inspection opportunity unless the parties mutually agree otherwise, and the Lead Warning Statement with the purchaser's signed acknowledgement, attached to the contract on a separate sheet. Three consequences. A "no" or "unknown" written on item 4 of the Texas notice does not satisfy § 4852d: the § 5.008 notice is not the pamphlet, is not the Lead Warning Statement, and carries no inspection period. A completed federal lead addendum does not satisfy § 5.008 either; it says nothing about drainage, fault lines, floodplain, or the rest of the Texas form. And the § 5.008(e) exemptions are exemptions from the **Texas** notice only — a transfer exempt from § 5.008 tells you nothing about whether § 4852d applies, and a lease is outside § 5.008 while target housing offered for lease is squarely inside § 4852d(a)(1). The two documents also allocate responsibility differently: § 5.008 places the notice duty on the seller, while § 4852d(a)(4) places on the seller's or lessor's **agent** the duty "to ensure compliance with the requirements of this section."

**Other Texas disclosures with environmental content.** Property Code § 5.013(a) requires a seller of unimproved real property to be used for residential purposes to give the purchaser written notice disclosing "the location of a transportation pipeline, including a pipeline for the transportation of natural gas, natural gas liquids, synthetic gas, liquefied petroleum gas, petroleum or a petroleum product, or a hazardous substance," with "hazardous substance" taking the meaning assigned by Health and Safety Code § 361.003 as § 5.013(e) provides. Under § 5.013(c), if the notice is not delivered on or before the effective date of a binding executory contract, "the purchaser may terminate the contract for any reason not later than the seventh day after the effective date of the contract." Property Code § 5.019 requires a **notice of water level fluctuations** for property adjoining an impoundment constructed and maintained under Chapter 11, Water Code, with a storage capacity of at least 5,000 acre-feet at normal operating level; § 5.019(e) preserves a misrepresentation action after conveyance where the seller failed to give the notice and had actual knowledge of the fluctuation.

Water Code § 11.086(a) is the Texas surface-water rule that most often becomes a neighbor dispute: "No person may divert or impound the natural flow of surface waters in this state, or permit a diversion or impounding by him to continue, in a manner that damages the property of another by the overflow of the water diverted or impounded." Section 11.086(b) gives the injured owner remedies at law and in equity and recovery of damages. A grading plan that solves one lot's drainage by moving water onto the lot next door is not merely a nuisance complaint waiting to happen; it is a statutory violation with a damages remedy. Texas has also placed an environmental tool inside the zoning power itself: Local Government Code § 211.003(a)(6) authorizes a municipality to regulate "the pumping, extraction, and use of groundwater by persons other than retail public utilities" — a term Water Code section 13.002 defines — "for the purpose of preventing the use or contact with groundwater that presents an actual or potential threat to human health." The stated purpose is a limit as well as a grant: the power is given for that public-health purpose, not as a general licence over groundwater. Section 212.003(a) allows that kind of groundwater ordinance to be extended into the extraterritorial jurisdiction. Where such an ordinance covers a contaminated groundwater area, a buyer's plan to drill a well may be barred by the city even outside the city limits.

**The license holder's duty.** The obligation is specific and enforceable. Occupations Code § 1101.652(b)(3) makes it a ground for suspension or revocation if a license holder, while engaged in real estate brokerage, "makes a material misrepresentation to a potential buyer concerning a significant defect, including a latent structural defect, known to the license holder that would be a significant factor to a reasonable and prudent buyer in making a decision to purchase real property," and § 1101.652(b)(4) reaches the license holder who "fails to disclose to a potential buyer a defect described by Subdivision (3) that is known to the license holder." Section 1101.652(b)(1) separately reaches a license holder who "acts negligently or incompetently." Occupations Code § 1101.805(e) sets the boundary from the other side: a license holder "is not liable for a misrepresentation or a concealment of a material fact made by a party to a real estate transaction unless the license holder: (1) knew of the falsity of the misrepresentation or concealment; and (2) failed to disclose the license holder's knowledge of the falsity." You are not the guarantor of the seller's statements; you are responsible for what you know.

As a matter of practice and not of any statute read for this course, that translates into a short discipline. Disclose in writing what you know about the property's condition. Never characterize a stain, an odor, a slab crack, a fill area, an old tank, or a drainage pattern as harmless — you are not qualified to make that call, and a confident wrong answer is exactly the material misrepresentation § 1101.652(b)(3) describes. Refer the client to the right professional: an inspector, an environmental consultant, a licensed surveyor for boundary and stream questions, an engineer for drainage, and counsel for permitting and liability. Document the referral. On any pre-1978 residential listing or lease, work the § 4852d file as your own responsibility rather than the seller's, because § 4852d(a)(4) says it is: pamphlet delivered, known paint and hazards disclosed, available evaluation reports handed over, the 10-day opportunity given or a different period agreed, and the Lead Warning Statement signed and attached. And when the question is federal, separate the part you can state from a section you have actually read — § 4852d's disclosure requirements, § 9601's definitions, the declared purposes in § 4321 and § 1531 — from the far larger part you cannot: CERCLA's liability sections, the wetlands program, the asbestos and underground storage tank regimes, and every implementing regulation, including the lead rule itself. Those must be confirmed against the current federal authority rather than answered from memory.


For a purchase example, a vacant tract contains a concrete pad, vent pipes, and staining near an old commercial structure. The agent should describe what was observed and obtain known historical documents. The agent should not label the site contaminated or clean without qualified evidence. The buyer may need environmental counsel and a consultant to establish the investigation scope and legal risk. The contract must provide an appropriate decision process before the buyer becomes committed beyond the desired protection; a custom allocation of cleanup responsibility is attorney work.

## T04U8 — Homestead

Homestead protection and the residence-homestead tax exemption serve different purposes. One limits specified forced sales and encumbrances; the other reduces taxable value under applicable tax law. A tax exemption record does not resolve all constitutional homestead questions. A home can be separate property while a spouse has homestead rights relevant to the transaction.
### Adapted teaching: Protection, qualifying liens, acreage, and proceeds

Texas homestead law is the state's signature protection of the family home, and the Commission gives
it fifty minutes because a licence holder meets it constantly: in listing intake, in seller net
sheets, in refinances that cannot lawfully close, and in tax exemption questions every January. The
essential discipline is that **"homestead" names two distinct doctrines** that share a word: an
*exemption from creditors' seizure* in the Property Code, and an *exemption from part of property
taxation* in the Tax Code. Conflating them is the classic error; this unit separates them.

**Doctrine one: protection from forced sale.** Property Code § 41.001 exempts a homestead from
*seizure for the claims of creditors* — with a closed list of exceptions, the encumbrances that
*can* be properly fixed on homestead property. The read text names, among them: **purchase-money**
liens (the loan that bought the home); **taxes on the property**; and **work and material liens**
for improvements, when contracted for in the manner the law requires. The pattern to internalise:
the homestead is armoured against *general* creditors — credit cards, most judgments, business
debts — but not against the debts that created or preserved or improved the property itself. A
judgment creditor of an ordinary kind cannot force the sale of a Texas homestead; the judgment
simply waits, unable to touch the exempt home.

**How much land is protected.** Property Code § 41.002 draws the boundaries: an **urban** homestead
of not more than **10 acres** in one or more contiguous lots, used as a home or as both a home and a
place of business; a **rural** homestead of not more than **200 acres for a family** and **100 acres
for a single adult**, in one or more parcels, with the improvements. The urban/rural
classification follows the statute's own test (services and municipal location), and the acreage
caps are caps on *exempt quantity* — a 400-acre ranch homestead is protected as to 200 acres, and a
creditor's remedies operate on the excess.

**What this means in transactions.** Homestead protection does not make a home unsellable or
unmortgageable — the owner may always sell voluntarily, and may voluntarily grant the liens on the
statutory list. It means, instead, that *non-listed* liens do not attach to the homestead, and that
lending against a Texas homestead is a specialised, rule-bound practice: the categories of valid
homestead liens are limited, home-improvement contracts must be made "in the manner required" for
the work-and-material lien to be good, and a lender's error in this area can void its lien entirely.
The licence holder's role is recognition and routing: know that the homestead rules exist, spot the
transactions they touch — a seller's judgment that may not actually encumber the home; an
improvement contract signed at a kitchen table; a proposed second lien of a kind the list does not
allow — and insist the title company and counsel resolve them before anyone promises a closing.

**The complete list of liens that can bind a homestead.** Because "which debts can reach the home"
is the whole doctrine, the closed list of § 41.001(b) deserves to be read in full. Encumbrances may
be properly fixed on homestead property for: **(1)** purchase money; **(2)** taxes on the property;
**(3)** work and material used in constructing improvements, if contracted for in writing as
provided by §§ 53.254(a)–(c); **(4)** an **owelty of partition** imposed against the entirety of
the property by court order or by the parties' written partition agreement — including a debt of
one spouse to the other resulting from the award of a family homestead in a divorce; **(5)** the
**refinance** of a lien against a homestead, including a federal tax lien resulting from the tax
debt of both spouses (for a family homestead) or of the owner; **(6)** an **extension of credit**
meeting the requirements of Article XVI, § 50(a)(6) of the Texas Constitution — the
constitutionally regulated home-equity loan; and **(7)** a **reverse mortgage** meeting the
requirements of § 50(k)–(p) of that article. The structure teaches the policy: every valid
homestead lien either created the property interest (purchase money), preserves it against the
sovereign (taxes, tax-lien refinance), improved it under written formalities, adjusts ownership
itself (owelty), or is a constitutionally supervised borrowing the owner chose. Everything else —
however large the debt, however solemn the judgment — stays outside.

**The proceeds follow the protection — for six months.** Section 41.001(c) extends the shield past
the sale: *the homestead claimant's proceeds of a sale of a homestead are not subject to seizure
for a creditor's claim for six months after the date of sale.* A seller can therefore sell an
exempt home, hold the money, and reinvest it in a new homestead without the waiting judgment
intercepting the funds — provided the reinvestment happens within the window. For practice, this is
why a seller with known judgments still has a lawful path from one homestead to the next, and why
the timing of that path is a matter for the seller's counsel, not for a licence holder's assurance.

**Urban or rural is a statutory test, not an impression.** Under § 41.002(c), a homestead is
*urban* if, at the time of designation, the property is **(1)** located within the limits of a
municipality or its extraterritorial jurisdiction or a platted subdivision, **and (2)** served by
police protection, paid or volunteer fire protection, and at least three of five listed
municipally provided services: electric, natural gas, sewer, storm sewer, and water. Fail the test
and the homestead is rural, with the larger acreage allowances of § 41.002(b) — 200 acres for a
family, 100 for a single adult, in one or more parcels that need not be contiguous. And
§ 41.002(d) makes the definitions retroactive in reach: they apply *to all homesteads in this
state whenever created*. On the urban side the 10 acres must be contiguous lots; on the rural side
the parcels may be scattered — a farmhouse on forty acres and a hay meadow down the road can both
sit inside one rural homestead.


Read [Texas Constitution Article XVI, § 50](https://tcss.legis.texas.gov/resources/CN/htm/CN.16.htm) and [Property Code §§ 41.001–41.002](https://tcss.legis.texas.gov/resources/PR/htm/PR.41.htm) with the actual family and title facts. The seller’s statement that “it is only in my name” is not a complete signature analysis. Homestead encumbrances also have strict limits; ordinary unsecured debt does not become a valid homestead lien because a lender or seller writes the word “secured” into an agreement.

Suppose a married seller inherited a house and the spouses now use it as their home. The inheritance may support separate-property character, but homestead issues still require review. The agent should obtain the marital and occupancy facts and coordinate with title and counsel. Do not promise that the other spouse can be omitted. Likewise, an owner-financing proposal secured by a homestead requires proper legal documentation and evaluation of the permitted lien category, not a homemade note and lien sentence.


### Statute reading exercise: Three kinds of water

Read Water Code §§ 11.021, 11.086, and 36.002. Classify a creek diversion, a groundwater well, and a berm redirecting runoff onto a neighbor. Identify what each provision addresses and what additional tract-specific evidence is needed before advising a buyer about use.

Feedback: Section 11.021 concerns specified state water; § 36.002 recognizes groundwater ownership subject to its qualifications and district framework; § 11.086 addresses damaging diversion or impoundment of natural surface flow. A creek, well, and drainage berm therefore raise different questions. Ownership statements alone do not prove permits, capacity, quality, or absence of neighbor liability.

### Applied exercise: A sale of more than the seller owns

A life tenant offers a ranch “with all minerals and vacant possession.” A prior deed reserves minerals, and a grazing tenant occupies part of the ranch. Identify the three separate interests requiring review and the documents needed before the buyer signs.

Feedback: The life estate limits the seller’s estate, the prior reservation affects mineral ownership, and the lease affects possession. Obtain the creating deed, complete relevant title evidence, mineral instruments, and lease. Counsel and title professionals must evaluate who can convey what. The agent must not promise that a standard sales form removes these interests.

### Applied exercise: Homestead and a proposed repair lien

A homeowner proposes to secure an informal debt to a relative with the family home. The relative says any signed lien is valid because the debt was used for repairs. Identify the missing legal questions and explain the agent’s appropriate role.

Feedback: Homestead lien validity depends on constitutional and statutory categories and requirements, not only the stated purpose or signature. The repair arrangement, contract timing and formalities, ownership, marital status, and required joinder must be evaluated by counsel. The agent should not draft or validate the instrument and should advise consultation before execution.


### Module recap

Identify the estate, each burden, and the correct legal source before promising a conveyance or use. Governmental powers, private restrictions, water rights, mineral rights, environmental conditions, and homestead protections can operate together. Preserve documents, identify the buyer’s intended use, and obtain the appropriate professional evaluation while the contract still permits an informed decision.
