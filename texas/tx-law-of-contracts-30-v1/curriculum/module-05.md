## T05U1 — Real Estate Contracts

A transaction is a network of agreements. The listing employs the seller’s broker. A buyer agreement defines the buyer-side services and compensation. The purchase contract binds the buyer and seller. Financing documents establish debt and security. A lease grants possession. An inspection agreement defines a professional service. A title commitment offers insurance on stated terms. Confusing these instruments leads to wrong assumptions about who can enforce what.

Build the file by identifying parties, subject, promises, consideration, term, conditions, and enforcement provisions for each agreement. The same individual can sign in different roles: personally as buyer, as a manager for an entity, or as a guarantor of debt. A broker can be owed compensation under one agreement while the buyer has an independent termination right under another. Neither relationship should be assumed from the payment flow alone.

The form selection rule in [22 TAC § 537.11](https://www.trec.texas.gov/agency-information/rules-and-laws/trec-rules) applies within its stated scope and exceptions. An authorized form is a starting point for accurate completion, not a promise that every contemplated arrangement fits. Identify the actual business transaction before selecting a document. A long-term lease with a purchase option, retained title, and monthly principal credits may require analysis beyond an ordinary lease or ordinary sales form.

In a worked transaction, the buyer’s broker is to be paid a stated amount, the seller agrees to a negotiated contribution, and the lender limits particular closing credits. Those are three different questions: the buyer’s contractual compensation obligation, the seller’s contractual contribution, and the lender’s underwriting rules. Record them in their appropriate documents and reconcile them before closing. Do not assume a seller’s contribution automatically amends the buyer agreement or that an unused credit becomes cash payable to the buyer.

Contract administration begins with a complete inventory. Name the agreement, version, parties, signature status, effective date, and remaining obligations. Mark whether it is a proposal, executed agreement, amendment, disclosure, notice, or closing instrument. The inventory prevents an unsigned draft from being treated as operative and exposes a missing attachment while correction is still possible.

## T05U2 — Contract Law

Contract law supplies rules for formation, interpretation, performance, and remedies. The written terms should be read as a whole rather than as isolated phrases. Definitions, incorporated addenda, conditions, notice requirements, and remedy provisions can affect the meaning of a short sentence. A broker should not resolve a contested interpretation by choosing the clause most favorable to the client. The professional task is to provide accurate facts and appropriate referrals while carrying out authorized duties.

The parties’ objective agreement matters. An internal email saying “we intend to buy” differs from acceptance communicated to the seller. A revised document that changes financing is not the original offer simply because the filename stayed the same. A term that says the parties will be bound only upon a later executed definitive agreement requires the analysis illustrated by [Chalker Energy Partners III v. Le Norman Operating, opinion pages 7–13](https://www.txcourts.gov/media/1445957/180352.pdf). The parties’ expressed conditions of formation cannot be ignored for convenience.

Performance questions should distinguish a promise from a condition. A buyer may promise to apply diligently for financing, while financing approval also affects a contractual termination right. Failure of the condition does not necessarily excuse breach of a separate promised duty. Conversely, a properly exercised termination right is not automatically a breach. The exact provision, facts, notice, and timing must be established.
### Adapted teaching: Consent, accuracy, culpability, and the different legal consequences of a false statement

A buyer in Pflugerville asks a question you cannot answer. "Has this ever flooded?" "The seller said
the roof is four years old — is that right?" What you say next is the entire case, and its legal
character decides which body of law the buyer's lawyer reaches for and how much it costs you. Fraud,
misrepresentation and deception are not synonyms: they describe three levels of culpability, and
Texas law treats them very differently in who must prove what, what damages are available, and how
long the claimant has to sue.

**Start with what this course holds and what it does not.** The elements of common-law fraud in
Texas are stated in judicial opinions, not in a statute. No case reporter and no judicial opinion
were held for this course, and so **the common-law elements of fraud were not held and are not
stated here as law.** The same is true of the common-law elements of negligent misrepresentation and
of innocent misrepresentation: those formulations were not held. That is not a gap you should paper
over with something you half-remember from a study guide. It is the honest position, and it turns
out to matter very little in practice, for a reason you are about to see: the Texas Legislature has
written the distinction between these states of mind directly into the statutes that will actually
be used against you, and those statutes *were* held. Learn the statutory version. It is the one that
governs your license and your wallet.

**The statutory ladder of culpability.** Texas statutes describe at least four rungs of mental state
relevant to a false statement in a real estate transaction, each with a different consequence.

The lowest rung is *no mental state at all* — a statement that is simply false, made by someone who
believed it was true and had no particular reason to doubt it. Under the Deceptive Trade
Practices-Consumer Protection Act, Business & Commerce Code § 17.46(b) declares a long list of acts
to be "false, misleading, or deceptive acts or practices," and most of the entries on that list
contain no scienter requirement whatever. Section 17.46(b)(5), to take the one you are most likely
to trip over, makes it a deceptive act to be "representing that goods or services have sponsorship,
approval, characteristics, ingredients, uses, benefits, or quantities which they do not have or that
a person has a sponsorship, approval, status, affiliation, or connection which the person does not."
Read that subdivision again and notice what is missing. It does not say "knowingly representing." It
does not say "falsely representing with intent to deceive." It says *representing*. If the
characteristics are not there, the representation violates the subdivision, and your sincerity is
irrelevant to liability. That is the single most important sentence in this module.

The next rung up is *negligence* — a statement made without the care a reasonable license holder
would use. Texas has not, in any source held for this course, defined negligent misrepresentation by
statute. Carelessness is nonetheless a disciplinary offence in its own right: Occupations Code
§ 1101.652(b)(1) reaches a license holder who "acts negligently or incompetently." You need not have
lied; you need only have been sloppy.

The third rung is *actual awareness*. The DTPA defines it at § 17.45(9): "Knowingly" means "actual
awareness, at the time of the act or practice complained of, of the falsity, deception, or
unfairness of the act or practice giving rise to the consumer's claim or, in an action brought under
Subdivision (2) of Subsection (a) of Section 17.50, actual awareness of the act, practice,
condition, defect, or failure constituting the breach of warranty, but actual awareness may be
inferred where objective manifestations indicate that a person acted with actual awareness." Two
things deserve your attention in that definition. The first is the time reference: awareness "at the
time of the act or practice complained of," not awareness acquired later. The second is the last
clause, which is the one defendants always wish were not there — "actual awareness may be inferred
where objective manifestations indicate that a person acted with actual awareness." You cannot
defeat a knowing finding simply by taking the stand and saying you did not know. A jury may infer
your awareness from what you did, what you had in your file, what you wrote in an e-mail, and what
any competent license holder in your position would have understood.

The top rung is *intent*. Section 17.45(13) defines it, and this definition must be read whole,
because the last clause reverses the meaning of everything before it if you drop it. "Intentionally"
means "actual awareness of the falsity, deception, or unfairness of the act or practice, or the
condition, defect, or failure constituting a breach of warranty giving rise to the consumer's claim,
coupled with the specific intent that the consumer act in detrimental reliance on the falsity or
deception or in detrimental ignorance of the unfairness. Intention may be inferred from objective
manifestations that indicate that the person acted intentionally or from facts showing that a
defendant acted with flagrant disregard of prudent and fair business practices to the extent that
the defendant should be treated as having acted intentionally."

Sit with that last sentence. It says two separate things. First, intention may be inferred from
objective manifestations, the same evidentiary move the statute allows for knowledge. Second — and
this is the clause that catches license holders who thought "intentionally" meant a conscious plan
to cheat — intention may also be inferred "from facts showing that a defendant acted with flagrant
disregard of prudent and fair business practices to the extent that the defendant should be treated
as having acted intentionally." Flagrant disregard of prudent and fair business practices, if
flagrant enough, is treated as intent. A license holder who never formed a plan to deceive anyone,
but who behaved so far outside ordinary professional care that a jury describes the conduct as
flagrant, may be found to have acted intentionally. The words "to the extent that the defendant
should be treated as having acted intentionally" are not a limitation that saves you; they are the
bridge that carries recklessness across into intent. Quote the definition without that final clause
and you will teach yourself the opposite of the law.

**Where the Occupations Code draws its own lines.** TRELA grades fault along the same ladder, and
Law of Agency Module 10 teaches those grounds in full: § 1101.652(b)(3), a material misrepresentation to a
potential buyer about a significant defect known to the license holder; (b)(4), a failure to disclose
such a defect; (b)(5), a false promise made when the license holder "is unable or does not intend to
keep the promise"; (b)(6), a continued and flagrant course of misrepresentation; and
§ 1101.652(a-1)(1), misrepresentation, dishonesty or fraud in the license holder's own name or a
close relative's.

The Commission rule that gives § 1101.652(b)(5) its reach is taught here in full. Rule 22 TAC
§ 535.145 provides that the term false promise "includes both oral and written promises." It
continues: "The fact that a written agreement between the parties to a real estate transaction does
not recite a promise made by a real estate license holder to one of the parties or that a person did
not detrimentally rely on the false promise will not prevent the Commission from determining that a
false promise was made." And it closes the last escape hatch: "In determining whether this section
has been violated, neither a written contractual provision disclaiming oral representations nor the
Texas Rules of Evidence Rule 1004, the parol evidence rule, shall prevent the Commission from
considering oral promises made by a license holder." What you said in the car on the way to the
property is evidence.

**Who bears a misrepresentation, § 1101.805.** subsection (e)
operates here. A license holder "is not liable for a misrepresentation or a concealment of a material
fact made by a party to a real estate transaction unless the license holder: (1) knew of the falsity
of the misrepresentation or concealment; and (2) failed to disclose the license holder's knowledge of
the falsity of the misrepresentation or concealment." Both conditions must be met, and the second is
in your hands.

**Why the DTPA swallows all of this in practice.** In the abstract those are three different lawsuits
with three different burdens; in a Texas residential transaction they collapse into one, and it is
the DTPA. First, liability does not turn on your state of mind: § 17.50(a)(1) makes a consumer's
action available where the defendant used "a false, misleading, or deceptive act or practice" that is
"specifically enumerated in a subdivision of Subsection (b) of Section 17.46 of this subchapter" and
"relied on by a consumer to the consumer's detriment," provided that act was "a producing cause of
economic damages or damages for mental anguish." Producing cause, not proximate cause; enumerated
act, not proven lie. Second, the DTPA pays the plaintiff's lawyer, in one unconditional sentence at
§ 17.50(d): "Each consumer who prevails shall be awarded court costs and reasonable and necessary
attorneys' fees." Not *may* — which is why a $9,000 repair dispute becomes a filed lawsuit. Third,
§ 17.50(b)(1) multiplies the award where the conduct was knowing or intentional. And fourth,
§ 17.44(a) directs that the subchapter "shall be liberally construed and applied to promote its
underlying purposes."

**One place where the older causes of action still matter: time.** Civil Practice & Remedies Code
§ 16.004(a) provides that "[a] person must bring suit on the following actions not later than four
years after the day the cause of action accrues," and subdivision (4) of that list is "fraud."
Subdivision (5) is "breach of fiduciary duty." The DTPA carries its own two-year period at § 17.565,
so a claimant who sat on a claim for three years has lost the DTPA route and may still have a fraud
route. Chapter 16 does not name negligent misrepresentation anywhere in the sections read for this
course, and **which limitations period governs a negligent misrepresentation claim was not held.** Do
not guess at it, and do not advise a client about it; that is a question for their lawyer.


The preceding distinctions affect negotiation as much as litigation. A seller cannot make an inaccurate disclosure harmless by inserting a general disclaimer. A broker cannot claim a complete legal exemption merely because the seller supplied the information. The available statute, defense, and contractual provision must be evaluated on their actual requirements. A statement of fact should identify its source and known limitations, and new contradictory information should be communicated through the appropriate process.

For a worked example, the parties agree that specified repairs will be completed before closing. The seller supplies a receipt for a different repair and says it is equivalent. The file now requires a factual comparison between the agreed work and performed work. An inspection professional may evaluate completion; the principals may negotiate a change through the appropriate amendment; counsel may address a dispute. The agent should not quietly rewrite the original obligation or declare substantial performance as a legal conclusion.

## T05U3 — Discharge of Contracts

Discharge means that a contractual obligation ends. Full performance is the straightforward path, but agreements can also end or change by mutual agreement, exercise of a stated termination right, substitution of obligations, or operation of applicable law. Breach may produce remedies rather than making every obligation vanish. A party who stops performing should not assume that the other side’s duties, accrued claims, confidentiality obligations, or accounting rights disappear at the same moment.

Distinguish rescission by mutual agreement from a disputed claim to rescind as a legal remedy. Distinguish an assignment of rights from a novation substituting an obligor with the required consent. Distinguish expiration of agency authority from satisfaction of a compensation obligation already earned under the agreement. These are legal arrangements with consequences; nonstandard releases and substitutions require counsel.
### Adapted teaching: Termination of a service relationship and the obligations that do not disappear with authority

An agency relationship is not a possession; it is a state of affairs that persists only while its
conditions persist. It can end because the parties finished what they set out to do, because a date
arrived, because somebody quit, or because a fact in the world changed and there is nothing left to be
an agent about. The scope note the Commission attached to this topic — "Termination of an agent's
authority" — is worded precisely. What ends is *authority*. Your right to be paid, your duty of
confidentiality, and your obligation to account for money can all outlive the authority, and the third
unit takes those up. This unit is about the authority itself.

### A distinction to carry through the whole unit: the right and the power

General agency law distinguishes a principal's *right* to end the relationship from the principal's
*power* to end it. The power is the raw ability to strip the agent of authority; the right is the
question of whether doing so breaches the contract. In the ordinary case a principal has both, and an
agent likewise has the power to walk away and may or may not have the right. **The common-law rule
that a principal always retains the power to revoke, and the exception traditionally described as an
agency coupled with an interest, were not held for this course.** No held Texas statute or Commission
rule states either proposition, and this module therefore does not state them as Texas law. What the
distinction is good for — and the reason it is worth carrying — is that it explains a pattern you can
verify in the held sources: repeatedly, Texas ends the *authority* the moment a triggering fact
occurs, and then handles the money separately.

The clearest held example is a sales agent's sponsorship, below. The clearest everyday example is the
difference between § 1101.652(b)(12), which forces every brokerage service contract other than
property management to carry "a definite termination date that is not subject to prior notice," and
§ 1101.806(c), which lets a broker sue on a signed commission promise after the authority under that
contract has expired. The contract's authority ends on the date. The signed promise is still a signed
promise.

### Completion of the purpose

The ordinary and happy way an agency ends is that it does what it was created to do. The listing
broker sells the house; the transaction closes; there is nothing further to perform. Chapter 1101 does
not announce this as a rule, because it does not need to — a contract to accomplish a thing is
discharged when the thing is accomplished. But the held sources show the Commission thinking in those
terms: 22 TAC § 535.2 measures record retention from "the date of closing, termination of the
contract, or end of a real estate transaction," treating those as three distinct ending events, and
§ 1101.652(b)(31) speaks of disbursement "before the completion or termination of the real estate
transaction."

One frequent misunderstanding: the acceptance of an offer does not end a listing. It fixes the
transaction being performed under it. Rule 22 TAC § 535.156(a), switches off
exactly one duty and only by writing — the license holder stops having to submit offers after
acceptance only "if the principal has agreed in writing that offers are not to be submitted after the
principal has entered into a contract to buy, sell, rent, or lease a property." Absent that written
agreement, back-up offers keep coming to you and you keep delivering them.

### Expiration of the stated term

Because § 1101.652(b)(12) requires a definite termination date in almost every brokerage service
contract, expiration is the second-most common ending. Read the qualifier, because students skip it
every time. The date must be *definite*, and it must *not be subject to prior notice* — so a clause
providing that the agreement continues until either party gives thirty days' notice does not satisfy
the section. And the section carves out "a contract to perform property management services," which
may run open-ended. An agreement that expires on December 31, 2026 is definite. One that expires
ninety days after either party notifies the other is not. A property management agreement may run
without a definite termination date; a listing may not.

When the date passes, the authority to act under that agreement is gone. You are not the seller's
agent on the day after the listing expires; if you show that property the next week, you are showing
it without authority.

Practise the arithmetic on a real agreement. A Lubbock listing signed on February 1, 2026, with a
stated termination date of July 31, 2026, at a total compensation of six percent on a home that would
sell for $240,000, is an authority that runs 181 days and a fee of $14,400 if the sale closes within
it. On August 1 the authority is gone and the $14,400 is gone with it — unless the agreement contains
a protection or override clause, which is a contractual survival term the third unit takes up. Notice
how naturally students slide from the listing having expired to the commission having been lost. Those
are two separate questions governed by two separate instruments: the termination date governs
authority; the signed writing governs the fee under § 1101.806(c).

### Mutual agreement

Parties who created a relationship by agreement may end it by agreement. Nothing in the held sources
forbids it, and the Commission assumes it happens: § 535.2 speaks of "termination of the contract" as
a record-retention trigger, and § 1101.652(b)(31) speaks of "termination of the real estate
transaction." Two practical rules follow, both of which are practice rather than statute and are
labelled as such. First, put the release in writing and date it, because you will otherwise be
arguing about the date on which your authority ended and on which the four-year retention clock under
§ 535.2 started. Second, deal with the money in the same document. A mutual release that says nothing
about a protection period or about earnest money in the broker's trust account has resolved the easy
half of the problem and left the hard half.

### Revocation by the principal and renunciation by the agent

A principal who tells the broker to stop has revoked. An agent who tells the principal it is quitting
has renounced. Texas law as held here does not enumerate these as terminating events, and **the Texas
case law fixing the measure of damages for a wrongful revocation or a wrongful renunciation was not
held for this course.** What can be said from the held sources is more useful than a half-remembered
damages formula.

First, a revocation does not vaporize a signed promise to pay. Section 1101.806(c) conditions an
action for a commission on a written, signed promise or memorandum; it does not condition it on the
agency still being alive when suit is filed. If the seller signed and then revoked, the writing still
exists and the seller is still the party against whom the action would be brought.

Second, the limitations clock is generous and it runs both ways. Civil Practice & Remedies Code
§ 16.004(a) provides that "[a] person must bring suit on the following actions not later than four
years after the day the cause of action accrues," and the list includes "debt" and "breach of
fiduciary duty." A broker's suit for an unpaid commission and a client's suit for breach of fiduciary
duty sit in the same four-year window. A relationship you ended in March 2026 can produce a fiduciary
duty petition in February 2030.

Third, renouncing badly is a regulatory problem, not only a contractual one. A license holder who
abandons a client mid-transaction is exposed under § 1101.652(b)(1), which reaches negligence or
incompetency in brokerage, and § 1101.652(b)(2), which reaches conduct "that is dishonest or in bad
faith or that demonstrates untrustworthiness." And the two-calendar-day response obligation in 22 TAC
§ 535.157 does not stop applying because you have decided to withdraw. Silence is not a method of
resignation.

### Death or incapacity of a party

Here the held sources give you something concrete, and it is worth more than the doctrine most courses
recite. When a Texas seller dies, the property does not sit in limbo waiting for probate. Estates Code
§ 101.001(a) provides that if a person dies leaving a lawful will, "all of the person's estate that is
devised by the will vests immediately in the devisees" and "all of the person's estate that is not
devised by the will vests immediately in the person's heirs at law"; subsection (b) provides that "the
estate of a person who dies intestate vests immediately in the person's heirs at law." Both are
subject to § 101.051, under which the estate vests subject to the payment of "the debts of the
decedent, except as exempted by law" and "any court-ordered child support payments that are delinquent
on the date of the decedent's death."

Follow the consequence for your listing. Your listing agreement was signed by a person who owned the
property. At the instant of death, title vests in someone else — devisees or heirs. The person who
gave you authority no longer owns the thing you were authorized to sell, and the new owners never
agreed to anything with you. That is why a listing does not survive the owner's death as a practical
matter, whatever a course tells you about common-law rules, and this course does not need those rules
to reach the answer. Estates Code § 101.003 completes the picture: on issuance of letters testamentary
or of administration, "the executor or administrator has the right to possession of the estate as the
estate existed at the death of the testator or intestate," and shall recover and hold the estate "in
trust to be disposed of in accordance with the law."

Chapter 1101 anticipates that you will end up dealing with those representatives. Section 1101.551(2)
defines "Party," for the representation subchapter, as "a prospective buyer, seller, landlord, or
tenant or an authorized representative of a buyer, seller, landlord, or tenant, including a trustee,
guardian, executor, administrator, receiver, or attorney-in-fact." The term "does not include a
license holder who represents a party." So the executor of the Hidalgo County estate is a party with
whom you may contract; but you contract afresh, with a new signature and a new definite termination
date under § 1101.652(b)(12).

**On incapacity, the held sources are thinner and you should know it.** Section 1101.551(2) recognizes
a guardian and an attorney-in-fact as authorized representatives, and § 1101.005(2) exempts from
Chapter 1101 "an attorney-in-fact authorized under a power of attorney to conduct not more than three
real estate transactions annually." **The Texas statute governing durable powers of attorney, and any
rule about whether an agency survives a principal's incapacity, were not held for this course and are
not stated here.** If a client's capacity becomes doubtful mid-listing, that is a question for the
broker and for counsel, not for your judgment in the field.

### Destruction of the subject property

If the four-plex in Beaumont that you listed burns to the foundation, there is nothing left to sell in
the condition the agreement described. **A Texas statute or Commission rule stating that destruction
of the subject matter terminates an agency was not held for this course**, and this module does not
manufacture one. What the held sources do tell you is what your obligations look like the morning
after the fire. Rule 22 TAC § 535.156(c) imposes "an affirmative duty to keep the principal informed
at all times of significant information applicable to the transaction or transactions in which the
license holder is acting as agent for the principal," and a total loss is significant information.
Section 1101.652(b)(3) and (b)(4) continue to make it a disciplinary matter to misrepresent to a
potential buyer a significant defect known to the license holder, or to fail to disclose one. Whatever
happens to the agency, the property's condition does not become somebody else's problem.

### Bankruptcy

**Chapter 1101 does not state that a party's bankruptcy terminates an agency, and no held source in
this course does; that rule was not held and is not taught here as Texas law.** The only place
bankruptcy appears in the held chapter is the Real Estate Recovery Trust Account subchapter —
§ 1101.606(c) addresses an aggrieved person "precluded by action of a bankruptcy court from executing
a judgment or perfecting a judgment lien." That is a recovery-fund provision, not an
agency-termination provision. If a seller files, the practical reality is that the property becomes
subject to a federal proceeding and the person who signed your listing may no longer control it — but
the governing law is federal bankruptcy law, which this course does not hold, and the correct
professional response is to escalate to your broker.

### Termination of the sales agent's sponsorship

This is the terminating event most likely to happen to *you*, and it is the one Texas law states with
the most precision. Section 1101.351(c) makes sponsorship a condition of acting at all: a sales agent
"may not engage or attempt to engage in real estate brokerage unless the sales agent is sponsored by a
licensed broker and is acting for that broker." Section 1101.367(a) then supplies the mechanics: "When
the relationship of a sales agent with the sales agent's sponsoring broker terminates, the terminating
party shall immediately notify in writing both the other party and the commission. On receiving the
written notice, the commission shall place the sales agent license on inactive status."

Take that apart. The duty to notify falls on "the terminating party" — whichever side ended it. The
notice is written, it goes to *two* recipients, the other party and the Commission, and the timing
word is "immediately," not *promptly* and not *within thirty days*. And the consequence is automatic:
on receipt, the license goes inactive. An inactive sales agent has no authority to perform brokerage
for anyone, because § 1101.351(c) is unsatisfied. Every agency in which you were acting for the
brokerage ends as to you at that moment — not because you resigned from the client, but because you
lost the only lawful channel through which you could act.

Note the direction of travel. The client's agency was with the *broker*, and it does not follow you
out the door. The listing on the ranch outside Bandera belongs to your former brokerage, and the
seller's relationship is with that firm unless the seller and the firm agree otherwise. Section
1101.367(b) tells you how to get back to work: the Commission may return the license to active status
if, before the expiration date of the sales agent license, a licensed broker files a request advising
the Commission that the broker assumes sponsorship, accompanied by the appropriate fee. Subsection (c)
adds that an inactive sales agent whose license is not subject to the § 1101.454 education
requirements must, as a condition of returning to active status, provide proof of attending
continuing education as specified by § 1101.455 during the two years preceding the date the
application to return to active status is filed.

The mirror image also exists. Section 1101.366(c) provides that "[a] broker applying for inactive
status shall terminate the broker's association with each sales agent sponsored by the broker by
giving written notice to each sales agent before the 30th day preceding the date the broker applies
for inactive status." Thirty days' written notice, running backward from the application date. A
broker on inactive status, under § 1101.366(d), "may not perform any activity regulated under this
chapter" and "must pay renewal fees."

**Delegated supervision ends the same way — in writing, on the record.** Broker responsibility is
Law of Agency Module 09's subject, but the endings inside it belong here: under 22 TAC § 535.2 a sponsored sales
agent's authorized scope may be limited or revoked only *in writing*, any delegation of supervisory
responsibility must itself be in writing, and the broker must notify the Commission within 30 days
after a reportable delegation ends, just as at its start. An oral instruction not to handle
commercial leases leaves the broker responsible for commercial leases.

### Suspension or revocation of the license

A license the Commission suspends stops functioning as authority. Under 22 TAC § 535.141(g), taught
in Law of Agency Module 10, a suspended license holder "may not during the period of any suspension perform,
attempt to perform, or advertise to perform any act for which a license is required by the Act or
Commission rules," and subsection (h) requires written notice not later than the third day before the
suspension takes effect to everyone it will cut off. The item to remember is the last on that list: a
person who "holds money in trust in any transaction in which the person is acting as a broker" must
"remit such money in accordance with the instructions of the principals." Even where the regulator is
taking the authority away, the trust money must still be dealt with. That is the bridge into the
final unit.


A practical discharge record states what ended, the effective event or document, which duties survive, and what must happen to documents, keys, access, and funds. A listing termination does not by itself terminate a pending buyer-seller contract. A buyer-seller termination does not necessarily resolve the broker’s compensation or escrow entitlement. A release should be read by the affected parties and appropriate counsel rather than assumed to release every possible claim.

In a worked file, a seller fires the broker while a purchase contract remains pending. The broker’s authority and service relationship require review under the listing agreement and law. The seller’s promise to convey to the buyer is a separate obligation. The agent should notify the supervising broker, preserve the records, stop unauthorized activity, and coordinate any lawful transition. Do not tell the buyer that the sale automatically died because the listing ended.

## T05U4 — Contracts Used in the Real Estate Business

The business uses contracts not only to convey land but to authorize representation, management, marketing, referral, inspection, valuation, financing, title services, and occupancy. Each document should be matched to its actual purpose. A disclosure notice explains relationships or facts; it is not necessarily the employment contract. A receipt proves delivery of money; it is not necessarily a release of claims. A purchase offer is not proof that a lender has committed funds.
### Adapted teaching: Authority to act must match the transaction and the document

An agent acts for a principal. That is only tolerable if the agent's actions stay inside what the
principal actually authorised — which is why the second half of your topic's scope note is
"authority of agent." A student's instinct is that authority is whatever the client is willing to
let you do. The Texas instinct is different and sharper: your authority is a chain, and every link
is written down somewhere.

**A caution about vocabulary before you start.** The phrases you will hear in national textbooks —
*express authority*, *implied authority*, *apparent authority*, *authority by estoppel*,
*ratification* — are common-law categories developed in case law. **They do not appear in Chapter
1101 or in the Commission rules held for this course.** Texas case law was not held for this course,
so this module does not state the Texas tests for those doctrines and you should not attribute a
Texas holding to them on the strength of anything written here. What this module can do — and what
will actually keep you out of trouble in Texas — is teach the categories as the working concepts
they are, and then show you exactly where the held Texas text puts the boundaries.

**Actual authority: express.** Express actual authority is what the principal has told the agent, in
words, that the agent may do. In Texas brokerage practice that means the listing agreement, the
buyer or tenant representation agreement, the property management agreement, and any written
instruction that follows. Read them. The single most common source of an authority dispute is a
license holder who never read the paragraph limiting what he could do.

The statute polices the edges of express authority hard. Section 1101.652(b)(19) makes it a ground
for discipline to offer real property for sale or lease without the owner's knowledge and consent,
(b)(20) to offer it on terms other than those the owner authorised, and (b)(18) to place a sign on
property offering it for sale or lease without the owner's written consent. **Law of Agency Module 10 teaches §
1101.652.** Notice what these three have in common: each is a licence-threatening
offence built out of the simple idea that you may not act beyond what you were authorised to do. In
Texas, exceeding your authority is not merely a contract problem with your client. It is a
disciplinary matter with the Commission.

**Actual authority: implied.** Implied actual authority is the authority reasonably necessary to
carry out what was expressly authorised. A seller who signs a listing has, in the ordinary course,
authorised you to advertise the property, to hold showings, to receive and present offers — because
a listing that authorised none of those would authorise nothing at all. The boundary is worth
stating carefully, though: implied authority fills gaps, it does not override instructions. If the
seller instructs you in writing that no lockbox may be installed, no amount of *but that's how
listings normally work* creates authority to install one.

Two held provisions show you where the implied-authority boundary is drawn in Texas. First, on
money: trust money a broker accepts is held in a fiduciary capacity and must go to the broker's
designated trust account or to an authorised escrow agent, a sales agent may not maintain a trust
account, and trust money a sales agent receives must be delivered immediately to the sponsoring
broker (22 TAC § 535.146(b); Law of Agency Module 07 teaches § 535.146). Being authorised to sell the house
does not carry with it authority to hold the earnest money. Second, on binding the principal: §
1101.557(c)(1) says that for purposes of that section, "a license holder who has the authority to
bind a party to a lease or sale under a power of attorney or
a property management agreement is also a party to the lease or sale." The Legislature had to name
the two instruments that confer binding authority. That tells you an ordinary listing or buyer
representation agreement does not confer it. You negotiate; your client signs.

**Apparent authority, and the Texas answer to it.** Apparent authority is the classical name for the
situation where a third person reasonably believes the agent has authority because of something the
*principal* did or allowed, even though the agent in fact lacks it. Texas real estate law's most
concrete engagement with that idea is not in the Occupations Code at all. It is in the broker
responsibility rule, and you should learn its first paragraph close to word-perfect. 22 TAC §
535.2(a):

> A broker is required to notify a sponsored sales agent in writing of the scope of the sales
> agent's authorized activities under the Act. Unless such scope is limited or revoked in writing,
> a broker is responsible for the authorized acts of the broker's sales agents, but the broker is
> not required to supervise the sales agents directly. If a broker permits a sponsored sales agent
> to conduct activities beyond the scope explicitly authorized by the broker, those are acts for
> which the broker is responsible.

Three separate rules live in those three sentences. The scope of your activities must be given to
you **in writing**. Limiting or revoking that scope must also be **in writing** — an oral
instruction from your broker does not shrink your written scope for purposes of this rule. And if
the broker *permits* you to work outside the scope he explicitly authorised, the broker owns those
acts anyway. That last sentence is the Texas version of the apparent-authority problem: a broker
cannot hand you a narrow written scope, watch you exceed it for six months, and then disclaim your
conduct when the complaint arrives.

**Where a Texas sales agent's authority actually comes from.** Assemble the chain. The client
authorises the broker (§ 1101.557(a) makes the broker the party's agent). The broker authorises the
sales agent, in writing, and is responsible for those authorised acts (22 TAC § 535.2(a)). The sales
agent may not act at all except for that broker (§ 1101.351(c): "A licensed sales agent may not
engage or attempt to engage in real estate brokerage unless the sales agent is sponsored by a
licensed broker and is acting for that broker."). You do not derive authority directly from the
client. You derive it from your sponsoring broker, whose own authority comes from the client.

Two further links keep the chain intact at the points where students most want to shortcut it. 22
TAC § 535.2(f): "Listings and other agreements for real estate brokerage services must be solicited
and accepted in a broker's name." The listing is not yours; you cannot take it with you when you
change sponsorship, because it was never yours to take. And compensation travels the same route: §
1101.651(b) bars a sales agent from accepting compensation for a real estate transaction from anyone
other than the sponsoring broker (or the broker who was sponsoring her when she earned it), §
1101.651(c) bars a sales agent from paying a commission except through the sponsoring broker, and 22
TAC § 535.3 states the same requirement as one of the sponsoring broker's written consent. **Module
09 teaches § 1101.651(a)–(c) and § 535.3.** A buyer who wants to hand you a cheque at closing is
asking you to break the chain.

The chain can be extended but not cut. 22 TAC § 535.2(e) lets a broker delegate responsibility to
assist in administering compliance with the Act and Rules without relinquishing overall
responsibility for supervising sponsored license holders, requires any license holder who leads,
supervises, directs or manages a team to be delegated as a supervisor in writing, and requires
notice of the delegation to the Commission; **Law of Agency Module 09 teaches § 535.2, including the delegation
and notice mechanics.** If you join a team, the person leading it holds delegated authority, in
writing — not authority that materialised because everyone calls her the team lead.


The authority question is especially important when one person speaks for another. A sales agent acts through the sponsoring broker. An entity acts through properly authorized people. A principal may authorize an agent to transmit offers without authorizing acceptance. A property manager may be empowered to arrange ordinary repairs while lacking power to sell the property or grant a purchase option. Match the act to the scope of authority instead of relying on the broad title “agent.”

For a practical file review, place the purchase contract and brokerage agreement side by side. Identify which party owes which payment, when the obligation is earned or due, and what happens on termination. Then review service agreements separately. If an inspection agreement limits the inspector’s service scope, the purchase contract does not expand that professional engagement automatically. If the lender has conditions, a broker’s promise about a closing date does not waive them.

## T05U5 — Listing Agreements

A listing is a service and agency agreement between the owner and broker. It does not convey the property and does not by itself bind the owner to accept a purchaser’s proposed terms. The owner’s authorized asking price and marketing instructions matter, but a listing price is not a signed offer to every person willing to pay it. The actual listing language and applicable law govern services, authority, compensation, and termination.
### Adapted teaching: Listing obligations, compensation structures, authority, and required term discipline

A listing agreement is the most consequential document you will ask a consumer to sign, and most new
license holders misunderstand what it is. They think of it as a marketing authorization. It is that,
incidentally. What it actually is, is two things at once.

**First, it is an employment contract.** A seller is hiring a broker to perform services, and
promising to pay for them on stated conditions. Everything you know about contracts applies: offer,
acceptance, consideration, definite terms, capacity, a lawful object. If the promise to pay fails as
a contract, the broker does not get paid, no matter how well the broker worked.

**Second, it is the act that creates the agency relationship.** Occupations Code § 1101.557(a)
provides that "A broker who represents a party in a real estate transaction or who lists real estate
for sale under an exclusive agreement for a party is that party's agent" — two independent routes to
agency, joined by "or," and the second is the listing itself. Section 1101.557 is taught in full in
Law of Agency Module 02. What matters here is the consequence for this topic: sign the listing and you have
created a fiduciary relationship, whether or not you had one in mind at the kitchen table. Seller
agency in Texas is, in the ordinary case, listing agency.

Two boundaries frame the document before you read a word of it. Taking a listing is licensed
activity — § 1101.002(1)(A)(iii), (iv) and (viii) and 22 TAC § 535.4(f) put soliciting, negotiating
and taking listings inside the definition of brokerage. And the listing is not
yours; 22 TAC § 535.2(f) requires that listings "be solicited and accepted in a broker's name,"
taught in Law of Agency Module 09. A sales agent negotiates, signs and works listings, but the contracting party
on the brokerage side is the sponsoring broker. That allocation decides who may sue for the
commission, who must keep the file, and — as you will see below — who may lawfully pay you.

**The writing requirement, and why it is existential**

Every course tells students to get the listing in writing. Most of them tell you it is because of
the statute of frauds. That is not quite right in Texas, and the difference matters.

Open Business & Commerce Code § 26.01. Subsection (a) is the general rule: "A promise or agreement
described in Subsection (b) of this section is not enforceable unless the promise or agreement, or a
memorandum of it, is" — and then two requirements — "(1) in writing; and (2) signed by the person to
be charged with the promise or agreement or by someone lawfully authorized to sign for him." Now
read subsection (b), which lists what the rule reaches. Among the eight items are "a contract for
the sale of real estate", "a lease of real estate for a term longer than one year", "an agreement
which is not to be performed within one year from the date of making the agreement", and, in
subdivision (7), "a promise or agreement to pay a commission for the sale or purchase of" — and here
you must read the subparagraphs — "(A) an oil or gas mining lease; (B) an oil or gas royalty; (C)
minerals; or (D) a mineral interest".

Look at what is *not* there. The general statute of frauds in Chapter 26 does not, by its own terms,
list a promise to pay a commission for the sale of an ordinary house. It lists commission promises
for oil and gas leases, royalties, minerals and mineral interests. A student who has been taught
"the statute of frauds requires listings to be in writing" has been taught a conclusion without its
authority, and if a mineral-heavy West Texas transaction ever puts the question in front of that
student, the student will not know which statute is doing the work.

The statute that does the work for ordinary real estate commissions is in TRELA. Occupations Code
§ 1101.806 is titled LIABILITY FOR PAYMENT OF COMPENSATION OR COMMISSION, and it has four subsections
that you should be able to recite in substance. Subsection (a) carves two things out of the
section's reach: it "does not: (1) apply to an agreement to share compensation among license
holders; or (2) limit a cause of action among brokers for interference with business relationships."
Subsection (b) provides: "A person may not maintain an action to collect compensation for an act as
a broker or sales agent that is performed in this state unless the person alleges and proves that
the person was: (1) a license holder at the time the act was commenced; or (2) an attorney licensed
in any state." Subsection (c) is the writing requirement, and here is its entire text:

> A person may not maintain an action in this state to recover a commission for the sale or purchase of
> real estate unless the promise or agreement on which the action is based, or a memorandum, is in
> writing and signed by the party against whom the action is brought or by a person authorized by that
> party to sign the document.

Subsection (d) adds a fourth trap: "A license holder who fails to advise a buyer as provided by
Section 1101.555 may not receive payment of or recover any commission agreed to be paid on the
sale." Section 1101.555 — the written advice, when an offer is signed, that the buyer either have
the abstract examined by an attorney of the buyer's choosing or obtain a title policy — is taught in
Law of Agency Module 12, and the same failure is separately a disciplinary ground under § 1101.652(b)(29)
(Law of Agency Module 10). The point for a listing broker is the commission consequence: a broker who never speaks
to the buyer at all still has a fee that can be defeated by a missing written advisory in the
transaction file.

This is why the writing is *existential* rather than prudent. An unwritten listing is not merely
harder to prove. Under § 1101.806(c) the broker "may not maintain an action" on it. The claim does
not get to a jury to be weighed against the seller's denial; the claim does not get filed
successfully at all. A seller who shook your hand, watched you spend $4,000 on photography, staging
and print, accepted an offer you produced, closed, and then refused to pay has, on those facts,
given you nothing you can sue on. The statute is indifferent to how unfair that feels. It is a
bright line by design, because the Legislature decided that the cost of occasionally sheltering a
dishonest seller is lower than the cost of litigating swearing matches about oral commission
promises.

**What the listing must contain**

Here is an honest statement that most course materials will not make: **TRELA does not contain a
single enumerated list of the essential terms of a seller's listing agreement.** There is no section
headed "required contents of a listing." What Texas does instead is scatter the requirements through
prohibitions and rule-making provisions, and you assemble the list by reading them together. That is
worth knowing on its own, because it tells you where to look when a question arises that no
checklist answers.

The disciplinary grounds that supply most of the list are in § 1101.652, which Law of Agency Module 10 teaches in
full. Read here only for what each one forces into the document.

- **A definite termination date, not subject to prior notice** — § 1101.652(b)(12), which carves out
  only a contract to perform property management services and which reaches any contract for
  services requiring a license, buyer representation agreements included. "Until sold" is not a
  date; neither is a term that runs until one party gives thirty days' notice. An open-ended listing
  is a disciplinary matter, not a retention strategy.
- **Price and terms the owner has authorized** — § 1101.652(b)(20) (offering on terms other than
  those authorized by the owner) and (b)(19) (offering without the owner's knowledge and consent).
  The listing is where that authorization is recorded, and "the seller told me on the phone it was
  fine" is precisely the evidentiary problem the writing exists to avoid.
- **Written consent for the sign** — § 1101.652(b)(18), whose operative word is *written*. A signed
  listing that authorizes signage supplies the consent; "sure, put a sign up" does not.
- **The broker as the contracting party**, under 22 TAC § 535.2(f) (Law of Agency Module 09).

**Compensation, and a statement that it is negotiable.** Occupations Code § 1101.155(c) tells you
what the Commission must include if it adopts a listing contract form: "A listing contract form
adopted by the commission that relates to the contractual obligations between a seller of real
estate and a license holder acting as an agent for the seller must include: (1) a provision
informing the parties to the contract that real estate commissions are negotiable; and (2) a
provision explaining the availability of Texas coastal natural hazards information important to
coastal residents, if that information is appropriate." The negotiability statement is not a
courtesy; the Legislature put it in the statute that governs the form's contents. The same policy
appears on the buyer side in § 1101.563(c)(2). If a seller asks whether the
rate is fixed, the answer is no, and Texas law has told you twice to say so.

**Intermediary authorization, if the parties want it.** The intermediary machinery is
§§ 1101.559–1101.561 and § 1101.651(d). What belongs in this unit is that the
listing is the instrument that decides in advance whether your brokerage can serve both sides:
under § 1101.559(b) a written listing that authorizes intermediary status is itself sufficient to
establish the party's written consent, provided it specifies in conspicuous bold or underlined print
the conduct § 1101.651(d) prohibits. If the listing is silent, the brokerage does not have that
consent — and § 1101.561(b) requires a broker who agrees to represent both a buyer and a seller in
the same transaction to act as an intermediary.

**Whether TREC requires a particular listing form.** Read 22 TAC § 537.11(a) carefully. It requires
mandatory forms "When negotiating contracts binding the sale, exchange, option, or lease of any
interest in real property," and lists four exceptions, including "transactions for which no contract
form has been approved for mandatory use by the Commission," where the license holder may use a form
"prepared by an attorney licensed by this state, or a trade association in consultation with an
attorney licensed by this state" carrying the identifying content the rule then itemises. By its own
words that subsection is aimed at the instrument that binds the sale, exchange, option or lease.
Which listing agreement forms, if any, the Commission has approved for mandatory use is not
something the sources held for this course state, and this module therefore does not tell you. What
the held sources do tell you is what such a form must contain if the Commission adopts one
(§ 1101.155(c)) and what the alternative-form pathway requires (§ 537.11(a)(4)). In ordinary Texas
residential practice the listing form used is a trade-association form — that is a statement about
practice, not about law, and nothing is cited for it.

**The unauthorized-practice-of-law boundary at the listing table.** Section 537.11(b) forbids a
license holder to "practice law", to "directly or indirectly offer, give or attempt to give legal
advice", to "give advice or opinions as to the legal effect of any contract forms or other such
instruments which may affect the title to real estate", and to "draft or recommend language to be
included in a contract form defining or affecting the rights, obligations, or remedies of the
principals of a real estate transaction, including escalation, appraisal, or contingency clauses".
Subsection (c) preserves what you may do: the section does not "prevent the license holder from
explaining to the license holder's principals the meaning of informational items or choices in a
contract form, as long as the license holder does not practice law or give legal advice". Subsection
(d) adds that it is not the practice of law to add informational items to an authorized form, or,
"if specifically instructed in writing by a principal, add language to or strike language from a
contract form, as long as any change is made conspicuous, including underlining additions, striking
through deletions, or employing some other method which clearly indicates the change being made."
Two habits follow. When a seller asks you to change the listing's language, get the instruction in
writing and make the change conspicuous. And under § 537.11(f), "A license holder shall advise the
license holder's principals that the instrument they are about to execute is binding on them" — say
it out loud before the seller signs, every time.

**Keep the file.** 22 TAC § 535.2(h) requires the broker to retain listings and other commission
agreements, disclosures, substantive communications, offers, contracts and compensation records for
at least four years from closing, termination of the contract, or the end of the transaction — not
four years from the date of signature. Law of Agency Module 09 teaches § 535.2.

**The three listing types and what each costs**

The labels **exclusive right to sell**, **exclusive agency** and **open listing** are the vocabulary
of practice. The Texas statutes held for this course do not define the three types. They use the
related terms "exclusive agreement" in § 1101.557(a) and "exclusive agency" in § 1101.652(b)(22)
without setting out a taxonomy of listing forms. So learn the types as economics, which is what they
are, and attach them to the statutory language you do have.

**Exclusive right to sell.** The seller agrees that the listing broker earns the agreed compensation
if the property sells during the listing period, regardless of who produces the buyer — the listing
broker, a cooperating broker, a neighbour, or the seller personally. To the broker, this is the only
structure that reliably justifies spending money before there is any certainty of a closing. To the
seller, it is the most expensive structure in one specific scenario: the seller finds the buyer
without any help and still owes the full amount.

**Exclusive agency.** The seller agrees that no other *broker* will be engaged during the term, but
reserves the right to sell the property personally without owing compensation. To the seller, this
looks like a free option. To the broker it is a structural conflict, because the broker's marketing
generates the very inquiries the seller can then convert commission-free, and every buyer who
telephones the seller directly is a threat to the fee. Expect a broker to price this risk, or
decline it.

**Open listing.** The seller may engage any number of brokers, and owes compensation only to the one
who produces the buyer; the seller may also sell personally and owe nothing. To a broker, an open
listing is a lottery ticket, and brokers spend accordingly.

Work the money. A seller in Waco lists a house and agrees to a total compensation of 5.5 percent of
the sales price. The property sells for $350,000. Five and a half percent of $350,000 is $19,250.
Under an exclusive right to sell, the listing broker's entitlement is $19,250 no matter who found
the buyer, so a seller whose own cousin walks in and buys still owes $19,250. Under an exclusive
agency listing, the same seller who genuinely produced that cousin without the broker's involvement
owes nothing, and keeps the $19,250. That $19,250 gap is the entire practical difference between the
two forms, and it is the number a seller is really asking about when the seller says, "what if I
sell it myself?"

You should be able to answer that question without evasion, and to say what the brokerage will and
will not do under each structure. What you must not do is take an exclusive right to sell while
telling the seller it behaves like an exclusive agency: that is a false promise likely to influence a
person to enter an agreement under § 1101.652(b)(5), and conduct that is dishonest or demonstrates
untrustworthiness under § 1101.652(b)(2), both taught in Law of Agency Module 10.

One more concept belongs here and cannot be sourced: the industry doctrine of **procuring cause**,
used to decide which of two competing brokers earned a fee. The phrase "procuring cause" does not
appear anywhere in the Occupations Code chapter or the Commission rules held for this course, and no
source stating the doctrine's elements was held. It is therefore not taught here. What is held is
§ 1101.806(a)(1) and (2), which put compensation-sharing agreements among license holders outside
§ 1101.806 and preserve interference claims among brokers — which is to say that Texas leaves
broker-versus-broker fee disputes to the brokers' own agreements and to other law.

**Net listings**

A net listing inverts the broker's incentive, and Texas regulates it by rule rather than banning it
outright. 22 TAC § 535.16 opens with the standard the rest of the section protects: "A broker is
obligated under a listing contract to negotiate the best possible transaction for the principal, the
broker has agreed to represent." Then it defines the arrangement:

> A "net listing" is a listing agreement in which the broker's commission is the difference ("net")
> between the sales proceeds and an amount desired by the owner of the real property.

Then the restriction, which you must quote with its condition attached: "A broker may not take net
listings unless the principal requires a net listing and the principal appears to be familiar with
current market values of real property." Two conditions, joined by "and," and both must hold. The
principal must *require* the net listing — the broker may not propose it, sell it, or steer the
seller into it — and the principal must *appear to be familiar with current market values of real
property*. Familiarity is not assumed from ownership. An heir in Bastrop who inherited a house she
has never lived in, in a market she has never studied, does not appear familiar with current market
values, and a broker who takes her net listing has violated the rule regardless of how insistently
she asked for it.

The rule then explains itself, which is unusual and useful: "The use of a net listing places an
upper limit on the principal's expectancy and places the broker's interest above the principal's
interest with reference to obtaining the best possible price." That is the whole objection in one
sentence. Under a percentage commission, broker and seller move in the same direction — a higher
price pays both of them more. Under a net listing they move in opposite directions above the net
figure, because every dollar of price above the seller's number goes entirely to the broker. The
arrangement therefore contradicts the duty the same rule states in its first sentence, and the
fiduciary command in 22 TAC § 531.2 that the license holder place no personal interest above the
client's (Law of Agency Module 02).

Finally the two protective conditions the rule forces into the agreement itself: "If a net listing
is used, the listing agreement must assure the principal of not less than the principal's desired
price and limit the broker to a specified maximum commission." A **floor** for the seller and a
**ceiling** for the broker, both written into the listing. Miss either one and the net listing is
non-compliant even if the seller demanded it and knows the market cold.

**Pricing the listing, and the price-opinion obligation**

The third paragraph of 22 TAC § 535.16 is easy to skim past and is the one most likely to catch a
new license holder: "A real estate license holder is obligated to provide a broker price opinion or
comparative market analysis on a property when negotiating a listing or offering to purchase the
property for the license holder's own account as a result of contact made while acting as a real
estate agent." Two separate triggers. The obvious one — **when negotiating a listing**, you owe the
seller a broker price opinion or CMA. And the second, which exists to stop a specific abuse: if you
make contact with an owner while acting as a real estate agent and then decide to buy the property
*for your own account*, you still owe that owner the price opinion. You may not use your position to
acquire a property from an owner you have left uninformed about its value.

What you produce is governed by 22 TAC § 535.17: a license holder may not
perform an appraisal or provide an opinion of value without the appraiser credential Chapter 1103
requires, and what keeps the brokerage product lawful is the Commission's prescribed disclaimer,
which must be reproduced verbatim in at least 12-point font as part of any written opinion, analysis
or estimate of worth or sale price. Because the listing belongs to the broker, so does the CMA: a
sales agent may prepare, sign and present one, but must submit it in the broker's name, and the
broker is responsible for it. Two related duties sit alongside it — § 1101.652(a)(6), which reaches a
license holder who fails to consider market conditions for the specific geographic area being served
(Law of Agency Module 10), and 22 TAC § 535.2(i)(1), which requires the broker's written policies to ensure each
sponsored sales agent has competence in the geographic market area where the agent represents
clients (Law of Agency Module 09). Pulling county-wide averages for a house in a specific Fort Worth neighbourhood,
where two streets can differ by twenty percent, is the conduct those provisions describe.

**Getting paid, and who may pay you**

Compensation out of a listing flows only in the channels § 1101.651 permits, and Law of Agency Module 09 teaches
that section's first three subsections in full. Three consequences belong to a listing broker.

A broker may pay a commission only to a license holder, or to a broker licensed in another state who
conducted none of the Texas negotiations — § 1101.651(a), with 22 TAC § 535.131 confirming that only
Texas license holders may handle negotiations physically conducted within Texas.

A sales agent may accept compensation only from "the broker that is sponsoring the sales agent or
was sponsoring the sales agent when the sales agent earned the compensation" — § 1101.651(b). Do not
drop that final clause, because it solves the most common real problem in a license holder's career:
you list a house in April under Broker A, move your sponsorship to Broker B in June, and the house
closes in August. Broker A may pay you. Without the clause, changing brokerages would forfeit every
pending file. What you may never do is take the money from the seller, from the title company
directly, or from the cooperating brokerage. Section 1101.651(c) closes the loop in the other
direction: a sales agent may pay a commission only through the sponsoring broker.

And an unlicensed referral source cannot be paid. Section 1101.652(b)(11) makes it a disciplinary
ground to pay or divide a commission or fee with a person who is neither a license holder nor a
broker or sales agent licensed in another state (Law of Agency Module 10), and dressing the payment up as a gift
card does not change the analysis — 22 TAC § 535.20(a) treats merchandise gifts worth more than $50,
rent bonuses and discounts as valuable consideration (Law of Agency Module 09).


When comparing listing types, treat the written terms as controlling. An “exclusive right to sell” label commonly allocates compensation differently from an “exclusive agency” label, but negotiated exceptions and conditions can matter. A reserved prospect, protected period, termination clause, or definition of earned compensation should not be replaced by a sales slogan. The broker should explain permitted informational items accurately and refer legal effect questions as required.

A worked intake begins with an owner who wants to reserve a sale to a named relative. The broker should identify that business request and use the appropriate authorized document or attorney assistance to reflect it. The owner should know the agreement’s actual compensation event and duration before signing. An agent must not describe an exclusive-right arrangement as commission-free whenever the seller finds the buyer if the document says otherwise.

## T05U6 — Buyer Agency Agreement

A buyer agreement defines the broker’s service relationship with the buyer. The current Texas residential framework distinguishes representation from a narrowly limited showing-only relationship. The agreement is not the IABS notice and is not the purchase contract. A consumer can receive an IABS notice without hiring that brokerage; a buyer can sign a service agreement without agreeing to purchase any particular property.
### Adapted teaching: The current residential agreement requirement and its required contents

Start with the date, because this unit is different from the one your predecessors studied. Two
new sections were added to The Real Estate License Act by Acts 2025, 89th Leg., R.S., Ch. 1172
(S.B. 1968), Sec. 10, effective **January 1, 2026**: Occupations Code § 1101.562, "REAL PROPERTY
SHOWINGS WITHOUT REPRESENTATION," and Occupations Code § 1101.563, "WRITTEN AGREEMENT REQUIRED."
The Texas Real Estate Commission published an explanatory article on those changes, "What Changes
in 2026 About Buyer/Tenant Representation in Texas," dated 01-07-2026, which says of the two
sections that "While these two sections are separate, they go together and should be read
together." That article is guidance, not law; the statutory text controls, and this unit works
from the text. But the Commission's advice to read the two sections together is sound, and this
module follows it.

Before 2026, a Texas license holder could work with a buyer for weeks without any signed
agreement, and many did. That is no longer permissible for residential buyers. If you learned the
business before January 1, 2026, or if you are being trained by someone who did, you will hear
habits described as normal that the statute now forbids. Learn the current text.

**Whose agreement is it?** A buyer representation agreement is a contract between the prospective
buyer and a **broker**. It is not a contract between the buyer and you, if you are a sales agent.
Commission rule 22 TAC § 535.2 requires that listings and other agreements for real estate
brokerage services "must be solicited and accepted in a broker's name"; Law of Agency Module 09 teaches that
rule. The consequence for this topic is that when you sit at a kitchen table in Cedar Park and ask
a buyer to sign, the buyer is engaging your broker, your broker's name goes on the agreement, your
broker is answerable for its performance, and you sign as the broker's associate. Students
routinely get this wrong and speak of *my buyer agreement*. The client is the brokerage's client.
That matters when you change sponsorship, when a complaint is filed, and when compensation is
claimed.

**What the agreement does.** In substance, a buyer representation agreement is an employment
contract that engages the broker to perform acts of real estate brokerage for the buyer, defines
what those acts are, sets how long the engagement lasts, states whether the buyer may engage other
brokers at the same time, and fixes what the broker will be paid and by whom. Signing one carries
a statutory consequence: "A broker who represents a party in a real estate transaction or who
lists real estate for sale under an exclusive agreement for a party is that party's agent,"
§ 1101.557(a). Once the broker represents the buyer, the whole apparatus of
agency duties attaches.

**The § 1101.563 mandate — the centre of this module.** Section 1101.563 is short, and every
clause of it does work. Read it in order.

Subsection (a) defines the term that limits the section's reach. "In this section, 'residential
real property' means" three things, and only three: "a single-family house"; "a duplex, triplex,
or quadraplex"; and "a unit in a multiunit residential structure in which title to an individual
unit is transferred to the owner of the unit under a condominium or cooperative system." That is
the whole definition. Notice what is not in it. Unimproved land is not in it — the ten-acre tract
outside Bastrop your buyer wants for a future homesite is not a single-family house. A fifty-unit
apartment building held in one ownership is not in it, because title to individual units is not
transferred to unit owners under a condominium or cooperative system. An office suite, a retail
strip centre, a warehouse in Pharr, a working ranch in Coleman County — none of these fall inside
the definition. TREC's article puts the same limit in plain words: "The written agreement
requirement does not apply to commercial purchasers or residential/commercial tenants."

That last clause deserves emphasis because it is the most common misreading. **Section 1101.563
reaches prospective buyers only.** Its operative sentence in subsection (b) speaks of "a
prospective buyer of residential real property," and every subsequent reference is to "the
prospective buyer." A tenant is not a buyer. A person looking to lease a house in Lubbock for a
year is outside § 1101.563 entirely, no matter how residential the house is. Do not tell a leasing
prospect that state law requires them to sign a representation agreement before you can show them
a rental. That is not what the statute says.

Subsection (b) states the mandate and the moment it attaches: "A license holder who performs any
act of real estate brokerage for a prospective buyer of residential real property must enter into
a written agreement with the prospective buyer before" one of two events. The first is "showing
any residential real property to the prospective buyer." The second is "if no residential real
property will be shown, presenting an offer to purchase residential real property on behalf of the
prospective buyer."

Take those triggers apart. The duty falls on "a license holder," which by § 1101.002(4) means "a
broker or sales agent licensed under this chapter" — so it binds you personally as a sales agent,
not merely your broker. The condition that puts you inside the section is that you are performing
"any act of real estate brokerage" for a prospective residential buyer, and the meaning of that
phrase is broad: § 1101.004(a) and the acts described by § 1101.002(1)(A), which include aiding
"in locating or obtaining real estate for purchase or lease" and negotiating "the listing, sale,
exchange, purchase, or lease of real estate," are taught in Law of Agency Module 01. Helping a buyer find a
house is an act of real estate brokerage. That is the whole business.

The two "before" clauses fix the outer limit. You must have the written agreement in hand **before
you show any residential real property** to that prospective buyer. Not before you write the
offer; not before you open escrow; before the showing. And in the case where you are never going
to show anything — the buyer has already found the house on their own, or is buying from a
relative, or is buying at a distance — the agreement must be in place before you present the offer
to purchase on the buyer's behalf.

Notice the exact wording of the first trigger: "showing any residential real property." Not
*showing the residential real property*. If you have a written agreement with a Frisco buyer that
covers only a specific address, and you then take them to look at a second house, you have shown
residential real property to a prospective buyer, and you need a written agreement that covers it.
Write the agreement to cover the engagement, not one door.

Subsection (c) tells you what must be in the writing. The agreement "must" state each of the
following, and must make one disclosure:

- "the services to be provided by the license holder";
- "the termination date of the agreement";
- "whether the agreement is exclusive or non-exclusive";
- "as applicable," that the license holder either "represents the prospective buyer as the buyer's
  agent" or "does not represent the prospective buyer as the buyer's agent if the only act of real
  estate brokerage being performed is showing real property under Section 1101.562";
- "the amount or rate of compensation the broker will receive and how this amount will be
  determined."

And the agreement must "disclose in conspicuous language that broker compensation is not set by
law and is fully negotiable."

Work through those. The **services** clause forces you to say what you are actually agreeing to
do. TREC's article notes that representation agreements "also can limit the services a broker
agrees to provide to prospective buyers—such as limiting their services to showing only—but they
cannot waive the minimum duties owed to clients which are provided by existing TREC rules and
law." That last qualification is the one to remember: a limited-service agreement narrows the
scope of the engagement; it does not shrink the fiduciary standard within whatever scope you
accepted. You may agree to do less. You may not agree to do it worse.

The **termination date** requirement is not new in substance, and it has independent teeth.
Occupations Code § 1101.652(b)(12) has long made it a disciplinary ground to fail "to specify a
definite termination date that is not subject to prior notice in a contract, other than a contract
to perform property management services, in which the license holder agrees to perform services
for which a license is required under this chapter"; Law of Agency Module 10 teaches § 1101.652. Read the
qualifier carefully, because it is the part students drop. The date must be **definite**, and it
must be "not subject to prior notice" — meaning an agreement that runs until one side gives thirty
days' notice does not satisfy the section. A buyer representation agreement is not a property
management contract, so it needs a definite termination date, and now § 1101.563(c)(1)(B) requires
the date to be stated in the writing as well.

The **exclusive or non-exclusive** clause tells the buyer whether they may engage another broker
at the same time. Under an exclusive agreement, the buyer commits to work through your brokerage
for the term; under a non-exclusive one, they do not. Neither is unlawful; the statute requires
only that the agreement say which it is. There is a hard limit on exclusivity for one narrow kind
of agreement, which we come to in a moment.

The **representation status** clause is the hinge between § 1101.563 and § 1101.562. There are two
boxes, and they are not both available in every case. If you are representing the buyer, the
agreement says you represent the prospective buyer as the buyer's agent. The other box — "does not
represent the prospective buyer as the buyer's agent" — is available only "if the only act of real
estate brokerage being performed is showing real property under Section 1101.562." That is a
narrow gate. You may check the non-representation box only when showing is genuinely the only
brokerage act you are performing for that person.

The **compensation** clause requires "the amount or rate of compensation the broker will receive
and how this amount will be determined." Both halves matter: the number or the rate, and the
method. *To be determined* is not compliance. *Three percent of the sales price* states a rate
and, read with a definition of sales price, states the method. We return to compensation at length
in Law of Agency source unit 5.

The **negotiability disclosure** must be in "conspicuous language" and must say "that broker
compensation is not set by law and is fully negotiable." A comparable statement has long been
required in listing contract forms the Commission adopts — § 1101.155(c)(1) — but the Commission
has not adopted a buyer representation form, and § 1101.563(c)(2) reaches the agreement itself
regardless of who drafted it. Put the sentence in, make it conspicuous, and do not bury it in a
paragraph of recitals.

**Showing-only agreements.** Subsections (d) and (e) govern the special case of an agreement
entered into solely so that you can show property to someone you do not represent. Subsection (d)
provides: "A license holder who enters into a written agreement with a prospective buyer for the
sole purpose of showing real property under Section 1101.562 shall enter into a separate agreement
with the prospective buyer if additional real estate brokerage acts are to be provided after
showing the real property." So the showing-only agreement does not silently upgrade itself. The
moment you begin doing more than showing — advising, negotiating, drafting an offer — you need a
separate agreement, and that separate agreement is the one where the representation box gets
checked differently.

Subsection (e) puts two hard limits on the showing-only agreement. It "may not" "be an exclusive
agreement with the license holder," and it may not "state a termination date more than fourteen
days from the date the agreement is entered into." Note that the statute writes **fourteen days**
in words, not as a numeral, and note that fourteen days is a ceiling, not a default: nothing stops
a shorter term, and for a single Saturday of showings in Round Rock a one-day or three-day term is
perfectly lawful and often cleaner. What you may not do is sign a person to a ninety-day exclusive
showing-only arrangement. The Legislature plainly did not want the non-representation posture used
to tie consumers up.

**The consequence of getting it wrong.** The same 2025 act that created § 1101.563 added a
matching disciplinary ground. Occupations Code § 1101.652(b)(34), added by Acts 2025, 89th Leg.,
R.S., Ch. 1172 (S.B. 1968), Sec. 11, effective January 1, 2026, lets the Commission suspend or
revoke a license or take other disciplinary action if the license holder, while engaged in real
estate brokerage, "fails to enter into a written agreement with a prospective buyer as required by
Section 1101.563." The writing requirement is not a best practice or a trade-association rule. It
is a licensing obligation with its own enumerated sanction.

**Which form do you use?** The Commission does not supply one. TREC's contracts page states: "TREC
does not promulgate listing or buyer representation agreements, property management contracts,
forms for commercial property, or residential leases (other than temporary residential leases used
in connection with a sale). Contact your attorney or a real estate trade association for such
forms." Practically, you will use a form your broker supplies, drawn either by an attorney or by a
trade association. What you may not do is draft one yourself: 22 TAC § 537.11(b) forbids a license
holder to practice law, to offer or give legal advice, or to draft or recommend contract language
defining or affecting the rights, obligations or remedies of the principals to a real estate
transaction; Law of Agency Module 04 teaches § 537.11. Use your broker's form. If the buyer wants it changed in
a way that alters legal rights, the buyer needs a lawyer.

**Keep the paper.** Commission rule 22 TAC § 535.2 requires the broker to maintain "commission
agreements such as listing agreements, buyer representation agreements, or other written
agreements relied upon to claim compensation," along with "disclosures" and "substantive
communications with parties to the transaction," "in a format that is readily available to the
Commission for at least four years from the date of closing, termination of the contract, or end
of a real estate transaction"; Law of Agency Module 09 teaches the record-keeping rule. A buyer representation
agreement that expired unused still starts a four-year clock on the date it terminated.


Compensation must be described by amount or rate and how determined, with the required negotiability disclosure under Occupations Code § 1101.563(c). A buyer should understand what services are promised, whether the agreement is exclusive, when it terminates, and how compensation may be satisfied. Do not promise that a seller always pays or that the buyer can never owe a difference. The service agreement and any seller contribution are separate documents that must be reconciled.

In a worked conversation, a buyer asks for “just opening doors” but then asks the agent to recommend an offer price and negotiating strategy. The request reaches beyond the showing-only conditions of § 1101.562. The broker must identify and establish the appropriate relationship and written agreement before proceeding as required. A label saying “nonrepresentation” does not authorize advice prohibited by that limited pathway.

## T05U7 — Leasing Real Estate

A lease transfers a right of possession or use for a period under agreed terms while the landlord retains the underlying ownership interest. Leasing affects both parties’ present obligations and a later purchaser’s expectations. A seller cannot promise vacant possession without examining an existing tenant’s rights. A purchaser should obtain the leases and relevant deposits, rent records, amendments, and notices through the transaction process.
### Adapted teaching: Residential and commercial leasing relationships and transaction intake

Leasing is brokerage. That single sentence resolves most of the confusion license holders have about rental work. Occupations Code § 1101.002(1)(A) defines a "broker" as a person who, "in exchange for a commission or other valuable consideration or with the expectation of receiving a commission or other valuable consideration, performs for another person" any of a list of acts, and leasing appears throughout that list: one who "sells, exchanges, purchases, or **leases** real estate" (i); "negotiates or attempts to negotiate the listing, sale, exchange, purchase, or **lease** of real estate" (iii); "lists or offers, attempts, or agrees to list real estate for sale, **lease**, or exchange" (iv); "aids or offers or attempts to aid in locating or obtaining real estate for purchase or **lease**" (vii); or "procures or assists in procuring a prospect to effect the sale, exchange, or **lease** of real estate" (viii).

Subdivision (x) reaches further and catches people who never think of themselves as agents: a broker also means a person who "**controls the acceptance or deposit of rent from a resident of a single-family residential real property unit**." That is a rent-collection act, not a marketing act. A person who, for compensation, takes in and banks the rent on someone else's rent house is performing a broker's act under § 1101.002(1)(A)(x) — even having never showed the house, advertised it, or negotiated a term.

Section 1101.004(a) ties the definition to conduct: a person is engaged in real estate brokerage by performing any act described by § 1101.002(1) for another with the expectation of valuable consideration, "as a part of a transaction or as an entire transaction." Note *as a part of a transaction* — doing one piece of a lease deal for pay is enough. Section 1101.351(a) closes the loop: unless a person holds a license, the person may not "(1) act as or represent that the person is a broker or sales agent; or (2) act as a residential rental locator." A **residential rental locator** is "a person who offers for consideration to locate a unit in an apartment complex for lease to a prospective tenant," excluding "an owner who offers to locate a unit in the owner's complex" (§ 1101.002(6)). Locating apartments for a fee is licensed activity in its own right. And under § 1101.351(c) a sales agent may not engage in brokerage "unless the sales agent is sponsored by a licensed broker and is acting for that broker." A sales agent's leasing work is the broker's work.

Against that broad definition, § 1101.005 sets out who the chapter does not apply to. Four of its subdivisions matter constantly in leasing. Section 1101.005(8) exempts "**an owner or the owner's employee who leases the owner's improved or unimproved real estate**." An owner leasing her own duplex needs no license, and neither does the salaried employee who leases it for her. Section 1101.005(7) exempts "an on-site manager of an apartment complex" — the leasing office staff at a conventional apartment community are outside chapter 1101 by name. Section 1101.005(9)(C) exempts a transaction involving "the lease or management of a hotel or motel." And § 1101.005(10) and (11) exempt leases of real estate owned by a limited partnership or a limited liability company when handled by a general partner, manager, managing member, or an employee who negotiates the transaction in the course of employment.

Draw the boundary carefully, because the exemptions are about **whose property it is**, not about how modest the transaction is. The owner exemption is the owner's; it does not travel to an independent contractor the owner hires, and it does not cover leasing someone else's property. A person who manages four houses for four different owners is not "an owner or the owner's employee" as to any of them. A common error is to assume that a small lease, a short lease, or a lease of a single house is too minor to require a license. Nothing in § 1101.002(1) or § 1101.005 turns on size.

What the license holder actually does in a lease transaction tracks the sale side closely. On the landlord's side: analyzing the rental market, advising on asking rent and term, marketing and showing the unit, receiving and forwarding applications, coordinating the screening decision, preparing the lease on the form the parties have agreed to use, documenting move-in condition, and handling the money. On the tenant's side: identifying suitable properties, arranging showings, disclosing the representation relationship, assisting with the application, and negotiating terms. As a matter of practice, residential leasing compensation in Texas is commonly paid by the landlord out of the first month's rent or as a percentage of the term's rent, and commercial commissions are commonly a percentage of total rent over the term — market customs, not legal requirements, and nothing in the held sources sets a rate.

Handling money is where leasing work most often generates discipline. Occupations Code § 1101.652(b) lists grounds for suspension or revocation, and four are aimed squarely at funds: failing "within a reasonable time to properly account for or remit money that is received by the license holder and that belongs to another person" (b)(9); commingling, since (b)(10) reaches a license holder who "commingles money that belongs to another person with the license holder's own money"; failing "to deposit, within a reasonable time, money the license holder receives as escrow or trust funds in a real estate transaction... in trust with a title company authorized to do business in this state" or "in a custodial, trust, or escrow account maintained for that purpose in a banking institution authorized to do business in this state" (b)(30); and disbursing money from such an account "before the completion or termination of the real estate transaction" (b)(31). Application deposits, security deposits, and rent that come into a brokerage's hands belong to someone else. They go into a trust or escrow account, they are accounted for, and they are not spent.

Section 1101.652(b)(32) applies to leasing in terms: a license holder may not discriminate "against an owner, potential buyer, **landlord, or potential tenant** on the basis of race, color, religion, sex, disability, familial status, national origin, or ancestry, including directing a prospective buyer or tenant interested in equivalent properties to a different area based on" any of those characteristics. Steering a rental prospect is a licensing violation as well as a fair housing violation.


The statutory regime depends on the property and relationship. [Property Code § 92.002](https://tcss.legis.texas.gov/resources/PR/htm/PR.92.htm) limits Chapter 92 to residential landlord-tenant relationships. Chapter 91 contains generally applicable provisions; commercial and other arrangements can involve additional chapters and law. A broker should not copy a residential rule into a commercial lease explanation without checking its applicability.

Lease intake should establish parties, premises, term, possession date, rent, deposits, permitted use, occupants, maintenance, utilities, access, insurance, default, notices, and renewal or termination provisions. Third-party ownership of appliances or equipment must also be identified. The broker’s role is authorized completion and administration, with legal drafting and contested interpretation referred to counsel.

## T05U8 — Leasehold Estates

The leasehold classification identifies duration and how the possession right ends. An estate for years has a defined term; it need not last a literal year. A periodic tenancy continues through recurring periods until properly ended. A tenancy at will depends on the arrangement’s terminable character. A tenant remaining after the right to possess ends presents a holdover issue; continued possession alone does not establish consent to a new fixed term.
### Adapted teaching: Term, periodic, at-will, and holdover relationships

A lease does two things at once. It is a **contract** — mutual promises about rent, repairs, conduct, and term — and it is a **conveyance**, transferring a possessory interest in land from the owner to the tenant for a period of time. Texas law recognizes the second half explicitly: Occupations Code § 1101.002(5) defines "real estate" for licensing purposes as "any interest in real property, **including a leasehold**, located in or outside this state." When a landlord signs a lease, the landlord has parted with something, not merely promised something.

What the landlord parts with is exclusive possession. What the landlord keeps is the **reversion** — the right to have possession come back at the end of the term. The tenant's interest is the **leasehold estate**; the landlord's remaining interest is the **reversionary estate**. Together they account for the whole of the ownership. A common error is to say the landlord *still owns the property and the tenant just rents it*, as though the tenant held nothing. During the term the tenant holds the present right to possess, and the landlord's right to enter is limited to whatever the lease and the statutes allow. Property Code § 92.001(5) shows how large that possessory interest can be: "premises" means "a tenant's rental unit, any area or facility the lease authorizes the tenant to use, and the appurtenances, grounds, and facilities held out for the use of tenants generally."

Property law sorts leasehold estates into four categories. Those labels are general property-law doctrine rather than definitions written into the Texas Property Code; the Code supplies the operating rules — how notice is given, when a tenancy ends, what a landlord must do — that make the categories matter. Learn the four names, but hang the Texas rules on them.

**The tenancy for years** (also called an **estate for years**) is a leasehold with a fixed beginning and a fixed ending stated in advance. The name misleads: a tenancy for years can run six months, eighteen months, or ten years. What defines it is that the ending date is known when the lease is made. It is created by agreement, and Property Code § 92.001(3) tells you that for a residential dwelling that agreement can be oral, defining a **lease** as "any written or oral agreement between a landlord and tenant that establishes or modifies the terms, conditions, rules, or other provisions regarding the use and occupancy of a dwelling." Oral, however, does not mean enforceable at any length. Business & Commerce Code § 26.01 — Texas's statute of frauds — makes an agreement unenforceable "unless the promise or agreement, or a memorandum of it, is (1) in writing; and (2) signed by the person to be charged," and § 26.01(b)(5) applies that to "a lease of real estate for a term longer than one year." A fourteen-month oral lease is exposed in a way an eleven-month oral lease is not.

A tenancy for years terminates by the simple expiration of its stated term. Because the ending date was fixed at the start, no notice of termination is required to end it. That is the single most useful thing to remember about this estate.

**The periodic tenancy** runs from period to period — month to month is the common Texas residential form, though week to week and year to year are also periodic — renewing automatically at the end of each period until one party gives notice. No ending date is fixed at the outset. Chapter 91, which supplies rules applicable to landlords and tenants generally, governs how a Texas month-to-month tenancy ends. Section 91.001(a): "A monthly tenancy or a tenancy from month to month may be terminated by the tenant or the landlord giving notice of termination to the other." Either side may end it; neither needs cause. Section 91.001(b) fixes the effective date: "If a notice of termination is given under Subsection (a) and if the rent-paying period is at least one month, the tenancy terminates on whichever of the following days is the later: (1) the day given in the notice for termination; or (2) one month after the day on which the notice is given." Where the rent-paying period is shorter than a month, § 91.001(c) substitutes a period equal in days to the rent-paying period.

Two refinements matter. Section 91.001(d) provides that if the tenancy ends on a day that does not line up with a rent-paying period, "the tenant is liable for rent only up to the date of termination" — no whole final month for occupying four days of it. And § 91.001(e) makes the scheme a default rather than a mandate: subsections (a) through (d) "do not apply if: (1) a landlord and a tenant have agreed in an instrument signed by both parties on a different period of notice to terminate the tenancy or that no notice is required; or (2) there is a breach of contract recognized by law." A lease requiring sixty days' written notice is enforceable on its own terms. Read the lease first, then fall back on § 91.001.

**The tenancy at will** exists when a person occupies with the owner's consent but with no fixed term and no agreed periodic term — possession continues at the will of both parties and may be ended by either. Lacking both a fixed end date and automatic renewal, it is the estate most likely to be created by accident: an owner lets a relative move in with no agreement about rent or duration, or a seller lets a buyer take possession informally before closing. The distinction matters, because chapter 92's protections attach to a "tenant," defined in § 92.001(6) as "a person who is authorized by a lease to occupy a dwelling to the exclusion of others and, for the purposes of Subchapters D, E, and F, who is obligated under the lease to pay rent." Read the whole definition: for the security-device, disclosure-of-ownership, and smoke-alarm subchapters, the occupant must also be obligated by the lease to pay rent, so an authorized occupant who owes no rent is not a "tenant" for those three subchapters even though he is one elsewhere in the chapter. And § 92.001(3) counts an oral understanding as a lease.

**The tenancy at sufferance** is the odd one out, because it is not consensual. It arises when a tenant who entered lawfully stays past the end of the right to possess — the classic **holdover**. The tenant is no longer a rightful possessor and is not a trespasser who entered wrongfully; the tenant is simply there, at the owner's sufferance, until the owner either evicts or accepts the situation and creates a new tenancy. Texas statutes assume the category without naming it: Property Code § 92.332(b) lists as "valid grounds for eviction or lease termination in any event" that "the tenant holds over after giving notice of termination or intent to vacate" (b)(4), and that "the tenant holds over after the landlord gives notice of termination at the end of the rental term" (b)(5).

Chapter 91 adds three rules cutting across all four estates. Section 91.005: "During the term of a lease, the tenant may not rent the leasehold to any other person without the prior consent of the landlord" — subletting is prohibited by statute unless the landlord consents, the opposite of what many students assume. Section 91.004 makes a landlord who "fails to comply in any respect with the lease agreement" liable to a non-defaulting tenant for the resulting damages, and gives that tenant a lien on the landlord's nonexempt property in the tenant's possession and on the rent due. And § 91.006(a): "A landlord has a duty to mitigate damages if a tenant abandons the leased premises in violation of the lease," a duty § 91.006(b) makes non-waivable. A landlord whose tenant walks out in month four of a twelve-month lease may not simply sit on the vacant unit and bill for the remaining eight months.


The distinction matters in a sale. If a purchaser expects immediate occupancy, identify the actual tenant’s term and any termination or renewal rights. A month-to-month label still requires attention to notice and applicable law. A fixed-term lease does not automatically end because the landlord sells. The sale agreement should address the existing lease and possession accurately, and the buyer should receive appropriate legal advice when a dispute or nonstandard arrangement appears.

In a worked file, the written lease expired last month and the landlord accepted another rent payment. The parties’ conduct, lease language, and governing law determine the resulting relationship. An agent should not announce that the occupant is trespassing or that a new year-long tenancy automatically exists. Obtain the actual records and route the legal characterization to counsel.

## T05U9 — Lease Agreements

Read a lease in the order a tenancy operates: creation, move-in, ongoing use, maintenance, payment, default, and move-out. Define the premises clearly, including parking, storage, common areas, and excluded spaces. Identify who is a tenant and who is an authorized occupant. A person listed as an emergency contact is not automatically obligated to pay rent. The actual signature and guarantee terms matter.
### Adapted teaching: Reading the lease as a system of possession, payment, and maintenance obligations

A lease is read clause by clause, and each clause is doing a job. For every provision, ask what risk it allocates and to whom.

**Parties.** The lease names the landlord and every tenant, and getting this right is not clerical. Property Code § 92.001(2) defines "landlord" as "the owner, lessor, or sublessor of a dwelling, but does not include a manager or agent of the landlord unless the manager or agent purports to be the owner, lessor, or sublessor in an oral or written lease" — a property manager who signs as though he were the owner has made himself the landlord for chapter 92 purposes. An occupant not named as a tenant is not a tenant and, under § 92.001(6), does not hold the tenant's statutory remedies. Where someone guarantees the lease, § 92.021(a) limits the exposure — but states the limit with its own exception attached: "A person other than a tenant who guarantees a lease is liable only for the original lease term except that a person may specify that the person agrees to guarantee a renewal of the lease as provided by Subsection (b)." The exception is part of the rule, not a footnote to it: a guarantor can be reached beyond the original term, but only on the statute's terms. A renewal guaranty is not effective merely because the guaranty form says so: under § 92.021(b) the original lease must state the last date, specified by the guarantor, on which a renewal will renew the guarantor's obligation; that the guarantor is liable under a renewal occurring on or before that date; and that the guarantor is liable only if the renewal involves the same parties as the original lease and "does not increase the guarantor's potential financial obligation for rent that existed under the original lease."

**Premises.** The clause identifies exactly what the tenant gets. Section 92.001(5) reaches "any area or facility the lease authorizes the tenant to use, and the appurtenances, grounds, and facilities held out for the use of tenants generally," so a lease promising a garage bay, storage closet, or pool access has enlarged the premises and with it the area the landlord's duties cover.

**Term.** The term clause states the beginning and ending dates — what makes the lease a tenancy for years rather than a periodic tenancy — and usually what happens at expiration. Under Business & Commerce Code § 26.01(b)(5), a term longer than one year must be in a signed writing to be enforceable.

**Rent, and when it is due.** The clause fixes the amount, the due date, the place and method of payment, and whether partial payments are accepted. Method matters in Texas: under § 92.011(a) "a landlord shall accept a tenant's timely cash rental payment unless a written lease... requires the tenant to make rental payments by check, money order, or other traceable or negotiable instrument." A landlord who wants to refuse cash must say so in the written lease; one who takes cash must give a written receipt and record the payment (§ 92.011(b)).

**Late fees.** Section 92.019(a) permits a late fee only if "(1) notice of the fee is included in a written lease; (2) the fee is reasonable; and (3) any portion of the tenant's rent has remained unpaid two full days after the date the rent was originally due." Section 92.019(a-1) adds a safe harbor: a fee is reasonable if not more than 12 percent of the rental-period rent for a dwelling in a structure with not more than four units, or 10 percent for a structure with more than four; a larger fee is permitted only up to the landlord's uncertain damages from the late payment. An initial fee plus a daily fee count as one late fee. A landlord who violates the section owes "$100, three times the amount of the late fee collected in violation of this section, and the tenant's reasonable attorney's fees" (§ 92.019(c)), and any waiver is void.

**Security deposit.** The clause states the amount, what it secures, and the conditions on its return. Its Texas rules are the next unit's subject; note here only that § 92.103(b) makes a lease requirement of advance notice of surrender "effective only if the requirement is underlined or is printed in conspicuous bold print in the lease." Formatting is substantive.

**Use and occupancy limits.** The use clause confines the premises to residential use; the occupancy clause names who may live there and how long a guest may stay. Texas caps occupancy by statute: under § 92.010(a) "the maximum number of adults that a landlord may allow to occupy a dwelling is three times the number of bedrooms in the dwelling," with "adult" meaning a person 18 or older and "bedroom" meaning "an area of a dwelling intended as sleeping quarters," excluding kitchens, dining rooms, bathrooms, living rooms, utility rooms, and closets. Section 92.010(b) requires a higher rate where a fair housing law requires one, and permits one for an adult "seeking temporary sanctuary from family violence" for up to one month.

**Maintenance and repair allocation.** This clause divides responsibility: landlord for systems and structure, tenant for cleanliness and for damage the tenant causes. The allocation is not fully negotiable in a Texas residential lease. Section 92.006(c) makes subchapter B's duties and remedies unwaivable "except as provided in Subsections (d), (e), and (f)." Subsection (f) permits an agreement making the tenant pay for repair of damage from wastewater stoppages caused by foreign or improper objects in lines exclusively serving the dwelling, damage to doors, windows, or screens, and damage from windows or doors left open — but only if the § 92.006(e)(4) formalities are met: a written lease; the repair agreement "either underlined or printed in boldface in the lease or in a separate written addendum"; "specific and clear"; and "made knowingly, voluntarily, and for consideration."

**Alterations.** The clause bars painting, holes, fixtures, and installations without consent and states who owns improvements at the end. Section 92.163 intersects it: a security device installed, changed, or rekeyed under subchapter D "becomes a fixture of the dwelling," and, except for repair-and-deduct, a tenant "may not remove, change, rekey, replace, or alter a security device" without the landlord's permission.

**Entry and access.** The clause states when the landlord may enter and on what notice. Texas backstops the tenant's possession with two hard rules taken up in the next unit — § 92.008 on utility interruption and § 92.0081 on removing doors and locks and excluding the tenant.

**Assignment and subletting.** An **assignment** transfers the tenant's entire remaining interest; a **sublease** transfers something less, leaving the original tenant as sublessor with a reversion of his own. Section 91.005 supplies a restrictive Texas default: during the term "the tenant may not rent the leasehold to any other person without the prior consent of the landlord."

**Default and remedies.** The clause defines what counts as a breach and what the landlord may do. Section 91.006(a)'s non-waivable duty to mitigate means an abandonment clause cannot promise the landlord the whole remaining term without effort to re-let.

**Holdover and renewal.** The holdover clause states the consequence of staying past the term, commonly a stated daily or monthly rate; § 92.332(b)(4) and (5) confirm that holding over after either party's notice is a valid eviction ground and not retaliation. The renewal clause states whether the lease renews automatically, converts to month to month — in which case § 91.001's notice rules take over — or simply ends. Finally, § 92.024(a) requires that "not later than the third business day after the date the lease is signed by each party to the lease, a landlord shall provide at least one complete copy of the lease to at least one tenant who is a party to the lease."

### Adapted teaching: Statutory duties that shape residential lease terms and administration

Property Code chapter 92 is the core of Texas residential landlord-tenant law, and § 92.002 fixes its reach: "This chapter applies only to the relationship between landlords and tenants of residential rental property." Commercial leases are governed by their own terms and by chapter 91. Do not carry chapter 92's deadlines into a commercial deal.

**Waiver.** Start with § 92.006, which tells you which rules can be bargained away. Section 92.006(a) is categorical: "A landlord's duty or a tenant's remedy concerning security deposits, security devices, the landlord's disclosure of ownership and management, or utility cutoffs, as provided by Subchapter C, D, E, or G, respectively, may not be waived." The same subsection bars waiver of the landlord's duty to install a smoke alarm under subchapter F and of the tenant's remedy for noninstallation, while allowing the smoke alarm inspection and repair duty to be waived by written agreement. Movement in the tenant's favor is allowed on a narrower list: under § 92.006(b) the duties and remedies concerning security devices, disclosure of ownership and management, or smoke alarms "may be enlarged only by specific written agreement." And § 92.006(h) bars waiver of the tenant's right to a jury trial. A lease clause contradicting these sections is not merely unwise; as to the covered duties it is ineffective.

**The duty to repair.** Section 92.052(a) states it: "A landlord shall make a diligent effort to repair or remedy a condition if: (1) the tenant specifies the condition in a notice to the person to whom or to the place where rent is normally paid; (2) the tenant is not delinquent in the payment of rent at the time notice is given; and (3) the condition: (A) materially affects the physical health or safety of an ordinary tenant; or (B) arises from the landlord's failure to provide and maintain in good operating condition a device to supply hot water of a minimum temperature of 120 degrees Fahrenheit."

Three things follow. The trigger is notice to the rent-payment address, not to whoever the tenant happens to reach — though § 92.060 makes a managing agent, leasing agent, or resident manager the landlord's agent for notice. The tenant must be current in rent when the notice is given; a tenant who withholds rent first and complains second has forfeited the statute. And the standard is not "annoying" but a condition materially affecting health or safety, with the 120-degree hot water device named so no one has to argue about it. Section 92.052(b) removes the duty where the tenant, an occupant, a family member, or a guest caused the condition, "unless the condition was caused by normal wear and tear" — defined in § 92.001(4) as "deterioration that results from the intended use of a dwelling," excluding "deterioration that results from negligence, carelessness, accident, or abuse." Under § 92.052(d), the notice "must be in writing only if the tenant's lease is in writing and requires written notice."

**Notice mechanics and time for repair.** Section 92.056(b) sets six conditions for landlord liability, and rewards the tenant who sends notice trackably: notice to the rent-payment address; a condition materially affecting health or safety; "a subsequent written notice to repair or remedy the condition after a reasonable time to repair" **or** a first notice sent "by certified mail, return receipt requested, by registered mail, or by another form of mail that allows tracking of delivery"; a reasonable time elapsed; no "diligent effort" by the landlord; and a tenant "not delinquent in the payment of rent at the time any notice required by this subsection was given." The tracked-mail option is what lets a single notice do the work of two.

How long is a reasonable time? Section 92.056(d): "there is a rebuttable presumption that seven days is a reasonable time. To rebut that presumption, the date on which the landlord received the tenant's notice, the severity and nature of the condition, and the reasonable availability of materials and labor and of utilities from a utility company must be considered." Note the last item: availability of utility service from the utility company is part of the calculation, not just materials and labor. Seven days is a presumption, not a deadline; a burst sewer line and a slow-closing window are not on the same clock. Section 92.053(b) adds pressure: if the landlord gives no written explanation for delay "on or before the fifth day after receiving from the tenant a written demand for an explanation," the landlord carries the burden of proving diligence.

**The tenant's remedies.** Under § 92.056(e) a tenant to whom the landlord is liable may terminate the lease, have the condition repaired under § 92.0561, deduct the repair cost from rent "without necessity of judicial action," and obtain judicial remedies under § 92.0563. A tenant who terminates gets a pro rata rent refund and the deposit back but gives up repair-and-deduct, and § 92.056(g) requires the lease to carry "language in underlined or bold print" informing the tenant of these remedies.

Repair and deduct is capped: the deduction "may not exceed the amount of one month's rent under the lease or $500, whichever is greater" (§ 92.0561(b)), and repeated use is allowed so long as the monthly total stays within that cap. It reaches only the conditions listed in § 92.0561(d)(3) — failure to remedy "the backup or overflow of raw sewage inside the tenant's dwelling or the flooding from broken pipes or natural drainage inside the dwelling," a total cessation of potable water the landlord agreed to furnish, or inadequate heat or cooled air or another condition a local official has certified in writing to materially affect health or safety. Waiting periods under § 92.0561(e) then run: immediately for sewage or flooding; three days after notice of intent to repair for a water cessation or inadequate heat or cooling; seven days otherwise. Repairs must be made by "an independent company, contractor, or repairman," not by the tenant or the tenant's family or employees, and the bill and receipt go to the landlord with the rent balance. A landlord who is genuinely stuck may deliver a sworn **affidavit for delay** under § 92.0562, buying 15 days where parts cannot be obtained without the landlord's fault or 30 days after a natural disaster.

Judicial remedies under § 92.0563(a) include an order to repair, a rent reduction "in proportion to the reduced rental value," "a civil penalty of one month's rent plus $500," actual damages, costs, and fees. The traffic runs both ways: under § 92.058(a), a tenant who withholds rent or deducts in bad-faith violation of the subchapter, after written warning, owes the landlord "a civil penalty of one month's rent plus $500."

**Security deposits.** Section 92.101 sets the reach of subchapter C: "This subchapter applies to all residential leases." Section 92.102 defines the term: "A security deposit is any advance of money, other than a rental application deposit or an advance payment of rent, that is intended primarily to secure performance under a lease of a dwelling that has been entered into by a landlord and a tenant." The label on the money does not control; its function does. The deadline is § 92.103(a): except as provided by § 92.107, "the landlord shall refund a security deposit to the tenant **on or before the 30th day after the date the tenant surrenders the premises**." Section 92.107(a) is the exception: the landlord "is not obligated to return a tenant's security deposit or give the tenant a written description of damages and charges until the tenant gives the landlord a written statement of the tenant's forwarding address for the purpose of refunding the security deposit." That is a suspension of the duty, not a forfeiture of the money — § 92.107(b) provides that the tenant "does not forfeit the right to a refund of the security deposit or the right to receive a description of damages and charges merely for failing to give a forwarding address to the landlord." Under § 92.1041 the landlord is presumed to have complied if the refund or accounting is mailed and postmarked on or before the required date.

Section 92.104 governs deductions. Subsection (a) permits deduction of "damages and charges for which the tenant is legally liable under the lease or as a result of breaching the lease," but subsection (b) is flat: "The landlord may not retain any portion of a security deposit to cover normal wear and tear." Subsection (c) requires the landlord who retains any part to give the tenant the balance "together with a written description and itemized list of all deductions," with one exception: no itemization is required if "(1) the tenant owes rent when he surrenders possession of the premises; and (2) there is no controversy concerning the amount of rent owed."

The consequences of getting this wrong are the most heavily tested figures in Texas residential law. Section 92.109(a): "A landlord who **in bad faith** retains a security deposit in violation of this subchapter is liable for an amount equal to the sum of $100, three times the portion of the deposit wrongfully withheld, and the tenant's reasonable attorney's fees in a suit to recover the deposit." Section 92.109(b): a landlord who in bad faith fails to give the written description and itemized list "forfeits the right to withhold any portion of the security deposit or to bring suit against the tenant for damages to the premises" and owes the tenant's attorney's fees. Section 92.109(c) puts on the landlord the burden of proving retention "was reasonable." And § 92.109(d) makes the deadline bite: a landlord who fails either to refund or to itemize "on or before the 30th day after the date the tenant surrenders possession is presumed to have acted in bad faith."

Tenants face a matching prohibition. Section 92.108(a): "The tenant may not withhold payment of any portion of the last month's rent on grounds that the security deposit is security for unpaid rent"; a tenant who does is presumed to act in bad faith and owes "three times the rent wrongfully withheld" plus the landlord's attorney's fees. Under § 92.105(a) a new owner taking title by sale, assignment, death, receivership, or bankruptcy "is liable for the return of security deposits... from the date title to the premises is acquired."

**Security devices.** Subchapter D requires hardware without the tenant having to ask. Under § 92.153(a) a dwelling must have a window latch on each exterior window; a doorknob lock or keyed dead bolt on each exterior door; a sliding door pin lock and either a handle latch or security bar on each exterior sliding glass door; and "a keyless bolting device and a door viewer on each exterior door." These "must be installed at the landlord's expense" (§ 92.153(c)). Section 92.154 requires a keyed dead bolt or keyless bolting device to sit "not lower than 36 inches from the floor" and no higher than 48 inches if installed on or after September 1, 1993, with a bolt throw of at least one inch.

Rekeying runs on a clock: under § 92.156(a) a device operated by key, card, or combination "shall be rekeyed by the landlord at the landlord's expense **not later than the seventh day after each tenant turnover date**" — the date, per § 92.151(15), a tenant moves in "after all previous occupants have moved out." Further rekeying at the tenant's request is at the tenant's expense, with unlimited requests allowed. Section 92.158 requires the landlord to repair or replace an inoperable device on request, and § 92.161(a) presumes a reasonable compliance time of "not later than the seventh day after the date the request is received," shortened by § 92.161(c) to a presumed 72 hours where the tenant reports a recent unauthorized entry or attempt, or a crime of personal violence in the complex. Remedies under § 92.164(a) include install-and-deduct, unilateral termination without court proceedings after the statutory window, and suit for a court order, actual and punitive damages, "a civil penalty of one month's rent plus $500," costs, and fees.

**Disclosure of ownership and management.** Section 92.201(a) requires the landlord to disclose the name and street or post office box address of "the holder of record title, according to the deed records in the county clerk's office," and, where an off-site entity primarily manages the dwelling, the management company's name and street address. Section 92.201(b) gives three ways to make that disclosure: in writing "on or before the seventh day after the day the landlord receives the tenant's request for the information"; by continuously posting the information in a conspicuous place in the dwelling or at the on-site manager's office within the same seven-day period; or by including it in a copy of the lease or in written rules given to the tenant before the request. Remedies under § 92.205(a) include a disclosure order, the tenant's actual costs of discovering the information, "a judgment against the landlord for one month's rent plus $100," costs and fees, and "unilateral termination of the lease without a court proceeding."

**Utility interruption and lockouts.** These are the two self-help temptations Texas forecloses. Under § 92.008(a) a landlord or landlord's agent "may not interrupt or cause the interruption of utility service paid for directly to the utility company by a tenant unless the interruption results from bona fide repairs, construction, or an emergency," and § 92.008(b) applies the same rule to service the landlord furnishes as an incident of the tenancy. The tenant may then "either recover possession of the premises or terminate the lease" and recover "actual damages, one month's rent plus $1,000, reasonable attorney's fees, and court costs, less any delinquent rents" (§ 92.008(f)); any waiver is void. A narrow exception in § 92.008(h) lets a landlord who submeters or allocates electricity interrupt service for an unpaid electric bill, but only under a written lease and with the specific notices the statute spells out. Separately, § 92.301 gives the tenant remedies when the *utility company* cuts off service the landlord agreed to furnish because the landlord did not pay the bill.

Section 92.0081(a) forbids removing "a door, window, or attic hatchway cover or a lock, latch, hinge, hinge pin, doorknob, or other mechanism connected to" one, or landlord-furnished furniture, fixtures, or appliances, "unless the landlord removes the item for a bona fide repair or replacement." Section 92.0081(b): "A landlord may not intentionally prevent a tenant from entering the leased premises **except by judicial process**" unless the exclusion results from bona fide repairs, construction, or an emergency; removing the contents of abandoned premises; or "changing the door locks on the door to the tenant's individual unit of a tenant who is delinquent in paying at least part of the rent."

That last exception is narrow and conditional. Under § 92.0081(d) the lockout right must be in the lease, the tenant must be delinquent, and the landlord must have mailed notice "not later than the fifth calendar day before" the lockout or hand-delivered or posted it inside the main entry door "not later than the third calendar day before," stating the earliest proposed date, the amount that would prevent the lockout, where rent may be paid, and, "in underlined or bold print, the tenant's right to receive a key to the new lock at any hour, regardless of whether the tenant pays the delinquent rent." A notice on the front door at the time of the lockout must give a 24-hour on-site key location or a 24-hour phone number with a key delivered within two hours (§ 92.0081(c)), and § 92.0081(f) is unconditional: the landlord "must provide the tenant with a key to the changed lock... without regard to whether the tenant pays the delinquent rent." The landlord may not change locks "when the tenant or any other legal occupant is in the dwelling" or "more than once during a rental payment period," and may not lock the tenant out of common areas. Violation costs possession or termination at the tenant's election plus "a civil penalty of one month's rent plus $1,000, actual damages, court costs, and reasonable attorney's fees" (§ 92.0081(h)), with an additional month's rent for refusing the key. Section 92.009 gives the locked-out tenant a **writ of reentry** from the justice court on a sworn complaint; § 92.0091 gives an equivalent **writ of restoration of utility service**.

**Retaliation.** Section 92.331(a) forbids retaliation because the tenant in good faith exercises a right or remedy "granted to the tenant by lease, municipal ordinance, or federal or state statute"; gives a notice to repair or exercises a chapter 92 remedy; complains in good faith to a code-enforcement body, public utility, or civic or nonprofit agency about a code violation or utility problem; or "establishes, attempts to establish, or participates in a tenant organization." Section 92.331(b) makes the window explicit: a landlord may not, "**within six months after the date of the tenant's action**," retaliate by filing an eviction proceeding except on the § 92.332 grounds, "depriving the tenant of the use of the premises, except for reasons authorized by law," "decreasing services to the tenant," "increasing the tenant's rent or terminating the tenant's lease," or "engaging, in bad faith, in a course of conduct that materially interferes with the tenant's rights under the tenant's lease."

Section 92.332 preserves the landlord's legitimate grounds: raising rent "under an escalation clause in a written lease for utilities, taxes, or insurance" is not retaliation, nor is "increasing rent or reducing services as part of a pattern of rent increases or service reductions for an entire multidwelling project." Note what that second one requires: a *pattern* across the project, not a single targeted increase. Section 92.332(b) then provides that "[a]n eviction or lease termination based on the following circumstances, which are valid grounds for eviction or lease termination in any event, does not constitute retaliation": rent delinquency when the landlord gives notice to vacate or files an eviction action, intentional property damage or threats to personal safety, material breach other than holding over, and the holdover situations described earlier. The tenant's remedy under § 92.333 is "a civil penalty of one month's rent plus $500, actual damages, court costs, and reasonable attorney's fees," less delinquent rent; and under § 92.335, "in an eviction suit, retaliation by the landlord under Section 92.331 is a defense and a rent deduction lawfully made by the tenant under this chapter is a defense for nonpayment of the rent to the extent allowed by this chapter."

**Applications.** Subchapter I governs the front of the transaction. An **application deposit** is refundable if the applicant is rejected; an **application fee** is "a nonrefundable sum of money... given to the landlord to offset the costs of screening an applicant" (§ 92.351(1), (1-a)). Under § 92.352(a) an applicant "is deemed rejected by the landlord if the landlord does not give notice of acceptance... on or before the seventh day after" the completed application is submitted on the landlord's form, and bad-faith failure to refund costs "$100, three times the amount wrongfully retained, and the applicant's reasonable attorney's fees" (§ 92.354).

Finally, § 92.061 explains how this fits with older law: subchapter B's duties and remedies "are in lieu of existing common law and other statutory law warranties and duties of landlords for maintenance, repair, security, habitability, and nonretaliation." In Texas residential leasing, the statute is the field.


Chapter 92 limits waiver of specified duties and remedies. Section 92.006 should be read before accepting a clause shifting all repairs to the tenant. Sections 92.052 and 92.056 address the duty to repair specified conditions and the prerequisites for remedies. A tenant should not be told to stop paying rent or deduct repair costs without evaluating the particular statutory procedure. An owner should not be told any signed waiver is effective.

A good lease file preserves condition evidence at entry and exit. Photographs, inventories, notices, repair requests, invoices, and communications help distinguish normal wear from damage and identify who knew what when. Documentation supports the parties’ rights; it does not permit a landlord to charge for normal wear where the statute prohibits it or to retain a deposit without the required basis and accounting.

## T05U10 — Types of Leases

Lease labels describe economic allocation as well as duration. A gross lease commonly includes specified expenses in a single rent amount. A net lease allocates identified expenses to the tenant in addition to base rent. Percentage rent ties part of payment to a defined measure of sales. A ground lease concerns use of land, often with improvement rights and long-term obligations. The actual definitions, exclusions, calculation periods, and remedies determine the bargain.
### Adapted teaching: Gross, net, percentage, ground, and specialized lease structures

The four leasehold estates classify a lease by its **duration**. This unit classifies leases by their **rent structure** — who pays which of the property's operating costs, and how the rent is computed. These categories are commercial practice and market vocabulary rather than Texas statutory definitions; no held Texas source defines "triple net" or "percentage lease," and you should present them to a client as customary terms whose meaning comes from the lease document itself, never from a statute. That caution is not academic: two landlords in the same market can use "net lease" to mean two different cost splits. Always read the expense clauses.

**Gross lease.** The tenant pays a single stated rent and the landlord pays the operating expenses out of it — property taxes, hazard insurance, common area maintenance, and often some utilities. The tenant's cost is predictable; the landlord absorbs the risk that taxes or insurance rise mid-term. Most Texas residential leasing is effectively gross. A **modified gross** lease, common in office space, is a middle ground in which the tenant reimburses the landlord for increases in specified expenses over a base year while the landlord keeps paying the base amounts.

**Net lease.** The tenant pays a base rent plus some operating costs directly, and practice names the variants by counting the categories the tenant picks up. A **single net (N)** lease commonly adds property taxes; a **double net (NN)** lease adds taxes and insurance; a **triple net (NNN)** lease adds taxes, insurance, and maintenance, leaving the landlord structural and capital items and a nearly passive income stream. An **absolute net** or bondable lease pushes even structure and casualty risk onto the tenant. The economic logic is straightforward: the further along that scale, the lower the base rent a landlord can command, because the tenant is absorbing both the costs and the risk that they rise.

**Percentage lease.** Used in retail — shopping centers, malls, and pad sites — a percentage lease sets a base rent plus a percentage of the tenant's gross sales above a stated **breakpoint**, aligning the landlord's return with the tenant's success. A **natural breakpoint** is the sales volume at which percentage rent alone would equal the base rent: base rent divided by the percentage rate. Parties may instead negotiate an **artificial breakpoint** at any number they choose. Percentage leases customarily also define "gross sales" in detail, require sales reporting, give the landlord audit rights, and often require continuous operation — all contract terms, not statutory rules.

Here is a worked computation. **The numbers below are an illustration invented for teaching; no Texas statute sets base rents, percentage rates, or breakpoints.**

> A retail tenant leases 2,400 square feet in a Texas shopping center. Base rent is $4,000 per month, or $48,000 per year. The lease calls for percentage rent of 6 percent of annual gross sales above a stated breakpoint of $800,000. In the lease year just ended, the tenant's gross sales were $1,150,000.
>
> Step 1 — Base rent for the year: $4,000 × 12 = **$48,000**.
> Step 2 — Sales above the breakpoint: $1,150,000 − $800,000 = **$350,000**.
> Step 3 — Percentage rent: $350,000 × 0.06 = **$21,000**.
> Step 4 — Total rent for the year: $48,000 + $21,000 = **$69,000**.
>
> Check the breakpoint against the base rent. The natural breakpoint at a 6 percent rate would be $48,000 ÷ 0.06 = $800,000. Here the stated breakpoint equals the natural breakpoint, so the tenant pays percentage rent only on sales beyond the point at which 6 percent of sales would have covered the base rent by itself. Had the parties instead set an artificial breakpoint of $600,000, percentage rent would have been ($1,150,000 − $600,000) × 0.06 = $33,000, and total rent $81,000 — the same sales, $12,000 more rent, purely because of where the breakpoint was drawn.

A common error is to multiply the percentage rate by *total* sales rather than by sales *above* the breakpoint. In the illustration, 6 percent of $1,150,000 is $69,000, which would nearly double the percentage rent actually owed. Read the clause: overage rent is computed on the excess.

**Ground lease.** The tenant leases land only and builds the improvements at the tenant's expense, typically under a term long enough to amortize a building. Ground leases are usually net in structure. The central negotiating issue is what happens to the improvements at the end of the term: ordinarily they revert to the landowner with the land, which is why ground lease terms are long and why lenders financing leasehold improvements scrutinize the remaining term. Recall that the tenant's interest here is real estate under Occupations Code § 1101.002(5), which is why a leasehold can be mortgaged, assigned, and brokered.

**Index lease and escalation clauses.** An **index lease** adjusts rent periodically by reference to a published index, so rent moves with a measure of general price levels rather than by a flat negotiated step. An **escalation clause** more broadly is any provision raising rent on a stated trigger — a fixed annual step-up, an index adjustment, or a pass-through of increases in a defined expense. Texas law notices this last variety in one narrow place: Property Code § 92.332(a)(1) provides that a landlord is not liable for retaliation for "increasing rent under an escalation clause in a written lease for utilities, taxes, or insurance." That provision does not authorize escalation clauses — the lease does — but it confirms that such a clause is a legitimate reason for a rent increase and not evidence of retaliation.

**Sale-leaseback.** An owner-occupant sells the property to an investor and simultaneously leases it back, becoming the tenant of the property it formerly owned. The seller-tenant converts locked-up equity into cash while keeping possession and operating continuity; the buyer-landlord acquires a property with a creditworthy tenant and a signed lease already in place. These are commonly structured as long-term net leases. As a matter of practice the seller-tenant trades an owned asset for a stream of rent obligations and gives up future appreciation; whether that trade is advantageous is an accounting, tax, and capital-allocation question for the client's CPA and counsel, not one a license holder answers.

Two habits will keep you out of trouble here. Classify a lease by reading its expense and rent clauses, not by the name typed at the top of page one. And when a client asks what a lease type *requires*, the answer in commercial leasing is almost always *whatever this document says* — the statutory floor from the previous unit is a residential floor, confined by § 92.002 to "the relationship between landlords and tenants of residential rental property."


In a retail example, base rent is $3,000 monthly and percentage rent equals 5 percent of defined annual gross sales exceeding $720,000. If qualifying sales are $900,000, the excess is $180,000 and the percentage component is $9,000 for the year. That calculation is only as accurate as the definition of qualifying sales and the stated structure. Do not assume every percentage lease charges a percentage of all revenue or permits the same exclusions. Audit rights, reporting, and adjustment provisions require review.

A “triple net” label also does not settle responsibility for every roof replacement or structural repair. The lease may allocate taxes, insurance, and operating expenses while treating capital costs differently. Identify the actual clauses, caps, exclusions, and reconciliation process. A broker should explain the economic terms within authorized bounds and obtain counsel for legal allocation disputes.

## T05U11 — Discharge of Leases

A lease can end through expiration, a valid notice terminating a periodic tenancy, an agreed surrender, a contractual termination right, a statutory right, or a judicial process. These routes differ. A tenant leaving the keys does not automatically obtain a release of all future obligations. A landlord accepting keys may create a disputed surrender issue depending on the facts. A lock change is not a universal lawful substitute for possession proceedings.

[Property Code § 91.001](https://tcss.legis.texas.gov/resources/PR/htm/PR.91.htm) addresses termination of specified periodic tenancies and its exceptions. Section 91.006 imposes a landlord’s duty to mitigate damages when a tenant abandons in violation of the lease and makes waiver of that duty void. The actual notice, rental period, written agreement, and facts must be evaluated. Avoid the shorthand that every tenant always owes all remaining rent without any mitigation analysis.

Residential tenants may have statutory rights concerning family violence, specified sexual offenses or stalking, military service, and other defined circumstances. [Property Code §§ 92.016–92.017](https://tcss.legis.texas.gov/resources/PR/htm/PR.92.htm) contain specific conditions and documentation requirements. Federal military protections may also apply. A broker should identify the claimed ground and refer the detailed legal analysis; neither a landlord’s disbelief nor the tenant’s label settles eligibility.

Repair-related termination likewise depends on statutory prerequisites. Notice, delinquency status, the nature of the condition, reasonable time, and other requirements can matter under §§ 92.052 and 92.056. A tenant should not be instructed that any defect permits immediate departure without consequences. A landlord should not be instructed that rent payment eliminates repair duties. Preserve the notices and condition evidence and obtain timely professional advice.

At move-out, possession, rent, deposits, utilities, abandoned property, and access should be addressed through their separate rules. Section 92.103 generally requires refund of the security deposit within 30 days after surrender, subject to the statutory framework; § 92.107 addresses the forwarding address. Section 92.104 limits retention to amounts for which the tenant is legally liable and excludes normal wear and tear. Section 92.109 addresses bad-faith retention and accounting. Keep the conditions with each number instead of treating “30 days” as the whole rule.

A worked file involves a tenant who vacates early and provides a forwarding address, while the landlord quickly relets at the same rent after reasonable expenses. The landlord’s claimed loss should account for the reletting and applicable mitigation duty. The security deposit accounting should identify lawful deductions supported by the facts. The agent should not prepare a punitive charge merely to discourage early departure or label normal aging as damage.

A sale does not automatically discharge a tenant’s lease. The purchase contract and closing process should address transferred deposits, prepaid rent, existing leases, notices, and responsibility. The buyer’s wish to occupy the property is a business objective that must be reconciled with lawful possession rights before closing. If a tenant disputes surrender or refuses to leave, appropriate legal process is required; an agent should not improvise removal or utility interruption.

## T05U12 — Options

An option gives its holder a defined election under specified terms. A purchase option commonly allows the holder to buy at an agreed price or through an agreed pricing method during a stated period. An option fee can support the promise to keep that right available. The holder’s exercise method, timing, recipient, and conditions must be clear. An option is not merely an invitation to negotiate if the owner later feels willing.

Separate a purchase option from a right of first refusal. A right of first refusal commonly becomes relevant when the owner proposes to sell on qualifying terms and gives the holder a contractual opportunity. The precise trigger, matching terms, notice, and time depend on the instrument. A purchase option and a first-refusal right can affect marketability and title, but they are not interchangeable. Drafting either involves legal rights and should be handled by counsel where an authorized form does not fit.

Separate both from a buyer’s termination option in an existing sales agreement. The termination option provides a limited exit under the purchase contract; exercising it does not create a purchase obligation. A stand-alone purchase option can lead into the obligation to purchase upon proper exercise. The direction of the election matters. Do not tell a client that “the option means you can change your mind anytime” without identifying which instrument and period applies.

In a worked purchase option, a developer pays an option fee for a stated 120-day election to buy a tract. The developer spends money on studies but never gives the required exercise notice. Study expenditures do not necessarily substitute for exercise. If the developer later asks to buy on changed financing terms, that may be a new proposal rather than performance of the option. The agent should preserve the written terms and communications and seek counsel rather than characterize the election from the parties’ intentions alone.

The underlying writing requirement remains important. [Business & Commerce Code § 26.01](https://tcss.legis.texas.gov/resources/BC/htm/BC.26.htm) governs listed real-estate promises, and recording or conveyance issues may arise under the Property Code. An oral promise to keep a land deal available should not be treated as a secure investment right. Obtain appropriate legal documentation and verify the holder’s exact interest before marketing or assigning it. Occupations Code § 1101.0045 also requires attention to disclosure of an equitable interest when its conditions apply.

## T05U13 — Contract for Deed

A contract for deed commonly allows the purchaser to possess and pay over time while the seller retains legal title until the agreed performance is completed. The structure differs from a deed delivered at closing with a purchase-money note and deed of trust securing the debt. Retained title does not make the arrangement legally simple. Texas applies detailed protections to specified residential executory contracts under [Property Code Chapter 5, Subchapter D](https://tcss.legis.texas.gov/resources/PR/htm/PR.5.htm).

Begin with § 5.062’s applicability. The subchapter addresses transactions involving executory contracts for conveyance of property used or to be used as the purchaser’s residence or the residence of a qualifying relative, with stated exceptions and additional provisions. Some lease-option arrangements can fall within its reach. Do not decide applicability from the document title alone. A “rental purchase plan” may create the same legal issues as an instrument labelled contract for deed. Conversely, not every ordinary executory purchase agreement belongs in this specialized regime.

The subchapter regulates the information and instruments given before and during the transaction. Sections 5.069–5.072 address seller disclosure and contract-related requirements. The buyer needs the required property, tax, insurance, financing, and transaction information, and the seller must follow applicable form and language requirements. Where negotiations occur in a language other than English, the statutory language requirements must be evaluated. A broker should not use a short homemade payment memo to substitute for a regulated conveyance arrangement.

Section 5.073 addresses prohibited terms. Section 5.074 provides a purchaser’s cancellation right within its stated period and conditions. Section 5.076 addresses recording, and § 5.077 requires annual accounting containing specified information. These protections serve different stages: informed entry, review after signing, public notice, and ongoing transparency. Keeping the purchaser in possession does not excuse failure to deliver required documents or account for payments.

Default remedies depend on the purchaser’s payment history and the governing provisions. Sections 5.063–5.066 establish notices and restrictions on forfeiture, rescission, and related enforcement; the protections change when the purchaser has paid the specified portion of the amount due or the specified number of monthly payments. Section 5.066 provides sale-of-property procedures under its stated conditions. A seller should not be advised that one missed payment always permits immediate forfeiture of years of equity. The actual thresholds, notice contents, cure period, and remedy must be evaluated by counsel.

Conversion and title-delivery protections also matter. Sections 5.079, 5.081, and related provisions address conveyance or conversion under their respective circumstances. The purchaser’s rights must be read from the statute rather than inferred from a seller’s promise to “give a deed eventually.” A seller retaining title may still have mortgages, liens, taxes, insurance obligations, or title defects affecting the purchaser. Section 5.085 contains important requirements concerning fee-simple title and encumbrances. Exceptions and specific transaction conditions require careful legal analysis.

A worked comparison uses a $190,000 residential transaction with a down payment and monthly installments. In arrangement A, the seller conveys by deed at closing and receives a properly documented purchase-money note secured by a deed of trust. In arrangement B, the seller keeps the deed until the final installment. The same price and payment schedule do not make the legal structures identical. The instruments, record interests, disclosure obligations, default process, and purchaser protections differ. The agent should establish which transaction the parties intend and obtain counsel before selecting or drafting documents.

In another file, the seller offers to “evict” a purchaser after years of installment payments. That word may describe the seller’s desired outcome but not the legally available process. Payment thresholds and the statutory protections must be examined. The agent should not issue a homemade forfeiture notice, change locks, or market the property as free of the purchaser’s interest. Obtain the contract, payment ledger, notices, recorded documents, title evidence, and legal advice.

The educational objective is recognition plus disciplined handling. A student should recognize retained-title installment sales and related residential arrangements, identify Subchapter D as the controlling statutory inquiry, and understand why ordinary lease remedies may be inappropriate. A full legal compliance review requires the current statutory text and the actual file. The broker’s duty is to identify the specialized transaction early and connect the parties with counsel, not to create a simplified substitute.

## T05U14 — Lease Purchase Agreements

“Lease purchase,” “lease option,” and “rent to own” are market labels that can conceal different obligations. A lease with an option may allow a future election to buy. A lease coupled with a binding purchase obligation may already require a later conveyance. Rent credits may or may not reduce the future price. A nonrefundable payment may serve a different purpose from a security deposit. The documents, not the label, establish the arrangement and the statutory questions.

Read the lease and purchase-related document together. Identify the property, parties, term, option or purchase price, payment allocations, election method, financing assumptions, default consequences, maintenance duties, insurance, taxes, recording, and title-delivery provisions. Confirm whether a default under one document affects rights under the other. The parties should understand whether they are merely renting, holding an election, or already obligated to purchase. Custom interaction between those documents is legal drafting.

Property Code § 5.062 specifically requires attention to covered residential lease-and-option arrangements within Subchapter D. Do not tell a seller that adding the word “lease” avoids contract-for-deed protections. The combined arrangement’s substance, intended residential use, timing, and statutory exceptions matter. Counsel should determine whether the specialized disclosures, recording, accounting, cancellation, and default protections apply.

Property Code § 5.0621 separately explains the interaction with residential landlord-tenant law. Subject to its subsection (b), both Subchapter D and Chapter 92 apply to the residential-lease portion of the arrangement described in § 5.062(a)(2). Once the tenant exercises the option to purchase, § 5.0621(b) states that Chapter 92 no longer applies to that lease. The classification is therefore sensitive to the actual transaction stage. Keep the option, exercise evidence, and relevant dates available for counsel. Do not assume that every later dispute continues to be governed by the same tenancy provisions merely because possession began under a lease, or that the word option by itself establishes a completed exercise.

For a worked example, a tenant pays $1,800 monthly, with $300 described as a future purchase credit, and pays an additional $8,000 at signing. The documents should explain what happens to both amounts if the tenant does not exercise, financing fails, the landlord defaults, or the property is damaged. Without those terms the parties may imagine incompatible bargains. The agent should not supply a confident answer from custom. The actual authorized legal documents must address the arrangement before funds change hands.

Financing assumptions require particular attention. A future loan is not guaranteed by today’s lease payments. Credit qualification, property condition, appraisal, program rules, and available funds can change. A promise that the tenant will “definitely qualify after twelve months” is an unsupported representation unless the speaker has a legally and factually sound basis, and even then the exact commitment matters. The broker should distinguish a planning projection from a lender obligation.

Possession and title risk also continue during the lease period. The landlord may have an existing mortgage, tax obligations, insurance, or liens. A purchaser’s future right can be affected by matters the tenant cannot see. Obtain the documents and appropriate advice; do not assume a payment receipt protects against every recorded lien. If the owner proposes subject-to or wraparound features, additional financing and disclosure rules may apply.

At the transaction desk, these arrangements should trigger a careful scope decision. The agent can gather facts, explain the difference between an election and an obligation at a conceptual level, identify current primary law, and refer the actual document structure to counsel. The agent should not paste an option into a residential lease and treat the result as an ordinary rental. A specialized bargain deserves specialized documentation and accurate client understanding.

### Applied exercise: Three compensation promises

Identify the three documents or decisions requiring reconciliation and prepare a factual discrepancy note without promising who ultimately owes the difference.

Feedback: Compare the buyer agreement’s obligation, the seller’s signed contribution agreement, and the closing/lender treatment. ### Applied exercise: The lease that ends after the sale

A seller promises vacant possession at closing in six weeks, but the tenant’s signed lease has seven months remaining and no agreed surrender. Identify the conflicting commitments, evidence needed, and responsible next steps.

Feedback: The proposed possession promise conflicts with the apparent existing lease. Obtain the lease, amendments, notices, and any actual surrender agreement. The seller should obtain legal advice about options; the buyer should receive the relevant information and negotiate a suitable transaction. The agent must not promise that a sale itself ends the tenant’s rights.

### Applied exercise: Rent credit or installment purchase

A proposed “rent to own” document gives immediate possession, monthly purchase credits, and a promise of a deed after ten years. The owner wants to use a two-page lease and a handwritten option. Identify the substantive issues and the statutory review needed before any signature or payment.

Feedback: The retained-title and purchase structure raises Property Code Chapter 5, Subchapter D applicability, including § 5.062’s treatment of related arrangements. Price, credits, election or obligation, title, financing, recording, disclosures, accounting, cancellation, and default need competent legal documentation. ### Module recap

Separate each agreement’s parties and obligations. A listing, buyer agreement, purchase contract, lease, option, debt instrument, and title document do different work. Read compensation and termination provisions precisely. Lease labels do not override statutory duties, and retained-title or rent-to-own arrangements require the specialized residential executory-contract analysis. Use authorized forms and competent legal assistance before unusual rights are created.
