## T07U1 — Contingencies

A contingency makes an identified obligation, right, or continuation of the transaction depend on stated facts or events. It should identify the condition, the actor responsible for any required effort, the period, the method of determining satisfaction or failure, the notice, and the consequence. A buyer’s personal concern is not automatically a contractual contingency. The contract must supply the right the buyer intends to use.

Distinguish a condition from a covenant. A financing addendum may require the buyer to apply diligently and provide documents while also allowing termination on specified approval conditions. The duty to cooperate is a promise; the approval requirement is a condition with its own contractual operation. A party cannot assume that causing a condition to fail through nonperformance guarantees a protected exit. The actual language and legal principles must be evaluated.

Contingencies are also distinct from a general termination option. An option can allow termination within its stated period without a specified factual reason if the contractual requirements have been met. A financing, title, survey, or sale-of-other-property provision may be narrower, tied to particular events and notices. An inspection report does not automatically extend an option period. A pending repair negotiation does not necessarily suspend it. A party should make decisions before the applicable protection expires.

For a calendar, use a row for each protection. State the trigger, action, responsible party, recipient, method, deadline, and consequence. Include the source paragraph and any amendment. Do not put “all contingencies end Friday” into a file unless each actual provision supports that conclusion. A title-objection deadline can be triggered by receipt of documents, while another period runs from the effective date and another depends on closing. These clocks can overlap without matching.

Financing illustrates the practical need for separate tracks. Borrower approval concerns the buyer’s qualifications under the addendum and loan process. Property approval concerns the lender’s acceptance of the property under the applicable terms. An appraisal is one input, not a promise of funds. A buyer may have sufficient income but inadequate funds to close after a low appraisal. A property may appraise but require repairs or insurance unavailable on anticipated terms. The actual addendum and lender evidence determine the issue.
### Adapted teaching: What the lender investigates and why a preliminary approval is conditional

The credit application is the point at which an abstract buyer becomes an underwritten file. It is also the point at which the largest body of consumer-protection law in this module attaches, because it is the moment when a creditor is deciding about a person.

**What the underwriter is deciding.**

Lenders describe the underwriting question in three words that have been standard vocabulary in the business for decades: **capacity**, **credit**, and **collateral**. Capacity asks whether the borrower's income and existing obligations leave enough room to make this payment. Credit asks whether the borrower's history of paying obligations suggests that the borrower will make it. Collateral asks whether the property, if the borrower does not pay, is worth enough to satisfy the debt. Some versions of the framework add capital — the borrower's assets and down payment — as a fourth. This vocabulary is industry practice, not a statutory test, and no held source states it; treat it as a useful organizing scheme rather than as law.

Texas law does, however, impose a repayment-ability rule for one category of loan. Finance Code § 343.204(b) provides that a lender "may not engage in a pattern or practice of extending credit to consumers under high-cost home loans based on the consumers' collateral without regard to the obligor's repayment ability, including the obligor's current and expected income, current obligations, employment status, and other financial resources, other than the obligor's equity in the dwelling that secures repayment of the loan." Section 343.204(a) defines "obligor" to mean "a person obligated to pay a loan, including a borrower, cosigner, or guarantor," and provides that where more than one person is obligated, the term refers to them collectively. Read that carefully: the prohibited practice is lending *on the collateral alone*, and the statute lists the borrower attributes a lender must not disregard.

**Documentation.**

Applications are supported by documentation of income and assets — pay records, tax returns, business records for the self-employed, and statements showing the funds available for the down payment and closing costs. The specific documents a given lender requires are a function of the standards the loan will be sold to, and this course holds no source prescribing them, so no list here should be read as a legal requirement. What the license holder should tell a client is structural and reliably true: the underwriter is trying to establish that the income is real, that it is likely to continue, and that the money for closing exists and can be traced. Documentation requests that feel intrusive are almost always aimed at one of those three points.

**Credit reports and scores.**

Underwriting relies on a **credit report** — a compiled history of the applicant's credit obligations and payment record — and on a **credit score** derived from that history. In general terms, a score summarizes the report into a number intended to rank the likelihood of repayment. This course holds no source that states the scoring models in use, the ranges they produce, or the thresholds any lender applies, so this module states none, and a license holder should never quote a client a score threshold as though it were a rule.

The Equal Credit Opportunity Act does address scoring systems in one specific respect. It is not discrimination for a creditor "to use any empirically derived credit system which considers age if such system is demonstrably and statistically sound in accordance with regulations of the Bureau, except that in the operation of such system the age of an elderly applicant may not be assigned a negative factor or value" (15 U.S.C. § 1691(b)(3)). A statistically validated system may use age; it may not use age against an elderly applicant.

**ECOA: what a creditor may not do.**

The Equal Credit Opportunity Act states its core prohibition in one sentence. Under 15 U.S.C. § 1691(a), "[i]t shall be unlawful for any creditor to discriminate against any applicant, with respect to any aspect of a credit transaction —

> (1) on the basis of race, color, religion, national origin, sex or marital status, or age (provided the applicant has the capacity to contract);
> (2) because all or part of the applicant's income derives from any public assistance program; or
> (3) because the applicant has in good faith exercised any right under this chapter."

Note three features of that text. First, the prohibition reaches "any aspect of a credit transaction," not merely the yes-or-no decision — pricing, terms, the vigor with which an application is processed, and the encouragement or discouragement of an application are all aspects of a credit transaction. Second, the age protection carries a qualifier: age is protected "provided the applicant has the capacity to contract." Third, § 1691(a)(2) protects the *source* of income: a creditor may not discriminate because income derives from a public assistance program.

Section 1691(b) then lists what does **not** constitute discrimination, and these carve-outs are where students most often go wrong. A creditor may "make an inquiry of marital status if such inquiry is for the purpose of ascertaining the creditor's rights and remedies applicable to the particular extension of credit and not to discriminate in a determination of credit-worthiness" (§ 1691(b)(1)). A creditor may "make an inquiry of the applicant's age or of whether the applicant's income derives from any public assistance program if such inquiry is for the purpose of determining the amount and probable continuance of income levels, credit history, or other pertinent element of credit-worthiness as provided in regulations of the Bureau" (§ 1691(b)(2)). And a creditor may inquire about or consider the age of an elderly applicant "when the age of such applicant is to be used by the creditor in the extension of credit in favor of such applicant" (§ 1691(b)(4)).

A common error is to conclude from § 1691(a) that a lender may never ask about marital status or age. The statute says the opposite. It permits the inquiry for defined purposes — marital status because a state's marital property and remedies law may determine what the creditor's lien reaches, which in a community property state like Texas is a live question, and age or public assistance because the amount and probable continuance of income is a legitimate underwriting factor. What is forbidden is using the answer to discriminate.

Section 1691(c) adds that it is not a violation for a creditor to refuse credit offered under "any credit assistance program expressly authorized by law for an economically disadvantaged class of persons," a similar program administered by a nonprofit, or a qualifying "special purpose credit program offered by a profit-making organization to meet special social needs," where the refusal "is required by or made pursuant to such program." Targeted assistance programs may have eligibility rules, and applying those rules is not discrimination.

**Adverse action.**

The Act also regulates the *answer*. Under 15 U.S.C. § 1691(d)(1), "[w]ithin thirty days (or such longer reasonable time as specified in regulations of the Bureau for any class of credit transaction) after receipt of a completed application for credit, a creditor shall notify the applicant of its action on the application." Section 1691(d)(2) entitles each applicant against whom adverse action is taken to a statement of reasons, which the creditor satisfies either by giving written reasons as a matter of course, or by giving written notice of the adverse action disclosing "(i) the applicant's right to a statement of reasons within thirty days after receipt by the creditor of a request made within sixty days after such notification, and (ii) the identity of the person or office from which such statement may be obtained." Section 1691(d)(3) is the provision that gives the rule teeth: "A statement of reasons meets the requirements of this section only if it contains the specific reasons for the adverse action taken." A vague reason is not compliance.

Section 1691(d)(6) defines the term: "the term 'adverse action' means a denial or revocation of credit, a change in the terms of an existing credit arrangement, or a refusal to grant credit in substantially the amount or on substantially the terms requested." The definition then excludes a refusal to extend additional credit under an existing arrangement where the applicant is delinquent or in default, or where the additional credit would exceed a previously established limit. Notice that "adverse action" is broader than denial: approving a borrower for materially less than requested, or on materially different terms, is adverse action.

**The appraisal copy.**

Section 1691(e) gives the applicant a right that arises in nearly every Texas purchase. Each creditor "shall furnish to an applicant a copy of any and all written appraisals and valuations developed in connection with the applicant's application for a loan that is secured or would have been secured by a first lien on a dwelling promptly upon completion, but in no case later than 3 days prior to the closing of the loan, whether the creditor grants or denies the applicant's request for credit or the application is incomplete or withdrawn" (§ 1691(e)(1)). The applicant may waive the three-day timing but not the right to the copy (§ 1691(e)(2)); the applicant may be charged a reasonable fee to reimburse the cost of the appraisal (§ 1691(e)(3)), but "the creditor shall provide a copy of each written appraisal or valuation at no additional cost to the applicant" (§ 1691(e)(4)); and the creditor must notify the applicant in writing of this right at the time of application (§ 1691(e)(5)).

**What must be delivered at and after application.**

Several delivery duties cluster at application. Under Regulation X, the lender must provide the special information booklet on settlement costs "by delivering it or placing it in the mail to the applicant not later than three business days (as that term is defined in § 1024.2) after the application is received or prepared." The count runs on *business days* as Regulation X defines that term, not on calendar days, and the duty lapses if the lender denies the application before the three business days are up. And "[i]f a borrower uses a mortgage broker, the mortgage broker shall distribute the special information booklet and the lender need not do so" (12 C.F.R. § 1024.6(a)(1)). Certain transaction types are excepted from the booklet requirement, including refinancings, closed-end subordinate-lien loans, reverse mortgages, and "[a]ny other federally related mortgage loan whose purpose is not the purchase of a 1- to 4-family residential property" (12 C.F.R. § 1024.6(a)(3)).

Regulation X also requires a written list of homeownership counseling organizations "not later than three business days after a lender, mortgage broker, or dealer receives an application, or information sufficient to complete an application," obtained no earlier than thirty days before it is given, from the Bureau's website or from data made available by the Bureau or HUD (12 C.F.R. § 1024.20(a)(1)). Reverse mortgage transactions subject to 12 C.F.R. § 1026.33(a) are exempt from that list requirement (12 C.F.R. § 1024.20(c)(1)) — and § 1026.33(a) is held for this course, so you can read what it exempts: a non-recourse consumer credit obligation secured by the consumer's principal dwelling on which nothing is due, absent default, until the consumer dies, the dwelling is transferred, or the consumer stops occupying it as a principal dwelling. The *Financing Legislation* unit takes up § 1026.33 in full, and the *Texas Loan Programs* unit takes up the Texas constitutional reverse-mortgage rules that sit alongside it.

Texas adds two of its own. Finance Code § 343.104 prohibits a lender from offering prepaid single-premium credit life, disability, or unemployment insurance with a home loan "unless the following notice is provided to each applicant for the loan by hand delivery or mail to the applicant not later than the third business day after the date the applicant's application for a home loan is received." The prescribed notice tells the applicant in terms that the coverage "is NOT required as a condition of closing the mortgage loan and will be included with the loan only at your request," that a single premium "will be financed at the interest rate provided for in the mortgage loan," and that the applicant has a right to cancel — with the statutory notice stating in capitals, "YOU MUST CANCEL WITHIN 30 DAYS OF THE DATE OF THE LOAN TO RECEIVE A FULL REFUND OR CREDIT."

Finance Code § 343.105 requires a written notice at closing, on a separate document, in at least 14-point type, warning that "[i]ntentionally or knowingly making a materially false or misleading written statement to obtain property or credit, including a mortgage loan, is a violation of Section 32.32, Texas Penal Code," and that the offense is, "depending on the amount of the loan or value of the property," one "punishable by imprisonment for a term of 2 years to 99 years and a fine not to exceed $10,000" (§ 343.105(b)(3)). Read those punishment words for what they are: the Finance Code's prescribed *notice* language, not the grading provision of the Penal Code. Penal Code § 32.32(b) creates the offense, and § 32.32(c) grades it by "the value of the property or the amount of credit" — a Class C misdemeanor below $100, rising through the misdemeanor and felony grades to "a felony of the first degree if the value of the property or the amount of credit is $300,000 or more." The notice's "2 years to 99 years" describes the top of that ladder, so a license holder should never tell a client that every misstatement on an application carries a 99-year exposure. The same prescribed notice (§ 343.105(b)(3)) includes the applicant's representation "that all statements and representations contained in my/our written home loan application, including statements or representations regarding my/our identity, employment, annual income, and intent to occupy the residential real property secured by the home loan, are true and correct as of the date of loan closing," and § 343.105(c) requires the applicant to verify the information and execute the notice. Section 343.105(d) adds that a lender's failure to give a complying notice "does not affect the validity or enforceability of the home loan by any holder of the loan."

That last statute deserves a moment of the license holder's attention. Texas requires a home loan applicant to sign, at closing, an acknowledgment that lying on the application — including about intent to occupy — is a felony-range offense. A license holder who is ever tempted to suggest that a buyer describe an investment purchase as an owner-occupied one should read § 343.105 again.


The loan process must be coordinated with the contract without conflating them. A lender can require additional information without extending the buyer’s contractual deadline. An agent can request an extension, but only the parties’ properly documented agreement changes the purchase terms. A lender’s estimate that approval will arrive tomorrow is useful planning information, not a signed amendment. The buyer should understand the difference while a lawful decision remains available.

Title and survey protections are another distinct track. A title commitment can list exceptions, requirements, and insurance conditions. A survey can reveal an encroachment, access issue, or discrepancy. The contract determines the delivery and objection process and the consequences of unresolved objections. A buyer’s dissatisfaction alone is not the entire analysis. Identify the objected-to matter, whether it falls within the provision, the required specificity, timing, and seller response process.
### Adapted teaching: Survey evidence, boundaries, and the investigation that feeds a contract decision

**What surveyors do.** A survey translates words into ground and ground into words: the surveyor
retraces record boundaries, locates monuments, sets new ones where old ones are gone, measures
improvements and encroachments, and reports the result as a drawing with certified field notes. In
a transaction the survey answers the questions the deed records cannot: where the boundaries
actually run, whether the improvements sit inside them, what physical evidence of easements or
encroachments exists, and whether the fences match the calls.

**Field notes: the statutory anatomy.** For surveys of public land, Natural Resources Code § 21.041
prescribes the contents of the surveyor's field notes, and the list doubles as a reader's guide to
any Texas survey: the county; the authority for the survey and a true description; proper field
notes with the calls and connections needed for identification, observing the Spanish measurement by
varas; a diagram of the survey; State Plane Coordinate values for the beginning point under the
Texas Coordinate Systems, with zone, mapping angle, grid distances, acreage and the reference
station; the names of the field personnel; the date; and the surveyor's signature. Section 21.001 of
the same chapter supplies a definition with practical bite on rural land: a **navigable stream** is
a stream *retaining an average width of 30 feet from the mouth up* — and because beds of navigable
streams belong to the state, the 30-foot test decides where private land stops at the water. A
licence holder who lists creek-crossed acreage should hear "navigable" as a surveyor's and lawyer's
question with a statutory measuring stick, not as a canoeing observation.

**Modern positioning.** Contemporary Texas surveys tie boundaries to the **Texas Coordinate
Systems** — the state plane grid the statute itself references — using GPS-derived positions, which
is why new field notes carry coordinate values alongside the traditional calls. Coordinates
supplement, not replace, the monument-and-call fabric: the hierarchy of calls still runs through
monuments first.

**Survey products a licence holder will meet.** The everyday residential product is the boundary
survey with improvements — commonly prepared to title-industry specifications so the title company
can delete or limit its survey exception — showing boundary lines, found and set monuments,
improvements, visible utilities, platted easements and any encroachments. Rural and commercial work
uses fuller boundary retracement surveys; construction adds staking; lenders sometimes accept a
prior survey with an affidavit that nothing has changed, a cost-saving practice whose risk (the
affidavit can be wrong) belongs in the buyer's decision, not buried by the agent's enthusiasm.
Elevation and floodplain questions produce elevation certificates — a distinct product for a
distinct risk.

**Reading a survey against a transaction: a five-point pass.** (1) Does the survey's description
match the contract's and the commitment's? (2) Do improvements sit within boundary lines and inside
building setback lines shown on the plat? (3) Are there fences off the line, drives crossing
boundaries, structures over easements — the encroachment findings of Principles I Module 03? (4) Are platted and
record easements plotted, and does anything visible on the ground (that worn path, that overhead
line) lack a record explanation? (5) Does the acreage or lot dimension materially differ from what
the parties assumed — and if the price was per-acre, does the contract say what happens when the
survey says otherwise? Every "no" or "yes, and it's odd" answer routes to surveyor, title company or
counsel — with the licence holder's contribution being that the question got asked before closing
rather than after.

**When the survey and the record disagree.** The most instructive moments in this unit are the
mismatches, and a licence holder should know the standard families. *Fence-line discrepancies* —
the fence stands off the surveyed line — are the everyday case: fences follow convenience and old
agreements, not calls, and a fence long acquiesced in raises exactly the adverse-possession and
boundary-agreement questions Principles I Module 07's limitation statutes govern; the agent's job is to disclose
the discrepancy, never to declare who owns the strip. *Overlaps and gaps (gores)* — two record
descriptions claim the same ground, or leave a sliver claimed by neither — are title defects that
only surveyors and title companies can resolve, typically tracing both chains back toward the
original survey fabric this module began with. *Acreage surprises* — the survey finds materially
more or less land than the parties priced — are contract problems: whether the price adjusts is
answered by what the contract says, which is why per-acre deals should say it expressly and in
advance. *Unexplained occupation* — a neighbour's shed, drive or garden inside the surveyed
boundary — is Principles I Module 03's encroachment doctrine arriving with a drawing attached, and it runs both
directions: what the client occupies beyond the line is someone else's problem with the client's
name on it. In every family the pattern is identical: the survey does not *create* the problem, it
*reveals* one that closing would otherwise have buried, which is why the survey is ordered early,
read against the commitment, and never waved through as a formality.


A sale-of-other-property contingency links two transactions, but it does not merge them into one contract. The buyer’s existing home may need to be sold and closed within stated terms. A seller may have a contractual right to continue marketing and invoke a specified notice process. The buyer must understand the exact conditions and consequences. An agent should not promise that a contingency means the seller must wait indefinitely or that any offer on the buyer’s home satisfies the requirement.

In a worked chain, Priya buys a new home contingent on selling her current home. Her current purchaser’s financing is delayed. The delay is a fact in the first transaction; whether it gives Priya a right in the second depends on the second contract’s actual contingency. She may need a negotiated extension, timely notice, or another lawful decision. The agent should track both documents and avoid treating the first lender’s delay as an automatic amendment of the second closing date.

Appraisal-related protections require similar care. The lender’s appraisal and a separate negotiated appraisal addendum can create different conditions. A buyer’s promise to bring additional cash may affect a particular protection without waiving every financing condition. A general willingness to pay above appraised value should not be translated by the agent into a custom clause. Use the current applicable promulgated form and obtain attorney assistance where the requested allocation is not adequately addressed.

Casualty, condemnation, and government-required repairs can trigger provisions different from ordinary inspection objections. A storm before closing is not handled by assuming the buyer has a new option period. Identify the actual casualty provision, damage, restoration prospects, insurance information, and available contractual choices. Counsel may be needed promptly. The agent’s role is accurate fact delivery and coordination, not a legal prediction about who bears every loss.

The final step in any contingency analysis is the consequence. Does failure excuse performance automatically, create a right that must be exercised, require a particular notice, allow cure, or produce another result? Does the provision address earnest money? Does it distinguish fees? Is there a separate escrow release process? The parties need the answer from the governing instrument and appropriate legal advice; the word “contingency” by itself supplies none of these details.

## T07U2 — Amendments

An amendment changes an existing agreement through the parties’ required assent. A proposed amendment is only a proposal until accepted through the proper process. One party’s request for repairs, a lower price, or a later closing date does not itself change the contract. Preserve the original contract and attach the signed amendment so the record shows both the original promise and its authorized change.

The current [TREC Amendment to Contract, Form 39-11](https://www.trec.texas.gov/agency-information/contracts), effective July 1, 2026, provides the Commission’s current standard amendment instrument for its stated use. Read its actual choices and blanks; do not assume an older training specimen has identical language. [22 TAC § 537.11](https://www.trec.texas.gov/agency-information/rules-and-laws/trec-rules) continues to govern completion and prohibit drafting or recommending legal language affecting rights or remedies.

The amendment process starts with a concrete business decision. What exactly is changing: price, repairs, closing date, option arrangements, expense allocation, or another authorized item? Which original term is affected? Are related figures or documents also affected? Who must approve? How will the final signed version be delivered and incorporated in the file? A vague email saying “seller agreed to take care of it” does not answer these questions.

For repair negotiations, describe the actual requested work through the authorized form and the parties’ decisions. Identify the system or item accurately and avoid substituting an inspection report’s entire list for a negotiated scope without careful review. An inspector’s recommendation can inform the parties; it does not automatically become the seller’s contractual obligation. If the parties need warranties, indemnities, holdbacks, or custom enforcement terms, counsel should draft them.

Consider a roof leak report with three observations: damaged flashing, stained interior drywall, and possible concealed damage. The buyer asks for “roof fixed.” The parties need a clear lawful agreement about the actual work and any necessary documentation or verification. The agent can identify the unresolved facts and use the authorized instrument; the agent should not invent an unlimited warranty against future leaks. The buyer may choose further investigation or a different negotiated economic term with appropriate advice.

Money amendments must reconcile. If the parties reduce the sales price by $8,000, examine whether cash and financing amounts, seller contributions, lender underwriting, title fees, or other dependent information require updates. A reduction is not necessarily equivalent to a closing credit: cash needed, lender limits, valuation, taxes, and financing structure can differ. Explain the business distinction and obtain lender or legal clarification rather than promising the two choices produce identical results.

A closing extension changes a date only when properly agreed. It may also require attention to possession, rate lock, insurance, lease termination, movers, per-diem expenses, and related transactions. These practical consequences should be identified while the parties negotiate. Do not add them silently to the amendment or assume changing one date automatically changes every other deadline. Read the original provisions and obtain the appropriate decisions.

A worked timeline illustrates the difference between negotiation and agreement. The buyer requests an option extension at noon; the seller says at 2 p.m. that the request is under consideration; the existing option ends later that day. At 6 p.m. a draft amendment is emailed without signatures. The request, discussion, and draft do not by themselves establish a completed extension. The agent must promptly explain the status accurately and help the client obtain timely advice and make an informed choice under the actual contract. An agent should never represent a pending request as granted.

When an amendment is accepted, update the transaction calendar from the signed instrument. Keep superseded deadlines visible as historical entries and identify the new controlling date. Notify the appropriate participants who need the changed terms, such as the escrow holder and lender, without disclosing unrelated confidential information. A calendar update is administrative evidence; it does not replace the amendment itself.

Do not alter signed content cosmetically. Removing a conspicuous strikeout, retyping a signed paragraph, merging selected pages, or replacing an attachment can change the record. If a typographical error needs correction, obtain proper party agreement and use the appropriate process. A clean-looking file that misstates the actual agreement is worse than an honest record of the correction.

Amendments can also fail through identity or authority errors. The same parties who hold the relevant rights and obligations must act through proper authority. If a party has died, entered bankruptcy, changed entity management, or assigned an interest, legal questions may arise. An agent should not simply request a different person’s signature and assume the substitution is effective. Preserve the facts and obtain counsel.

The amendment is therefore both a new agreement and part of an existing contractual system. Its meaning must be read with the original document, prior amendments, and incorporated addenda. A broad custom sentence can unintentionally conflict with several provisions. The authorized form helps address recurring changes, but unusual legal arrangements require professional drafting. The client should understand the actual completed document before signing.

### An amendment changes only what the parties agree to change

Suppose a buyer asks for three changes after receiving an inspection report: replace a failed water heater, extend the closing date, and increase a seller contribution. The seller agrees to replace the heater but rejects the other requests. The final amendment should reflect the actual agreement. The buyer’s original request is not proof that all three proposals became binding, and the seller’s willingness to discuss them does not supply acceptance.

Read the signed amendment together with the existing contract. Identify the paragraph or obligation changed, the new term, and any related item requiring reconciliation. If the closing date changes, a possession arrangement or repair deadline may require attention. Do not assume that every date automatically moves by the same number of days. If the parties intend a related change, document it through the authorized process.

An amendment can also leave important matters untouched. A price reduction does not necessarily release a seller from an existing repair obligation. A new closing date does not necessarily revive an expired termination right. A changed lender does not by itself rewrite every financing condition. The agent should explain the factual scope of the completed entries and refer legal-effect questions when the documents are disputed or ambiguous.

Version control is essential during negotiation. Label the proposed amendment as a proposal until it is agreed and executed through the required process. Preserve rejected drafts without circulating them as current obligations. When the final signed amendment arrives, verify that both parties signed the same terms and that no material attachment is missing. Distribute the operative change to appropriate transaction participants and update the task calendar with the amendment as its source.

Consider a closing officer who receives a price-reduction email but no signed amendment. The officer asks whether the settlement statement should be changed. The agent should obtain the actual evidence of agreement and coordinate the proper documentation. An internal instruction to adjust a number should not substitute for the parties’ contract modification. Conversely, when a signed amendment is complete, failing to deliver it can cause the closing figures to remain inconsistent with the bargain.

The current [TREC Amendment to Contract](https://www.trec.texas.gov/forms/amendment) supplies an authorized structure for specified changes. It does not authorize a license holder to add custom releases, indemnities, or remedy clauses outside the permitted scope. If the business agreement requires a legal term the form does not provide, the correct next step is legal drafting. A blank labelled other modifications is not an exemption from § 537.11.

The learner’s practical goal is to produce a reliable change record: original term, agreed replacement, effective document, related tasks, and completion evidence. This record helps prevent a buyer from relying on a request the seller never accepted and helps prevent a seller from assuming that a narrow amendment settled unrelated issues. It is administrative discipline grounded in the actual agreement.

## T07U3 — Addenda

An addendum supplements the base contract with terms addressing a particular subject. A financing addendum, a sale-of-other-property addendum, an owners-association addendum, a mineral reservation addendum, or a temporary lease does different work. A disclosure form can also accompany the contract but should not be assumed to operate as a negotiated condition. Read the document’s purpose, required use, actual language, and incorporation method.

Select addenda by facts. Identify the financing structure, ownership regime, seller reservations, existing leases, pre-1978 housing status, buyer’s dependent sale, temporary possession, and other applicable property or transaction conditions. Then match the facts to the current Commission library and the form-use rule. Do not choose documents merely because they were in the last transaction’s packet. An unnecessary addendum can introduce conflicting terms just as an omitted required addendum can leave a missing protection.

Incorporation requires attention to the actual contract and signatures. Check the base agreement’s attachment list against the documents present. Confirm that each attachment identifies the correct property, uses the intended version, contains completed selections, and has required signatures. An emailed brochure or unsigned draft does not automatically become a negotiated term. The parties should receive the complete agreement they are signing.
### Adapted teaching: Accurate supporting information and the danger of treating disclosure as a guarantee

This is the longest unit in the module because it is where licences are lost. Almost every
disclosure complaint is a variation on one of four fact patterns: you said something untrue; you
stayed silent about something you knew; you repeated what someone else told you; or you gave an
opinion the listener heard as a fact.

**First: disclose versus investigate.** Occupations Code § 1101.652(b)(3) and (b)(4), taught in
Law of Agency Module 10, make it a ground for discipline to make a material misrepresentation to a potential buyer
about, or to fail to disclose to a potential buyer, a "significant defect, including a latent
structural defect, known to the license holder that would be a significant factor to a reasonable
and prudent buyer in making a decision to purchase real property." Three qualifiers carry the rule
and none may be dropped. The defect must be **significant**; it must be one that **would be a
significant factor to a reasonable and prudent buyer** in deciding to purchase; and it must be
**known to the license holder**.

The third qualifier is where Texas draws the line between disclosure and investigation. The duty
attaches to what you know. **No source held for this course imposes on a Texas license holder an
affirmative duty to inspect the property, to test systems, to open the attic, or to research the
property's history in order to discover defects.** The Commission's disclosure architecture — the
seller's notice under Property Code § 5.008 and the buyer's right to obtain inspections — assumes
the physical investigation is done by the seller's disclosure and by a licensed inspector, not by
you.

Do not overcorrect. Three things narrow the comfort that offers.

The first is 22 TAC § 531.3's "prudence and caution." A license holder who deliberately arranges not
to learn something — who tells a seller "don't tell me about the foundation, I'd have to disclose
it" — is not exercising prudence and caution to avoid misrepresentation by omission. Held Texas
sources do not use the phrase "wilful blindness," and this course does not attribute that doctrine
to Texas law; what the sources say is that a license holder must be prudent and cautious to avoid
misrepresentation by act of omission, and an arrangement designed to preserve ignorance is difficult
to square with that.

The second is § 1101.652(b)(1), negligence and incompetence: not knowing something a competent agent
in that market would have known is itself a ground for discipline, independent of any disclosure
duty, and § 1101.652(a)(6) points the same way, reaching a license holder who fails to consider
market conditions for the specific geographic area in which the license holder is providing a
service.

The third is that once you *do* know, the duty is immediate and does not depend on being asked.
Subdivision (b)(4) is a failure-to-disclose ground. Nobody has to ask you a question first.

**Second: puffing versus a statement of fact.** Every student arrives with a half-remembered idea
that agents are allowed to "puff." The common-law doctrine of puffery, as such, was not held for
this course, and this module does not state its elements. What Texas *does* hold, in statute, is a
line between opinion and fact that does the same work more precisely.

Business & Commerce Code § 17.49(i) — the broker exemption, taught with the rest of the DTPA in
Law of Agency Module 11 — exempts from the Deceptive Trade Practices Act "a claim against a person licensed as a
broker or salesperson" under Chapter 1101, Occupations Code, "arising from an act or omission by the
person while acting as a broker or salesperson." Read only that far and you would think license
holders are immune. Read the rest of the subsection. The exemption "does not apply to" three things:
"an express misrepresentation of a material fact that cannot be characterized as advice, judgment,
or opinion"; "a failure to disclose information in violation of Section 17.46(b)(24)"; or "an
unconscionable action or course of action that cannot be characterized as advice, judgment, or
opinion."

That is your puffing rule, in statutory language. **A statement that can honestly be characterised
as advice, judgment or opinion sits inside the exemption. An express misrepresentation of a material
fact does not.** "This is a wonderful family home in a great school district" is opinion; "This home
is zoned for Bowie Elementary" is a fact, and if it is wrong the exemption does not protect you.
"You'll love the light in the mornings" is opinion; "The square footage is 2,410" is a fact.

Two warnings about the line. First, the exemption is from the DTPA. It is not an exemption from
TRELA discipline or from common-law claims; § 1101.652(b)(2) reaches dishonest conduct whether or
not the DTPA applies. Second, an opinion stated with the trappings of expertise starts to sound like
a fact, which is why 22 TAC § 535.17, requires a broker price opinion or
comparative market analysis to carry a disclaimer separating it from an appraisal.

**Third: silence versus concealment.** Silence and concealment are not the same thing, and Texas
separates them.

Silence is the absence of a statement. Where no rule requires disclosure, silence is generally
permissible — that is the premise of § 1101.556, which we reach in Law of Agency source unit 6. Concealment is
affirmative conduct or a strategic omission that makes a false impression, and Texas reaches it in
several places at once. Section 531.3 forbids misrepresentation "by acts of commission or omission."
Section 1101.805 speaks of "a misrepresentation or a concealment of a material fact." And DTPA
§ 17.46(b)(24) makes it a false, misleading or deceptive act to fail "to disclose information
concerning goods or services which was known at the time of the transaction if such failure to
disclose such information was intended to induce the consumer into a transaction into which the
consumer would not have entered had the information been disclosed."

That subdivision is the DTPA provision most likely to be pleaded against a license holder, and
§ 17.49(i)(2) expressly preserves it against the broker exemption. Its elements are cumulative —
information concerning goods or services, known at the time of the transaction, a failure to
disclose, and an intent to induce the consumer into a transaction they would not otherwise have
entered — and that intent element separates ordinary silence from actionable concealment.

So the practical line is this. If you know a material fact about the property's condition and keep
quiet because disclosing it would cost your seller the deal, you are on the wrong side of
§ 17.46(b)(24) and § 1101.652(b)(4). If you have simply not volunteered your opinion of the
neighbourhood, you are fine.

**Fourth: passing on the seller's word.** This is the fact pattern that produces the most confusion,
and the section that governs it is the one students most often misstate.

Occupations Code § 1101.805 is titled "LIABILITY FOR MISREPRESENTATION OR CONCEALMENT." Subsection
(a) adopts the § 1101.551 definition of "party"; subsection (b) provides that "[t]his section
prevails over any other law, including common law"; and subsection (c) provides that the section
"does not diminish a broker's responsibility for the acts or omissions of a sales agent associated
with or acting for the broker." Then come the two operative rules, mirror images of each other.

Subsection (d), protecting the *party* from the *license holder's* conduct:

> A party is not liable for a misrepresentation or a concealment of a material fact made by a
> license holder in a real estate transaction unless the party: (1) knew of the falsity of the
> misrepresentation or concealment; and (2) failed to disclose the party's knowledge of the falsity
> of the misrepresentation or concealment.

Subsection (e), protecting the *license holder* from the *party's* conduct:

> A license holder is not liable for a misrepresentation or a concealment of a material fact made by
> a party to a real estate transaction unless the license holder: (1) knew of the falsity of the
> misrepresentation or concealment; and (2) failed to disclose the license holder's knowledge of the
> falsity of the misrepresentation or concealment.

Now the misstatements to avoid.

**Misstatement one: "Section 1101.805 means an agent is never liable for repeating what a seller
said."** It does not say that. It says the license holder is not liable for a misrepresentation
*made by a party* unless two things are both true. The shield covers the *party's*
misrepresentation. It does not convert the license holder's own independent assertion into the
party's statement.

**Misstatement two: "The agent is liable if the agent had reason to doubt the seller."** The
statute's word is **"knew."** Not "should have known," not "had reason to suspect," not "was
negligent in believing." The two conditions are conjunctive and both must be satisfied before the
shield drops: the license holder must have known of the falsity *and* have failed to disclose that
knowledge. A license holder who knew the statement was false and *did* disclose that knowledge to
the affected person has satisfied the second condition and remains protected by the section's terms.

**Misstatement three: "So if I don't actually know, I'm safe."** Safe from *this section*, on *this
claim*. Section 1101.805 addresses liability for the misrepresentation of another. It does not
address discipline under § 1101.652, and § 1101.652(b)(1) reaches negligence, § 531.3 reaches
carelessness, and 22 TAC § 535.156 imposes an affirmative duty to convey *accurate* information to
members of the public. A license holder who repeats a doubtful statement without qualification, and
the statement turns out to be false, has conveyed inaccurate information to a member of the public
regardless of what § 1101.805 does with the civil claim. The lesson is not "you are protected"; the
lesson is "the protection is narrower and more conditional than it looks, and it does not touch your
licence."

There is also a housekeeping point. Subsection (f) of § 1101.805 was repealed by Acts 2025, 89th
Leg., R.S., Ch. 1172 (S.B. 1968), Sec. 13(2), effective January 1, 2026. TREC's own article on the
2026 changes, held for this course as guidance rather than as law, explains that two references to
subagency were removed from TRELA and states that "subagency has not been eliminated generally as a
legal concept." That is a change to the statute's text, not to the underlying common law, which was
not held for this course.

**Fifth: what you are affirmatively protected for saying.** Section 1101.804 is short and useful: "A
license holder or nonprofit real estate board or association that provides information about real
property sales prices or the terms of a sale for the purpose of facilitating the listing, selling,
leasing, financing, or appraisal of real property is not liable to another person for providing that
information unless the disclosure of that information is specifically prohibited by statute."
Sharing comparable sales data for a legitimate transactional purpose is protected — subject to that
closing qualifier, which you must not truncate.

**The practical protocol.** Four habits follow. *Attribute*: when the information came from the
seller, the appraisal district, an HOA or an inspection report, say so — attribution converts your
assertion into a report of someone else's, which is where § 1101.805(e) operates. *Refer out*: flood
risk to the flood map and the insurer, zoning to the city, structural questions to an engineer,
value to a licensed appraiser under Chapter 1103, because 22 TAC § 535.17 forbids you to supply an
opinion of value without that credential. *Deliver the statutory notices* on time and keep proof:
§ 1101.558(b) and (b-1), § 1101.555, and, for the seller side, the § 5.008 notice. *Correct the
record*: correction is the act that satisfies the second condition of § 1101.805(e) and most
reliably defuses a § 1101.652(b)(2) complaint.

**The DTPA, and where to find the rest.** Law of Agency Module 11 covers the Deceptive Trade Practices Act in
depth, including the § 17.46(b) laundry list; this module touches it only to frame your exposure to
a non-client, and the exercise below turns on three of its provisions. Section 17.50(b)(1) permits
recovery of economic damages, and if the trier of fact finds the conduct was committed
**knowingly**, mental anguish damages plus up to **three times** economic damages, and if committed
**intentionally**, mental anguish damages plus up to three times the sum of mental anguish and
economic damages. Section 17.45(9) defines "knowingly" as "actual awareness, at the time of the act
or practice complained of, of the falsity, deception, or unfairness of the act or practice," adding
that "actual awareness may be inferred where objective manifestations indicate that a person acted
with actual awareness." And § 17.565 requires suit within two years of the act or of discovery in
the exercise of reasonable diligence, extendable by 180 days in the circumstances that section
describes.


Disclosures and contingencies can interact without being identical. The seller’s notice under Property Code § 5.008 carries a statutory late-delivery consequence under subsection (f). A financing addendum carries contractual conditions. A lead disclosure serves federal statutory and regulatory requirements. An owners-association addendum may establish a document and objection process under its terms. Calendar each source separately and do not assume signing a receipt waives every underlying right.

The current form inventory matters. This course’s current verification identifies resale Form 20-19 and Amendment 39-11, the current relevant farm/ranch, new-home, condominium, unimproved-property, association, and related forms effective July 1, 2026; Third Party Financing Addendum 40-11 effective January 3, 2025; and current temporary leases 16-7 and 15-7 effective January 5, 2026. Lead form 56-0 is a separate current document. Use the [Commission’s actual inventory](https://www.trec.texas.gov/agency-information/contracts) to identify the precise document needed for the transaction, rather than treating this list as a substitute for its instructions.

A mineral reservation provides a useful distinction. The addendum concerns a reservation by the seller. It does not prove the seller owns the reserved fraction, remove prior third-party reservations, or establish that the buyer will have unrestricted surface use. The agent should obtain the underlying title and lease documents and refer legal interpretation. A separate groundwater or surface-water issue should be handled through the appropriate current documents and authorities, not folded into a casual statement about minerals.

A temporary lease also changes more than the key-delivery date. It creates an occupancy arrangement with rent, deposits, condition, maintenance, insurance, default, and possession consequences under its terms. The current TREC forms are designed for their stated short-term transaction uses; they are not general substitutes for every residential lease. Confirm which party occupies, whether occupancy is before or after closing, and whether the intended arrangement fits the form.

In a worked packet review, the base contract checks an association addendum, but the packet contains only a seller’s disclosure. The property is a condominium, while the base document is a non-condominium resale form. The buyer also plans to assume the seller’s loan but the financing attachment describes a new third-party loan. These are three distinct mismatches: missing attachment, ownership/form mismatch, and financing mismatch. They should be resolved before execution, with appropriate professional help where necessary.

Addenda can conflict with each other or with a later amendment. Do not assume the latest email automatically controls. Read the actual order-of-priority or conflict language, identify the signed instruments, and obtain counsel when meaning is uncertain. The agent should surface inconsistencies, not improvise a legal hierarchy favorable to the client. Clear business terms and accurate documentation reduce the need for that dispute.

The final packet check should be performed from the client’s perspective. Can the client see every term and attachment? Are blanks and choices explained within the permitted role? Are unusual matters referred to counsel before execution? Is there a complete signed copy for every signer? Does the transaction calendar reflect the incorporated documents? These checks make the addendum operational rather than merely present in a folder.


### Statute reading exercise: One event, different sources of rights

Read Property Code § 5.008(f), 22 TAC § 537.11(b)(5)–(6), and the current Third Party Financing Addendum’s approval and termination provisions. Compare late seller disclosure, a lender approval problem, and a buyer’s requested family-approval clause. Identify which source could create a right and which request requires attorney drafting if no suitable authorized form fits.

Feedback: Late seller disclosure invokes the statute’s stated consequence. Financing rights depend on the actual addendum and its conditions, evidence, notice, and timing. A custom family-approval contingency creates legal rights and is not for the agent to draft or recommend. An applicable mandatory form should not be replaced with a paraphrase in special provisions.

### Applied exercise: Extension requested, extension not signed

At 11 a.m. a buyer requests three additional option days. The seller says the request is being considered. A draft amendment is sent before the existing deadline, but no accepted amendment is documented. Prepare the status explanation and list the documents and times the file must preserve.

Feedback: A request and draft do not establish an agreed extension. Preserve the operative contract, deadline calculation, request, replies, draft, signatures if any, and delivery record. Promptly involve the broker and appropriate counsel; the buyer must understand the actual status while deciding what to do under the existing agreement. Do not represent the extension as granted.

### Applied exercise: A revised price and three unchanged numbers

The parties sign an $8,000 price reduction. The financing amount, seller contribution, and closing worksheet still reflect the old price. Identify which numbers require review and explain why the agent should not silently change them all by $8,000.

Feedback: Each amount has its own contractual or lender basis. Reconcile cash plus financing to the agreed price, verify the seller contribution’s actual terms and any lender limits, and correct the closing draft through the proper participants. A price amendment does not authorize arbitrary changes to separate obligations. Preserve the signed amendment and obtain any additional required agreement.


### Module recap

A contingency works only through its actual condition, duties, notice, timing, and consequence. A pending amendment does not change an existing deadline. An addendum must fit the facts and be part of the completed agreement. Keep each document and clock distinct, reconcile dependent terms, and obtain counsel for custom legal language and contested effect.
