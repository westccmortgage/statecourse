## T06U1 — Offer and Acceptance

An offer proposes a bargain; acceptance must address the actual proposal. Read the whole offered version, including attachments and conspicuous changes. A seller’s acceptance of a price while changing possession or financing is not acceptance of the buyer’s unchanged proposal. A counteroffer should be identified and communicated as such. Preserve the version history instead of overwriting the original offer.
### Adapted teaching: Offers, counteroffers, communication, and the effective-date record

**2. Offer and acceptance (mutual assent).** One party proposes definite terms; the other accepts
them unequivocally. A purported acceptance that changes terms is a **counteroffer** — a rejection
joined to a new offer, which kills the original and reverses the roles. Offers may be revoked before
acceptance; communicated acceptance closes the loop and forms the contract. In practice the licence
holder is the courier of offer and acceptance, and sloppiness here — un-communicated acceptances,
initialed changes never re-signed — creates the disputes that fill contract litigation.

**How offers die.** An offer is a power in the offeree, and it ends in five standard ways, each of
which a licence holder should recognise on sight: **lapse** — the offer's own deadline passes, or a
reasonable time expires where none was stated (which is why every written offer should carry an
express acceptance deadline); **revocation** — the offeror withdraws before acceptance, which the
offeror may generally do even inside a stated period unless an option (separate consideration)
holds it open; **rejection** — the offeree declines, extinguishing the power; **counteroffer** —
rejection plus a new offer, as above, the most common death in residential practice; and **death or
incapacity** of a party before acceptance. The transaction-desk corollaries: a buyer "sleeping on"
a seller's counter holds a power that the seller can revoke at any moment before acceptance is
communicated — so speed is protection; and once a counter is made, the original offer cannot be
"accepted after all" — it is gone, and only a new agreement on its old terms revives it.

**Acceptance and the effective date.** Acceptance must mirror the offer and must be *communicated* —
in Texas form practice, the final contract exists when the last party to sign has signed and that
acceptance is communicated back, and the forms then have the parties fill in an **effective date**
from which every deadline in the document counts. The licence holder's mechanical duties cluster
exactly here: transmit offers and counters promptly, obtain full execution (every change initialed
by all parties — an un-initialed strike-through is an acceptance that varies the offer, i.e. a
counteroffer nobody noticed), communicate final acceptance, and complete the effective date. More
contract disputes grow from this paragraph than from any doctrine in the module.


The general offer rules need the governing facts and terms. An offer deadline does not by itself create a paid option that prevents earlier revocation. A seller’s separate negotiations with another purchaser do not substitute for properly communicating a withdrawal where required. An agent should promptly transmit instructions and preserve the evidence; a disputed sequence should go to the broker and counsel. Avoid categorical legal conclusions based on a partial message thread.

A worked chronology shows the record needed. At 8:15 a buyer transmits version A. At 9:20 the seller returns version B with a different price. At 10:05 the buyer asks about changing the closing date. At 10:30 a document arrives with some but not all changes. The agent should identify which document is a proposal, which terms remain unresolved, and whether the parties have agreed on the same version. A signature service’s status does not answer those questions. Once the actual acceptance is communicated, record the effective date accurately and distribute the complete agreement.

Do not confuse a request for clarification with a new counteroffer automatically. The language and context matter. “Would the seller consider Tuesday?” may be an inquiry; “I accept only if closing is Tuesday” proposes a condition. The agent should accurately relay the principal’s intended communication and obtain clarity before characterizing it to another party. Contract-formation disputes are legal questions; accurate records reduce the uncertainty.

## T06U2 — The Document

The sales contract is a coordinated instrument. Party and property identification define who must perform and what is being sold. Price and financing identify the economic bargain. Money-delivery provisions identify deposits and fees. Title, survey, condition, and disclosure provisions establish investigation and response processes. Closing and possession provisions establish performance. Default, mediation, expenses, and notices address consequences and communication. Incorporated addenda can materially change the operation.

The current [TREC One to Four Family Residential Contract (Resale), Form 20-19](https://www.trec.texas.gov/agency-information/contracts), is used here as the current residential reference, with its July 1, 2026 effective-use status verified in the Commission’s inventory. Other property types require their appropriate forms. The student should open the actual current published document when studying its paragraphs; a classroom summary is not the instrument signed by the parties.

Read in two passes. On the first, identify the bargain: parties, legal description, included and excluded property, sales price, financing, repairs, closing, possession, and incorporated documents. On the second, build the calendar: triggering event, required action, responsible person, recipient, delivery method, due date or time, and consequence. This second pass converts passive reading into contract administration.

The completed instrument must be internally consistent. Cash plus financing should reconcile to price. The contract’s attachment list should match the attached documents. A checked box saying the buyer received a disclosure should match actual delivery. A promised closing date should be considered alongside financing and title timing. A seller’s reservation should agree with the intended deed. Blank fields and incompatible selections should be resolved before signing through authorized completion and the principals’ decisions.

The document also records facts that should come from reliable sources. Use the actual party’s name and capacity; obtain the legal description from proper records; distinguish ownership of equipment from third-party leases; identify the escrow agent accurately; and confirm notice addresses. Auto-filled text can be wrong because it came from a prior transaction. A familiar platform should not reduce verification.

In a worked review, the price is $460,000, the cash component is $80,000, and the financing field shows $390,000. The sum is $470,000. The agent should ask the principal to resolve the business terms before submission rather than silently choosing which field to change. A discrepancy is evidence of an unresolved bargain, not permission for the agent to negotiate autonomously.

An accepted document should be preserved as accepted. Later repair negotiations require the proper amendment rather than editing the original PDF. Notices should be retained with their delivery evidence. The file should distinguish the signed contract, drafts, attachments, amendments, termination notices, and closing records. This integrity allows a client, broker, title reviewer, or attorney to reconstruct the transaction without guessing.

### Reading the document as a connected set

A purchase agreement is not a collection of independent blanks. A price entry can affect financing amounts and cash due. A closing date can affect title delivery, possession planning, lender readiness, and the feasibility of repairs. An attachment can qualify an obligation stated in the main form. Read the agreement from beginning to end, then trace each selected addendum back to the subject it changes or supplements.

Consider a draft that states a financed purchase price but includes no completed financing addendum. The agent should not assume the buyer’s prequalification letter supplies the omitted contract terms. The letter is evidence from a lender, not necessarily an agreement between buyer and seller about financing conditions. Identify the intended arrangement and complete the authorized documents accurately before presenting the offer.

A second draft lists an attached addendum that is absent from the packet. The signature page does not tell a future reader what missing terms were agreed. Obtain the actual attachment, confirm its version and completion, and ensure the parties are agreeing to the same document set. Do not insert a later-created attachment into the signed packet without the appropriate agreement and record.

A third draft contains conflicting entries: the main form provides one possession arrangement, while an attached temporary lease describes another date. The agent should identify the inconsistency and obtain clarification through the permitted process before execution. Do not choose whichever date is more convenient and tell the parties that the other one is harmless. Resolving the legal effect of conflicting executed provisions may require counsel.

The same discipline applies to names and notices. A party’s nickname in one location and entity name in another can obscure who is undertaking the obligation. An obsolete notice address can interfere with delivery. Verify factual entries from appropriate sources and the parties’ instructions. The rule permitting informational completion does not authorize the agent to determine disputed ownership or draft a new notice regime.

Finally, preserve a coherent final set: the accepted main agreement, every incorporated addendum, all executed amendments, and the relevant notices and receipts. A convenient consolidated copy may help administration, but it should not erase the signed originals or the chronology. A transaction file should allow someone unfamiliar with the negotiation to reconstruct the parties’ obligations without guessing which attachment controlled.

## T06U3 — Financing Information

The purchase price and the source of funds are related but different. A buyer may pay cash from existing assets, obtain third-party financing, assume permitted existing financing, or obtain seller financing. Each arrangement creates different conditions and documents. Calling an offer “cash” while relying on a loan does not automatically create a financing protection. The actual contract must reflect the negotiated arrangement.

The [TREC Third Party Financing Addendum, Form 40-11](https://www.trec.texas.gov/agency-information/contracts), effective January 3, 2025, should be read for its actual financing terms, buyer-approval provisions, property-approval provisions, diligence obligations, and notice requirements. The lender’s approval of the borrower is not the same as approval of the property. An appraisal, insurance issue, required repair, or other underwriting matter may affect property approval under the stated terms.

Paragraph 2A has a specific consequence when the selected Buyer Approval period passes without termination under that paragraph: the contract is no longer subject to obtaining Buyer Approval. This is not automatic contract termination or an automatic earnest-money refund. The selected period, required notice, and lender statement must be read together. Paragraph 2B governs a separate property-approval mechanism, with its own timing and documentary conditions. Do not transplant the buyer-approval deadline into the property provision or assume that expiration of one clock silently changes another. A properly maintained file records which provision is invoked, the actual qualifying event, the required delivery evidence, and the resulting status without expanding the client’s rights by informal assurance.

Prequalification is preliminary information; it is not the same as an unconditional funding commitment. Even an approval letter may contain conditions. The buyer must understand required funds, documents, timing, and possible limits. A broker should not guarantee approval based on a marketing letter or assume that a lender’s delay extends a contractual deadline. Coordinate with the lender while tracking the contract independently.

In a worked file, the buyer receives approval based on income and credit but the lender requires repairs before funding. The agent should identify the property condition and the applicable contract/addendum provision, preserve the lender’s written requirement, and help the buyer obtain timely advice and decisions. The buyer may negotiate repairs, pursue another permissible solution, or evaluate a contractual right. The agent should not simply label the event “loan denied” and assume the same notice and remedy apply to every financing failure.

Seller financing requires separate attention to note terms, lien structure, legal documentation, and applicable lending laws. A seller’s willingness to accept monthly payments does not create a complete financing agreement. The amount financed, interest, repayment, maturity, security, priority, default, and required disclosures must be addressed through the appropriate instruments and professionals. The financing module develops those subjects.

## T06U4 — Disclosures

Disclosures communicate facts and relationships; they do not all serve the same purpose. The seller’s condition notice concerns the seller’s knowledge. Agency disclosures explain representation and brokerage roles. Federal lead disclosures concern target housing. Other Texas notices concern particular property or transaction conditions. Receipt of one document does not satisfy every duty.
### Adapted teaching: Seller knowledge, statutory scope, delivery, and the late-notice consequence

Everything you have studied so far in this module imposes duties on *you*. Property Code § 5.008
does not: it imposes a duty on the **seller**. Your obligations around § 5.008 are real, but they
are obligations to get the seller's statutory duty performed correctly and on time, not to perform
it yourself and not to vouch for its contents.

**Who it applies to.** Subsection (a): "A seller of residential real property comprising not more
than one dwelling unit located in this state shall give to the purchaser of the property a written
notice as prescribed by this section or a written notice substantially similar to the notice
prescribed by this section which contains, at a minimum, all of the items in the notice prescribed
by this section."

Parse the scope. **Residential** real property — not raw land, not a strip centre, not an office
condo. **Comprising not more than one dwelling unit** — a single-family house is inside; a duplex is
not, because a duplex comprises two dwelling units. **Located in this state.** And the notice goes
to "the purchaser," so the section speaks to sales rather than leases.

Also parse the form requirement. The statute lets the seller use either the notice it prescribes or
one "substantially similar" that contains, "at a minimum, all of the items in the notice prescribed
by this section." A shorter form is not compliance. A longer one can be.

**What the notice contains.** Subsection (b) provides that "[t]he notice must be executed and must,
at a minimum, read substantially similar to the following," and then sets out the form. Its caption
is the part a buyer's agent should be able to paraphrase from memory: "THIS NOTICE IS A DISCLOSURE
OF SELLER'S KNOWLEDGE OF THE CONDITION OF THE PROPERTY AS OF THE DATE SIGNED BY SELLER AND IS NOT A
SUBSTITUTE FOR ANY INSPECTIONS OR WARRANTIES THE PURCHASER MAY WISH TO OBTAIN. IT IS NOT A WARRANTY
OF ANY KIND BY SELLER OR SELLER'S AGENTS." The notice reports knowledge, not condition; it is dated
to the day of signature; and it is not a warranty, not by the seller and not by you.

The body runs to eleven numbered items, and the optional reference at the end of this module walks
them one by one.

**The knowledge qualifier — subsection (d).** This is the qualifier students truncate most often, so
here it is whole:

> The notice shall be completed to the best of seller's belief and knowledge as of the date the
> notice is completed and signed by the seller. If the information required by the notice is unknown
> to the seller, the seller shall indicate that fact on the notice, and by that act is in compliance
> with this section.

Two consequences follow. First, the notice is a **knowledge document**: a seller who genuinely does
not know the age of the roof and writes "Unknown" has complied with § 5.008 — the statute says so in
terms. Second, the safe harbour operates only where the information genuinely *is* unknown.
"Unknown" written to avoid an answer the seller in fact has is a false statement on the notice, and
the kind that supports a fraud or DTPA claim later.

Do not tell a buyer that the § 5.008 notice is a warranty, and do not let a buyer treat it as an
inspection. The form's own caption forecloses both readings.

**The exemptions — subsection (e).** The section "does not apply to a transfer" in eleven listed
situations. Read them in the statute; what matters in practice is the pattern rather than the
recital.

The exemptions cover transfers where the transferor has no meaningful personal knowledge of the
property — a court order or foreclosure sale, a trustee in bankruptcy, a mortgagee or deed-of-trust
beneficiary that took the property at foreclosure or by deed in lieu, a fiduciary administering a
decedent's estate, guardianship, conservatorship or trust, and a governmental entity — together with
transfers within a family or between co-owners, brand-new construction never occupied, and land
where the improvements are trivial relative to the whole, which is subdivision (11)'s five percent
test. Two come up constantly: subdivision (10) is why a builder selling a house nobody has lived in
delivers no notice, and subdivision (5) is why the executor selling grandmother's house in
Nacogdoches delivers none either. Note what does *not* change: the exemption is from § 5.008, not
from § 1101.652(b)(4). If you list that estate sale and you know the slab has moved, you still owe
the disclosure to a potential buyer under TRELA.

**The timing and the remedy — subsection (f).** The whole subsection:

> The notice shall be delivered by the seller to the purchaser on or before the effective date of an
> executory contract binding the purchaser to purchase the property. If a contract is entered
> without the seller providing the notice required by this section, the purchaser may terminate the
> contract for any reason within seven days after receiving the notice.

Take the deadline first. Delivery is due **on or before the effective date** of the binding contract
— not at closing, and not within some number of days after the offer. In practice the notice is
delivered during marketing and acknowledged by the buyer before or with the offer, which is why the
file should hold the executed notice long before an offer arrives.

Now the remedy, and read it exactly. If the contract is entered without the notice, "the purchaser
may terminate the contract for any reason within seven days after receiving the notice." Three
points students miss.

**The clock starts on receipt, not on the effective date.** A seller who delivers a late notice on
day twelve gives the buyer a seven-day termination right running from day twelve. A seller who never
delivers the notice at all never starts the clock, and the termination right does not expire on its
own under this subsection's terms.

**"[F]or any reason."** The buyer does not have to point to anything in the notice. The right is
unconditional once it exists. A buyer who receives a late notice, reads nothing objectionable in it,
and simply changes their mind about the neighbourhood may terminate within those seven days.

**Section 5.008 states no damages remedy.** It gives a termination right, and it contains no clause
making that right exclusive. Compare Property Code § 5.012, the property owners' association notice,
which adds in subsection (e): "The purchaser's right to terminate the executory contract under
Subsection (d) is the purchaser's exclusive remedy for the seller's failure to provide the notice
required by this section." Section 5.008 contains no such sentence, and the other notice statutes in
the same subchapter anchor their counts differently again — read each one rather than assume they
run alike.

**Your role, precisely stated.** Section 5.008 imposes no duty on a license holder, but four things
are true about your position. You should get the notice completed and delivered on time, because a
seller who misses the subsection (f) deadline hands the buyer a free termination right — a practice
standard, not a statutory one, and this course cites no rule for it. You must not complete the
notice for the seller: it states the seller's knowledge and is dated as of the seller's signature
under subsection (d). You must keep it, because under 22 TAC § 535.2, a broker
must maintain "disclosures" among the records preserved "for at least four years from the date of
closing, termination of the contract, or end of a real estate transaction." And your own TRELA duty
stands independent of the seller's: if the notice says "No" to a question and you know the answer is
"Yes," § 1101.652(b)(4) requires you to disclose the defect to a potential buyer.

### Adapted teaching: Representation disclosure and the written brokerage-services notice

Everything in this module so far has been about what agency *is* and what it *requires*. This unit
is about telling people. Texas consumers cannot protect themselves against a relationship they do
not know exists, and so TRELA imposes two entirely separate disclosure duties, at two different
moments, in two different forms, with two different sets of exceptions. Students merge them
constantly. Do not.

**Duty one: the representation disclosure, § 1101.558(b).** "A license holder who represents a party
in a proposed real estate transaction shall disclose, orally or in writing, that representation at
the time of the license holder's first contact with" either "another party to the transaction" or
"another license holder who represents another party to the transaction."

Four things to hold. It applies only if you *represent* someone. It may be **oral or in writing** —
this is the one disclosure Texas lets you make by speaking. It is due at **first contact**, not
first substantive communication. And its audience is the other side: another party, or the license
holder representing another party. Section 1101.558(b) has no exception list attached to it. The
exceptions in subsection (c), which you will meet in a moment, are addressed to the notice required
by Subsection (b-1), not to the (b) disclosure.

The disciplinary backstop is § 1101.652(b)(7): failing "to make clear to all parties to a real
estate transaction the party for whom the license holder is acting."

**Duty two: the written notice, § 1101.558(b-1).** This is the Information About Brokerage Services
notice — the IABS. The statute provides: "At the time of a license holder's first substantive
communication with a party relating to a proposed transaction regarding specific real property, the
license holder shall provide to the party written notice in at least a 10-point font that:" Four
contents follow.

> (1) describes the ways in which a broker can represent a party to a real estate transaction,
> including
> as an intermediary;
> (2) describes the basic duties and obligations a broker has to a party to a real estate
> transaction
> that the broker represents;
> (3) describes the basic obligations a broker has to a party to a real estate transaction that the
> broker does not represent; and
> (4) provides the name, license number, and contact information for the license holder and the
> license
> holder's supervisor and broker, if applicable.

Contrast it with duty one at every point. It is owed whether or not you represent anyone — the
trigger is a substantive communication with a party, not a representation you hold. It must be
**written**, in at least a 10-point font. It is due at the **first substantive communication with a
party relating to a proposed transaction regarding specific real property** — all of those
qualifiers matter; a general conversation about the market, with no specific property in view, is
not the trigger the statute describes. Its audience is the party you are communicating with, not the
other side's agent. And note what subdivision (4) requires: the name, license number and contact
information for **you**, for **your supervisor** and for **your broker**, "if applicable." That is
why a completed IABS carries several licence numbers.

Section 1101.558(b-2) hands the drafting to the Commission: "The commission by rule shall prescribe
the text of the notice required under Subsections (b-1)(1) and (2) and establish the methods by
which a license holder shall provide the notice." You do not write this notice. TREC does.

**A note on the section's history, because it is a trap.** Subsections (a), (d) and (e) of §
1101.558 were repealed by Acts 2015, 84th Leg., R.S., Ch. 1158, Sec. 92, effective January 1, 2016;
the statute as held records the repeals without preserving the repealed text, so this module does
not state what those subsections said. The section was most recently amended by Acts 2025, 89th
Leg., R.S., Ch. 1172 (S.B. 1968), Sec. 9, effective January 1, 2026. If you have an older study
guide that cites § 1101.558(c) for the notice requirement or quotes subsection (d), throw it away.
The notice duty is now in subsection (b-1) and subsection (c) is the exception list.

**When the notice is NOT required: § 1101.558(c).** The section opens: "A license holder is not
required to provide the notice required by Subsection (b-1) if:" Three exceptions follow.

> (1) the proposed transaction is for a residential lease for less than one year and a sale is not
> being
> considered;
> (2) the license holder meets with a party who the license holder knows is represented by another
> license holder; or
> (3) the communication occurs at a property that is held open for any prospective buyer or tenant
> and
> the communication concerns that property.

Read each carefully. Exception (1) has two elements joined by "and": a residential lease of **less
than one year** *and* no sale being considered. A fourteen-month lease is outside it. A twelve-month
lease with a purchase option under discussion is outside it. Exception (2) turns on your
**knowledge** that the party is represented by another license holder — not on your assumption, and
not on the party's vague reference to "my realtor." Exception (3) is the open-house exception, and
it is narrow in two ways: the property must be "held open for any prospective buyer or tenant," and
"the communication concerns that property." Turn the conversation at the open house to the buyer's
own house down the road, and the exception by its terms no longer covers that communication.

These three exceptions relieve you of the **(b-1) notice**. They do not touch the § 1101.558(b)
representation disclosure, they do not touch § 1101.563's written-agreement requirement, and — as
you will see — they do not touch the website-link duty in the Commission's rule.

**The rule: 22 TAC § 531.20.** The Commission's IABS rule opens by adopting the form: "The
Commission adopts by reference the Information About Brokerage Services Notice, TREC No. IABS 1-2
(IABS Notice). The IABS Notice is published by and available from the Texas Real Estate Commission,
P.O. Box 12188, Austin, Texas 78711-2188, www.trec.texas.gov." **The IABS Notice form itself is held for
this course, but its text is not taught here**, so this module does not reproduce or paraphrase the
language printed on the form; what it teaches is § 531.20, the rule that adopts the form, and the
statute that governs its use.

Subsection (b) imposes two duties. Under (b)(1), each license holder shall provide "a link to a
completed IABS Notice in a readily noticeable place on the homepage of each business website,
labeled:" — and then one of two labels, either "Texas Real Estate Commission Information About
Brokerage Services", in at least 10 point font, or "TREC Information About Brokerage Services", in
at least 12 point font. Two label options, two different minimum font sizes; the longer label may be
smaller. Under (b)(2), each license holder shall provide "the completed IABS Notice at the first
substantive communication as required under §1101.558, Texas Occupations Code." Note that the rule
as held cross-references § 1101.558 as a whole; the statutory duty it is implementing now sits in §
1101.558(b-1).

The term business website is defined in the rule as "a website on the internet that: is accessible
to the public; contains information about a license holder's real estate brokerage services; and the
content of the website is controlled by the license holder." All three elements. A personal
Instagram account that never mentions your brokerage services is not a business website under that
definition; one that advertises your listings is. For social media, the rule says the link required
under subsection (b)(1) "may be located on: the account holder profile; or a separate page or
website through a direct link from the social media platform or account holder profile." You may
satisfy it from the profile bio, provided the link is there and labelled as the rule requires.

**How the notice may be delivered.** The rule states the permitted methods: "For purposes of
§1101.558, Texas Occupations Code, the completed IABS Notice can be provided: by personal delivery
by the license holder; by first class mail or overnight common carrier delivery service; in the body
of an email; or as an attachment to an email, or a link within the body of an email, with a specific
reference to the IABS Notice in the body of the email." Four routes: hand it over; mail it first
class or send it by overnight common carrier; paste it into the body of an email; or attach it or
link it, **with a specific reference to the IABS Notice in the body of the email**. That last
qualifier is the one people fail. An attachment named *iabs.pdf* with an email body that says only
that it was great talking today does not comply, because the body contains no specific reference to
the IABS Notice.

And the rule adds a flat prohibition: "The link to a completed IABS Notice may not be in a footnote
or signature block in an email." Every year, license holders decide the tidy solution is to drop the
IABS link into the email signature so it goes out on everything. The rule forecloses exactly that.

**Reproducing the form.** The last requirement in § 531.20 is one students never expect: "License
holders may reproduce the IABS Notice published by the Commission, provided that the text of the
IABS Notice is copied verbatim and the spacing, borders and placement of text on the page must
appear to be identical to that in the published version of the IABS Notice, except that the Broker
Contact Information section may be prefilled." You may reproduce it — many brokerages build it into
their transaction software — but the text must be **verbatim** and the spacing, borders and
placement of text must **appear to be identical** to the published version. The single permitted
deviation is that the Broker Contact Information section may be prefilled. You may not add your logo
into the body, reformat it to fit a page, trim a paragraph you find confusing, or otherwise tidy it
up. If your brokerage's template does any of that, it is out of compliance with § 531.20.

**A companion notice that is not the IABS.** 22 TAC § 531.18 adopts a different document by
reference, the Consumer Protection Notice, TREC No. CN 1-5, which carries a display-in-the-office
requirement the IABS rule does not, is not delivered at first substantive communication, and means
two links on your homepage rather than one. **Law of Agency Module 08 teaches § 531.18.** The Consumer Protection
Notice form itself was not held for this course.

**How the 2026 changes wire into all of this.** Section 1101.562(b), which you met in Law of Agency source unit 3, routes
a non-representation showing through this unit's two duties: before showing property without
representing the party, the broker must, as applicable, "disclose to the party as required by
Section 1101.558(b) if the broker represents the owner of the real property" or "provide to the
party the written notice described by Section 1101.558(b-1) if the broker does not represent the
owner of the real property." So the disclosure you owe depends on which side, if any, you are on.
Represent the owner: make the (b) representation disclosure, orally or in writing. Represent nobody
in the transaction: deliver the (b-1) written notice — the IABS.

**The habit to build.** Nothing in this unit is difficult. It fails in practice for one reason:
license holders treat the IABS as paperwork rather than as the first thing they do. Build the
sequence into muscle memory. Before you talk about a specific property with a party, deliver the
notice by one of the four permitted routes and keep the proof — and remember that 22 TAC § 535.2(h),
taught in Law of Agency Module 09, requires your broker to keep disclosures among the brokerage's records,
available to the Commission, for at least four years. If you cannot produce the IABS you say you
sent, your broker has a records problem as well as a disclosure problem.


The governing source for seller condition disclosure is [Property Code § 5.008](https://tcss.legis.texas.gov/resources/PR/htm/PR.5.htm). Its exemptions are exemptions from that section, not a general license to conceal known material facts. The license holder’s own duties under Occupations Code § 1101.652(b)(3)–(4) remain a separate inquiry. When a seller is exempt, document the actual basis and still communicate information the law requires.

In a worked transaction, an estate fiduciary claims a § 5.008 exemption, but the listing broker has an old engineering report identifying movement. The exemption does not decide the broker’s independent known-defect disclosure obligation. The agent should provide the information through the proper process and seek counsel where the scope is disputed. Do not tell the buyer that an exempt seller means the property has no disclosure issues.

## T06U5 — Conveyance of the Property

The sales contract promises a conveyance; the deed or other proper instrument performs it. [Property Code § 5.021](https://tcss.legis.texas.gov/resources/PR/htm/PR.5.htm) requires the specified conveyances to be written, subscribed, and delivered by the conveyor or the conveyor’s agent authorized in writing. Section 5.022 provides a statutory form and states that a warranty covenant is not required. Section 5.023 identifies implied covenants arising from specified language unless restricted. The actual deed and legal advice determine the conveyance and warranties.

The seller should convey the estate promised in the contract, subject to the agreed exceptions and reservations. A proposed closing deed that adds an unagreed mineral reservation presents a discrepancy requiring review. A legal description that omits an agreed tract likewise requires correction through the proper professionals. An agent must not draft or certify a deed; the role is to identify the conflict and preserve the parties’ opportunity to resolve it before execution.

Distinguish title transfer from physical possession. A tenant may retain possession under a lease. The seller may have a properly documented temporary post-closing lease. The buyer may have early possession under a separate arrangement. The deed alone does not describe every occupancy obligation. Match the purchase contract, leases, and closing instructions so the buyer knows when lawful possession will occur.

In a worked file, the deed is signed but the escrow agent has not confirmed funding or release. The agent should not deliver keys based solely on the signature. The contract’s possession provision, escrow instructions, and actual closing status must be followed. Signing, delivery, funding, recording, and possession are related events that may occur at different times.

## T06U6 — Signatures

A signature indicates assent only when connected to the intended document and proper person or authority. Confirm the party name, capacity, version, all attached documents, and conspicuous changes. A seller’s personal signature does not automatically bind a separate LLC. A person with authority to show property does not necessarily have authority to convey it. A spouse’s absence may raise marital or homestead questions even if one name appears on the deed.

For electronic records, [Business & Commerce Code §§ 322.005, 322.007, and 322.009](https://tcss.legis.texas.gov/resources/BC/htm/BC.322.htm) address the electronic-transaction framework. Agreement to conduct the transaction electronically, attribution, and the substantive agreement still matter. Retain the signed document and available completion or audit evidence. Do not paste a signature image into a changed contract or treat access to another person’s email as authority to sign for that person.

The “person to be charged” in § 26.01 is the person against whom enforcement of the promise is sought. This is not necessarily the person who initially drafted the document or the party who wants to sue. A writing signed only by the buyer presents a different enforcement question against the seller. In practice, obtain the required signatures and authority evidence before treating the agreement as complete; contested signature or authority issues require counsel.

A worked review finds two versions: the buyer signed one with a refrigerator included, and the seller signed one excluding it. Even though both signatures are authentic, the parties may not have signed the same bargain. The agent should identify the difference and obtain clear agreement through the correct process. The task is not to combine signature pages into a third version that neither party signed.

## T06U7 — Statute of Frauds

The statute of frauds is a writing requirement for specified promises, not a general rule that oral statements never have consequences. [Business & Commerce Code § 26.01(a)](https://tcss.legis.texas.gov/resources/BC/htm/BC.26.htm) requires the covered promise or agreement, or a memorandum, to be written and signed by the person to be charged or a lawfully authorized signer. Subsection (b) identifies the categories, including real-estate sales, leases longer than one year, and agreements not to be performed within one year of making.

Identify the promise before applying the rule. A seller’s promise to convey a house is distinct from a broker’s commission promise, a guaranty of another’s debt, or a lease. Occupations Code § 1101.806(c) supplies the separate signed-writing rule for a real-estate sale or purchase commission action. Section 26.01(b)(7) specifically addresses commissions involving listed mineral interests. A classroom answer that cites only “statute of frauds” leaves the controlling provision unidentified.

The writing must reflect the necessary agreement, not merely prove that people were talking. A stack of emails may require analysis of whether the documents concern the same transaction and contain the essential terms. A description such as “my land” can create serious identification questions. Do not invent omitted terms or a property description after a dispute arises. Counsel must evaluate legal sufficiency; the agent should establish complete, accurate documentation at the outset.

The rule also does not mean electronic signatures are automatically excluded. UETA addresses electronic recognition, while the substantive writing requirements still apply. A signed email with incomplete essential terms is not cured by being electronic; a complete electronic agreement is not invalid merely because no ink was used. The legal questions are content, intent, attribution, authority, and applicability.

Exceptions and defenses to the statute of frauds are matters for legal advice. Expenditures, possession, partial payment, or oral acknowledgments may be relevant in litigation but should not be presented as a routine substitute for proper contracting. An agent who tells a buyer to move in so the buyer can force an oral sale is directing risky legal strategy. Obtain the proper written agreement and appropriate counsel.

In a worked example, a purchaser signs a written offer, the seller orally agrees, and the purchaser orders inspections. The inspections do not supply the seller’s missing signature. The agent should not treat the expenses as an automatic exception. Preserve the documents and communications, clarify status before further commitments, and refer legal enforcement questions. The practical standard is accurate contracting before performance creates avoidable loss.

## T06U8 — Default and Breach of Contract

A breach is an unexcused failure to perform an obligation when due. A default provision identifies contractual consequences and possible elections. Before alleging default, identify the actual duty, due event, required performance, evidence of failure, any condition or excuse, and applicable notice or cure mechanism. An unfavorable event is not automatically a breach. A permitted termination is different from refusal to perform without an available right.

The current TREC resale contract’s default paragraph must be read in its actual text. It provides choices under specified buyer and seller defaults; it should not be reduced to “the seller always keeps earnest money.” A deposit can be disputed, and the escrow holder’s release process is separate from the legal merits. The broker should not decide entitlement by disbursing trust funds or promising a result.

For a worked comparison, buyer A timely exercises an existing termination right with the required notice. Buyer B simply refuses to close after the right has expired. The outcomes can differ because the governing obligation and available exit differ. The agent should assemble the accepted contract, all amendments, notices, delivery evidence, lender information, and chronology. Counsel can then evaluate whether the claimed right was available and properly exercised.

Similarly, a seller’s failure to complete agreed repairs can be different from a buyer’s dissatisfaction with work that meets the agreement. Establish what was promised and what was done. Qualified inspection may resolve factual completion; the principals may negotiate an amendment; legal advice may be needed for default and remedies. Do not rewrite the promise after the fact to match the completed work.

Repudiation, waiver, prevention of performance, material breach, and other doctrines can affect the legal analysis. A license holder should recognize the issue without offering a definitive legal strategy. If a party threatens not to perform, promptly involve the broker, preserve the statement, and encourage legal advice. Continuing informal negotiations should not cause a client to miss a contractual or statutory deadline.

### Breach analysis begins with the actual duty

When a participant reports a breach, identify the promised performance before discussing remedies. Who was required to act? What act was required? What event triggered the duty? When and how was performance due? What evidence shows performance, nonperformance, or a condition that affected the duty? These questions are more useful than beginning with a demand for earnest money.

For example, a buyer says the seller failed to repair the heating system. Compare that assertion with the signed agreement and amendment. Did the seller promise a particular repair, replacement, professional evaluation, or no repair at all? An inspection recommendation alone is not necessarily a seller promise. A negotiation email may not be the final agreed term. The agent should assemble the operative text and evidence before characterizing the seller’s conduct.

Next distinguish a performance dispute from a notice dispute. A party may claim to have exercised a contractual right while the other challenges its timing, content, recipient, or delivery. Preserve the actual notice and transmission evidence, not merely a summary that it was sent. A screenshot of a draft message does not prove delivery. The legal effectiveness of the notice requires the governing provision and facts.

Finally, separate the merits from custody of funds. The escrow holder’s authority to disburse is not identical to a party’s view of who breached. A broker should not treat one party’s demand as a final adjudication or promise immediate release without applying the governing agreement and applicable rules. A contested remedy belongs with counsel and the appropriate dispute process. Accurate coordination can continue while entitlement remains unresolved.

## T06U9 — Remedies

Contract remedies can include damages, specific performance, termination under the agreement, rescission where legally available, and an agreed liquidated-damages election. Each serves a different purpose and has requirements. Damages seek compensation under the applicable measure. Specific performance seeks an order requiring promised performance. Rescission seeks to undo a transaction under the relevant legal grounds. Liquidated damages use an agreed measure subject to applicable law. They should not be promised as an automatic menu from which a party can collect everything at once.

The sales contract’s remedy provisions and the facts control available contractual elections. A seller who elects earnest money as liquidated damages under a provision providing that election must understand its consequences. A buyer seeking specific performance needs counsel to evaluate the contract, readiness and ability to perform, equitable requirements, defenses, and timing. Land’s uniqueness is relevant background, not a guarantee of an order compelling a sale.

[Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 16.004(a)(1)](https://tcss.legis.texas.gov/resources/CP/htm/CP.16.htm) identifies a four-year limitation for specific performance of a contract to convey real property. Other causes of action and defenses can have different accrual or limitations rules. A broker’s record-retention period is not a legal opinion about the deadline for every claim. Prompt referral is appropriate when a dispute appears; the client should not wait merely because a classroom chart names a period of years.

In a hypothetical rising market, a seller refuses to convey a uniquely useful adjacent parcel after signing a complete agreement. The buyer may ask counsel to evaluate damages and specific performance. The broker should preserve evidence of the bargain, property, communications, performance steps, and current transaction status. The agent should not guarantee that the buyer will receive the land or calculate recoverable damages by subtracting two marketing prices.

Consumer-protection remedies are a separate legal track where the claim’s requirements are met. They do not replace the need to establish contract remedies correctly.
### Adapted teaching: Economic loss, mental anguish, statutory multipliers, and why remedies require their own proof

Much of what makes the DTPA frightening is arithmetic. The difference between an ordinary finding, a
"knowingly" finding and an "intentionally" finding is the difference between a claim your carrier
absorbs and one that ends a career.

**Start with the base: economic damages.** Section 17.45(11) defines economic damages as
"compensatory damages for pecuniary loss, including costs of repair and replacement. The term does
not include exemplary damages or damages for physical pain and mental anguish, loss of consortium,
disfigurement, physical impairment, or loss of companionship and society." The item to notice in the
exclusive half is mental anguish: a separate category, available only as § 17.50(b)(1) allows.

**Section 17.50(b)(1) is the damages engine.** In a suit filed under the section,
"each consumer who prevails may obtain: (1) the amount of economic damages found by the trier of
fact. If the trier of fact finds that the conduct of the defendant was committed knowingly, the
consumer may also recover damages for mental anguish, as found by the trier of fact, and the trier
of fact may award not more than three times the amount of economic damages; or if the trier of fact
finds the conduct was committed intentionally, the consumer may recover damages for mental anguish,
as found by the trier of fact, and the trier of fact may award not more than three times the amount
of damages for mental anguish and economic damages".

Break that sentence into its three tiers.

*Tier one — an ordinary violation.* The consumer recovers "the amount of economic damages found by
the trier of fact." No mental anguish. No multiplier. Economic damages, full stop — plus the costs
and fees that § 17.50(d) makes mandatory.

*Tier two — conduct committed knowingly*, as § 17.45(9) defines "knowingly." Two things unlock:
"the consumer may also recover damages for mental anguish, as found by the trier of fact," and "the
trier of fact may award not more than three times the amount of economic damages." At this tier the
multiplier's base is *economic damages only*, so mental anguish is recoverable but is not multiplied.
And "may award not more than" is a ceiling, not an automatic tripling.

*Tier three — conduct committed intentionally*, as § 17.45(13) defines "intentionally." Mental
anguish is again recoverable, and now the multiplier's base expands: "the trier of fact may award
not more than three times the amount of damages for mental anguish and economic damages." Mental
anguish joins the base. That is the entire difference between tier two and tier three, and in a case
with substantial mental anguish findings it is an enormous difference.

**Worked example one: the Round Rock square footage claim.** The buyers paid $505,000 for a house
advertised at 2,900 square feet that measures 2,640. Assume economic damages of $22,000, attorneys'
fees of $48,000 and court costs of $3,200.

At tier one, with no culpability finding, the judgment is economic damages of $22,000, plus $48,000
in fees and $3,200 in costs, for a total of **$73,200**. The fee award is nearly twice the damages.
That is not an anomaly; it is the ordinary shape of a DTPA judgment in a modest case, and it is why
these cases get filed.

Now assume the jury also finds that you acted knowingly — you had the appraisal district record in
your file and entered 2,900 anyway. The jury finds mental anguish of $15,000. Tier two allows
economic damages of $22,000, mental anguish of $15,000, and additional damages of "not more than
three times the amount of economic damages." Three times $22,000 is $66,000. If the jury awards the
maximum, the damages total $22,000 + $15,000 + $66,000 = **$103,000**, and fees and costs of $51,200
are added on top, for **$154,200**.

Now assume instead a finding that the conduct was committed intentionally. Tier three allows
additional damages of "not more than three times the amount of damages for mental anguish and
economic damages." The base is now $22,000 + $15,000 = $37,000, and three times that is $111,000.
Maximum damages become $22,000 + $15,000 + $111,000 = **$148,000**, plus $51,200 in fees and costs,
for **$199,200**. The same false number in the same MLS field produced a $73,200 exposure, a
$154,200 exposure, or a $199,200 exposure, depending entirely on what a jury concluded about your
state of mind — and remember from Law of Agency source unit 1 that both § 17.45(9) and § 17.45(13) permit the jury to
infer that state of mind from objective manifestations.

**Do not include fees in the multiplier base.** Section 17.50(e) is explicit: "In computing
additional damages under Subsection (b), attorneys' fees, costs, and prejudgment interest may not be
considered." That is why the tier three base was $37,000 and not $37,000 plus $51,200. A student who
trebles the whole judgment has misread the statute.

**Prejudgment interest.** Section 17.50(f): a court "may not award prejudgment interest applicable
to: (1) damages for future loss under this subchapter; or (2) additional damages under Subsection
(b)." Interest does not run on the multiplier.

**Attorneys' fees, in both directions.** Section 17.50(d) is unconditional for the consumer: "Each
consumer who prevails shall be awarded court costs and reasonable and necessary attorneys' fees."
But § 17.50(c) points the other way: "On a finding by the court that an action under this section
was groundless in fact or law or brought in bad faith, or brought for the purpose of harassment, the
court shall award to the defendant reasonable and necessary attorneys' fees and court costs." Also a
"shall," and it requires a finding by the court, not the jury.

**The other relief the statute authorises.** Section 17.50(b)(3) permits "orders necessary to
restore to any party to the suit any money or property, real or personal, which may have been
acquired in violation of this subchapter" — under which a commission you were paid can be ordered
returned. Section 17.50(b)(4) permits "any other relief which the court deems proper,
including the appointment of a receiver or the revocation of a license or certificate authorizing a
person to engage in business in this state if the judgment has not been satisfied within three
months of the date of the final judgment." Then the limitation: "The court
may not revoke or suspend a license to do business in this state or appoint a receiver to take over
the affairs of a person who has failed to satisfy a judgment if the person is a licensee of or
regulated by a state agency which has statutory authority to revoke or suspend a license or to
appoint a receiver or trustee." TREC holds revocation authority over you, so the civil court will not
revoke your real estate license. TREC may still do so.

**Chapter 41 does not apply.** Section 17.50(g) states flatly: "Chapter 41, Civil Practice and
Remedies Code, does not apply to a cause of action brought under this subchapter." The general Texas
exemplary damages caps are unavailable to a DTPA defendant; the only ceiling is the one inside
§ 17.50(b)(1) itself.

**A limit on mental anguish that students miss.** Section 17.49(e): "Except as specifically provided
by Subsections (b) and (h), Section 17.50, nothing in this subchapter shall apply to a cause of
action for bodily injury or death or for the infliction of mental anguish." The DTPA is not a vehicle
for a stand-alone emotional distress claim.


The professional response to a remedy question is concrete: identify the disputed obligation and source, preserve the record, transmit authorized notices promptly, coordinate with the supervising broker and escrow holder, and advise legal consultation. A broker can advocate for a client’s business objective while leaving the legal determination to the proper process.


### Statute reading exercise: The signature belongs to whom?

Read Business & Commerce Code § 26.01(a)–(b), Property Code § 5.021, and Occupations Code § 1101.806(c). For a buyer-signed offer, a seller-signed deed, and a seller-signed listing, identify which promise or conveyance each document evidences and whose signature matters to the proposed enforcement action.

Feedback: Each instrument concerns a different relationship. The buyer’s offer does not itself supply the seller’s signature for enforcement against the seller. A deed concerns conveyance under § 5.021; a listing can supply a signed commission promise under § 1101.806(c). Identify the promise and the person to be charged rather than treating any signature in the file as sufficient.

### Applied exercise: An offer with two attachment sets

The buyer signs a resale offer with third-party financing and a temporary seller lease. The seller signs the same base pages but attaches no lease and changes the financing amount. Identify what remains unresolved and prepare an accurate status note before anyone inserts an effective date.

Feedback: The attachments and financing terms differ. Establish whether the parties agreed to the same bargain and whether acceptance was communicated. Preserve both versions and obtain clear authorized agreement. Do not combine pages into an artificial fully signed contract or treat the date blank as proof of formation.

### Applied exercise: The disputed deposit

A buyer emails a termination after a financing problem. The seller claims the notice was late and demands the deposit. Identify the evidence needed to evaluate the claimed right and explain what the agent should communicate to the escrow holder.

Feedback: Obtain the operative contract and financing addendum, effective date, all amendments, lender information, buyer performance evidence, termination notice, and delivery record. The escrow holder should receive accurate status and appropriate authorized documents, not an agent’s instruction based on an unadjudicated legal conclusion. Counsel should address the dispute and remedy election.


### Module recap

Treat the sales contract as both a bargain and a calendar. Verify the same document and attachments were accepted; distinguish borrower approval, property approval, disclosures, conveyance, and possession; identify the particular signature and writing requirement; and analyze alleged default through the actual obligation and notice process. Remedies require legal evaluation and evidence, not assurances about the deposit.
