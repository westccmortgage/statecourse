## T01U1 — Key Terms

Contract vocabulary is a set of questions about a transaction. A contract is an agreement the law recognizes as creating obligations. An offer proposes terms on which the offeror is prepared to be bound. Acceptance expresses assent to that offer through the required method. A counteroffer proposes a different bargain; it should not be treated as acceptance merely because someone has signed the paper. Consideration is the bargained-for legal value supporting a promise. Money is common consideration, but exchanged promises also matter. Earnest money is a deposit governed by the contract, not a universal substitute for every element of formation.

The parties are the persons assuming the contract’s rights and obligations. A principal employs or authorizes an agent. In an ordinary residential sale, the seller and buyer are parties to the sales contract; their brokers have distinct brokerage relationships and duties. The agent who transmits an offer does not thereby become the offeror. The title company receiving a deposit does not thereby become the seller. A lender’s prequalification letter does not make the lender a party to the purchase contract. These distinctions identify who may approve changes, who must sign, who receives notices, and who can enforce a promise.

The subject of the transaction is also specific. Land is the physical ground and associated natural features. Real estate includes land and improvements. Real property refers to rights in that real estate. Personal property may be included in a transaction but requires its own clear treatment. A fixture question asks whether an article has become part of the real property; a reservation asks whether the contract or conveyance keeps a specified interest for the seller. A deed is the conveyance instrument. A sales contract is the agreement under which a later conveyance will occur. Neither a deed of trust nor a title policy is interchangeable with that deed.

Read each deadline as a trigger, action, recipient, and consequence. “Effective date” commonly identifies the date on which the parties’ fully executed agreement becomes effective after acceptance is communicated. “Closing date” identifies a future performance date. “Possession” concerns the right to occupy, which can begin at another time if properly agreed. “Notice” is a communication that must satisfy the governing provision. “Delivery” may concern money, documents, or notice; each has different mechanics. A calendar containing only one date called “contract date” is inadequate.

An addendum supplements an agreement with additional terms. An amendment changes an existing agreement. A disclosure conveys information; it does not necessarily change the purchase obligations. A contingency makes a duty, right, or continuation of the transaction depend on stated conditions. A covenant is a promise to do or refrain from doing something. Default concerns failure to meet an obligation. A remedy is a legally available response to a wrong or failure. A termination right exercised as the contract permits is not automatically a breach. A disputed termination does not authorize a broker to decide the parties’ legal entitlement to escrow funds.

“Promulgated” means that the Commission has adopted the form for required use in the transaction category, subject to applicable exceptions. “Approved for voluntary use” describes a different status. “Attorney prepared” identifies authorship; it is not a universal exception allowing any form in every transaction. “Broker approved” is an office review decision, not a replacement for the Commission’s form rule. Under [22 TAC § 537.11](https://www.trec.texas.gov/agency-information/rules-and-laws/trec-rules), the transaction type, form status, and applicable exception must be evaluated together.

Consider a file labelled “cash deal, no lawyers needed.” The buyer will actually finance part of the price; the seller wants to reserve groundwater; a tenant will remain after closing. The label establishes none of those legal arrangements. A competent agent replaces shorthand with the actual facts: financing source, reserved interest, tenant’s existing agreement, required documents, deadlines, and professional review. The vocabulary is useful because each term points toward a different question and source.

## T01U2 — Texas Real Estate License Act

The Real Estate License Act regulates the person providing brokerage services. Contract law regulates the promises the parties make. Both apply simultaneously. An enforceable purchase agreement does not excuse an agent from using required forms, presenting offers, making disclosures, or handling trust money properly. Conversely, an agent’s regulatory violation does not by itself tell you whether the buyer may terminate a purchase agreement. Keep a separate record of the buyer’s and seller’s obligations and the brokerage’s duties.

The current statutory source is [Occupations Code Chapter 1101](https://tcss.legis.texas.gov/resources/OC/htm/OC.1101.htm), especially §§ 1101.002, 1101.155, 1101.555–1101.563, 1101.652, 1101.654, and 1101.806. The statute authorizes rules; the rules add operating requirements. A brokerage policy can be more specific about its agents’ workflow, but a policy cannot authorize what a statute or rule forbids.
### Adapted teaching: The Act’s definitions, consumer protection, and scope

Texas regulates real estate brokerage by statute. The statute is chapter 1101 of the Texas
Occupations Code, and § 1101.001 gives it the name you will hear used every working day of your
career: "This chapter may be cited as **The Real Estate License Act**." Practitioners shorten that to
**TRELA**, and the abbreviation is worth learning now, because when a broker tells you that something
"is a TRELA problem," the broker means it is governed by chapter 1101 rather than by a contract, a
brokerage policy, or a trade association's code of conduct. Those are four different sources of
obligation, and confusing them is the single most common orientation error a new license holder
makes.

Why does the state regulate this business at all? The Act does not open with a philosophical
statement of purpose, but the purpose is visible in the operative sections themselves, and reading
them together tells you plainly what the Legislature was worried about. Section 1101.401(c) requires
that the licensing examination "be of sufficient scope in the judgment of the commission to determine
whether a person is competent to act as a broker or sales agent **in a manner that will protect the
public**." Section 1101.155(a) authorizes the Commission to adopt rules "in the public's best
interest" requiring license holders to use standardized contract forms. Section 1101.204(h) requires
the Commission to ensure that it "gives priority to the investigation of a complaint filed by a
consumer and an enforcement case resulting from the consumer complaint," and to "assign priorities
and investigate complaints using a risk-based approach" whose first two listed factors are the degree
of potential harm to a consumer and the potential for immediate harm to a consumer. Section 1101.601
maintains a trust account "to reimburse aggrieved persons who suffer actual damages" caused by
license holder misconduct. Every one of those provisions points the same direction. The Act exists
because ordinary Texans buy and sell real estate rarely, at high stakes, using intermediaries who
know far more about the transaction than they do — and the Legislature decided that the intermediary
should be tested, licensed, supervised, disciplinable, and backed by a fund.

**What the Act covers, and what it does not.** TRELA is a licensing and conduct statute. It says who
must hold a license, how a license is obtained and kept, what a license holder may and may not do,
and what happens when a license holder does something the Act forbids. It is not a contract statute
and it is not a property statute. The rules that decide whether a deed conveyed title, whether a
landlord repaired a condition in time, or whether a lien attached are found elsewhere in Texas law.
When you are trying to place a problem, ask first whether the question is about a license holder's
conduct and status — that is TRELA — or about the parties' rights in the property, which is not.

**A person, not a company, is the anchor of the system.** Section 1101.002(4) defines a **license
holder** as "a broker or sales agent licensed under this chapter." Those are the only two categories
of real estate license the Act issues, and everything else in the statute hangs off them. Section
1101.002(7) defines a **sales agent** as "a person who is sponsored by a licensed broker for the
purpose of performing an act described by Subdivision (1)." Read that definition carefully: the
sponsorship is not a courtesy or an employment arrangement layered on top of the license. It is
written into the definition of what a sales agent *is*. A sales agent without a sponsoring broker is
not a lesser kind of practitioner; the person is, for practical purposes, not practicing at all, a
point § 1101.351(c) then makes explicit.

**The broker definition is a list of acts, not a job description.** Section 1101.002(1)(A) defines a
broker functionally: a person who, "in exchange for a commission or other valuable consideration or
with the expectation of receiving a commission or other valuable consideration, performs for another
person" one of twelve listed acts. Those acts run from the obvious to the surprising — selling,
exchanging, purchasing or leasing real estate, and *offering* to do so; negotiating or attempting to
negotiate a listing, sale, exchange, purchase or lease; listing real estate or agreeing to list it;
auctioning it; dealing in options, "including a lease to purchase"; aiding a person in locating or
obtaining real estate; procuring a prospect, or procuring property, to effect a sale, exchange or
lease; controlling "the acceptance or deposit of rent from a resident of a single-family residential
real property unit"; providing a written price opinion on the conditions the clause specifies; and
advising an owner "concerning the negotiation or completion of a short sale."

Three features of that list matter immediately. First, attempts and offers count. You do not have to
close a transaction to have performed an act of a broker; § 1101.002(1)(A)(ii) reaches a person who
merely "offers to sell, exchange, purchase, or lease real estate." Second, the compensation trigger
is forward-looking. The definition catches a person acting "with the *expectation* of receiving a
commission or other valuable consideration," so an unlicensed person who helps a friend find a rental
on the promise of a thank-you payment later has already crossed the line. Third, § 1101.002(1)(B)
sweeps in two additional categories that do not look like brokerage at first glance: a person
"employed by or for an owner of real estate to sell any portion of the real estate," and a person who
charges an advance fee under a contract requiring the person primarily to promote the sale of real
estate by listing it in a publication or referring information about it to brokers.

**"Real estate" is broader than you expect.** Section 1101.002(5) defines real estate as "any
interest in real property, including a leasehold, located in or outside this state," and adds that
"[t]he term does not include an interest given as security for the performance of an obligation." Two
consequences follow. A leasehold is real estate for TRELA purposes, so leasing activity is brokerage
activity — which is why the Act separately defines and licenses **residential rental locators** under
§ 1101.002(6) as persons who offer "for consideration to locate a unit in an apartment complex for
lease to a prospective tenant," excluding an owner who locates a unit in the owner's own complex. And
because a mortgage or deed of trust is an interest given as security, dealing in that security
interest is not, standing alone, dealing in real estate under this chapter.

**Qualifying education is defined in the Act itself.** Section 1101.003(a) lists eleven subjects that
constitute "qualifying real estate courses": agency law; contract law; principles of real estate;
property management; real estate appraisal; real estate brokerage; real estate finance; real estate
investment; real estate law; real estate marketing; and real estate mathematics. Subsection (b) lets
the Commission designate equivalents, and subsection (c) lets it prescribe the content of those
courses and the title and content of additional ones. Two provisions in the same section police how
the education is actually delivered. Section 1101.003(d) provides that "[a] daily course segment for
a qualifying course may not exceed 12 hours." Section 1101.003(e) provides that no applicant, license
holder or education provider may report completion of an alternative delivery or correspondence
qualifying course "until the elapsed time between the time the applicant or license holder registers
for the course and the time the completion of the course is reported exceeds twice the number of
hours for which credit is claimed." For a 30-hour course, more than 60 elapsed hours must pass between registration and the report of completion. That elapsed-time restriction is separate from required instruction and does not mean that a calendar week must pass.

**Who the Act does not reach.** Section 1101.005 lists the persons and transactions to which the
chapter does not apply, and a new license holder should recognize the main ones: an attorney licensed
in this state; an attorney-in-fact under a power of attorney conducting "not more than three real
estate transactions annually"; a public official engaged in official duties; an auctioneer licensed
under chapter 1802 conducting a real estate auction, "if the auctioneer does not perform another act
of a broker"; a person conducting a transaction under a court order or the authority of a will or
written trust instrument; a person employed by an owner in the sale of structures the owner erected in
the course of the owner's business; an on-site manager of an apartment complex; and "an owner or the
owner's employee who leases the owner's improved or unimproved real estate." The section also excludes
certain subject matter entirely — minerals, mining or a quarry, standing timber, or other energy
sources; cemetery lots; the lease or management of a hotel or motel; and a sale "under a power of sale
conferred by a deed of trust or other contract lien."

Section 1101.004 approaches the same boundary from the other side. Subsection (a) states that a
person is engaged in real estate brokerage if the person, expecting valuable consideration, performs
or offers, attempts or agrees to perform any act described by § 1101.002(1) "as a part of a
transaction or as an entire transaction." Subsection (b) then confirms that three activities do not
by themselves make a person a broker: constructing, remodeling or repairing a building; sponsoring,
promoting or managing an investment in real estate, or participating in one as principal, partner or
financial manager; and entering into an obligation to pay another person secured by an interest in
real property. A closely related provision, § 1101.0045, permits a person to acquire an option or an
interest in a contract to purchase real property and then sell or assign it without a license, but
only if the person does not use it "to engage in real estate brokerage" and "discloses in writing the
nature of the equitable interest to any seller or potential buyer." Fail the disclosure, and
§ 1101.0045(b) says the person *is* engaging in real estate brokerage.

**The shape of the chapter.** Chapter 1101 is organized into seventeen subchapters, lettered A
through Q, and the organization is not arbitrary — it tracks the life of the regulatory relationship.
Subchapter A states general provisions. Subchapters B through E build the agency: the Commission
itself, its staff, its powers, and its complaint machinery. Subchapter F creates the Broker-Lawyer
Committee that drafts contract forms. Subchapter G governs the schools that teach you. Subchapters H
through K cover getting and keeping credentials: license requirements, examinations, renewal, and the
separate certificate for easement and right-of-way agents. Subchapter L is the practice subchapter —
what you actually do in a transaction. Subchapter M is the consumer's safety net. Subchapters N
through P are enforcement: prohibited practices and discipline, administrative penalties, and other
penalties. Subchapter Q closes with liability rules. The contract file should identify which provision controls the particular decision.

One final structural provision belongs here. Section 1101.006 subjects the Commission to the Texas
Sunset Act, chapter 325 of the Government Code, and provides that unless continued in existence, "the
commission is abolished and this chapter and Chapter 1102 of this code and Chapter 221, Property
Code, expire September 1, 2033." The Act is not permanent furniture; the Legislature re-examines it
periodically, which is why what you learn now is best learned as a structure you can re-read rather
than a list you memorize once.

### Adapted teaching: Practice duties that arise while a contract is negotiated

**Subchapter L — Practice by License Holder (§§ 1101.551–1101.563).** This is the subchapter about
what you actually do, and the one you will consult most often in your first year. Section 1101.551
supplies two definitions: an **intermediary** is "a broker who is employed to negotiate a transaction
between the parties to a transaction and for that purpose may act as an agent of the parties," and a
**party** is a prospective buyer, seller, landlord or tenant or an authorized representative — a term
that expressly "does not include a license holder who represents a party."

Section 1101.552(a) requires a resident broker to "maintain a fixed office in this state," designated
on the broker's license, with a corrected license due within 10 days of moving; subsection (d) exempts
a nonresident broker. Subsection (e) requires every license holder to keep current contact
information on file with the Commission.

Section 1101.555 states a duty every buyer's agent must build into a routine: "When an offer to
purchase real estate in this state is signed, a license holder shall advise each buyer, in writing,
that the buyer should: (1) have the abstract covering the real estate that is the subject of the
contract examined by an attorney chosen by the buyer; or (2) be provided with or obtain a title
insurance policy." The consequence of skipping it appears twice: it is
a disciplinary ground under § 1101.652(b)(29), and under § 1101.806(d) a license holder who fails to
give that advice "may not receive payment of or recover any commission agreed to be paid on the sale."
Section 1101.556 runs the other way, relieving a license holder of any duty to inquire about or
disclose whether an occupant had or may have had AIDS, an HIV-related illness or an HIV infection, or
whether "a death occurred on a property by natural causes, suicide, or accident unrelated to the
condition of the property."

Section 1101.557(a) states the agency baseline: "A broker who represents a party in a real estate
transaction or who lists real estate for sale under an exclusive agreement for a party is that party's
agent." Subsection (b) requires such a broker to inform the party of material information received,
"including the receipt of an offer," and to "at a minimum, answer the party's questions and present
any offer to or from the party."

Section 1101.558 governs disclosure of representation. Subsection (b) requires a license holder who
represents a party to disclose that representation "orally or in writing … at the time of the license
holder's first contact with" another party or another party's license holder. Subsection (b-1)
requires more at the next stage: "At the time of a license holder's first substantive communication
with a party relating to a proposed transaction regarding specific real property," the license holder
must give written notice in at least a 10-point font describing how a broker can represent a party
"including as an intermediary," the duties owed to a represented party, the obligations owed to an
unrepresented one, and the license holder's and broker's identifying and contact information.
Subsection (c) excuses that notice for a residential lease "for less than one year" where a sale is
not contemplated, for a meeting with a party known to be represented, and for a communication at an
open house about that property.

Sections 1101.559 through 1101.561 handle the intermediary. A broker may act as intermediary if the
broker "obtains written consent from each party" and the consent "states the source of any expected
compensation to the broker" (§ 1101.559(a)); a listing or buyer representation agreement suffices as
consent if it "specifies in conspicuous bold or underlined print the conduct that is prohibited under
Section 1101.651(d)" (§ 1101.559(b)); and an intermediary "shall act fairly and impartially"
(§ 1101.559(c)). Under § 1101.560 the broker may appoint one associated license holder to each party,
but only if the written consent authorizes appointments and the broker gives written notice to all
parties; an appointed license holder "may provide opinions and advice during negotiations to the
party to whom the license holder is appointed." Section 1101.561(a) provides that intermediary duties
"supersede the duties of a license holder established under any other law, including common law," and
subsection (b) provides that a broker "must agree to act as an intermediary" if the broker agrees to
represent both sides.

Two sections added by the 89th Legislature and effective January 1, 2026 changed how a Texas license
holder starts work with a buyer, and they are now in force. Section 1101.562 permits a broker to show
property "without representing the party" only if the broker has not agreed to represent the party,
is not otherwise acting as the party's agent, "does not provide to the party opinions or advice
regarding the real property or real estate transactions in general," and "does not perform any other
act of real estate brokerage for the party." Before showing, the broker must either disclose
representation of the owner under § 1101.558(b) or give the written notice described in
§ 1101.558(b-1). Subsection (c) preserves a narrow allowance: the broker "may confirm information to a
party regarding the size, price, and terms of the real property available for sale or lease."

Section 1101.563 then requires a written agreement. Subsection (a) defines **residential real
property** as a single-family house; a duplex, triplex or quadraplex; or a unit held under a
condominium or cooperative system. Subsection (b) is the operative command: "A license holder who
performs any act of real estate brokerage for a prospective buyer of residential real property must
enter into a written agreement with the prospective buyer before: (1) showing any residential real
property to the prospective buyer; or (2) if no residential real property will be shown, presenting an
offer to purchase residential real property on behalf of the prospective buyer." Subsection (c)
prescribes the contents: the services to be provided; the termination date; whether the agreement is
exclusive or non-exclusive; whether the license holder represents the buyer as agent or does not,
where the only act is showing under § 1101.562; "the amount or rate of compensation the broker will
receive and how this amount will be determined"; and a disclosure "in conspicuous language that broker
compensation is not set by law and is fully negotiable." Subsection (e) constrains a showing-only
agreement: it "may not: (1) be an exclusive agreement with the license holder; or (2) state a
termination date more than fourteen days from the date the agreement is entered into." Failing to
enter the required agreement is its own disciplinary ground under § 1101.652(b)(34).


The most useful regulatory reading method is to mark the actor, verb, object, and timing. Section 1101.555 directs a license holder to advise each buyer in writing when an offer is signed. It does not say to wait until a title dispute develops. Section 1101.563 requires the applicable buyer agreement before the specified brokerage activity. It does not say the agent may cure missing paperwork merely by securing a signature at closing. Section 1101.557 addresses material information and offers. Its obligation belongs in the active negotiation process.

In a worked file, an agent emails a residential offer for a prospective buyer who has never toured a property. The agent believes the written buyer agreement can wait because no showing occurred. That overlooks the alternative trigger in § 1101.563(b): when no residential property will be shown, the agreement must precede presenting an offer on the buyer’s behalf. The correction is a workflow that checks the statute before the triggering act, not a note that the purchaser eventually signed a representation agreement.

## T01U3 — Unauthorized Practice of Law

The drafting boundary is easiest to see when a client asks for a result instead of a fact. “Put $7,500 in the earnest-money blank” supplies an informational item. “Write something that lets me terminate if my investment returns fall” asks for language defining legal rights. A license holder should identify the requested business outcome and connect the client with an attorney for the legal instrument. Rewording a clause found online is still drafting or recommending legal language.

[Occupations Code § 1101.654](https://tcss.legis.texas.gov/resources/OC/htm/OC.1101.htm#1101.654) and [22 TAC § 537.11(b)–(f)](https://www.trec.texas.gov/agency-information/rules-and-laws/trec-rules) govern this boundary. The rule expressly names escalation, appraisal, and contingency clauses among prohibited drafting or recommendation. It allows explanation of informational items or choices while retaining the prohibition on legal advice. It also permits specified conspicuous changes under a principal’s specific written instructions. That limited transcription permission does not turn the principal’s request into authority for the agent to originate the terms.
### Adapted teaching: The affirmative brokerage license and its professional boundaries

A license is a permission with edges. This unit draws both — first the affirmative scope of what a
Texas real estate license authorizes, then the boundaries that mark where the authority stops and
another profession's or another statute's territory begins.

**The affirmative scope.** A license authorizes you to perform, for another person and for
compensation or with the expectation of compensation, the acts listed in § 1101.002(1): to **sell,
exchange, purchase or lease** real estate for another and to **offer** to do so; to **negotiate** a
listing, sale, exchange, purchase or lease; to **list** real estate; to **auction** it; to **deal in
options**, including lease-purchase arrangements; to **aid a person in locating or obtaining** real
estate; to **procure a prospect** or **procure property** to effect a sale, exchange or lease; to
**control the acceptance or deposit of rent** from a resident of a single-family residential unit; to
provide a **written price opinion** meeting the three conditions of clause (xi); and to **advise an
owner about a short sale**. That is the positive content of the license, and it is broader than most
new license holders assume — it covers leasing and rent handling, not only sales.

**The core requirement.** Section 1101.351(a) provides that unless a person holds a license, the
person may not "act as or represent that the person is a broker or sales agent" or "act as a
residential rental locator." Two verbs there, not one: *acting* and *representing*. Telling people you
are an agent when you are not is itself the violation, even if you never touch a transaction.
Section 1101.351(a-1) applies the same rule to business entities, and § 1101.351(b) forbids an
applicant from acting before the license actually issues.

**The sponsorship requirement, stated three ways.** Section 1101.351(c) provides that "[a] licensed
sales agent may not engage or attempt to engage in real estate brokerage unless the sales agent is
sponsored by a licensed broker and is acting for that broker." Section 1101.363(b) says the same
thing from the licensing side: the Commission may issue an inactive sales agent license to a person
who meets every requirement, but "[t]he person may not act as a sales agent unless the person is
sponsored by a licensed broker who has notified the commission." And the Commission's rule says it a
third time in 22 TAC § 535.55(c): "An inactive sales agent may not practice as a licensed sales agent
until sponsored by an active Texas licensed broker." Three sources, one rule. A sales agent acts
*through* a broker, not beside one. Section 1101.803 explains why the law insists: the broker is
"liable to the commission, the public, and the broker's clients" for the sales agent's conduct, so
the state will not let a sales agent operate outside a broker's supervision and liability.

**Compensation boundaries.** A sales agent may not accept compensation for a real estate transaction
from anyone other than the sponsoring broker, or the broker who was sponsoring when the compensation
was earned (§ 1101.651(b)), and may not pay a commission to anyone except through the sponsoring
broker (§ 1101.651(c)). A broker may not compensate an unlicensed person for performing an act of a
broker, with the narrow exception for an out-of-state licensed broker who conducts none of the Texas
negotiations (§ 1101.651(a)). Dividing a fee with an unlicensed person is separately a disciplinary
ground under § 1101.652(b)(11). The recurring temptation this forecloses is the "referral fee" to a
friend who sent you a buyer: unless that friend holds a license, paying them for the referral is not
a gray area.

**Boundary one: the practice of law.** Section 1101.654(a) requires the Commission to suspend or
revoke the license of a non-attorney license holder who, for consideration or in connection with the
license holder's employment, agency or fiduciary relationship, "drafts an instrument, other than a
form described by Section 1101.155, that transfers or otherwise affects an interest in real property,"
or "advises a person regarding the validity or legal sufficiency of an instrument or the validity of
title to real property." Note the word "shall." This is not discretionary discipline. The protected
zone is defined by § 1101.654(b): completing a contract form for the sale, exchange, option or lease
of an interest in real property, incidental to acting as a broker, is not the unauthorized practice of
law if the form was adopted by the Commission for that type of transaction, prepared by a Texas
attorney and approved by that attorney for that type of transaction, or prepared by the property owner
or by an attorney and required by the owner.

So, concretely: you **may** fill in the blanks of a promulgated Commission contract form, choose among
promulgated addenda, and complete an owner-required attorney-prepared form. You **may not** draft a
deed, write a custom special provision that changes legal rights, tell a client whether a title
commitment's exceptions are legally sufficient, or opine on whether a chain of title is good. When the
question is "is this legally valid," the answer is "ask your attorney" — and § 1101.555 requires you
to advise every buyer in writing to have the abstract examined by an attorney of the buyer's choosing
or to obtain a title policy anyway.

**Boundary two: appraisal.** A separate statute, chapter 1103 of the Occupations Code, is cited as the
Texas Appraiser Licensing and Certification Act (§ 1103.001). Section 1103.003(1) defines an
**appraisal**, "regardless of whether prepared for a federally related transaction," as "(A) an opinion
of value; or (B) the act or process of developing an opinion of value." Section 1103.201(a) provides
that "[a] person may not perform an appraisal of real estate unless the person is licensed or
certified as an appraiser under this chapter, registered as a temporary out-of-state appraiser under
this chapter, or acting as an appraiser trainee under the supervision of a supervisory appraiser," and
subsection (b) forbids an unlicensed person from using the titles "certified real estate appraiser" or
"licensed real estate appraiser" or calling their work a "certified appraisal" or "licensed
appraisal."

The carve-out that makes a broker's price opinion lawful is § 1103.004(a)(2), which provides that
chapter 1103 does not apply to "a real estate broker licensed under Chapter 1101 or a sales agent
acting under the authority of a sponsoring broker who provides to another person a written analysis,
opinion, or conclusion relating to the estimated price of real property if the analysis, opinion, or
conclusion: (A) is not referred to as an appraisal; (B) is given in the ordinary course of the
broker's business; and (C) is related to the actual or potential acquisition, disposition,
encumbrance, or management of an interest in real property." That mirrors § 1101.002(1)(A)(xi)
almost word for word, and the two provisions should be read as a matched pair: the same document is
brokerage under chapter 1101 and outside chapter 1103, but only if all three conditions hold.

The condition that trips people is the first. Call your price opinion an appraisal — in the document,
in an email, or out loud to a client — and you have characterized your own work as something you are
not licensed to perform. Do not use the word. Do not let a client's phrasing pull you into it. If a
lender, a court or a party needs an appraisal, they need an appraiser.

**Boundary three: the § 1101.652 prohibitions.** The disciplinary list surveyed in Principles II source unit 2 is also a
list of things a license holder may not do, and it helps to group the selection by the interest each
item protects. *Competence and candor*: you may not act negligently or incompetently ((b)(1)),
misrepresent a known significant defect or fail to disclose one ((b)(3), (b)(4)), make a false promise
likely to influence a person to contract when you cannot or do not intend to keep it ((b)(5)), or
publish a misleading advertisement or one omitting your broker's name ((b)(23)). *Clarity about whose
side you are on*: you may not leave any party unclear about whom you represent ((b)(7)), take
compensation from more than one party without everyone's full knowledge and consent ((b)(8)), act as
broker and undisclosed principal at once ((b)(16)), or take an undisclosed rebate or profit on an
expenditure made for a principal ((b)(13)). *Other people's money*: you must account for and promptly
remit it ((b)(9)), must not commingle it with your own ((b)(10)), must deposit escrow funds with a
title company or in a proper trust account ((b)(30)), and must not disburse them before the
transaction completes or terminates ((b)(31)). *The owner's control of the owner's property*: you may
not put up a sign without written consent ((b)(18)), offer property without the owner's knowledge and
consent ((b)(19)) or on unauthorized terms ((b)(20)), guarantee future resale profits ((b)(17)), or
sell by lottery ((b)(14)) or deceptive practice ((b)(15)). *Other brokers' contracts*: you may not
induce a party to break a contract to substitute a new one ((b)(21)) or negotiate with a party you
know is under another broker's exclusive written agreement ((b)(22)). And you may not discriminate
against an owner, potential buyer, landlord or potential tenant "on the basis of race, color,
religion, sex, disability, familial status, national origin, or ancestry, including directing a
prospective buyer or tenant interested in equivalent properties to a different area" on those bases
((b)(32)).

**What happens if you act without a license.** The consequences stack. Acting as a broker or sales
agent without a license is a Class A misdemeanor (§ 1101.758). The unlicensed person is liable to the
state for a civil penalty "of not less than the amount of money received or more than three times the
amount of money received" (§ 1101.753(a)), and liable to the aggrieved person for the same measure
(§ 1101.754(a)). The Commission may seek an injunction without proving the absence of an adequate
legal remedy (§ 1101.751) and may issue a cease and desist order after notice and hearing
(§ 1101.759). And the unlicensed person cannot sue to be paid: § 1101.806(b) bars an action to
collect compensation for a brokerage act performed in Texas unless the person proves they were a
license holder when the act commenced or an attorney licensed in any state. A licensed person who
knowingly helps that arrangement along is exposed too, under § 1101.652(b)(26) (associating with a
non-license holder expected to act as one) and (b)(27) (aiding, abetting or conspiring to circumvent
the chapter).

**Where the license is not needed.** Balance the picture with § 1101.005 and § 1101.004(b), covered in
Principles II source unit 1. An owner leasing the owner's own property, an on-site apartment manager, a licensed Texas
attorney, an executor selling under a will, a person building homes and selling what they built, a
trustee conducting a foreclosure sale under a power of sale in a deed of trust — none of these needs a
real estate license for that activity. Knowing the exemptions is not a loophole exercise; it is how
you answer the question a friend or a client will eventually ask you, and answering it wrongly in
either direction is a disservice.


Suppose a buyer says, “My brother is a lawyer; he told me orally to waive every inspection protection. Just type a waiver.” The agent still lacks specific written instructions from the principal and should not manufacture a waiver from a conversational summary. The lawyer should communicate the actual language to the client, and the client should give the instruction in writing through the appropriate process. The agent must preserve conspicuous changes and advise that the instrument is binding. The form remains a legal instrument even when a client is impatient.

Suppose instead the seller asks whether an easement is “invalid because nobody used it.” That question concerns legal effect and title. The agent can disclose the recorded easement and the observed lack of use, provide the document to the appropriate reviewers, and help track a contractual objection deadline. The agent cannot turn those facts into a title opinion. Good service consists of timely fact delivery and competent referral; an unsupported legal conclusion can destroy the client’s opportunity to obtain useful advice before the deadline.

## T01U4 — The Broker-Lawyer Committee

Texas separates the drafting of standard contract language from the administrative decision to require a form’s use. The Broker-Lawyer Committee works on language capable of standardization. The Commission adopts forms and rules through its public processes. An item on a committee agenda is not an adopted form. A proposed revision is not effective merely because an agent has seen a draft. Always identify the form number, revision, published effective-use instruction, and transaction category before using it.
### Adapted teaching: Who drafts, who adopts, and why standardized safeguards matter

**Subchapter F — Texas Real Estate Broker-Lawyer Committee (§§ 1101.251–1101.254).** Every Texas
license holder uses forms this committee drafted. Section 1101.252(a) provides that the **Texas Real
Estate Broker-Lawyer Committee** consists of 13 members appointed as follows: "(1) six members
appointed by the commission; (2) six members of the State Bar of Texas appointed by the president of
the state bar; and (3) one public member appointed by the governor." Members serve staggered six-year
terms under § 1101.253. Section 1101.254
states the mandate: the committee "shall draft and revise contract forms that are capable of being
standardized to expedite real estate transactions and minimize controversy," and those forms "must
contain safeguards adequate to protect the principals in the transaction."

Hold three provisions in view together and the Texas forms system snaps into focus. The committee
drafts the forms (§ 1101.254). The Commission may require their use (§ 1101.155(a)) but may not
forbid an owner's own form or an attorney-prepared form the owner requires (§ 1101.155(b)). And a
license holder who fills out an adopted form incidental to acting as a broker "is not engaged in the
unauthorized or illegal practice of law" (§ 1101.654(b)). The committee's half-lawyer composition is
what makes that last protection defensible: attorneys did the drafting, so completing the blanks is
clerical rather than the practice of law.


Under [Occupations Code §§ 1101.252–1101.254](https://tcss.legis.texas.gov/resources/OC/htm/OC.1101.htm#1101.252), the committee includes six Commission appointees, six State Bar members appointed by the Bar president, and one public member appointed by the governor. Its charge is not to produce a contract favorable to whichever brokerage happens to use it. The statute directs standardization that expedites transactions, minimizes controversy, and provides adequate safeguards for principals. The public member’s participation reflects that the instrument is intended to serve people who are not professional negotiators.

The standardized structure helps parties compare different offers. If every broker rearranged earnest money, objections, repairs, casualty, default, and notices, an important change could be hidden by unfamiliar presentation. A familiar form reduces that burden, but no form can make incomplete facts accurate. A blank financing amount is still blank. An omitted addendum is still omitted. An incorrectly named party is still a problem. The committee supplies language; the license holder supplies competent fact gathering and authorized completion; the principals supply their negotiated decisions.

A sample revision review illustrates the process. Your office has an old signed training specimen and a newer form from the Commission’s website. The training specimen remains useful for teaching how a completed file looks, but it should be marked by its actual revision. A live transaction should use the currently authorized form as the rule requires. Compare the changed paragraphs to understand their effects on workflow, then obtain training or attorney advice as appropriate. Do not hide the older date or replace the form number manually. Version control protects the client’s understanding of which language was actually offered and accepted.

The committee’s existence also explains why the special-provisions blank is not a substitute drafting workshop. The form’s standardized clauses were developed through an authorized process. An agent’s improvised paragraph can create undefined conflicts with those clauses, change remedies inadvertently, or omit a necessary timing or notice mechanism. If the parties need a legal arrangement the available forms do not adequately address, they need appropriate counsel before execution. This is a professional boundary, not an invitation to pressure the parties into a standard arrangement that does not fit their goals.

## T01U5 — Use of Promulgated Contract Forms

Start with the property and proposed transaction. An existing one-to-four family resale, an unimproved tract, a condominium resale, a new home, and a farm or ranch do not present identical facts. Identify whether there is a mandatory form for the transaction. Then evaluate whether a stated exception applies. Only after selecting the correct path should you begin filling blanks. Starting with the last form you used and changing its title is not form selection.

The [TREC contract library](https://www.trec.texas.gov/agency-information/contracts) is the source for current published forms; [22 TAC § 537.11(a), (g), and (h)](https://www.trec.texas.gov/agency-information/rules-and-laws/trec-rules) controls authorized use and reproduction. Keep the published file’s identity with the executed document. Broker software may help complete a form, but the final copy must satisfy the rule’s text-and-format requirements. A reader should be able to compare the executed copy with the Commission’s published version and see every conspicuous party-directed change.
### Adapted teaching: Choosing the authorized form and preserving the published format

**Writing the offer: the § 537.11 boundary.** Now you sit down to write. Rule 537.11 opens with the
mandatory-form rule: "When negotiating contracts binding the sale, exchange, option, or lease of any
interest in real property, a license holder shall use only those contract forms approved for
mandatory use by the Commission for that type of transaction with the following exceptions:" and
then lists them. The exceptions are "transactions in which the license holder is functioning solely
as a principal, not as an agent;" "transactions in which an agency of the United States government
requires a different form to be used;" "transactions for which a contract form has been prepared by
the property owner or prepared by an attorney and required by a property owner;" and "transactions
for which no contract form has been approved for mandatory use by the Commission," in which case the
license holder may use a form "prepared by an attorney licensed by this state, or a trade
association in consultation with an attorney licensed by this state" carrying the identifying
content the rule specifies, or a form "prepared by the Texas Real Estate Broker-Lawyer Committee and
approved by the Commission for voluntary use by license holders."

Learn the exceptions precisely, because each has a limit built into it. The principal exception is
available only when you are "functioning solely as a principal, not as an agent". The owner-form
exception requires that the form be "prepared by the property owner or prepared by an attorney and
required by a property owner" — a builder's own contract qualifies; a form your buyer downloaded
does not. And the attorney-or-trade-association form is available only where the Commission has
approved *no* mandatory form for that type of transaction, and the form must contain the attorney's
or association's name, the name of the broker or trade association it was prepared for, a statement
of the transaction type approved, "any restrictions on the use of the form", and, if applicable, "an
explanation of how the form changes the rights, obligations, or remedies of a party under a contract
form approved for mandatory use by the Commission and a statement that license holders may not
practice law or give legal advice".

The Act backs this with § 1101.652(a-1)(3), which makes it a ground for discipline that a license
holder "fails to use a contract form required by the commission under Section 1101.155", and with
§ 1101.155(b), which limits the Commission in the other direction: it "may not prohibit a license
holder from using for the sale, exchange, option, or lease of an interest in real property a
contract form that is: (1) prepared by the property owner; or (2) prepared by an attorney and
required by the property owner."

Rule 537.11 also governs how the form may be reproduced: approved forms "may be reproduced,
including through use of a software application, provided that the text and format of the form,
including the sizing, spacing, and pagination, is identical to the Commission's published version,"
except that "the business name or logo of a broker, trade association, or other organization may
appear outside the form's border" and "a form may be scaled to accommodate viewing on smaller
screens, including mobile devices, as long as the final executed copy of the form otherwise complies
with this subsection." A brokerage that rebuilds the One to Four Family Residential Contract in its
own template engine with different pagination has broken this rule even if every word is right.

### Adapted teaching: Authorized completion, prohibited drafting, and specific written instructions

**What you may not do while filling it in.** Rule 537.11 states a list of prohibitions. A license
holder may not "practice law"; may not "directly or indirectly offer, give or attempt to give legal
advice"; may not "give advice or opinions as to the legal effect of any contract forms or other such
instruments which may affect the title to real estate"; may not "give opinions concerning the status
or validity of title to real estate"; may not "draft or recommend language to be included in a
contract form defining or affecting the rights, obligations, or remedies of the principals of a real
estate transaction, including escalation, appraisal, or contingency clauses"; may not "add
informational items to a form approved by the Commission if the Commission has approved another form
for mandatory use for that purpose"; may not "attempt to prevent or in any manner whatsoever
discourage any principal to a real estate transaction from employing an attorney"; and may not
"obtain legal advice from an attorney, directly or indirectly, for a principal in a real estate
transaction in which the license holder is acting as an agent."

The fifth of those is the one that catches competent, well-meaning agents in a competitive market.
Escalation clauses, appraisal-gap clauses and bespoke contingency language are named in the rule by
name. Drafting them is prohibited, and so is recommending the language. If your buyer wants an
escalation clause, the answer is that they need a lawyer to draft it, and you say so without
discouraging them from getting one.

The sixth is quieter and just as dangerous: you may not add informational items to a promulgated
form where the Commission has promulgated an addendum for that purpose. If there is a form for the
subject, use the form.

The rule then tells you what you *may* do. It "does not: limit a license holder's fiduciary
obligation to disclose to the license holder's principals all pertinent facts that are within the
knowledge of the license holder, including such facts which might affect the status of or title to
real estate;" and it does not "prevent the license holder from explaining to the license holder's
principals the meaning of informational items or choices in a contract form, as long as the license
holder does not practice law or give legal advice". So explaining what a blank is for is permitted;
telling your buyer what the paragraph will do to their legal position is not.

And it identifies two acts that are affirmatively not the practice of law: "add informational items
to a contract form authorized for use by this section" and, "if specifically instructed in writing
by a principal, add language to or strike language from a contract form, as long as any change is
made conspicuous, including underlining additions, striking through deletions, or employing some
other method which clearly indicates the change being made." Every element of that second permission
matters. The instruction must come from a principal, it must be specific, it must be in writing, and
the change must be conspicuous. A principal’s specific written instruction and a conspicuous deletion meet those stated mechanics, but do not authorize the license holder to recommend the deletion or advise on its legal effect. Your own decision to quietly
delete a sentence does not.

Finally, two affirmative advisory duties. "When a transaction involves unusual matters that should
be reviewed by an attorney before an instrument is executed, or if the instrument must be
acknowledged and filed of record, the license holder shall advise the license holder's principals to
consult an attorney before executing the instrument." And: "A license holder shall advise the
license holder's principals that the instrument they are about to execute is binding on them."

Section 1101.654, puts a licence-ending penalty behind the same line — the
Commission "shall", not may, suspend or revoke for drafting or advising on legal sufficiency — and
its subsection (b) protects the ordinary act of completing an approved or attorney-prepared form.
Completing authorized informational items remains subject to competence, accuracy, and all the rule’s limits. Drafting rights or remedies exceeds that permission.

**Earnest money: the handling, in order.** The buyer's earnest money is trust money, and 22 TAC
§ 535.146 governs what happens to it from the moment it reaches a hand. Four steps.

*Receipt.* "A sales agent shall not maintain a trust account. Any trust money received by a sales
agent must be immediately delivered to the sales agent's sponsoring broker." Not deposited by you.
Not held in your desk over the weekend. Immediately delivered to your broker.

*Deposit.* Trust money accepted by a broker "is held in a fiduciary capacity and must be maintained
in a designated trust account maintained by the broker or delivered to an escrow agent authorized in
Texas in accordance with the agreement of the principals of the transaction." The deadline is the
number to memorise: unless a different time "is expressly agreed upon in writing by the principals
to the transaction," deposit or delivery must occur "not later than the close of business of the
second working day after the date the broker receives the trust money." Second working day, close of
business, counted from receipt *by the broker*.

*Custody.* The broker may not commingle trust money with the broker's personal or other non-trust
money, or hold it in a personal account or any kind of business account, and "placing trust money in
a broker's personal or operating account" is prima facie evidence of commingling. Money the broker
becomes entitled to — an earned commission — must leave the trust account "not later the 30th day
after the date the broker acquires ownership of the money." If a trust-money check is dishonoured,
"the broker shall immediately notify all parties to the transaction in writing."

*Disbursement.* Money leaves the account only "in accordance with the agreement under which the
money was received." On a written demand for payment by any or all parties, the broker must pay the
party or parties entitled "not later than the 30th day after the date the demand is made," and the
same thirty-day limit runs from receipt of a subsequent written agreement of all parties to a
different disbursement. In both cases "The broker must immediately notify all parties in writing of
any disbursement of trust money". And where entitlement genuinely cannot be determined, the rule
provides the exit: "If the broker cannot reasonably determine to which party or parties the trust
money should be paid, the broker may pay the trust money into the registry of a court and interplead
the parties."

Documentation of the account is kept "for four years from the date the document is received or
created by the broker." The Act makes the same failures disciplinary grounds — § 1101.652(b)(9) for
failing to account for or remit money belonging to another, (b)(10) for commingling, (b)(30) for
failing to deposit within a reasonable time, and (b)(31) for disbursing before the transaction is
completed or terminated — and Law of Agency Module 10 teaches them.


A disciplined completion review follows the contract’s operation. First identify parties and the property, including the source of the legal description and any excluded or reserved interests. Next reconcile the sales price with its cash and financing components. Then identify money-delivery obligations, the escrow recipient, and supporting financing addenda. Continue through title, survey, disclosures, condition, closing, possession, expenses, notices, and signatures. Check incorporated addenda against the documents actually attached and signed. A document list is not proof that the referenced document is present.

Consider three property files. A builder requires its attorney-prepared contract for a home it owns. That is a possible owner-required exception; the agent still must recommend counsel for unusual provisions and cannot rewrite the document. A resale buyer downloads a commercial form because it has a short inspection paragraph. That preference does not itself establish an exception for a residential transaction with an applicable mandatory form. A lease transaction has no mandatory TREC lease for its actual duration and type. The permitted alternative-form pathway still requires the rule’s authorship, identification, transaction approval, and restriction information; the absence of a mandatory form is not permission to draft a lease from scratch.

Every form choice should leave a short factual record in the brokerage file: the transaction category, current form used, required addenda, and the factual basis for any exception. This is a practical documentation method, not an extra statutory form. It helps a supervising broker review the decision while the client can still benefit from correction. It also exposes hidden assumptions, such as treating a home in a condominium regime as an ordinary fee-simple subdivision lot.

Review signatures last, but prepare for them first. The person whose name appears as seller may act through an entity representative, trustee, personal representative, or attorney-in-fact. Authority and title questions should be resolved through the appropriate reviewers before execution. A signature block should not silently transform an individual into an entity’s authorized manager. An agent should not certify capacity merely because the person controls the email account. A complete contract file identifies the actual party and the capacity in which a human signs.

Use clear client explanations within the rule. “This blank records the amount you and the seller agree will be deposited with the escrow agent” explains an informational item. “This deposit guarantees that your maximum liability can never exceed that amount” states a legal consequence that may be false and exceeds authorized explanation. The difference is particularly important under pressure: a client may hear certainty in a casual statement and rely on it when selecting terms.

Version discipline continues after acceptance. Do not edit an accepted PDF and call the altered file the executed contract. Preserve the original and document a later negotiated change by the appropriate signed amendment. Do not replace a signed addendum with an unsigned revised one. Do not remove conspicuous deletions because the document would look cleaner. Integrity means the file tells a future reader what was actually agreed, by whom, and when.

### Form selection as a documented decision

Consider a buyer purchasing an existing detached residence on several acres. Acreage alone does not answer every form-selection question. Identify the property’s actual character, use, improvements, rights, and proposed transaction. A residential resale, farm and ranch sale, unimproved property sale, condominium resale, and builder transaction can require different documents. Review the Commission’s current promulgated inventory and the circumstances covered by each form. The title at the top of a familiar template is not enough.

Now suppose the buyer’s attorney supplies a contract for the specific transaction. The exception in § 537.11 must be evaluated on its stated conditions. It is not a general exception permitting the agent to reuse that attorney’s document for every later buyer. Record the source and the transaction for which the document was prepared. If a later transaction differs, return to the form-selection rule rather than treating the old file as a permanent private form library.

An owner may also present a document and insist that the agent add several legal protections. The fact that the owner supplied the document does not authorize the license holder to draft new rights, remedies, or legal descriptions. Apply the permission to use the instrument separately from the prohibition on legal drafting and advice. The agent can accurately communicate the principal’s business objectives to counsel while declining to invent the legal language.

For an authorized form, informational accuracy still requires care. A blank for an amount needs the parties’ agreed amount, not the agent’s estimate of what seems typical. A checkbox needs the actual selection, not the default setting retained from the previous transaction. An attachment list should identify the addenda actually included and agreed to. A legal description should come from an appropriate reliable source and be handled within the rule’s limits; a mailing address should not silently substitute for the required property identification.

The operational record should therefore explain three things: why the form fits the transaction, why any exception is being used, and where the factual entries came from. This is a practical method of applying [22 TAC § 537.11](https://www.trec.texas.gov/agency-information/rules-and-laws/trec-rules). It does not create additional authority for the agent. If a necessary term cannot be expressed through permitted completion or an applicable authorized addendum, obtain legal drafting before asking the parties to sign.

## T01U6 — Deceptive Trade Practices Consumer Protection Act (DTPA)

A contract explanation can become a factual representation about a consumer’s rights. Saying “you can cancel whenever you want” is not harmless shorthand if the agreement gives only a conditional, time-limited right. Saying “the seller disclosed everything, so you have no claim” combines an unsupported factual assertion with legal advice. The contract course therefore treats consumer protection as part of accurate communication, not merely as litigation after a sale.

Read the [Business & Commerce Code Chapter 17](https://tcss.legis.texas.gov/resources/BC/htm/BC.17.htm) provisions cited below in their current context. The operative inquiry includes consumer status, the particular cause of action, reliance where required, producing cause, damages, and any exemption or defense. A license is not universal immunity; a dissatisfied consumer is not automatically entitled to every remedy.
### Adapted teaching: Consumer status and the specific misleading practices relevant to contracts

Business & Commerce Code § 17.41 states the short title: "This subchapter may be cited as the
Deceptive Trade Practices-Consumer Protection Act." It runs from § 17.41 through § 17.63.

**The interpretive instruction comes first, because it colours everything else.** Section 17.44(a)
directs that the subchapter "shall be liberally construed and applied to promote its underlying
purposes, which are to protect consumers against false, misleading, and deceptive business
practices, unconscionable actions, and breaches of warranty and to provide efficient and economical
procedures to secure such protection." Where a court must choose between two readings, the statute
says which way to lean. Subsection (b) adds one cross-reference: "Chapter 27, Property Code, prevails
over this subchapter to the extent of any conflict." Chapter 27 is the residential construction
liability chapter. **Property Code Chapter 27 was not held for this course**, so this module states
nothing about its content; it says only that § 17.44(b) gives Chapter 27 priority, which makes a
construction-defect claim in a residence a matter for a lawyer.

**Who is a "consumer," and why you can be sued by someone who never paid you a dime.** Standing
under the DTPA runs through one definition. Section 17.45(4): "Consumer" means "an individual,
partnership, corporation, this state, or a subdivision or agency of this state who seeks or acquires
by purchase or lease, any goods or services, except that the term does not include a business
consumer that has assets of $25 million or more, or that is owned or controlled by a corporation or
entity with assets of $25 million or more."

Every clause does work.

*"Seeks or acquires."* No completed purchase is needed, so a buyer who walks away because of what you
said is still a consumer.

*"By purchase or lease."* Your leasing and property-management practice sits inside the Act.

*"Any goods or services."* Section 17.45(1) defines "Goods" as "tangible chattels or real property
purchased or leased for use," so real property is expressly a good, and § 17.45(2) defines "Services"
as "work, labor, or service purchased or leased for use, including services furnished in connection
with the sale or repair of goods," so brokerage is a service.

*The $25 million exclusion.* A "business consumer," defined at § 17.45(10) as "an individual,
partnership, or corporation who seeks or acquires by purchase or lease, any goods or services for
commercial or business use," loses consumer status at $25 million in assets, or when "owned or
controlled by a corporation or entity with assets of $25 million or more." An asset test, not a
revenue test.

Now the point students most often miss. **Read the definition again and find the word "privity." It
is not there.** Section 17.45(4) asks who sought or acquired goods or services, not from whom, and it
does not require that the consumer bought anything from you, paid you, or had a contract with you.
That is why a buyer represented by a different brokerage, who never signed anything with you, can sue
*you*, the listing agent: the buyer sought to acquire real property, and you engaged in a practice
enumerated in § 17.46(b) that was a producing cause of the buyer's economic damages.

**The four doors into a DTPA action.** Section 17.50(a) provides that "[a] consumer may maintain an
action where any of the following constitute a producing cause of economic damages or damages for
mental anguish," and then lists four. Subdivision (1) is the laundry-list door: "the use or
employment by any person of a false, misleading, or deceptive act or practice that is: (A)
specifically enumerated in a subdivision of Subsection (b) of Section 17.46 of this subchapter; and
(B) relied on by a consumer to the consumer's detriment." Subdivision (2) is breach of an express or
implied warranty. Subdivision (3) is "any unconscionable action or course of action by any person."
Subdivision (4) is a violation of Chapter 541 of the Insurance Code, which is not your subject.

Three features of subdivision (1) matter. It requires an act "specifically enumerated" in a
subdivision of § 17.46(b) — a general sense that you behaved badly is not enough. It requires
detrimental reliance. And it applies to the act of "any person," which § 17.45(3) defines as "an
individual, partnership, corporation, association, or other group, however organized." Section
17.46(d) locks exclusivity down from the other direction: the term false, misleading, or deceptive
acts or practices "is limited to the acts enumerated in specific subdivisions of Subsection (b) of
this section." For a private consumer suit the laundry list is a closed list.

**The laundry list, applied to real estate.** Section 17.46(b) opens by providing that, "Except as
provided in Subsection (d) of this section," the term false, misleading, or deceptive acts or
practices "includes, but is not limited to, the following acts". Most of the thirty-four subdivisions
that follow — odometer tampering, kosher labelling, pyramid schemes — never touch your practice.
These will.

*§ 17.46(b)(5) — "representing that goods or services have sponsorship, approval, characteristics,
ingredients, uses, benefits, or quantities which they do not have or that a person has a
sponsorship, approval, status, affiliation, or connection which the person does not."* The workhorse
subdivision in Texas real estate litigation, and the one to memorise. "Characteristics" and
"quantities" are the operative words: a listing that states "2,860 sq. ft." when the measurement says
2,455, or "not in a flood zone" when the tract sits partly in a mapped floodplain. Copying a seller’s figure does not establish its accuracy; any applicable statutory defense must be evaluated on its own conditions.

*§ 17.46(b)(7) — "representing that goods or services are of a particular standard, quality, or
grade, or that goods are of a particular style or model, if they are of another."* The listing
advertising a "new HVAC system" when only the compressor was replaced. Quality claims are checkable.

*§ 17.46(b)(12) — "representing that an agreement confers or involves rights, remedies, or
obligations which it does not have or involve, or which are prohibited by law."* Tell a buyer that
the option period "lets you get out any time before closing," or a seller that a listing agreement
"can be cancelled by either side on a week's notice" when it cannot, and you have represented that an
agreement confers rights it does not involve — which is how careless explanations of contract forms
become a DTPA claim.

*§ 17.46(b)(13) — "knowingly making false or misleading statements of fact concerning the need for
parts, replacement, or repair service."* Note the "knowingly." A license holder who steers a seller
into an unnecessary $14,000 foundation job with a preferred contractor, knowing the statements about
the need are false, is inside this subdivision.

*§ 17.46(b)(14) — "misrepresenting the authority of a salesman, representative or agent to negotiate
the final terms of a consumer transaction."* Telling a buyer "I can accept that counter for the
seller right now" without that authority violates subdivision (14) — a distinctively agency-law
deceptive practice.

*§ 17.46(b)(20) — "representing that a guaranty or warranty confers or involves rights or remedies
which it does not have or involve, provided, however, that nothing in this subchapter shall be
construed to expand the implied warranty of merchantability as defined in Sections 2.314 through
2.318 and Sections 2A.212 through 2A.216 to involve obligations in excess of those which are
appropriate to the goods."* "The warranty covers the whole HVAC system, so you don't need to worry
about the age" is a representation about what a residential service contract confers; if it excludes
pre-existing conditions or caps the payout, subdivision (20) is engaged.

*§ 17.46(b)(24) — "failing to disclose information concerning goods or services which was known at
the time of the transaction if such failure to disclose such information was intended to induce the
consumer into a transaction into which the consumer would not have entered had the information been
disclosed."* The silence subdivision, and the one to think about hardest: it is a provision specifically addressing nondisclosure. Both express conditions must be met — the information must
have been "known at the time of the transaction," and the failure to disclose must have been
"intended to induce the consumer into a transaction into which the consumer would not have entered
had the information been disclosed" — so a fact you forgot, or never knew, is outside it. And
subdivision (24) is carved out of *both* statutory exemptions that would otherwise protect you.

You list a house in Friendswood, and in the file from a prior listing sits an inspection report
noting active seepage at the north foundation wall. The seller's disclosure notice says no known
water penetration; it states the seller's knowledge and belief, so the seller’s knowledge must be evaluated independently. That does not answer the question about you. If a fact-finder concludes you kept
quiet to get the deal closed, subdivision (24) is satisfied — and § 1101.652(b)(4) independently
makes that silence a disciplinary ground.

**Unconscionable action or course of action.** The second major door, § 17.50(a)(3), does not depend
on the laundry list at all. It reaches "any unconscionable action or course of action by any
person." Section 17.45(5) defines an unconscionable action or course of action as "an act or
practice which, to a consumer's detriment, takes advantage of the lack of knowledge, ability,
experience, or capacity of the consumer to a grossly unfair degree."

Every word is doing work. There must be harm — "to a consumer's detriment." The practice must "take
advantage of" one of four vulnerabilities: lack of knowledge, ability, experience or capacity. And it
must do so "to a grossly unfair degree," a bar ordinary sharp dealing does not clear. But notice what
the definition does *not* require: no false statement, no state of mind, no reliance. A course of
conduct can be unconscionable though no statement in it was false — the recently bereaved widow
persuaded to list her homestead well under market so the license holder's investor client can buy it,
with no comparative market analysis and no explanation of what a listing price means.

**Waivers require every statutory condition.** Section 17.42(a): "Any waiver by a consumer of
the provisions of this subchapter is contrary to public policy and is unenforceable and void;
provided, however, that a waiver is valid and enforceable if: (1) the waiver is in writing and is
signed by the consumer; (2) the consumer is not in a significantly disparate bargaining position;
and (3) the consumer is represented by legal counsel in seeking or acquiring the goods or services."
All three subsection (a) conditions must be met. Section 17.42 also prescribes the form and prominence of the waiver and independence of counsel; satisfying these three conditions alone is not the complete waiver analysis.

**The DTPA does not replace other law; it stacks on it.** Section 17.43 provides that "[t]he
provisions of this subchapter are not exclusive" and that its remedies "are in addition to any other
procedures or remedies provided for in any other law." It then draws a line students blur: "A
violation of a provision of law other than this subchapter is not in and of itself a violation of
this subchapter." Breaking a TREC rule is not automatically a DTPA violation — though the same
conduct can violate TRELA and the DTPA at once, through two independent routes.

**The notice letter, and what you do the day it arrives.** Section 17.505(a) sets a condition
precedent: "As a prerequisite to filing a suit seeking damages under Subdivision (1) of Subsection
(b) of Section 17.50 of this subchapter against any person, a consumer shall give written notice to
the person at least 60 days before filing the suit advising the person in reasonable detail of the
consumer's specific complaint and the amount of economic damages, damages for mental anguish, and
expenses, including attorneys' fees, if any, reasonably incurred by the consumer in asserting the
claim against the defendant. During the 60-day period a written request to inspect, in a reasonable
manner and at a reasonable time and place, the goods that are the subject of the consumer's action
or claim may be presented to the consumer."

Sixty days, written, itemised. That letter starts the two clocks that matter, both running from
receipt: the sixty days in § 17.505(a) and the thirty-day tender window in § 17.506(d).

What you do the day it lands is practice, not law, and this course states no authority for it. Note
the date of receipt. Coordinate the response with the broker, insurer, and counsel; preserve a complete and accurate record. Notify your sponsoring broker and your E&O carrier the same day, because
late notice can forfeit coverage. Preserve the whole file, alter nothing, and get the letter to
counsel within days.

Two situations excuse the notice. Section 17.505(b): "If the giving of 60 days' written notice is
rendered impracticable by reason of the necessity of filing suit in order to prevent the expiration
of the statute of limitations or if the consumer's claim is asserted by way of counterclaim, the
notice provided for in Subsection (a) of this section is not required, but the tender provided for by
Subsection (d), Section 17.506 of this subchapter may be made within 60 days after service of the
suit or counterclaim." A consumer racing limitations, or one asserting a counterclaim, may skip the
letter — and you get sixty days from service to tender.

### Adapted teaching: The brokerage and transaction exemptions require their stated conditions

**The exemptions in § 17.49, beginning with the one written for you.** Section 17.49(i):
"Nothing in this subchapter shall apply to a claim against a person licensed as a broker or
salesperson under Chapter 1101, Occupations Code, arising from an act or omission by the person
while acting as a broker or salesperson." Then, immediately: "This exemption does not apply to: (1)
an express misrepresentation of a material fact that cannot be characterized as advice, judgment, or
opinion; (2) a failure to disclose information in violation of Section 17.46(b)(24); or (3) an
unconscionable action or course of action that cannot be characterized as advice, judgment, or
opinion."

The general rule genuinely does exempt you. Note the wording: "broker or salesperson," the older term
TRELA has replaced with "sales agent," and the exemption is tied to an act done "while acting as"
one, so an act outside your brokerage capacity — flipping a house in your own name — is not within it
at all. The carve-outs are where every real case lives.

*Carve-out (1): "an express misrepresentation of a material fact that cannot be characterized as
advice, judgment, or opinion."* Four conditions in one clause: *express*, so a claim built on
implication is outside it; a *misrepresentation*, so a true statement is outside it; about a
*material fact*; and a statement that *cannot be characterized as advice, judgment, or opinion* —
which is why experienced license holders speak as they do. "In my opinion this neighbourhood has held
its value well" is opinion. "This house is 2,900 square feet" is a fact. "I think the roof looks to be
in good shape" is judgment. "The roof was replaced in 2021" is a fact. A court reads the substance,
so dressing a hard factual claim in the words "I think" will not convert it into an opinion.

*Carve-out (2): "a failure to disclose information in violation of Section 17.46(b)(24)."* Silence.
Every other subdivision must fight through carve-out (1) or (3); subdivision (24) is named by number
and walks straight through the exemption.

*Carve-out (3): "an unconscionable action or course of action that cannot be characterized as
advice, judgment, or opinion."* This is why § 17.45(5) matters so much: unconscionability requires no
false statement at all, and survives the exemption whenever the conduct cannot be recharacterised as
advice or opinion.

**The professional services exemption, § 17.49(c).** Alongside it sits a broader exemption for a
claim based on the rendering of a professional service "the essence of which is the providing of
advice, judgment, opinion, or similar professional skill." Its first three carve-outs match
§ 17.49(i) word for word; it adds two, for breach of an express warranty that cannot be characterised
as advice, judgment or opinion, and for a violation of § 17.46(b)(26).

**The two transaction-size exemptions, with their exact thresholds.** Section 17.49(f): "Nothing in
the subchapter shall apply to a claim arising out of a written contract if: (1) the contract relates
to a transaction, a project, or a set of transactions related to the same project involving total
consideration by the consumer of more than $100,000; (2) in negotiating the contract the consumer is
represented by legal counsel who is not directly or indirectly identified, suggested, or selected by
the defendant or an agent of the defendant; and (3) the contract does not involve the consumer's
residence."

All three conditions, joined by "and." More than $100,000 — not $100,000 or more. Section 17.45(12)
defines "Residence" as a building "(A) that is a single-family house, duplex, triplex, or quadruplex
or a unit in a multiunit residential structure in which title to the individual units is transferred
to the owners under a condominium or cooperative system; and (B) that is occupied or to be occupied
as the consumer's residence" — both the building type *and* the occupancy. A $640,000 house a family
will live in is over $100,000 but involves the consumer's residence, so condition (3) fails, and most
residential buyers have no lawyer, so condition (2) fails too. **The § 17.49(f) exemption almost
never protects a residential license holder.**

Section 17.49(g) is larger and simpler: "Nothing in this subchapter shall apply to a cause of action
arising from a transaction, a project, or a set of transactions relating to the same project,
involving total consideration by the consumer of more than $500,000, other than a cause of action
involving a consumer's residence." No written-contract or counsel requirement, but again the
residence exception: a $2.4 million industrial building sale is outside the DTPA; a $2.4 million
house the buyer will occupy is not. Commit both numbers exactly: **$100,000** at § 17.49(f),
**$500,000** at § 17.49(g).


In a worked exchange, an agent forwards the seller’s statement that all additions were permitted. Later the agent receives a city email identifying a permit discrepancy. Repeating the original statement without explaining the conflict can mislead the buyer. The practical correction is to transmit the actual information, identify its source and date, recommend appropriate investigation, and preserve the record. Whether a particular claim satisfies § 17.50 or an exception to § 17.49 remains a legal determination. The agent can improve the facts without promising the outcome of litigation.


### Statute reading exercise: Separate the form mandate from the drafting prohibition

Read Occupations Code §§ 1101.155 and 1101.654 together with 22 TAC § 537.11(a)–(f). On paper make four rows: owner-prepared form, owner-required attorney form, transaction with no mandatory form, and an agent-created contingency. For each identify what fact must be established, whether the rule supplies a permitted form pathway, and which drafting limitation still applies. Then explain why an exception to mandatory-form use does not confer authority to give a title opinion.

Feedback: The first two pathways are protected by § 1101.155(b) and addressed by § 537.11(a). No-mandatory-form transactions require the alternative-form conditions in the rule. An agent-created contingency is prohibited drafting or recommendation under § 537.11(b)(5). None of these pathways removes the prohibitions on legal advice or title opinions. A correct response identifies both the permission and its boundary.

### Statute reading exercise: Trace a consumer claim to the actual provision

Read Business & Commerce Code §§ 17.45(4)–(5), 17.46(b)(12), (14), and (24), 17.49(i), and 17.50(a). For each statement identify the potential statutory issue: “I can accept for the owner”; “the option never expires before closing”; and intentional silence about a known material water problem. Identify the facts still needed before anyone can conclude that the consumer has a successful claim. Explain why simply calling a statement “an opinion” is insufficient.

Feedback: The first concerns represented authority, the second represented contractual rights, and the third known information intentionally withheld to induce the transaction. The analysis still needs the applicable claim’s conditions, including consumer status, detrimental reliance where required, producing cause, damages, and an exemption analysis. Section 17.49(i) retains exceptions for specified material factual misrepresentations, § 17.46(b)(24) nondisclosure, and specified unconscionability. Substance controls the characterization.

### Applied exercise: The extra paragraph before midnight

A buyer wants an offer submitted tonight. The buyer asks the agent to promise a free termination if the buyer’s stock portfolio declines more than 8 percent, and suggests pasting a paragraph from a friend’s old contract. Prepare a response of three sentences explaining the role boundary and an operational next step. Identify the documents the file should preserve if counsel prepares language and the principal later directs a change.

Feedback: The requested provision creates a new contingency and legal termination right. The agent must not draft or recommend that language. The buyer needs attorney assistance before signing the unusual arrangement; the agent can coordinate timing and transmit authorized documents. Preserve the attorney-prepared language delivered through the client, the principal’s specific written instruction where the agent makes a change, the conspicuous change, the final signed instrument, and communications establishing the transaction timeline. A rushed deadline does not expand the license.

### Applied exercise: Four files, four different form decisions

File A is an ordinary residential resale. File B is a builder-owned home with the builder’s required attorney-prepared form. File C is a commercial lease for which no mandatory TREC form exists. File D is the agent’s personal purchase while the agent is also representing another participant. For each file explain the form-selection path and one factual check before relying on an exception. Use the exact word “solely” in your answer for File D.

Feedback: A begins with the applicable current mandatory form. B requires establishing the owner-required attorney-form facts and reviewing unusual matters through counsel. C needs an authorized alternative form meeting § 537.11(a)(4), not an agent-drafted lease. D cannot rely on the solely-as-principal exception while also acting as an agent; another applicable pathway must be evaluated. The fact that a license holder has an ownership interest does not alone satisfy “solely.”
