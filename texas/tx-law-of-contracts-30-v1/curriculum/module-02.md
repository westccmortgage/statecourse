## T02U1 — Real Property and the Law

Real property law determines what interests exist and how they may be held, transferred, burdened, and protected. Contract law determines the obligations parties accept concerning those interests. The two questions meet whenever a seller promises more than the seller owns or a buyer’s plan requires rights that are absent. A seller can sign a promise to convey a tract, but a signature alone does not create a missing access easement or eliminate an existing mineral reservation.

Texas transactions draw authority from several places. The Constitution establishes protections such as homestead limits. Statutes govern conveyance, recording, disclosure, family property, probate, and specific remedies. Administrative rules govern regulated participants and particular activities. Local ordinances and recorded private restrictions control uses. Judicial decisions interpret statutes and develop common-law doctrines. A signed contract adds obligations within those legal limits. No single document replaces all the others.

Use a source hierarchy when evaluating a statement. A marketing sheet is evidence of what was advertised, not the legal instrument creating title. A county appraisal record may identify the taxpayer and property for taxation without deciding every ownership question. A recorded deed provides important evidence, but the effect of that deed can depend on marital property, probate, reservations, prior instruments, and later events. A survey depicts identified physical and boundary information; it does not itself insure title. Each source answers a defined question.

The agent’s practical task is to gather facts and preserve the client’s opportunity to evaluate them. Obtain the vesting deed, legal description, known leases, surveys, restrictions, and disclosure documents early. Identify inconsistencies explicitly. A buyer who wants a repair business in a detached garage needs use information before a termination or objection deadline expires, not an assurance based on a neighbor’s similar activity. A seller whose deceased spouse appears in the chain needs appropriate title and probate review before promising an unrealistic closing schedule.

Primary authorities for this topic include [Property Code §§ 5.001–5.008](https://tcss.legis.texas.gov/resources/PR/htm/PR.5.htm), [Family Code §§ 3.001–3.003](https://tcss.legis.texas.gov/resources/FA/htm/FA.3.htm), [Estates Code §§ 101.001–101.002](https://tcss.legis.texas.gov/resources/ES/htm/ES.101.htm), and the public and private land-use provisions discussed below. Classification in a classroom example is preparation for recognizing an issue; a contested title determination belongs to appropriate counsel and title professionals.

## T02U2 — Land, Real Estate, and Real Property

Identify both the physical tract and the rights included in the bargain. An address is convenient for locating a house, but a transaction needs a legally sufficient description of the interest being sold. A tract can include improvements while excluding specified mineral interests; a condominium unit can include appurtenant common-element rights; a lease can grant possession for a term without conveying the owner’s fee. The sales price has meaning only when attached to the correct subject.
### Adapted teaching: The land, improvements, and bundle of rights

The three terms are often used interchangeably in conversation. In analysis they are layers, each
adding something to the one below.

**Land** is the physical layer: the surface of the earth within the parcel's boundaries, everything
beneath it to the centre of the earth, and the airspace above, together with what nature attached —
soil, minerals in place, native trees and standing water. Texas treats the severable natural
components as capable of separate ownership; mineral interests in particular are routinely severed
from the surface and traded on their own, which is why The Real Estate License Act's exemptions you
met in Principles I Module 01 carve mineral transactions out of ordinary brokerage.

**Real estate** is land plus **improvements**: everything humans have permanently attached —
buildings, fences, pavement, wells, irrigation works. The additions are called improvements whether
or not they improve value; the term marks permanence, not quality.

**Real property** is the legal layer: real estate plus the **bundle of rights** ownership carries.
The classical bundle is possession (occupy the property), control (determine its lawful use),
enjoyment (use it without outside interference), exclusion (keep others out), and disposition (sell,
lease, mortgage, give or devise it). Every later module of this course is about some stick in that
bundle: estates measure how much of the bundle you hold and for how long; encumbrances are other
people's claims on particular sticks; leases transfer possession while ownership keeps the rest;
mortgages pledge the bundle as security.

Texas statute defines the trading unit of the licensed business in exactly these layered terms:
Occupations Code § 1101.002(5) defines **"real estate"** for licensing purposes as *any interest in
real property, including a leasehold, located in or outside this state* — but not an interest held
merely as security. Notice what that does: the licensed commodity is not dirt but *interests* — a
fee, a life estate, a leasehold, an easement are all "real estate" in a broker's hands, and Principles I Module 03
is a catalogue of those interests.

**Water deserves a Texas-specific note now**, ahead of its unit in Principles I Module 03: much of the water a
buyer sees is not part of the parcel at all. Water Code § 11.021 declares the water of the ordinary
flow, underflow and tides of every flowing river, natural stream and lake — and the storm and flood
water of every natural watercourse — to be **the property of the state**. A parcel bounded by a
creek includes land; the flowing water belongs to Texas.

**Why the layers matter in practice.** A contract to sell "the property at 214 Brazos Lane" passes
the land, the improvements and the seller's bundle of rights — but only the sticks the seller holds.
If the minerals were severed by a prior owner, the buyer takes the surface without them; if a
recorded easement lets a neighbour cross, the buyer takes subject to it. The licence holder's job at
the contract stage is to know that "the property" is shorthand for a specific, possibly incomplete,
bundle — and that the documents, not the fence line, say what is actually being sold.

### Adapted teaching: Identifying the land the contract concerns

**What a legal description is for.** A legal description identifies land with enough certainty that a
stranger — a court, a title examiner, a surveyor a century later — can locate its boundaries on the
ground from the words alone. A street address fails that test: addresses are postal conveniences,
reassigned and duplicated, and they describe a building's door rather than a parcel's boundary. Tax
parcel numbers fail it too: they are an appraisal district's filing system. Contracts and deeds
therefore carry a *legal* description, and the licence holder's first discipline in this module is
never to draft one — the job is to *copy* the existing description exactly from the prior deed or the
title commitment, and to route any doubt to the title company or a surveyor.

**Texas's foundation: the original land grant and survey system.** Texas differs from most states in
a way that shapes every description in it. Because Texas kept its public lands when it joined the
Union, the federal rectangular (government) survey system of townships and sections was never
extended over Texas. The state's land fabric is instead a quilt of **original surveys** — grants
made over three sovereign eras (Spanish and Mexican grants; Republic of Texas grants; State of Texas
grants) to individuals, colonists, railroads and school funds. Each original survey is identified by
its **grantee name, survey number and abstract number** — the abstract number being the General Land
Office's index for the original grant within each county. Land in Texas is ultimately described *by
reference to the original survey it lies within*: a deed may convey "10.00 acres out of the John
Smith Survey, Abstract No. 123, Freestone County," with the tract's boundaries then given by metes
and bounds. Two statutory anchors, both read for this course, tie this system to the present day:
the field notes of a survey of public land must state *the authority under which the survey is made*
and connect it by *calls and connections for identification* (Natural Resources Code § 21.041), and
a recorded subdivision plat must *locate the subdivision with respect to an original corner of the
original survey of which it is a part* (Local Government Code § 232.001(b)(2)). Every modern
description, however new, hangs from that original fabric.

**The Spanish measure survives.** Texas surveying carries its civil-law history in its units:
Natural Resources Code § 21.041(3) directs that field notes give the land *"by proper field notes
with the necessary calls and connections for identification, observing the Spanish measurement by
varas."* A **vara** is the old Spanish yard used throughout Texas land records — treated in Texas
practice as **33⅓ inches**, so that 36 varas equal 100 feet — and old descriptions state distances
in varas as a matter of course. A licence holder need not convert units professionally but must
recognise them: a call of "950 varas" in an old deed is roughly 2,639 feet, and mistaking varas for
feet misplaces a boundary by nearly two-thirds of its length.

**Working with varas.** Because the vara appears constantly in older records, a short drill fixes
the conversions. Treating the vara as 33⅓ inches: one vara is 2.778 feet; **36 varas = 100 feet**;
1,900.8 varas = 5,280 feet, one mile. Old grants also speak of *leagues* and *labors* — larger Spanish land
quantities a licence holder should recognise as historical units to be resolved from the grant
documents themselves, not converted from memory. Run the arithmetic both
ways until it is mechanical: a call of "540 varas" is 540 × 2.778 ≈ 1,500 feet; a fence line paced
at 250 feet is 250 × 0.36 = 90 varas. What the drill buys is not surveying competence but error
detection: when a seller's old abstract says the tract runs "950 varas along the creek" and a
modern flyer says "950 feet of creek frontage," the licence holder who knows the units sees a
2,639-foot boundary being advertised at a third of its length — or, worse, the reverse.

**Method one: metes and bounds.** The oldest and most flexible method describes a tract by its
perimeter: beginning at a defined **point of beginning (POB)**, the description runs boundary by
boundary — each a **call** stating a direction (a bearing such as "S 45° 30′ E") and a distance —
around the tract and back to the POB, "containing 10.00 acres of land, more or less." The
description's skeleton is built from **monuments**: physical or record reference points, either
natural (a creek, a marked tree) or artificial (an iron rod set, a concrete monument, an adjoiner's
surveyed line). Reading one is mechanical once the vocabulary is known:

> *"BEING a 2.000 acre tract out of the Maria Vela Survey, Abstract No. 87, Hays County, Texas,
> described as follows: BEGINNING at a ½-inch iron rod found at the southeast corner of the Garza
> 5.00 acre tract; THENCE N 01° 15′ 00″ E, 417.42 feet to a ½-inch iron rod set; THENCE S 88° 45′
> 00″ E, 208.71 feet to a point in the west line of County Road 214; THENCE S 01° 15′ 00″ W along
> said road, 417.42 feet to a ½-inch iron rod found; THENCE N 88° 45′ 00″ W, 208.71 feet to the
> POINT OF BEGINNING, containing 2.000 acres of land, more or less."*

Two doctrines about metes and bounds matter even at the principles level. *First*, when calls
conflict, the construction hierarchy generally prefers monuments over courses and distances, and
courses and distances over stated area — because a physical monument is harder to mistake than a
transcribed number, and "containing X acres" is the weakest call of all, which is why it travels
with "more or less." *Second*, a description must **close**: followed call by call, it must return
to the point of beginning. A description that fails to close, or that begins at an unlocatable
point, may fail entirely — and a conveyance that fails for description is at best salvageable as a
contract to convey (recall Property Code § 5.002), a lawyer's problem and not a form-filler's.

**Method two: lot and block (recorded plat).** Subdivision converts metes and bounds into
convenience. The developer's surveyor draws the whole tract once — streets, blocks, lots, easements —
onto a **plat**, which is recorded; thereafter each parcel is described by reference: *"Lot 7,
Block C, Oak Hollow Section 2, an addition to the City of Waco, McLennan County, Texas, according to
the plat recorded in Volume 412, Page 88, Plat Records of McLennan County."* The plat, incorporated
by that reference, supplies every boundary, dimension and dedicated easement. Texas law makes the
plat mandatory for land division outside municipal limits: Local Government Code § 232.001 requires
the owner of a tract outside a municipality who divides it into **two or more parts** to lay out a
subdivision, lots, or streets and other public parts to have a plat prepared; the section expressly
adds — closing an old evasion — that a division *by metes-and-bounds description in a deed of
conveyance, a contract for a deed, or an executory contract* is still a division. The plat must
describe the subdivision by metes and bounds, tie to the original survey corner, state dimensions,
be acknowledged like a deed, and be filed with the county clerk. (Municipalities impose parallel
platting regimes inside their limits and their extraterritorial jurisdictions under separate law;
the principle a licence holder carries is that *dividing land in Texas is a regulated act*, and
"we'll just deed off five acres to the buyer" is a sentence that should trigger the platting
question every time.)

**Reading the plat once it is found.** A lot-and-block description is only the address of the real
description — the plat itself. When the plat is pulled (title companies attach it; county clerks
index it by volume and page), a licence holder reads five layers off the sheet: the **boundary** of
the whole subdivision with its tie to the original survey corner (the § 232.001(b)(2) requirement
at work); the **blocks and lots** with each lot's dimensions; the **streets and other public
parts** dedicated by the plat; the **platted easements** — utility and drainage strips drawn lot by
lot, which encumber every lot they cross exactly as Principles I Module 03 described; and the **building
setback lines** many plats draw, which operate alongside any recorded covenants. The habit to
build: whenever a contract says "Lot 7, Block C," the plat is part of the description, and the
buyer deserves to see it — the easement running under the planned pool addition is on that sheet,
not in the deed.


A worked review starts with three documents: a listing describes “ten acres,” a deed describes two adjoining tracts, and a tax map labels one tract as nine acres. The agent should not pick the most convenient figure. The file needs the actual conveyance description, the survey or other appropriate acreage evidence, and confirmation of which tract or tracts the parties intend to include. If part of a tract is to be conveyed, subdivision and description issues may arise. An agent cannot create a new legal description simply by drawing a line on a marketing map.

## T02U3 — Real Property vs. Personal Property

Disputes over fixtures often emerge when the seller removes an object the buyer assumed was included. The prudent time to identify the item is before the offer. Ask about wall-mounted televisions and brackets, smart-home equipment, leased solar systems, decorative lights, attached shelving, outdoor structures, and business equipment. Describe the agreed treatment through authorized forms and appropriate professional assistance where ownership, financing, or fixture law is uncertain.
### Adapted teaching: Fixtures, trade fixtures, severance, and competing security interests

Everything that is not real property is **personal property** (also *personalty* or *chattels*):
movable things — furniture, vehicles, equipment — and intangible rights. The classification sounds
academic until you watch it decide who owns the dishwasher.

**Crossing the line: annexation and severance.** Personal property becomes real property by
**annexation** — permanent attachment to land or improvements. A pile of lumber is personalty; the
deck built from it is part of the real estate. The reverse movement is **severance**: standing timber
is realty, felled logs are personalty; growing minerals in place are realty, produced oil in the tank
is personalty. Cultivated annual crops (**emblements**) are treated as personal property of the one
who planted them even before harvest — the classic accommodation to agriculture.

**The fixture tests.** A **fixture** is an item that began as personal property and has become part
of the real estate. When a sale contract is silent, fixtures pass with the deed and personalty stays
with the seller — so the classification allocates real money. The common-law tests, applied by Texas
courts and taught in every serious course, ask three questions:

1. **Method of annexation.** How is it attached? Built-in, plumbed, wired or cemented items lean
   real; items resting by their own weight or plugged into an outlet lean personal. Damage on removal
   is strong evidence of annexation.
2. **Adaptation to the realty.** Was the item fitted to this property — custom cabinetry, a pool
   pump, a garage-door opener sized to this door? Adaptation leans real even where attachment is
   light.
3. **Intention of the parties.** The dominant test: would a reasonable person, viewing the
   attachment and adaptation, understand the item to have been made a permanent part of the property?
   Intention is judged objectively from the circumstances, not from a party's private plan.

**The practice answer is always the same: do not litigate the tests — draft around them.** A
well-made Texas contract lists what stays and what goes. The refrigerator, the mounted televisions,
the smart thermostat, the propane tank: name them, and the fixture tests never get called. Principles I Module 06
returns to this when it treats the promulgated contract forms.

**Trade fixtures.** Items a commercial tenant attaches for the tenant's business — shelving, ovens,
salon chairs — are **trade fixtures**: as between landlord and tenant they remain the tenant's
personal property, removable at or before the end of the term, with the tenant responsible for the
damage removal causes. Left behind after the tenancy ends, they typically become the landlord's by
abandonment. The category exists so that renting space does not mean donating your equipment to the
building.

**Financed fixtures: where two bodies of law meet.** A dishwasher bought on credit is personal
property subject to a **security interest** under Article 9 of the Business & Commerce Code; built
into a kitchen it becomes a fixture in real estate that may itself be mortgaged. Which creditor wins?
Business & Commerce Code § 9.334 draws the lines: no Article 9 security interest exists at all in
*ordinary building materials incorporated into an improvement* — the lumber merges into the realty,
full stop — but for true fixtures, a **purchase-money security interest** perfected by a **fixture
filing** made before the goods become fixtures **or within 20 days after** takes priority even over
an earlier recorded real property encumbrancer, and readily removable office machines and replacement
domestic appliances get their own priority track. A licence holder does not practise secured-
transactions law; what the licence holder must recognise is the pattern — a financed water softener,
solar array or HVAC system may carry a lien that does not appear in the deed records' mortgage
chain — and the cure, which is asking, in every transaction, *is anything attached to this house
still being paid for?*

**Manufactured housing** is the classification question at its most consequential — a dwelling that
may be personal property (titled like a vehicle) or may have become real property depending on its
installation and the election made under the applicable titling law. This course flags the category;
the dedicated treatment belongs to later courses in the program. What Principles I asks of you is
only the reflex: *never assume a manufactured home is real estate; verify how it is held.*


Consider a seller who bought a refrigerator on credit but leased a roof-mounted solar system. The two items cannot be treated alike merely because both serve the residence. One may be movable personal property owned subject to a financing arrangement; the other may be equipment owned by a third party under a contract with assignment or removal requirements. The real-estate purchase agreement cannot convey a third party’s ownership without the necessary arrangement. Obtain the actual agreement and direct legal or lender questions to the appropriate professionals.

An exclusion should also be clear about the physical result. If a seller reserves a chandelier, the parties may need to address its replacement and damage caused by removal. The agent can identify those unresolved facts and use the appropriate authorized instrument. The agent should not leave the parties with a vague reference to “seller’s sentimental items,” because the buyer cannot determine what property will remain. Practical specificity reduces the need for a later fixture dispute.

## T02U4 — Characteristics of Real Property

The familiar physical and economic characteristics explain why contracts devote so much attention to inspection, title, financing, and timing. They are analytical concepts, not statutory guarantees about any investment’s value. Land’s durability does not mean a house cannot be destroyed; uniqueness does not mean a court automatically awards specific performance; scarcity in one location does not make every parcel marketable.
### Adapted teaching: How immobility, uniqueness, and investment affect negotiation

Real estate markets behave unlike markets for shares, cars or groceries, and the differences follow
from physical and economic characteristics of the asset itself. This unit is economics rather than
law: no statute is cited because none governs it. What follows is the standard analytical vocabulary,
applied to Texas conditions.

**Physical characteristics.**

- **Immobility.** Land cannot be moved. Every parcel's value is therefore captured by its location,
  and markets are irreducibly local: a surplus of housing in one metropolitan area cannot ship to
  relieve a shortage in another. Immobility is also why real estate is governed and taxed where it
  sits, and why a licence to broker it is issued state by state.
- **Indestructibility.** Land endures. Improvements wear out; the land under them does not. This
  permanence is what makes land a durable store of value and makes long instruments — thirty-year
  loans, ninety-nine-year leases — sensible.
- **Uniqueness (heterogeneity).** No two parcels are identical, because no two occupy the same
  location. The law takes this seriously — you will see in Principles I Module 06 that Texas allows four years to
  sue for **specific performance** of a contract to convey real property, a remedy premised on the
  idea that money cannot fully substitute for a particular parcel — and valuation takes it seriously
  too, which is why appraisal compares and adjusts rather than looking up a price.

**Economic characteristics.**

- **Scarcity.** Usable land in a given location is finite even where land in general is abundant —
  a truth vivid in Texas, where vast rural acreage coexists with intense competition for land in the
  growth corridors of the major metropolitan areas.
- **Improvements and modification.** Investment in a parcel changes its character and value, and the
  character of surrounding parcels; a subdivision, a highway or a reservoir re-prices its neighbours.
- **Permanence of investment.** Capital placed in land and improvements is long-lived and largely
  irreversible — the return arrives over decades, which ties real estate to credit markets and makes
  interest rates a dominant force in demand.
- **Location preference (situs).** People's preference for one location over another — commute,
  schools, flood risk, prestige — is the economic engine of value differences between physically
  similar properties.

**What these characteristics do to the market.** Because the asset is immobile and unique, buyers and
sellers cannot meet in a central exchange; they find each other through search, which is costly and
slow. Because transactions are infrequent, prices are discovered deal by deal rather than posted
continuously. Because supply responds slowly — construction takes years, and land in a location
cannot be increased at all — demand shifts move prices sharply before supply can answer. Economists
summarise all this by calling real estate markets **imperfect**: information is incomplete, products
are non-standard, entry is slow, and local conditions dominate. The brokerage business exists
precisely because the market is imperfect — the licence holder's stock in trade is local information,
search and negotiation in a market that lacks a ticker.


Imagine two similar houses offered at the same price. One has an ordinary lot with public access, and one depends on a disputed private driveway. Their physical improvements may be comparable, but the second bargain carries a different legal risk. A purchase decision should account for the access evidence, lender requirements, and available contractual protections. Calling land unique is useful only when it prompts investigation of what makes the specific property different.

Slow supply response also affects negotiations. A buyer needing occupancy before school begins may accept a different closing or possession arrangement than an investor. That preference belongs in the actual agreement, not an informal promise about flexibility. Market pressure changes the parties’ bargaining choices; it does not shorten the rule against drafting unauthorized legal provisions or excuse an inaccurate statement about a deadline.

## T02U5 — Forms of Real Estate Ownership

Start an ownership review with the number of holders and the applicable legal regime. Then identify whether the proposed seller can convey the entire interest the buyer expects. A person with an undivided one-third interest cannot unilaterally promise that the other co-owners will convey their shares. A surviving spouse’s possession does not by itself establish that every deceased spouse’s interest passed automatically to that survivor.
### Adapted teaching: Severalty, undivided shares, partition, and survivorship

**Ownership in severalty** is title in one holder — one human or one entity — "severed" from all
others. One name signs, one name conveys. Simplicity is its virtue; exposure (everything rides on
one owner's life, debts and capacity) is its cost. Note at once that in Texas, "one name on the
deed" does not end the inquiry for a married owner: community property (two units ahead) can give a
spouse ownership rights the deed never mentions, which is why Texas practice asks every seller's
marital status and history.

**Co-ownership** is title in two or more persons at the same time. The two working forms:

- **Tenancy in common** — each co-owner holds an undivided fractional interest in the whole
  (undivided: no one owns a particular corner; each owns a share of every square foot). Shares may
  be unequal; each owner may sell, mortgage or devise the owner's share independently; and on death
  the share passes to the owner's estate. Tenancy in common is the Texas default whenever two or
  more take title without more.
- **Joint ownership with right of survivorship** — the decedent co-owner's interest sweeps to the
  survivors, bypassing the estate. Texas treats this form with statutory suspicion, as the next
  paragraphs show: it exists here only by written agreement.

**Ending co-ownership: partition.** Co-ownership is voluntary in a way sole ownership is not — it
lasts only while every co-owner tolerates it. Texas law recognises **partition**: the division of
co-owned property among its owners, either **in kind** (the land itself is divided) or, where land
cannot be fairly divided, by sale and division of proceeds. The doctrine has already appeared in
this course wearing its homestead clothes: Property Code § 41.001(b)(4) speaks of *an owelty of
partition imposed against the entirety of the property by a court order or by a written agreement
of the parties to the partition* — an **owelty** being the equalising payment charged against the
property when a physical division cannot be made exactly even, and the read text shows Texas
treating it as one of the few encumbrances valid even against a homestead, including the
divorce-decree award of a family home to one spouse. For the licence holder, partition is mostly a
listing-intake fact pattern: inherited land held by five siblings, one of whom "wants out," is a
co-ownership on its way to agreement, buyout, or a partition proceeding — and which of those it
becomes is counsel's work. What the agent contributes is the early, accurate map: who owns what
undivided share, and whether every necessary signature can actually be obtained.

**The Texas survivorship rule — the anti-default.** In many states, titling property "as joint
tenants" creates survivorship automatically. Texas law reverses the presumption. Estates Code
§ 101.002 provides that if two or more persons hold an interest jointly and one dies before
severance, the decedent's interest *does not survive* to the remaining joint owners — it *passes by
will or intestacy as if the decedent's interest had been severed*. Survivorship must be added by
agreement: Estates Code § 111.001 authorises joint owners to *agree in writing* that the interest of
a deceased joint owner survives to the surviving owners, and adds, in terms every licence holder
should memorise, that such an agreement *may not be inferred from the mere fact that property is
held in joint ownership*. So: joint names on a Texas deed, without a written survivorship agreement,
means tenancy in common in effect at death — the decedent's share goes to heirs or devisees, not to
the co-owner. Family arrangements built on the opposite assumption discover the truth in probate.

**Practice posture.** Buyers routinely ask agents "how should we take title?" The honest structure
of the answer is: *here are the forms that exist; the choice among them changes your estate,
creditor and tax position; that choice is legal advice, and an attorney should make it with you.* An
agent who explains what tenancy in common *is* teaches; an agent who tells a couple which form to
choose practises law without a licence.

### Adapted teaching: Marital classification, proof, and signature questions

Texas marital property is the co-ownership system most licence holders touch daily, because most
buyers and sellers are married, and Texas is a **community property** state — a civil-law
inheritance shared by only a minority of states.

**The classification, in the statute's words.** Family Code § 3.001 defines **separate property**:
what a spouse owned or claimed *before marriage*; what a spouse acquires during marriage *by gift,
devise, or descent*; and recoveries for personal injuries (except lost earning capacity). Family
Code § 3.002 defines **community property** as the property, *other than separate property*,
acquired by either spouse during marriage. The default is community: earnings, and things bought
with earnings, belong to the marital community regardless of whose name is on the paycheck, the
account or the deed.

**The presumption with teeth.** Family Code § 3.003 supplies the rule of proof: property possessed
by either spouse *during or on dissolution of* marriage is **presumed community**, and the spouse
claiming separate character must prove it by **clear and convincing evidence** — a heightened
standard. Tracing a house to a premarital account or an inheritance is exactly the kind of proof
this demands, and it is lawyer's work.

**Consequences at the transaction desk.** Community property means a home bought during marriage is
generally owned by the community even if deeded to one spouse alone — so Texas practice expects both
spouses' involvement in dealing with it; title companies ask marital history as routinely as they
ask for identification; and a seller's casual "it's only in my name" is the beginning of the
inquiry, not the end. Homestead rules add their own layer for the family home. The licence holder's
job description in this area is one sentence long: *identify marital status early, tell the title
company everything, and promise nothing about who must sign until the title company and counsel have
answered.*

**The classification, drilled.** Run § 3.001, § 3.002 and § 3.003 against the standard fact
patterns until the sorting is reflexive. *A house bought by one spouse two years before the
wedding* — separate: owned before marriage, § 3.001(1); marriage does not convert it. *The same
house's mortgage paid down during marriage from salary* — the house stays separate, but the
community's contributions raise reimbursement questions between the estates: lawyer's territory,
and exactly why the agent reports facts rather than conclusions. *A rental duplex inherited by one
spouse during marriage* — separate: acquired by devise or descent, § 3.001(2). *Rent collected from
that duplex during marriage* — here Texas parts company with several community-property states: the
default classification of income from separate property is a classic tracing battleground, and the
presumption of § 3.003 puts the burden — by clear and convincing evidence — on the spouse claiming
separate character. *A personal-injury settlement to one spouse* — separate under § 3.001(3),
except the portion for lost earning capacity during marriage, which the statute carves back to the
community. *A boat bought during marriage, titled in one spouse's name* — presumed community under
§ 3.003(a): possession during marriage raises the presumption, and the title certificate's single
name does not answer it. The through-line: **title is not classification**. The deed tells you who
holds record title; §§ 3.001–3.003 decide who owns.


The practical distinction between title, ownership classification, and authority is critical. The deed may contain one name, the Family Code may create a community-property presumption, and homestead law may require a spouse’s joinder even if the property is separate. The license holder gathers marital history, acquisition facts, and relevant documents; title professionals and counsel determine their effect. Promising that a spouse need not participate because the name is absent from the deed converts an incomplete observation into a legal conclusion.

## T02U6 — Ownership of Real Estate by Business Organizations

An entity’s ownership is different from ownership of interests in that entity. Buying a parcel from an LLC ordinarily concerns the LLC’s asset. Buying membership interests concerns the organization itself and can involve liabilities and legal arrangements beyond a routine real-estate sale. Confirm the proposed transaction before selecting a form. The entity name, governing documents, authority evidence, and signature capacity matter independently of the individual negotiator’s confidence.
### Adapted teaching: Entity ownership and evidence of a human signer’s authority

Real estate is held not only by people but by entities, and a licence holder needs both directions
of that fact: entities as *owners* of homes and investment property, and the entity rules of the
licensing law itself.

**Why entities hold real estate.** Corporations, limited liability companies and partnerships hold
title in severalty as legal persons. Investors use entities for liability separation (the entity's
debts are the entity's), for continuity (the entity outlives its members), and for pooling capital.
The transaction-desk consequences are mechanical but unforgiving: an entity acts only through
authorised humans, so a contract or deed from "Lone Star Holdings LLC" requires proof of the
entity's existence and of the signer's authority — questions the title company will insist on
resolving, and which the agent should surface at intake, not at closing.

**The licensing law's entity rules — already met, now completed.** The Real Estate License Act
defines a "business entity" by reference to the Business Organizations Code (Occupations Code
§ 1101.002(1-a): a domestic or foreign entity qualified to transact business in Texas), and
§ 1101.351(a-1) requires a business entity that acts as a broker to hold its *own* broker licence —
brokerage through an entity is not covered by the humans' licences alone. And recall from Module
01's exemption catalogue that a limited partnership's or LLC's *internal* transactions — its general
partner, manager or negotiating employees dealing with the entity's own real estate — sit outside
the Act (§ 1101.005): entities selling their own land are owners, not brokers.

**The authority questions, as a checklist.** Entity transactions fail at predictable joints, so the
intake questions are worth listing in the order a title company will ask them. *Existence*: is the
entity validly formed and in good standing — and is the name on the contract the name on file, not
a d/b/a or a near-miss? *Capacity*: is a foreign (out-of-state) entity qualified to transact
business in Texas, as the licensing law's own entity definition presumes (§ 1101.002(1-a) speaks of
an entity *qualified to transact business in this state*)? *Authority*: which humans may bind the
entity for THIS act — selling land is not day-to-day business for most companies, and the governing
documents may reserve it to members, boards or specified officers; the title company will want the
resolution or consent that proves it. *Signature form*: the block signs the entity, by the human,
in the capacity — "Lone Star Holdings LLC, by Maria Ortiz, Manager" — because a bare personal
signature binds the wrong party or nobody. An agent cannot cure a defect in any of the four, but an
agent who asks the four questions at intake turns closing-week emergencies into first-week
paperwork.

**Home and business in one asset.** Texas homestead law, from Principles I Module 03, already showed one meeting
point: the urban homestead may serve as *both* a home and a place of business within the 10-acre cap
(Property Code § 41.002). The other meeting point is a caution: moving a personal home *into* an
entity has consequences — homestead character, insurance, lending and taxation all shift — and the
agent's role, once again, is to flag the move as an attorney-and-accountant decision.

### Adapted teaching: Trustees, beneficiaries, and the authority to convey

A **trust** splits ownership into management and benefit. Property Code § 111.004(4) defines an
**express trust** as *a fiduciary relationship with respect to property which arises as a
manifestation by the settlor of an intention to create the relationship and which subjects the
person holding title to the property to equitable duties to deal with the property for the benefit
of another person*. Three roles: the **settlor** creates the trust and supplies the property; the
**trustee** holds legal title, bound by fiduciary duties; the **beneficiary** holds the benefit.
Section 111.003 trims the field: for the Texas Trust Code, "trust" means an *express* trust only —
not resulting or constructive trusts (court-imposed remedies), not business trusts, and *not a deed
of trust*, despite the name. That last exclusion untangles a Texas vocabulary knot once and for
all: the **deed of trust** of Principles I Modules 03 and 08 is a mortgage instrument, not a trust in this sense;
only the word is shared.

**Why property rides in trusts.** Revocable "living" trusts hold homes for probate avoidance and
incapacity planning; testamentary trusts hold property for minors or dependents; land is bought and
sold by trustees of every stripe. For the licence holder the signals and moves are: title in "Jane
Doe, Trustee of the Doe Family Trust" means the *trustee* is the party who contracts and conveys;
the trustee's authority comes from the trust instrument, which the title company will want to see
(or a certification of it); and recall from Principles I Module 01 that a person conducting a transaction *under
the authority of a written trust instrument* is exempt from licensing (§ 1101.005) — the trustee
selling trust land is not thereby a broker. What the agent never does is interpret the trust: whether
this trustee may sell this land on these terms is a question for the instrument, the title company
and counsel.

**The trust vocabulary, completed.** Two more defined terms from the read text of § 111.004 round
out the working set. A **beneficiary** is *a person for whose benefit property is held in trust,
regardless of the nature of the interest* — so contingent, future and partial interests all make
beneficiaries, which is why "who has to approve this sale?" can have a longer answer than a seller
expects. And an **interest** in the Trust Code's sense means *any interest, whether legal or
equitable or both, present or future, vested or contingent, defeasible or indefeasible* — the
statute deliberately sweeping in every gradation this course's estates module taught. Hold the two
titles apart with one sentence: *the trustee holds legal title and signs; the beneficiaries hold
equitable interests and benefit.* A buyer cannot take good title from a beneficiary, however
sincere; a trustee's deed conveys because the trustee is the title holder the trust instrument
empowers.

**The trust transaction, step by step.** When "Trustee" appears in a chain of title or on a listing
intake, the working sequence is: identify the trust exactly as named in the vesting deed; obtain
the trust's authority evidence for the title company — the instrument or the certification
practice the title company specifies; confirm the *current* trustee (settlors die; trustees
resign; successor trustees take over, and the succession must be documented); and write the
contract in the fiduciary capacity — "Jane Doe, Trustee of the Doe Family Trust," never "Jane Doe"
alone, because the capacities are different parties in law. None of this is trust interpretation;
all of it is accurate transmission — and the distinction is the unit's licence-boundary lesson
applied.


An organization chart is not authority evidence. A person described as “founder” may have transferred management power; a member may lack authority for a major asset sale; a trustee may act subject to limitations or required consents. Request the evidence the title company or counsel needs and preserve the distinction between the entity as party and the human signing for it. Do not substitute the human’s personal name in a signature block merely to make an electronic-signing platform accept the document.

## T02U7 — Condominiums, Cooperatives, Town Houses & Time-Shares

A condominium is a form of ownership, not a building style. A townhouse describes a physical arrangement and may be owned as a separate lot and improvements, as a condominium unit, or under another lawful structure. A cooperative generally places ownership in an entity while an occupant holds an ownership or membership interest coupled with an occupancy right. A timeshare divides or organizes use through a plan that can involve different forms of interest. These distinctions control which documents identify the asset, what ongoing obligations exist, and which contract form is appropriate.

[Property Code § 82.003](https://tcss.legis.texas.gov/resources/PR/htm/PR.82.htm) supplies the Uniform Condominium Act’s definitions. The declaration creates and describes the condominium under §§ 82.051 and 82.055. Common elements, limited common elements, allocated interests, association powers, and resale information should be reviewed as the governing law and documents require. Section 82.002 addresses applicability; older condominiums can involve Chapter 81 and specified Chapter 82 provisions. Never decide the applicable chapter solely from the age of a roof or a remodeled kitchen.

In a condominium purchase, review the unit identifier and appurtenant interests rather than substituting an ordinary lot description. A balcony or parking area that feels private may be a limited common element rather than separately owned land. The declaration and other governing instruments identify the legal arrangement. Association budgets, assessments, insurance allocation, restrictions, litigation, and maintenance duties can affect the buyer’s decision and financing. A seller’s informal statement that “the association takes care of everything” does not establish the scope of those duties.

Consider a three-story townhouse with a small enclosed yard. The buyer plans to enlarge the patio. If the yard is part of a fee-simple lot, recorded restrictions and municipal requirements may control. If it is a limited common element, the declaration and association authority become essential. Identical fences can conceal very different ownership rights. The agent should establish the ownership regime before giving the buyer a property-description explanation or selecting a resale contract.

Cooperative interests require attention to the entity and occupancy agreement. A buyer may need approval, a transfer of shares or membership, and a new or assigned proprietary lease. The fact that the unit is used as a residence does not turn the transaction automatically into an ordinary deed conveyance. A lender’s willingness to finance a condominium is not evidence that the same loan product finances a cooperative interest. Refer legal document selection and nonstandard transfers to counsel while coordinating factual information and deadlines.

[Property Code Chapter 221](https://tcss.legis.texas.gov/resources/PR/htm/PR.221.htm), the Texas Timeshare Act, defines accommodations, plans, and interests in § 221.002 and provides a specialized statutory framework. A timeshare may be a real-property interest or another right under its governing documents. The buyer should understand precisely what is owned, when use is available, what assessments recur, how reservations work, and what transfer and cancellation rights actually apply. Do not promise that a timeshare can be resold like an ordinary home or that unused reservations retain a particular cash value. Those are separate factual and legal questions.

The contract-intake habit for shared ownership is therefore document-specific. Identify the legal regime; obtain the declaration, occupancy or plan documents as relevant; identify required disclosures and approvals; confirm financing compatibility; and record the buyer’s intended use. The outcome is not simply a checked box called “HOA.” It is an accurate description of the rights and obligations the buyer is considering.

## T02U8 — Laws Affecting Real Estate

An owner’s ability to use a property can be limited by several independent systems. A local permit does not erase a private restriction. An association’s consent does not authorize a use prohibited by government. Neither tells the lender whether the property satisfies underwriting requirements. Evaluate each source separately, then explain the unresolved facts and deadlines to the client.
### Adapted teaching: Public land-use regulation and recorded private restrictions

Land in Texas is controlled from two directions at once, and the two systems are written down in different places, enforced by different people, and answerable to different rules. The first is **public control**: regulation imposed by a governmental body under the police power. The second is **private control**: restrictions imposed by recorded instrument, running with the land, enforced by neighbors and property owners' associations. A license holder who checks only one of the two has done half the job.

**The police power as the basis for public regulation.** Public land-use regulation is not justified by the government owning anything. It is justified by the government's authority to regulate for the protection of the public, and the Texas Legislature says so in the first line of the municipal zoning chapter. Local Government Code § 211.001 states that the powers granted under that subchapter "are for the purpose of promoting the public health, safety, morals, or general welfare and protecting and preserving places and areas of historical, cultural, or architectural importance and significance." That is the whole theory of zoning in one clause: a municipality may tell an owner what to do with the owner's own land, without paying for it, because the regulation serves a public purpose. When governmental power stops being regulation and becomes an acquisition, the analysis changes entirely — the subject of the next unit.

**What a Texas municipality may regulate.** Local Government Code § 211.003(a) lists the tools. The governing body of a municipality may regulate "the height, number of stories, and size of buildings and other structures"; "the percentage of a lot that may be occupied"; "the size of yards, courts, and other open spaces"; "population density"; "the location and use of buildings, other structures, and land for business, industrial, residential, or other purposes"; and the pumping, extraction, and use of groundwater by persons other than retail public utilities where the groundwater "presents an actual or potential threat to human health." Section 211.003(b) adds authority over construction, reconstruction, alteration, or razing of buildings in designated places of historical, cultural, or architectural significance, and § 211.003(c) permits a home-rule municipality to regulate the **bulk** of buildings.

Notice the shape of that list. The **use** regulation — what activity may occur on the land — is one item among six; the rest are **bulk, height, and area** regulations. A buyer's plan can be defeated by either: a use may be permitted in the district and the project still fail because the building will not fit inside the required yards.

**The comprehensive plan requirement.** Zoning is not a free-floating power. Section 211.004(a) provides that "[z]oning regulations must be adopted in accordance with a comprehensive plan" and must be designed to serve listed ends — lessening street congestion, securing safety from fire and panic, promoting health and the general welfare, providing adequate light and air, preventing overcrowding, avoiding undue concentration of population, or facilitating adequate transportation, water, sewers, schools, and parks. Section 211.005(a) then lets the municipality divide itself into **districts** of the number, shape, and size the governing body considers best, and § 211.005(b) requires regulations to "be uniform for each class or kind of building in a district," though they "may vary from district to district."

**How a zoning regulation is adopted.** Section 211.006(a) provides that a regulation or district boundary "is not effective until after a public hearing on the matter at which parties in interest and citizens have an opportunity to be heard," with notice published before the 15th day before the hearing in an official newspaper or newspaper of general circulation and, if the municipality maintains a website, on that website. Section 211.006(a-1) adds a targeted written notice where a proposed change would turn a current conforming use into a nonconforming one, delivered to each owner and occupant not later than the 10th day before the hearing and containing, in bold 14-point type or larger, the warning that the municipality "IS HOLDING A HEARING THAT WILL DETERMINE WHETHER YOU MAY LOSE THE RIGHT TO CONTINUE USING YOUR PROPERTY FOR ITS CURRENT USE."

The **zoning commission** is the drafting body. Under § 211.007(a) the governing body of a home-rule municipality *shall*, and of a general-law municipality *may*, appoint one; it recommends the original district boundaries and the regulations for each district. Section 211.007(b) requires a preliminary report and at least one public hearing before a final report goes to the governing body, which "may not take action on the matter until it receives the final report." Section 211.007(c) requires written notice, before the 10th day before the hearing, to each owner of real property within 200 feet of the property whose classification is proposed to change. Neighbors also have a formal lever: § 211.0061(b) allows a written protest signed by the owners of at least 20 percent of the area covered by the change or of the adjoining area extending 200 feet from it, and under § 211.0061(d) a validly protested change then needs the affirmative vote of at least three-fourths of all members of the governing body.

**The board of adjustment, variances, and special exceptions.** Section 211.008(a) authorizes a municipality to appoint a **board of adjustment** and, in the regulations adopted under the subchapter, to authorize that board "in appropriate cases and subject to appropriate conditions and safeguards, to make special exceptions to the terms of the zoning ordinance that are consistent with the general purpose and intent of the ordinance and in accordance with any applicable rules contained in the ordinance." Read the whole grant: a special exception is not free-form relief. It is available only in the cases the ordinance contemplates, on the conditions and safeguards the ordinance sets, and it must square with both the ordinance's general purpose and its applicable rules. The board must have at least five members appointed for two-year terms (§ 211.008(b)), and each case "must be heard by at least 75 percent of the members" (§ 211.008(d)).

Section 211.009(a) sets out what the board may do: hear and decide an appeal alleging error by an administrative official enforcing the ordinance; hear and decide special exceptions when the ordinance requires it; and "authorize in specific cases a **variance** from the terms of a zoning ordinance if the variance is not contrary to the public interest and, due to special conditions, a literal enforcement of the ordinance would result in unnecessary hardship, and so that the spirit of the ordinance is observed and substantial justice is done." Section 211.009(b-1) lists grounds the board *may* consider on the hardship question for a structure, among them that the cost of compliance exceeds 50 percent of the structure's appraised value on the most recent certified appraisal roll, or that compliance would cost the lot at least 25 percent of its physically developable area. Under § 211.009(c), a concurring vote of 75 percent of the members is necessary to reverse an official's decision or to authorize a variance.

A common error is to treat a variance as a request to be excused from a rule the applicant dislikes, or to treat "I paid too much for the lot" as hardship. The standard is about *special conditions* and a result still faithful to the ordinance's spirit. A second common error confuses the remedies: a **special exception** is a use or condition the ordinance itself contemplates and delegates to the board, while a **variance** is a departure from the ordinance's terms. Deadlines matter too. Section 211.010(b) requires an appeal to the board not later than the 20th day after the decision, and § 211.010(d) requires the board to decide not later than the 60th day after filing. Under § 211.011 a person aggrieved, a taxpayer, or a municipal officer, department, board, or bureau may present a verified petition to a district court, county court, or county court at law, and § 211.011(b) requires it "within 10 days after the date the decision is filed in the board's office."

**Nonconforming uses.** When a district is created or changed, existing uses do not always fit. Section 211.019(b) provides that a person using property in a manner considered a nonconforming use as a result of the adoption of or change to a zoning regulation or boundary "may continue to use the property in the same manner unless required by a municipality to stop the nonconforming use." That is what "grandfathering" means in Texas: continuation as of right, not immunity forever. If the municipality does require the use to stop, § 211.019(d) gives the owner or lessee a choice between a payment from the municipality — costs directly attributable to ceasing the use, plus the diminution in market value — or continued nonconforming use until that amount is recovered through continued business activity. Section 211.019(o) removes the protection entirely for a use "intentionally abandoned for at least six months." Section 211.012 lets the municipality make a violation a misdemeanor, provide civil penalties, and sue to restrain, correct, or abate it, and § 211.013(a) resolves overlaps by a **stricter-standard rule**: where the zoning regulation imposes higher standards than another statute or local ordinance the zoning regulation controls, and where the other law is stricter the other law controls.

**Texas reality: zoning is a municipal option, not a statewide mandate.** Chapter 211 grants zoning power to "the governing body of a municipality," and it sits in a subtitle titled *Municipal Regulatory Authority*. Nothing in the chapter grants that power to a county. Section 211.006(a) speaks of "[t]he governing body of a municipality **wishing to exercise** the authority relating to zoning regulations" — permissive language, not a command. Section 211.015 lets the voters of a home-rule municipality repeal the municipality's zoning regulations in their entirety by charter election or, on initial adoption, by referendum. And the Legislature has written the possibility of a Texas city without zoning into another chapter: Local Government Code § 212.151 makes the municipal deed-restriction-enforcement subchapter available to a municipality with a population of 1.5 million or more that passes a qualifying ordinance and, separately, to a municipality "that does not have zoning ordinances and passes an ordinance that requires uniform application and enforcement of this subchapter with regard to all property and residents."

Houston is the well-known example of a very large Texas city that has not adopted conventional use-district zoning; that is common knowledge about how Texas cities have chosen to govern themselves, not a statutory rule, and no source read for this course states it. What the statutes *do* establish is the structure that makes it possible. Two consequences follow. First, land in the unincorporated portion of a Texas county is not zoned the way land inside a city is, because chapter 211 does not reach it. Second, in a municipality without zoning, recorded private restrictions do far more of the work of separating uses — which is exactly why § 212.151 exists.

**Subdivision regulation and platting.** Even a municipality that never adopts a use-district ordinance regulates how raw land becomes lots. Under § 212.002 a municipality may, after a public hearing, adopt rules governing plats and subdivisions within its jurisdiction. Section 212.004(a) requires a plat whenever an owner of a tract inside the limits or in the extraterritorial jurisdiction of a municipality divides it into two or more parts to lay out a subdivision, lots, streets, alleys, squares, parks, or other parts intended to be dedicated to public use — and the division counts however accomplished, "by using a metes and bounds description in a deed of conveyance or in a contract for a deed, by using a contract of sale or other executory contract to convey, or by using any other method." The subsection excludes "a division of land into parts greater than five acres, where each part has access and no public improvement is being dedicated." Section 212.004(d) requires the plat to be filed and recorded with the county clerk.

Approval is largely ministerial and is on a clock. Section 212.005(a) states that the responsible municipal authority "must approve a plat or replat that is required to be prepared under this subchapter and that satisfies the requirements of this subchapter." Section 212.006(a) identifies that authority as the municipal planning commission, or the governing body where there is none. Section 212.009(a) requires approval, conditional approval, or disapproval "within 30 days after the date the plat is filed," and a plat "is approved by the municipal authority unless it is disapproved within that period and in accordance with Section 212.0091". Both halves of that condition matter: a disapproval that is late, or that does not comply with § 212.0091, does not stop the clock, and the plat is approved by the authority's inaction. Section 212.0091 requires a written statement clearly articulating each condition or reason, directly related to the subchapter's requirements and citing the law relied on where applicable, and it "may not be arbitrary." Section 212.010(e) gives an owner whose conforming plat is refused an action for a writ of mandamus with reasonable attorney's fees. Section 212.012(a) then forbids listed utility providers to "serve or connect any land with water, sewer, electricity, gas, or other utility service" unless presented with or otherwise holding the approval certificate issued under § 212.0115. And a replat may be recorded without vacating the preceding plat under § 212.014 only if it "does not attempt to amend or remove any covenants or restrictions" — a statutory acknowledgement that the public plat process does not override private covenants.

**Extraterritorial jurisdiction.** A Texas municipality's reach extends past its city limits, but not evenly. Section 212.001(1) defines **extraterritorial jurisdiction** by reference to Chapter 42 of the Local Government Code. Section 212.003(a) lets a municipality extend its subdivision ordinances into the ETJ — and then flatly limits what it may do there: "unless otherwise authorized by state law, in its extraterritorial jurisdiction a municipality shall not regulate: (1) the use of any building or property for business, industrial, residential, or other purposes; (2) the bulk, height, or number of buildings constructed on a particular tract of land; (3) the size of a building that can be constructed on a particular tract of land, including without limitation any restriction on the ratio of building floor space to the land square footage; (4) the number of residential units that can be built per acre of land" — and, on the narrow terms stated there, (5) the size, type, or method of construction of certain water or wastewater facilities. Note the opening words: the prohibition yields where state law elsewhere authorizes the regulation, so it is a default, not an absolute bar. Section 212.049 adds that the development-plat subchapter "does not authorize the municipality to require municipal building permits or otherwise enforce the municipality's building code in its extraterritorial jurisdiction." The teaching point is precise: in the ETJ expect **platting and subdivision** regulation, and do not assume **use, density, height, or bulk** regulation. This is the most misdescribed piece of Texas land-use law in ordinary conversation.

**Private controls: deed restrictions and restrictive covenants.** Now the other half. Property Code § 202.001(4) defines a **restrictive covenant** as "any covenant, condition, or restriction contained in a dedicatory instrument, whether mandatory, prohibitive, permissive, or administrative." A **dedicatory instrument**, under § 202.001(1), is "each document governing the establishment, maintenance, or operation of a residential subdivision, planned unit development, condominium or townhouse regime, or any similar planned development," and expressly includes the declaration, the bylaws, the association's properly adopted rules, and all lawful amendments. Section 202.001(2) defines the **property owners' association** through which the owners or their board manage or regulate the development. Chapter 202 reaches back — § 202.002(a) applies it "to all restrictive covenants regardless of the date on which they were created" — and the interpretive rule cuts in favor of the restriction: § 202.003(a) provides that "[a] restrictive covenant shall be liberally construed to give effect to its purposes and intent," subject to the family-home limitation in § 202.003(b).

Enforcement powers are substantial. Section 202.004(a) provides that "[a]n exercise of discretionary authority by a property owners' association or other representative designated by an owner of real property concerning a restrictive covenant is presumed reasonable unless the court determines by a preponderance of the evidence that the exercise of discretionary authority was arbitrary, capricious, or discriminatory." Section 202.004(b) allows the association to initiate, defend, or intervene in litigation affecting enforcement, and § 202.004(c) permits a court to "assess civil damages for the violation of a restrictive covenant in an amount not to exceed $200 for each day of the violation." Property Code § 5.006(a) adds that in an action based on breach of a restrictive covenant the court "shall allow to a prevailing party who asserted the action reasonable attorney's fees in addition to the party's costs and claim."

A worked illustration, using figures chosen only to show the arithmetic and not drawn from any required schedule: an owner leaves a detached workshop that violates a recorded setback covenant in place for 90 days after judgment. At the § 202.004(c) ceiling of $200 per day, the maximum civil damages for that period would be 90 × $200 = $18,000, plus the prevailing claimant's reasonable attorney's fees under § 5.006(a). The point is not the number; it is that private restrictions in Texas carry a real, statutorily quantified downside on top of an injunction requiring removal.

**Where private restrictions live, and the limits on them.** Section 202.006(a) requires a property owners' association to file all dedicatory instruments in the real property records of each county where the property is located, and § 202.006(b) is the sentence to remember: "A dedicatory instrument has no effect until the instrument is filed in accordance with this section." Section 202.006(c) adds that an association may not collect a regular assessment if the instrument authorizing it was not filed. So the private control system is a **county real property records** system while the public control system is a **city hall** system; they are searched in different buildings.

The Legislature has also carved out categories no association may prohibit outright, each with detailed allowances letting the association regulate appearance and location instead: water-conserving landscaping and rainwater harvesting (§ 202.007); **solar energy devices** (§ 202.010); certain durable or solar-generating shingles that match the surrounding property (§ 202.011); display of the United States, Texas, and armed-forces flags (§ 202.012); permanently installed **standby electric generators** (§ 202.019); lawful possession of firearms and ammunition (§ 202.021); conforming swimming pool enclosures (§ 202.022); security measures including cameras and perimeter fencing (§ 202.023); and renting based on the tenant's **method of payment**, which § 202.024(a) defines to include a housing choice voucher (§ 202.024). When a client asks whether the HOA can stop a solar array, the answer is usually that it cannot prohibit one outright but can regulate how and where it is installed. Separately, Property Code § 5.026(a) provides that a restriction or deed provision prohibiting use by, or sale, lease, or transfer to, a person because of race, color, religion, or national origin "is void," and § 5.026(b) requires a court to dismiss a suit to enforce it.

**Private restrictions can be stricter than public zoning — and a city can sometimes enforce them.** Nothing requires a covenant to stop where the ordinance stops. A district may permit a 35-foot building while the declaration permits 28 feet; a district may permit a duplex while the covenant permits one single-family dwelling. The stricter of the two governs the owner's actual freedom, because each is independently enforceable by its own enforcer. Texas also lets certain municipalities enforce private restrictions directly. Under Local Government Code § 212.153(a) a covered municipality "may sue in any court of competent jurisdiction to enjoin or abate a violation of a restriction contained or incorporated by reference in a properly recorded plan, plat, or other instrument that affects a subdivision located inside the boundaries of the municipality," though § 212.153(b) bars that suit if a property owners' association with authority to enforce files its own. Section 212.152 defines the covered "restriction" broadly — use character, setbacks, lot and structure size, permitted activities, architectural features, fences, landscaping, noise, and required maintenance — which is, in substance, a list of the things zoning would otherwise do. Section 212.156(a) allows the governing body by ordinance to require compliance and to sue for a civil penalty.

**The practical rule for a license holder.** Two searches, two places, every time. The public side is found at the municipality: the zoning map and ordinance, the district's permitted uses and dimensional standards, the plat, whether the property is inside the city limits or in the ETJ, and whether the city has zoning at all. The private side is found in the county real property records: the recorded plat, the declaration and its amendments, and the association's dedicatory instruments, which under § 202.006(b) have no effect until filed there. Property Code § 5.012(a) requires a seller of residential property subject to POA membership to give the purchaser a written notice stating that the restrictive covenants and dedicatory instruments "have been or will be recorded in the Real Property Records of the county in which the property is located" and that copies "may be obtained from the county clerk" — the statute is telling the buyer exactly where to look. Reading the ordinance and stopping there, or reading the declaration and stopping there, is how a client ends up owning land that cannot be used the way the client bought it to be used.


For a contract example, assume a buyer intends to convert a residence into a daycare. The buyer needs more than confirmation that another daycare exists nearby. The inquiry includes the exact parcel’s governmental jurisdiction, applicable use and dimensional rules, recorded restrictions, licensing requirements for the business, and other relevant approvals. The agent should identify these issues and arrange timely factual investigation; counsel should address legal interpretation and a custom contingency if one is needed. A contract that merely says “buyer intends daycare” may state an intention without making the intended use a condition of the purchase.


### Statute reading exercise: Record title does not settle marital classification

Read Family Code §§ 3.001–3.003 and Estates Code §§ 101.002 and 111.001. A deed names two siblings, one of whom is married; the married sibling says the interest came by inheritance. Write the facts needed to distinguish co-ownership shares, separate/community character, and survivorship. Identify what the deed alone does not establish.

Feedback: The siblings’ undivided interests are distinct from the marital classification of one sibling’s share. Inheritance can be separate property, but classification and proof require the acquisition facts. Joint holding alone does not establish survivorship. The agent should gather the deed, inheritance and marital information, and any written survivorship agreement for title and legal review.

### Applied exercise: The yard behind the townhouse

A buyer wants to build a detached studio in the fenced yard of a townhouse. The listing calls the home “fee simple,” while a recorded declaration describes the yard as a limited common element. List the conflict, the documents needed, and the decision that must occur before an applicable investigation deadline.

Feedback: The ownership description is unresolved. Obtain the deed, declaration, plats/plans, applicable association rules, survey, and governmental requirements. The legal regime and authority for the proposed construction must be evaluated. The agent should not rely on the fence or marketing label, promise approval, or draft a custom use contingency.

### Applied exercise: The seller owns the company

An LLC member wants to sign personally as seller for land titled to the LLC. The title company requests a manager consent. Explain why owning the company and owning its asset are different, and prepare a fact list for the broker and title reviewer.

Feedback: The LLC is the apparent title holder; a member’s ownership interest does not alone prove authority to bind it. Identify the exact entity, governing documents, management structure, required consents, proposed conveyance, and signer’s capacity. The correct signature and authority determination should precede execution. Do not solve the problem by replacing the entity name with the member’s name.


### Module recap

Before negotiating, identify the legal interest, the land or unit it concerns, the actual holder, and the authority to act. Separate fixtures from movable items and third-party equipment. Distinguish ownership form from building style. Then examine public restrictions, private restrictions, and the buyer’s intended use independently. A correct classification guides document selection and investigation; it does not replace a professional title or legal determination.
