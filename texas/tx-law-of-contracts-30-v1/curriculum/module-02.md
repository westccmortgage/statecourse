# Topic 02: Basics of Real Estate Law

This topic builds the property vocabulary that every later contract topic will use. A contract does not operate on “a house” in the abstract. It operates on a legally described parcel, one or more estates or interests, identified improvements and accessories, a particular owner or entity, and a network of public and private restrictions. The license holder's first discipline is therefore classification: identify what the parties are trying to transfer, who owns it, what documents create or limit the interest, and which authority answers each question.

The sources in this topic perform different jobs. A constitutional provision can classify marital property. A statute can state a conveyance presumption, create a condominium regime, authorize zoning, or prohibit discrimination. A recorded declaration can define a particular community. A TREC form can allocate identified items between buyer and seller. StateCourse may provide a workflow for organizing those sources, but that workflow is not law. Keep those layers separate throughout the topic.

This is instruction for license-holder competence, not transaction-specific legal advice. When title, marital rights, entity authority, fixture ownership, or enforceability is genuinely disputed, preserve the facts and documents, involve the broker, and direct the parties to appropriate counsel or another qualified professional. The aim is not to turn a license holder into a title lawyer. It is to prevent a license holder from using a form before understanding the kind of property and interest the form is supposed to address.

## Unit: Real Property and the Law

Real-property law answers a set of connected but distinct questions. What thing or interest is being transferred? What kind of estate does the transferor hold? Which written instrument describes the transfer? Who may sign for the owner? What public rules and private instruments limit use? Which interests will continue after closing? A good contract intake keeps those questions separate long enough to find the correct source for each one.

### Property, interests, and instruments

In ordinary conversation, people use *property* to mean the physical place: the lot, residence, fence, driveway, trees, and view. In legal analysis, property also refers to enforceable interests concerning a thing. Different people may simultaneously hold or claim different interests in the same land. An owner may hold present possessory title; a tenant may hold a leasehold; a lender may hold a security interest; an easement holder may possess a defined use right; a purchaser may acquire contract rights before the deed is delivered; and a governmental body may exercise regulatory power. Saying “Jordan owns the property” does not resolve all of those interests.

The first practical lesson is to avoid collapsing the physical object, the legal interest, and the document. A deed is not the land. It is an instrument used to convey an interest. A lease is not fee ownership. It is an agreement creating or governing possessory rights for a term. A deed of trust is not an express trust merely because its title contains the word *trust*. A survey depicts matters within its scope; it does not itself create every interest it displays. A contract creates contractual rights and duties; it does not, merely by being signed, answer every title question that a deed, record, statute, or court must answer.

The licensing statute supplies a useful example of an interest-based definition. Tex. Occupations Code § 1101.002 defines “real estate” for that chapter as an interest in real property, including a leasehold, whether the property is inside or outside Texas, while excluding an interest given as security for the performance of an obligation. That definition serves the licensing chapter. It should not be silently exported as the universal definition for every code, form, tax rule, title policy, or private instrument. Definitions are often scoped to the law in which they appear.

### The estate conveyed is a legal question

Land can support present and future interests of different duration and quality. Fee simple is commonly the broadest private estate, but the label should follow the conveyance and governing law rather than a casual assumption. Tex. Property Code § 5.001 supplies a Texas conveyance rule: an estate in land that is conveyed or devised is a fee simple unless the estate is expressly limited or a lesser estate is conveyed or devised by construction or operation of law. The statute also removes the need for old common-law words of inheritance to create that result.

That presumption is powerful but bounded. It does not make every signer the true owner, cure a defective legal description, eliminate an existing easement, convert community property into separate property, or erase a recorded restriction. It answers the quantum-of-estate question when a conveyance is otherwise operative and no express limitation or legal rule produces a lesser estate. The words of the instrument and the rest of the governing law still matter.

Texas also permits future estates to be created during life. Tex. Property Code § 5.041 allows an inter vivos conveyance of an estate of freehold or inheritance that commences in the future, in the same manner as by will. For a license holder, the lesson is not to draft future interests. The lesson is to recognize a title description that is not a simple present fee and stop treating it as a routine seller-owns-everything fact. An estate or reservation—such as a life estate, remainder, reversion, leasehold, or mineral reservation—may divide the property interest, while a contract right such as an option presents a different kind of claim. Either can change who must sign and what can be promised.

### Authority order for a property question

Use a disciplined sequence when a property label appears:

1. **Identify the decision.** Are you classifying an estate, identifying included items, confirming the record owner, determining signature authority, or evaluating a use restriction?
2. **Read the instrument that creates or allocates the claimed right.** That may be a deed, lease, declaration, entity document, easement, contract paragraph, or court order.
3. **Read controlling law for that decision.** Use the exact statute, constitutional provision, ordinance, or controlling case rather than a broad chapter label.
4. **Check record evidence and transaction evidence.** A representation by a party is not the same as a county record, title commitment, survey, or governing document.
5. **Escalate unresolved legal conflict.** Do not convert a disputed title or ownership conclusion into a special-provisions sentence drafted by a license holder.

This order prevents two opposite errors. One is *document absolutism*: assuming a private document can override a nonwaivable law or the rights of someone who did not sign it. The other is *statute absolutism*: citing a general statute while ignoring the deed, declaration, lease, or entity agreement that defines the particular transaction. Competent analysis ordinarily needs both the governing rule and the operative facts and instruments.

### Interests can be divided along several dimensions

Property interests can be divided by time, space, use, ownership share, and security. A lease separates possession for a term from the landlord's remaining estate. A mineral reservation can separate subsurface interests from the surface estate. An easement can grant a defined use across another person's land. A condominium separates a unit interest from common-element rights defined by its governing regime. A mortgage or deed of trust secures an obligation without becoming the fee estate conveyed in a sale. Concurrent owners can share title while having unequal contributions, rights created by agreement, or distinct survivorship consequences.

Do not use one dimension to answer another. The fact that a buyer will possess the home does not prove the quality of title. The fact that a life tenant has present possession does not erase a remainder interest created by the governing instrument. The fact that a roof is physically attached does not decide whether a seller may remove leased solar equipment. The fact that a restrictive covenant permits residential use does not establish that municipal zoning also permits the planned activity.

### Contract practice begins with a proposition ledger

For each material property statement, record four fields: the proposition, its source, its status, and the next action. “Seller says the shed stays” is a party statement that requires contract allocation. “Title commitment names Cedar Lake LLC as vested owner” is a document fact that triggers entity-authority review. “Declaration prohibits short-term rentals” is a private restriction that must be verified in the recorded governing instrument. “City staff said a variance is likely” is not an issued variance. The ledger exposes assumptions before they become promises.

The method also keeps legal conclusions out of factual entries. A license holder can accurately enter a street address or identify an agreed exclusion in the authorized field. The license holder should not announce that a spouse has no homestead right, that a deed created valid survivorship, or that an easement was abandoned merely because the transaction would be easier if that were true. Those are legal conclusions requiring the right documents and professional judgment.

### Checkpoint

When a party says, “I am selling my property,” ask: *Which interest, held by whom, shown by which source, subject to which other interests, and transferred by which instrument?* If those questions sound repetitive, that is useful. Repetition at intake is cheaper than discovering at closing that the named seller holds only a future interest while another person has present possession, or that the seller owns only a life estate while another person holds the remainder.

## Unit: Land, Real Estate, and Real Property

The words *land*, *real estate*, and *real property* overlap in practice, but they can focus attention on different parts of the transaction. This course uses a working vocabulary rather than claiming that one sentence supplies a universal Texas definition. **Land** points to the physical parcel and the natural space associated with it. **Real estate** is frequently used for the land and improvements or, in a scoped statute, for an identified interest in real property. **Real property** emphasizes legally recognized interests and rights concerning land. Always let the governing source define its own term.

### The physical parcel and its legal description

A street address is useful for finding a property; it is not a substitute for the legal description that identifies what will be conveyed. The legal description may use lot-and-block references, metes and bounds, condominium-unit descriptions, or another record-based method. A parcel may include more or less land than a fence line or lawn suggests. Encroachments, boundary overlaps, access questions, and easements demonstrate why visible occupation and legal identity are not identical.

The transaction team therefore uses different documents for different functions. The contract identifies the property according to its fields and attachments. A deed carries the conveyance description. A survey can depict boundaries and visible matters within its certification and scope. A title commitment states the proposed insurer's conditions, exceptions, and requirements; it is not itself a deed or a guarantee that every physical condition appears. Tax records may assist identification but do not replace the vesting instrument.

Tex. Occupations Code § 1101.002 again offers a deliberately scoped example: for the licensing chapter, real estate includes an interest in real property, including a leasehold, and may concern property outside Texas. The definition tells us that regulated brokerage can concern an interest rather than a whole physical parcel. It does not decide whether the seller holds fee simple, whether the lease is assignable, or whether an encumbrance will remain after closing.

### Improvements and accessories are transaction categories

The physical land may carry buildings, mechanical systems, landscaping, paving, fencing, utilities, and other improvements. A sale contract must address what is included because human expectations are unreliable. One person sees a wall-mounted television bracket as part of the home; another sees it as part of a personal electronics system. One person sees a leased propane tank as attached equipment; another sees it as a third party's asset. An item can be physically present without being owned by the seller.

TREC Form 20-19, page 1 provides a current instrument example rather than a universal property-law definition. Paragraph 1 identifies the parties and land. Paragraph 2 groups the land together with listed improvements and accessories, then provides a place for exclusions. The form's list and allocation matter in an eligible transaction because the parties' agreement is expressed through those terms. The list does not prove the seller owns a leased or third-party item, cure a lien, or decide a dispute outside the contract.

Read the form's categories operationally. “Improvements” can include structures and permanently installed or built-in items identified by the paragraph. “Accessories” can include specified items that may not satisfy every common-law fixture formulation but are allocated by the agreement. “Exclusions” allow the parties to identify items not included. Do not assume a generic word like *appliances* resolves whether a freestanding refrigerator, built-in oven, wine cooler, or garage freezer is included. Use the actual current form language and the facts.

### Horizontal, vertical, and severed interests

Land is not only a flat surface. Interests can concern airspace, subsurface minerals, water-related rights, access, support, or use at different elevations. A condominium is an obvious example of a legally described unit within a larger structure plus common interests. Mineral ownership may be severed from surface ownership. An easement may cross one part of a parcel. A utility interest may occupy a defined corridor. The purchaser's intended use may depend on dimensions or rights that a street view cannot show.

A license holder should not promise that title includes every imaginable vertical or subsurface right. Instead, identify the buyer's objective and route it into the documents and professional reviews designed to answer it. If the buyer needs mineral rights, water access, rooftop rights, parking, storage, or an unobstructed view, the issue must be investigated specifically. The broad phrase “the property” is not evidence that every one of those rights transfers.

### Natural components and human additions

Soil, naturally growing vegetation, and water features may be discussed as physical aspects of land, while buildings and installed systems are human additions. That distinction helps observation but does not independently determine ownership or contract allocation. Harvested crops, timber contracts, nursery stock, trade fixtures, leased equipment, manufactured housing, and removable improvements can require specialized rules and documents. A natural-versus-artificial label is therefore a starting prompt, not the final answer.

Ask three questions about a visible item:

- What is it physically, and how is it connected to the parcel?
- Who owns it or claims rights in it, according to what contract, filing, lease, or record?
- How does the sale contract allocate it between buyer and seller?

The answers can diverge. The seller may agree that an item stays but lack authority to transfer it because a lessor owns it. The buyer may expect a planted tree to remain, while a separate timber agreement affects it. A contract may exclude an installed item, yet removal could damage the improvements or violate another agreement. The purpose of classification is to reveal the additional question, not to force every item into a simplistic rule.

### Reading a property paragraph without inventing facts

Work from the document outward. First, identify the contract's land description and any referenced exhibit. Second, list the improvements and accessories covered by the current paragraph. Third, list every written exclusion. Fourth, compare that allocation with observations, seller statements, leases, disclosures, and title or lien information. Fifth, flag inconsistent or third-party claims. Do not silently “fix” ambiguity with custom legal language. Use an approved form or addendum when applicable and send nonstandard ownership or drafting questions to the broker and counsel.

The method distinguishes *included by agreement* from *owned and transferable*. A buyer can contract for delivery of an item, yet the seller's inability to convey it may create a dispute. A license holder's intake should surface that risk before execution: “Is the solar system owned, financed, or leased?” “Is the water softener included in a service agreement?” “Does anyone else claim the storage shed?” Those are factual questions. The legal consequences of the answers may require documents and professional review.

### Applied exercise: build the property stack

A buyer describes a desired purchase as “the blue house, everything attached, the dock, the mineral rights, and the parking space.” The listing shows a street address. The seller says the dock is shared, the solar panels are leased, and the reserved parking space is assigned by an association.

Prepare a four-column property stack. In column one, list each desired physical feature or interest. In column two, identify the document that could establish it: legal description, deed, survey, solar lease, association declaration, parking assignment, mineral reservation, or another record. In column three, classify the present information as verified, party-reported, conflicting, or unknown. In column four, state the next permissible action for the license holder.

Do not draft a clause to solve an unknown legal interest. Your completed stack should show why a street address and the phrase “everything attached” are insufficient, and why the form's allocation must be reconciled with third-party ownership and governing documents.

## Unit: Real Property vs. Personal Property

The real-versus-personal distinction affects what the parties expect to transfer, what the contract must identify, what a lender may claim, and what can be removed. Yet the classification should not be reduced to “attached means real, unattached means personal.” Contract language, ownership, financing, statutory rules, and the facts of installation can all matter. A license holder's safest role is to identify the issue, use the authorized form accurately, and avoid manufacturing a legal conclusion for a disputed item.

### Begin with the two legal objects

Real property concerns interests in land and the improvements or rights treated as part of it under governing law and documents. Personal property includes tangible movable items and intangible rights that are not themselves an estate in land. A chair is ordinarily tangible personal property. A deed transfers a land interest; an assignment may transfer a contract right; a bill of sale may be used for identified personal property. The correct instrument follows the interest, not the buyer's informal description.

An object can change classification or create competing claims. Goods installed in a building may become fixtures. Crops can be part of a land transaction or separately treated under particular rules. Materials incorporated into construction can lose a separate goods identity for secured-transactions purposes. Business equipment may be installed for a tenant's use yet remain subject to lease rights or removal duties. The transaction facts must therefore include who acquired the item, who paid for it, how it was installed, what agreements govern it, and how the contract allocates it.

### The contract allocation matters

TREC Form 20-19, page 1 demonstrates a practical allocation. Its property paragraph identifies the land and then addresses improvements and accessories, while permitting written exclusions. The instrument reduces uncertainty by naming categories and common items. It does not ask the license holder to decide an abstract fixture case before every entry. It asks the parties, through an authorized form, to express the transaction they are making.

That allocation has limits. A seller cannot convey what the seller does not own merely by leaving an item on an included list. A buyer's assumption that a visible item is included does not override an express exclusion. A third-party lease may create return, payment, or transfer conditions. A lien or security interest may survive unless handled through the appropriate payoff, release, assumption, or consent process. Physical presence is evidence of location, not proof of title.

### Article 9 supplies a bounded fixture rule

Tex. Business and Commerce Code § 9.334 is a held statutory source for a specific secured-transactions problem. It recognizes that goods may become fixtures and states that Article 9 does not prevent creation of an encumbrance on fixtures under real-property law. It also says that a security interest under Article 9 does not exist in ordinary building materials incorporated into an improvement on land. The section then supplies priority rules between a security interest in fixtures and an encumbrancer or owner of the related real property, including a purchase-money rule tied to a fixture filing made before or within the statutory twenty-day window after the goods become fixtures.

Do not turn that bounded statute into the slogan “anything bolted down belongs to the buyer.” The section addresses secured interests and priorities; it does not provide this course with a held appellate opinion establishing one universal Texas common-law fixture test for every ownership dispute. The source gap is deliberate. For instruction here, use the current contract's allocation, identify third-party or financing claims, and apply the held statutory rules only to the proposition they actually support.

Ordinary building materials deserve special attention. Lumber, drywall, roofing, concrete, and wiring incorporated into an improvement are not treated by the section as separately secured goods under Article 9 after incorporation. That does not answer every construction-payment or lien question; other law may apply. It simply prevents a learner from assuming that every component inside a building remains a separately removable financed good under this statute.

### A five-question item screen

For each disputed item, record:

1. **Identity:** What exactly is the item, including serial number or service agreement if relevant?
2. **Connection:** Is it freestanding, connected for ordinary use, built in, embedded, or incorporated into an improvement?
3. **Ownership:** Did the seller buy it, lease it, finance it, borrow it, or receive it with restrictions?
4. **Contract treatment:** Does the current authorized form include it, exclude it, or require another instrument?
5. **Competing claim:** Is there a lessor, secured party, tenant, association, contractor, or other person whose rights must be addressed?

This is an intake screen, not a substitute for legal analysis. The words *fixture* and *personal property* should not appear as unsupported conclusions in a file note. Record the facts that support the classification and the source that supplies the consequence. If the parties disagree, the license holder should not pick a winner or draft an improvised removal remedy.

### Common transaction examples

A freestanding refrigerator may be treated differently from a built-in oven under a contract list, but the actual current paragraph controls. A television may be removed while a mounting bracket is included, depending on the form. A leased security system may be physically integrated but governed by a service contract. A tenant's commercial shelving may be subject to a lease removal clause. A manufactured home may require title and attachment analysis beyond its appearance. A portable generator may be personal property even if wired through a transfer switch. Each example demonstrates why fact collection precedes classification.

Consider solar equipment. The panels may be owned outright, financed with a lien, subject to a power-purchase agreement, or leased. Those arrangements can look identical from the curb. The seller's statement “the solar comes with the house” is not enough. Obtain the governing documents, determine what the approved transaction forms require, and let qualified professionals address lien, assumption, payoff, or transfer consequences.

### Risk controls for removal and delivery

When an excluded item will be removed, consider the practical condition of the property afterward. Removal may leave holes, disconnected utilities, unfinished surfaces, or code issues. The standard form may already allocate repair or delivery obligations; nonstandard legal drafting is outside the license holder's role. The license holder can document observations, communicate the parties' stated concerns, and involve the broker or counsel early.

At final walk-through, compare the agreed allocation to the property's condition. Do not wait until possession to discover that an accessory disappeared or a leased item remains without transfer documents. A factual checklist can identify whether the item is present, removed, damaged, or replaced. It should not announce a legal remedy. If the observed facts conflict with the contract, follow the brokerage's escalation process.

### Statute reading exercise: classify the proposition, not just the object

Read the held text of the fixture provision and page 1 of the current resale contract. For each statement below, identify whether the source proves it, does not prove it, or requires another source:

1. Goods may become fixtures.
2. Ordinary building materials incorporated into an improvement remain separately secured goods under Article 9.
3. A purchase-money fixture filing can receive priority if the statutory timing and other conditions are met.
4. Every wall-mounted object is automatically owned by the fee owner.
5. The resale form allocates listed improvements, accessories, and exclusions between the parties.
6. Inclusion in the form proves the seller has title free of every third-party claim.

For each answer, write the exact fact still needed. The successful response will distinguish contract allocation from ownership and secured-priority analysis and will refuse to derive a universal fixture rule from a statute that does not state one.

## Unit: Characteristics of Real Property

Real estate education commonly groups property characteristics into physical and economic categories. Those categories can help a learner anticipate transaction concerns, but the labels are not self-proving rules of Texas law. The **StateCourse property-characteristics framework** used here is provider methodology. It organizes observation and due diligence; it does not determine value, title, appraisal results, environmental condition, or legal rights.

### Physical observation: location is fixed

A parcel cannot be moved to a better school district, away from a floodplain, or closer to employment. Improvements can be altered and some structures can be relocated, but the geographic situs of the legally described parcel remains fixed. That fixed location makes off-site facts unusually important. Access, neighboring uses, road projects, topography, drainage, utilities, zoning, deed restrictions, and emergency services can affect the usefulness of the same building design.

The transaction implication is to investigate place-specific facts rather than rely on a generic property type. Two identical houses can have different legal descriptions, easements, jurisdictions, utility providers, tax districts, restrictions, insurance conditions, and market demand. A buyer's objective—operate a home business, build a workshop, keep animals, rent short-term, add an accessory unit—must be tested against the particular parcel and governing documents.

### Physical observation: durability is not invulnerability

Traditional shorthand sometimes calls land indestructible. That word is too absolute for competent transaction practice. The parcel's legal identity may continue while soil erodes, a structure burns, subsidence occurs, contamination impairs use, a shoreline changes, or public action alters access. Improvements can depreciate, become obsolete, require major repair, or be removed. StateCourse therefore uses *durability with physical vulnerability*: land interests can be long-lived, while the physical condition and utility of the site and improvements can change materially.

This distinction matters for evidence. A past photograph does not prove current condition. A seller's repairs do not prove code compliance. A structure's continued existence does not prove insurability. A flood map does not replace the buyer's full insurance and inspection investigation. The license holder should recognize the issue, use required disclosures and contract processes, and avoid guaranteeing physical performance.

### Physical observation: parcels are specifically situated and not perfect substitutes

No two legal parcels occupy the same space. Even adjacent units or lots have different positions, orientations, views, access paths, noise exposure, or governing allocations. This does not mean every difference increases value or that a license holder may invent an appraisal adjustment. It means comparisons require judgment about relevant similarities and differences.

In contract intake, uniqueness raises the cost of a mistaken description. A buyer cannot necessarily receive an equivalent parcel if the promised one cannot be conveyed. It also explains why a property-specific inspection, title review, and document review matter. The learner should avoid the opposite exaggeration: uniqueness does not mean market comparison is impossible. It means the comparison must be reasoned and properly sourced.

### Economic observation: scarcity is use- and market-specific

Land is finite, but economically meaningful scarcity depends on the property type, permissible use, location, supply alternatives, infrastructure, and demand. A rural tract and an infill commercial corner may face different constraints. A zoning change can alter the supply of land usable for a purpose. Infrastructure can make previously impractical land usable. Environmental or access limitations can reduce effective supply.

Do not convert the concept into a sales promise that “land always appreciates.” Price can fall. Improvements can lose value. Demand can shift. Taxes, insurance, financing, regulation, and maintenance can change. Scarcity is one lens for asking why buyers compete for a particular use and location; it is not a forecast.

### Economic observation: improvements and surrounding investment interact

Capital invested on one parcel can affect nearby use and perception, while public and private investment in roads, schools, utilities, employment centers, parks, or disamenities can affect the area. Traditional instruction sometimes describes this as modification or the effect of improvements. StateCourse uses it as a due-diligence prompt: identify material existing and proposed conditions without implying control over them.

A planned road can improve access or add noise. A new employer can increase demand or traffic. A major repair may improve a building without returning every dollar in price. A neighboring use may support or undermine the buyer's objective. The license holder should distinguish verified current facts, publicly documented proposals, private plans, and speculation.

### Economic observation: real-estate investment is often long-lived

Roads, utilities, foundations, and major structures involve capital that cannot be redeployed as easily as many movable assets. Ownership transactions also carry financing, tax, title, and closing costs. This relative permanence can make errors expensive and due diligence valuable. It does not mean the owner can never change use, demolish improvements, or sell quickly. It describes the tendency of substantial real-estate investment to have long consequences.

For a buyer, the prompt is: what assumptions must remain true long enough for the planned investment to work? For a seller, the prompt is: what completed work, permits, warranties, leases, or obligations must be accurately disclosed or delivered? For a license holder, the prompt is: which claims require a qualified inspector, engineer, appraiser, lender, insurer, surveyor, title professional, tax adviser, or attorney?

### Economic observation: location preference is contextual

Area preference—sometimes called situs—reflects the market's response to location for a particular use and time. “Good location” is not a neutral fact. It can hide subjective judgments, demographic assumptions, unsupported school claims, or a failure to ask what the client actually values. Replace it with objective, client-directed investigation: commute time from a chosen point, verified transit access, published zoning, lot dimensions, proximity to a named facility, or another stated criterion.

StateCourse's provider method does not use demographic composition or coded desirability language as a location proxy. When a client asks for a subjective neighborhood judgment, translate the request into objective features, offer neutral sources consistently, and let the client evaluate them. The fair-housing unit later in this topic supplies the legal source layer; this provider-method paragraph does not classify a violation.

### The characteristics worksheet

Use four columns: observed characteristic, possible transaction effect, evidence needed, and professional boundary. “Fixed location near proposed road” may lead to a noise and access concern; evidence could include the public project record and survey; the boundary is no guarantee of completion or value. “Older foundation” may lead to an inspection concern; evidence could include reports and permits; the boundary is no structural diagnosis by the license holder. “Limited comparable supply” may affect marketing; evidence could include a competent comparative analysis; the boundary is no assurance of future appreciation.

This worksheet turns broad concepts into questions without pretending the concepts themselves decide the transaction. A sound entry never says “indestructible, therefore safe,” “unique, therefore worth more,” or “scarce, therefore guaranteed to appreciate.” It states the fact, the evidence, and the next action.

### Guided practice: convert characteristics into neutral diligence

A buyer wants a hillside home for a ten-year hold, values quiet outdoor space, and plans a detached studio. The listing advertises “irreplaceable views in the area's best neighborhood.” Public materials show a proposed road project nearby; the seller reports prior drainage work; the lot is subject to recorded restrictions; and no one has verified whether a detached studio is allowed.

Create a diligence plan using the StateCourse framework. For each physical or economic observation, write one neutral question, one source of evidence, and one prohibited leap. Include location, physical vulnerability, parcel specificity, surrounding investment, long-lived capital, and location preference. Do not use demographic composition or subjective neighborhood labels. Your plan should route structural, legal, land-use, appraisal, and insurance questions to the appropriate qualified source while preserving the buyer's stated goals.

## Unit: Forms of Real Estate Ownership

“Ownership” is not one box. A person may hold a present fee estate, a life estate, a future interest, an undivided concurrent share, marital-property rights, a beneficial trust interest, or ownership of an entity that itself owns land. Each structure changes the questions asked at intake. Who holds record title? Who has possession? Who must sign? What happens at death? Can one owner convey alone? Does a private agreement alter the default? Which interest—not merely which person—is the contract supposed to transfer?

### Fee simple and lesser estates

Tex. Property Code § 5.001 establishes a fee-simple presumption for an estate conveyed or devised unless the estate is expressly limited or a lesser estate arises by construction or operation of law. The presumption makes a deed easier to read, but it is not a title guarantee. A deed can convey fee simple subject to easements, covenants, liens, mineral reservations, or other interests. The grantor can convey only what the grantor has authority to convey.

A life estate divides present possession from the future interest. The life tenant's interest ordinarily measures duration by a life, while a remainderman or reversion holder has the future interest. The license holder should not assume that a life tenant alone can promise delivery of an unencumbered fee. Obtain title evidence and involve counsel or the title professional to determine required parties and documents. The same caution applies whenever a deed, will, trust, divorce decree, or court order shows divided present and future rights.

Tex. Property Code § 5.041 confirms that Texas permits an inter vivos conveyance of a future freehold or inheritance estate. The provision demonstrates that a future interest is not merely academic. A recorded instrument can create an interest that commences later. The license holder's task is recognition and escalation, not drafting a remainder or deciding whether a condition is valid.

### Concurrent ownership: share and survivorship are different questions

When two or more people hold title, identify the share each holds and then ask separately what occurs at death. Joint ownership by itself does not answer survivorship in Texas. Tex. Estates Code § 101.002 states the default that a jointly held interest does not automatically survive to the other joint owner; the decedent's interest passes as if it had been severed. That rule blocks the common mistake of treating every jointly titled Texas parcel as automatic survivorship property.

Tex. Estates Code § 111.001 provides a route for joint owners to create survivorship: they may agree in writing that the deceased owner's interest survives to the remaining owner or owners. The statute also says the agreement cannot be inferred merely from joint ownership. Therefore, a vesting line containing two names is not enough to promise a survivorship result. The actual written survivorship agreement and its compliance must be reviewed.

For contract practice, separate at least four propositions:

- **Current title:** who is shown as holding the interest now?
- **Fractional or undivided share:** what portion does each holder own, if the instrument states one?
- **Management or possession:** what agreement, court order, lease, or law governs use and decisions?
- **Death transfer:** is there a valid written survivorship agreement, or will the interest pass through another route?

Do not use loose labels such as “joint tenant” to answer all four. A title commitment, vesting deed, survivorship document, probate record, and marital-property instrument can serve different functions.

### Marital property is not a name-on-the-deed shortcut

Texas marital-property analysis begins above the statutory level. Tex. Const. art. XVI, § 15 defines a spouse's separate real and personal property to include property owned or claimed before marriage and property acquired afterward by gift, devise, or descent. It also authorizes spouses, in writing and subject to the provision, to partition or exchange community property and to agree that separate property becomes community property. The constitutional text makes acquisition source and valid agreement important; the name appearing on a deed does not alone resolve every marital right.

That matters when only one spouse appears to be the seller, when property was acquired before marriage but used as a family home, when proceeds were mixed, or when spouses signed a partition or conversion agreement. Homestead, community-property, separate-property, reimbursement, and creditor questions can be fact-intensive. A license holder should collect accurate facts, obtain the relevant instruments, and avoid declaring that a spouse “has no rights” or “does not need to sign.” Broker, title, and legal review should occur before a party is promised a signature result.

Community-property survivorship has its own written route. Tex. Estates Code § 112.051 allows spouses to agree that all or part of existing or later-acquired community property becomes the surviving spouse's property at death. Tex. Estates Code § 112.052 requires the agreement to be written and signed by both spouses, supplies phrases sufficient to create the right, and says survivorship cannot be inferred merely from a joint-account or similar label. Again, the recurring discipline is document first, conclusion second.

### Trust ownership requires identifying the trust relationship

The word *trust* appears in several unrelated settings. Tex. Property Code § 111.003 limits “trust” under the Texas Trust Code to an express trust and expressly excludes, among other things, a business trust and a security instrument such as a deed of trust, mortgage, or security interest. A deed of trust securing a loan is therefore not the same ownership arrangement as an express trust holding property for beneficiaries.

Tex. Property Code § 111.004 describes an express trust as a fiduciary relationship concerning property in which the person holding title has equitable duties to deal with the property for another person's benefit or a qualifying purpose. It identifies roles such as settlor, trustee, and beneficiary. For transaction intake, determine who holds title, in what capacity, under which trust instrument, and with what authority to sell or lease. A beneficiary's economic interest does not automatically make the beneficiary the person who executes a deed for the trust property.

The license holder should not interpret a trust instrument's disputed powers or advise on trust administration. Obtain the certification, trust excerpts, trustee documentation, court order, or other material requested by the title company or counsel. Keep personal data secure and collect only what the process requires. The objective is to verify execution authority, not to perform estate planning.

### Ownership, possession, and security may be split

A tenant can possess without owning the fee. A lender can hold a lien without owning the borrower's estate. A trustee under a deed of trust can have powers defined by a security instrument without occupying the role of an express-trust trustee. A life tenant can possess while another holds the remainder. Co-owners can share title while a separate agreement allocates use. Those divisions explain why possession, record title, beneficial interest, and control must not be treated as synonyms.

When a seller says “I have always paid for it,” record the statement but locate the instrument that shows the claimed interest. When a family member says “Mom left it to all of us,” obtain probate or title evidence rather than guessing who may sign. When an investor says “my company owns it,” determine whether the deed names the company or the individual. The facts may eventually support the statement, but the statement is not the evidence.

### A signature-authority workflow

Before treating the named party as able to convey, assemble:

1. the current vesting evidence and legal description;
2. any deed, probate, trust, marital, divorce, entity, power-of-attorney, or court document affecting authority;
3. the capacity in which each signer will act;
4. title-company or attorney requirements for proof and execution; and
5. unresolved interests that require consent, joinder, release, or another instrument.

Do not use a sales contract to adjudicate title. The contract can state the parties' obligations and closing framework; title defects and signature authority must be handled through the proper evidence and instruments. A special-provisions sentence written by a license holder cannot transform a non-owner into an owner or validate an otherwise ineffective survivorship arrangement.

### Ownership classification drill

For each situation, state only the next source needed—not the final legal conclusion:

- a deed names two unmarried owners but contains no survivorship language;
- a married seller acquired the home before marriage and later refinanced it;
- a deed names “River Trust,” while the contract identifies an individual beneficiary as seller;
- a will gives one person possession for life and another the property at the first person's death;
- two spouses show an unsigned note saying “everything goes to the survivor.”

A competent response identifies the vesting deed, written survivorship agreement, constitutional and marital documents, trust authority, probate evidence, and title or legal review as appropriate. It does not infer survivorship from two names, infer separate-property freedom from acquisition date alone, or infer trustee authority from a beneficiary's request.

## Unit: Ownership of Real Estate by Business Organizations

A business can own real estate as an entity distinct from the people who own or manage that business. That separation changes the contract party, vesting, signature capacity, delivery of notices, representations, and closing documents. The central error to avoid is substituting an owner's name for the entity shown in title records.

### Start with entity identity

Tex. Business Organizations Code § 1.002 supplies statutory definitions for the Code. It treats an organization as a broad category that can include corporations, partnerships, limited liability companies, business trusts, real estate investment trusts, cooperatives, associations, and other organizations. Its definition of “owner,” for purposes of Titles 1, 7, and 8, varies with the entity type—for example, a shareholder, partner, or LLC member. Read each definition within its stated scope rather than exporting it to a title the statute does not name.

The learner should therefore record the entity's exact legal name, jurisdiction, entity type, assumed name if relevant, and claimed signer's capacity. “Bluebird,” “Bluebird Properties,” and “Bluebird Properties, LLC” are not safely interchangeable. Search results or business cards do not replace the vesting deed and current entity record. A trade name may describe branding without being the legal person that holds title.

### An entity has property powers, but authority still must be shown

Subject to the Code's exceptions and limits, Tex. Business Organizations Code § 2.101 gives a domestic entity broad powers comparable to an individual, including acquiring, owning, improving, using, dealing in, and disposing of property. The listed powers include selling, conveying, mortgaging, pledging, leasing, exchanging, and otherwise disposing of property, as well as making contracts and incurring liabilities.

That general capacity does not prove that any employee, member, manager, officer, or partner may bind the entity in a particular deal. Capacity and authority are separate. Governing documents, resolutions, statutory rules, delegated authority, title requirements, and actual facts may matter. A license holder should not draft a corporate resolution or give an opinion on contested authority. Obtain the evidence requested by title or counsel and identify the signer's capacity accurately.

### LLC ownership illustrates entity-asset separation

Tex. Business Organizations Code § 101.106 states that an LLC membership interest is personal property. It also says that a member or assignee does not have an interest in specific company property. In plain operational language, a member does not own a direct slice of each company asset merely because the member owns part or all of the LLC.

Suppose Ava is the sole member of 700 Oaks LLC, and the deed names 700 Oaks LLC. Ava may control the company under its governing documents, but the contract seller should not automatically be written as “Ava, individually.” The entity owns the parcel; Ava owns a membership interest. The file must establish how Ava is authorized to act for the entity and how her capacity should appear in the signature block. Selling the membership interest is also not automatically the same transaction as conveying the parcel.

The distinction protects both sides. A buyer needs the actual owner bound. The entity needs the signer to act within authority. The title company needs consistent vesting and execution evidence. The license holder needs to avoid representing a private conclusion about alter ego, veil piercing, or beneficial ownership as though it changed record title.

### Partnership property follows statutory acquisition rules

Tex. Business Organizations Code § 152.101 states that partnership property is not the partners' property and that a partner or partner's spouse has no interest in partnership property. Thus, even a large economic share in a partnership is not direct ownership of each parcel the partnership owns. The title inquiry follows the partnership and the instrument by which it acquired the land.

Tex. Business Organizations Code § 152.102 then supplies classification rules and presumptions. Property acquired in the partnership's name is partnership property. Property acquired in one or more partners' names can also be partnership property when the transfer instrument indicates partner capacity or the existence of the partnership. Acquisition with partnership property creates a presumption of partnership property. Conversely, property acquired in partners' names without an indicated partner capacity or partnership and without partnership funds is presumed to be the partners' property even if used for partnership purposes.

Those rules show why “the partnership uses the building” is incomplete. Use and ownership can differ. Read the deed, identify the named grantee and capacity, and gather the partnership evidence. Do not solve a mismatch by changing the contract party without title and legal guidance.

### Corporations, trusts, cooperatives, and REIT labels

Corporations generally separate shareholder ownership from corporate assets in the same basic way: the shareholder owns shares; the corporation holds its property. Cooperative organizations and real estate investment trusts require their own governing structure. The broad definitions in the Business Organizations Code help classify the organization but do not prove a particular parcel's title or a signer's authority.

Tex. Business Organizations Code § 200.001 defines a Texas real estate investment trust for Chapter 200 as an unincorporated trust formed by one or more trust managers under Chapters 200 and 3 and managed under Chapter 200. Do not assume every investment vehicle called a “REIT” was formed under Texas Chapter 200; federal tax classification and another jurisdiction's entity law may also be relevant. Confirm the actual organization and governing documents.

### Entity diligence without practicing law

The license holder can collect and reconcile factual records:

- exact owner name from vesting evidence;
- entity name and status from an official filing source;
- claimed manager, member, officer, partner, trustee, or authorized representative;
- governing or authority evidence requested for closing;
- consistency among the listing, contract, title commitment, signature block, and closing documents.

The license holder should not declare that an entity is “valid,” that a manager's disputed act is binding, or that internal approval is unnecessary. Those conclusions belong to qualified legal and title review. A clean file note says what was reviewed, what matched, what did not, and who is resolving the mismatch.

### Entity intake example

The listing agreement names “Morgan Reed d/b/a Reed Holdings.” The deed names “Reed Holdings, LP.” Morgan says she owns all of the company through an LLC and wants the buyer's contract to name her individually to simplify financing. This file presents at least three distinct persons or labels: Morgan, the limited partnership, and a possible LLC owner or general partner.

Do not choose among them based on convenience. Obtain vesting and entity documentation, identify the partnership and any general partner, ask title or counsel what authority evidence is required, and use the correct authorized form fields. The fact that Morgan may ultimately control the chain does not make her individually the record owner. The contracting and signature structure must follow verified ownership and authority.

## Unit: Condominiums, Cooperatives, Town Houses & Time-Shares

These labels describe very different legal and economic arrangements. A condominium is an ownership regime created by the recorded instrument required for its applicable Texas chapter. A cooperative commonly combines an interest in an entity with occupancy rights under governing documents, but this course does not claim one universal Texas cooperative-housing statute from the held sources. A townhouse describes a building form or community label and does not alone identify the title estate. A timeshare creates recurring-use interests governed by a specialized statute. Never select a contract or explain ownership from the marketing label alone.

### Condominiums: the creating instrument and date control

Texas has a date-sensitive statutory structure. Tex. Property Code § 81.0011 generally confines Chapter 81 to condominium regimes created before January 1, 1994. Tex. Property Code § 82.002 generally governs condominium declarations recorded on or after that date and also applies specified Chapter 82 provisions to older regimes. The applicable creating-instrument date selects the starting chapter, although a complete analysis may require both the chapter and the particular creating and governing instruments.

Under the older framework, Tex. Property Code § 81.101 describes creation through a recorded master deed, master lease, or declaration satisfying the chapter. Under the Uniform Condominium Act, Tex. Property Code § 82.051 says a condominium is created only by recording a declaration executed in the same manner as a deed by the required holders of interests in the real property. The declaration must be recorded in each county where a portion is located, and creation does not displace governmental platting or building-code authority.

The practical lesson is decisive: a building does not become a condominium because units are separately occupied or marketed. For an older Chapter 81 regime, locate the recorded master deed, master lease, or declaration that created it; for a Chapter 82 regime, locate the recorded declaration. Then review amendments, the unit description, plats or plans, association records, and applicable resale information. The deeded interest ordinarily combines the unit with an allocated interest in common elements and obligations under the regime.

Tex. Property Code § 81.107 illustrates the common-element relationship for the older chapter: apartment owners share ownership of the common elements and may use them for their intended purposes without interfering with other owners' lawful rights. A buyer is not purchasing only the interior space seen during a showing. The buyer is also entering a recorded governance and expense structure.

### Condominium diligence

Before representing a unit transaction as routine, reconcile:

- the unit's legal description and declaration date;
- declaration, bylaws, rules, plats, plans, and amendments;
- common and limited common elements such as parking, storage, balconies, roofs, or yards;
- association assessments, special assessments, budgets, insurance, litigation, and resale materials within the applicable process;
- use, leasing, pet, architectural, and occupancy restrictions; and
- the contract and addenda required for the transaction.

Do not promise that a parking space is deeded simply because it is numbered, or that the owner may rent short-term because another resident does. The declaration may allocate the space as a limited common element, a separate unit, an association assignment, or another right. A current practice can violate a recorded restriction or depend on an approval not transferable to the buyer.

### Cooperatives: identify the actual bundle

Tex. Business Organizations Code § 1.002 defines cooperative and cooperative association in the business-entity code. Those definitions help recognize an organization but do not establish that every cooperative-housing resident owns a deeded real-property unit. In a common cooperative structure, an entity may hold title to the real estate while a participant owns an entity interest coupled with contractual occupancy rights. The exact bundle depends on the entity and governing documents.

This module deliberately does not claim a single held Texas cooperative-housing regime. Therefore, do not analogize automatically from condominium law. Ask: who is the record owner of the land; what interest does the participant own; what document grants occupancy; how is transfer approved; what financing and lien structure applies; and what governing restrictions bind the participant? Route unresolved classification and drafting issues to qualified counsel and the title or closing professionals appropriate to the transaction.

### Town houses: structure is not title

Tex. Property Code § 202.001 includes a townhouse regime within the definition of a dedicatory-instrument setting. That recognition does not convert *townhouse* into one uniform ownership estate. A row-style home can be a fee-simple lot governed by restrictions, a condominium unit, a leasehold, or another documented interest. The label on the sign or listing cannot answer which.

Verify the deed and legal description. Then review the declaration, plat, association documents, party-wall or maintenance arrangements, common-area rights, and insurance responsibilities. A fee-simple townhouse owner may own the lot and structure while sharing obligations through recorded covenants. A condominium townhouse owner may own a unit defined by declaration and share common elements. The correct form, disclosures, and diligence follow the actual regime.

### Timeshares: recurring use plus nonwaivable consumer protections

Tex. Property Code § 221.002 defines a detailed vocabulary for the Texas Timeshare Act, including accommodation, association, assessment, developer, managing entity, timeshare interest, timeshare plan, and timeshare property. The definitions show that a timeshare cannot be understood merely as “part ownership of a vacation home.” The purchaser may acquire a real-property interest or another timeshare interest within a plan, with use periods, assessments, management, reservations, and governing documents.

Tex. Property Code § 221.041 gives a purchaser a cancellation right that expires before the sixth day after the later of two events: signing and receiving the contract, or receiving the required timeshare disclosure statement. The right cannot be waived. The timing is event-based, not a casual “five-day” slogan divorced from delivery facts. Record when each document was signed and received and do not advise a party to waive a nonwaivable protection.

Timeshare transactions may involve registration, public-offering statements, resale representations, exchange programs, assessments, and management rights beyond this introductory unit. A learner should recognize the specialized regime and stop before applying an ordinary residential-resale assumption. Use the transaction's required documents and qualified specialists.

### Comparison grid

For each property, complete five fields:

1. **Record owner:** individual owner, entity, trustee, developer, or another person.
2. **Interest transferred:** deeded unit, fee-simple lot, entity interest plus occupancy right, timeshare interest, or another identified bundle.
3. **Creating and governing documents:** deed, declaration, plat, bylaws, proprietary lease or occupancy agreement, timeshare instrument, rules, or amendments.
4. **Shared obligations:** assessments, maintenance, insurance, reserves, use restrictions, approvals, or management.
5. **Special timing or consumer protection:** resale certificate, disclosure, statutory cancellation, or other applicable requirement.

If the grid cannot be completed from held documents, describe the missing evidence. Do not fill the blank with the marketing label.

## Unit: Laws Affecting Real Estate

No real-estate contract exists in a private vacuum. Federal, state, and local law can restrict marketing, selection of parties, property use, building form, disclosure, recording, financing, environmental condition, and enforcement. Private recorded instruments can add another layer. Competence requires matching the question to the proper layer and resisting the claim that one approval answers every other approval.

### Fair housing governs conduct and communication

Tex. Property Code § 301.021 prohibits specified conduct in the sale or rental of a dwelling because of race, color, religion, sex, familial status, or national origin. It reaches refusal to sell or rent after a bona fide offer, refusal to negotiate, making a dwelling unavailable, denial, and discrimination in terms, conditions, privileges, services, or facilities. Other provisions address disability, exemptions, enforcement, and remedies; do not treat one section as the entire Texas Fair Housing Act.

Tex. Property Code § 301.022 reaches notices, statements, and advertisements concerning a dwelling. The advertising rule reaches the message itself when it indicates a protected preference, limitation, discrimination, or intention because of race, color, religion, sex, disability, familial status, or national origin. A discriminatory advertisement can create a problem even before a particular applicant is rejected.

The federal counterpart is 42 U.S.C. § 3604. The held official federal text prohibits specified discriminatory sale, rental, negotiation, terms, service, advertising, availability, and blockbusting conduct and includes disability-related rules concerning modifications, accommodations, and covered multifamily design. Federal terminology in the held text includes “handicap,” while modern professional communication commonly uses disability. Preserve the statute's meaning without using outdated terminology as a label for people.

Federal and Texas layers overlap but should not be casually declared identical. Protected categories, definitions, exemptions, procedures, parties, and remedies require the correct source. Local ordinances may add protections. A brokerage policy may impose neutral controls. When a fact pattern depends on an exemption or a specialized housing program, verify the exact law rather than relying on a summary.

### Neutral service replaces steering

Steering can occur through recommendations, selective information, different showing patterns, coded descriptions, or discouragement. The answer is not to become unhelpful. It is to provide consistent service tied to lawful, client-selected criteria. Ask about price, property features, commute destination, accessibility features, lot size, or other objective needs. Offer the same categories of resources and opportunities to similarly situated clients.

When asked “Is this a good neighborhood for families like us?” do not answer with demographic judgments or coded substitutes. Explain that the client should define the desired objective characteristics, offer neutral sources within brokerage policy, and let the client evaluate. Do not infer a protected characteristic or choose communities based on one.

Advertising should describe the property, not the preferred person. “Third-floor walk-up,” when accurate and lawfully used, describes a physical fact; “ideal for women” indicates a sex preference. Even property descriptions require care when they imply exclusion. Review text, photographs, targeting settings, audience selection, and follow-up practices as one advertising process rather than assuming only the printed sentence matters.

### Zoning is a public land-use layer

Tex. Local Government Code § 211.003 authorizes a municipality to regulate listed subjects, including building height, size, and stories; lot coverage; yards and open spaces; population density; and the location and use of buildings, structures, and land. It also identifies certain additional authority. The section is a grant of municipal zoning power, not the zoning answer for a particular parcel.

To determine whether a buyer's proposed use is allowed, obtain the actual municipal map, ordinance, definitions, overlays, approvals, and any site-specific records. Confirm jurisdiction; an address outside city limits may be subject to a different regulatory structure. Distinguish use by right, conditional or special use, variance, nonconforming status, and a mere pending application. A staff conversation can guide research but is not necessarily a final written approval.

Zoning also does not prove private permission. A city may permit a home occupation while a recorded covenant prohibits it. A covenant may permit a use while zoning forbids it. A lender, insurer, landlord, condominium association, or licensing agency may impose another constraint. Run every relevant layer.

### Recorded restrictions are a private-law layer

Tex. Property Code § 202.001 defines a dedicatory instrument broadly for qualifying subdivisions, planned developments, condominium regimes, and townhouse regimes. It defines restrictive covenant as a covenant, condition, or restriction contained in a dedicatory instrument. The actual declaration, amendment, rule, plat, or other instrument supplies the particular restriction; the definition section alone does not tell the buyer whether short-term rental, fencing, solar equipment, leasing, or a detached studio is allowed.

Tex. Property Code § 202.006 requires a property owners' association to file its governing dedicatory instruments in the county real-property records and states that an instrument has no effect until filed. The filing rule creates a vital verification step. A manager's emailed summary is not a substitute for the recorded instrument. Conversely, finding a recorded document does not by itself decide whether it applies, was amended, is enforceable in the proposed way, or conflicts with another law.

Build a restriction chronology: original declaration, each amendment, supplements, annexations, plats, policies, and the parcel or phase each covers. Compare dates and recording data. If two provisions conflict or application is disputed, do not choose the one that helps the transaction. Obtain legal guidance.

### Other public-law questions require their own sources

Real estate can also be affected by building codes, subdivision and platting law, floodplain rules, environmental law, accessibility requirements, utility regulation, taxation, eminent domain, historic-preservation rules, landlord-tenant law, and licensing requirements for a planned business. This introductory topic does not claim that the sources held here answer all of those subjects. It teaches the routing discipline.

For each client objective, translate the goal into questions. “Build two homes” raises lot, platting, zoning, access, utility, setback, financing, and construction questions. “Operate a daycare” raises land-use, occupancy, licensing, insurance, and restriction questions. “Rent for weekends” raises municipal, association, deed-restriction, insurance, lender, and tax questions. The license holder can organize the inquiry without promising the outcome.

### Public approval is not a title or contract guarantee

A permit can show governmental permission for work within its scope; it does not prove ownership or private-covenant compliance. Zoning can permit a use; it does not require a lender or association to accept it. A certificate of occupancy concerns a regulatory condition; it does not prove the absence of defects. A recorded restriction can bind use; it does not prove physical compliance. A title policy can insure specified title risks subject to terms; it does not function as a property-condition inspection.

Label every document by function. That single habit prevents a client from hearing “approved” when the evidence supports only “one authority issued one approval for one stated purpose.”

### A layered-use analysis

Use this sequence for a proposed use:

1. Define the use precisely, including intensity, duration, occupants, structures, signage, parking, and any regulated activity.
2. Confirm the parcel and jurisdiction.
3. Check public land-use law and site-specific approvals.
4. Check recorded declarations, plats, easements, and restrictions.
5. Check lease, lender, insurance, association, and other contractual limits.
6. Check whether another license, permit, professional report, or consent is needed.
7. Separate verified permission from pending, conditional, reported, or unknown status.

The result should be a matrix, not a yes/no intuition. If one layer is unknown, state that it is unknown. A buyer can then decide whether to investigate, seek a contingency through an authorized form or attorney-prepared language, change the plan, or decline the transaction.

### Applied exercise: run every layer

A buyer wants a townhouse-style property for a ground-floor counseling practice and occasional short-term rental. The listing calls it “fee simple with no restrictions.” The deed has not been reviewed. The city website shows a residential zoning district. An association invoice exists, and the seller says the association “does not enforce much.” The online advertisement was targeted with an audience filter excluding users identified as men.

Create a layered issue matrix. Include ownership regime, deed and legal description, municipal use, building and accessibility questions, recorded dedicatory instruments, association rules, insurance and lender constraints, and federal and Texas fair-housing advertising. Identify which facts are verified and which are only statements. Explain why city residential zoning does not prove the counseling practice or short-term rental is permitted, why the townhouse label does not prove fee-simple ownership, why nonenforcement is not the same as absence of a recorded restriction, and why advertising controls apply before a purchaser is selected.

Your answer should propose neutral next steps and professional referrals. It should not draft a custom use contingency or give a final enforceability opinion.

### Source discipline under time pressure

When a party demands an immediate answer, use a short four-line response:

- **Known:** state the verified fact and source.
- **Not yet known:** identify the missing rule, instrument, or approval.
- **Risk:** explain the decision that cannot safely be made yet.
- **Next action:** identify who will obtain or interpret the missing evidence and by when.

For example: “The municipal map places the parcel in District R-4; the ordinance and any site approvals have not yet been reviewed; we cannot promise the proposed counseling use; the buyer will confirm with the city and counsel before relying on it.” That response is more useful than “should be fine,” and it preserves the difference between information and assurance.

### Module recap

Topic 02 is a classification and routing system. The physical parcel is not the same as the legal interest, and an instrument is not the same as either. A conveyance presumption does not cure title. Joint ownership does not itself prove survivorship. A spouse's deed label does not settle marital rights. An LLC member or partner can own an entity interest while the entity owns the land. Condominium, cooperative, townhouse, and timeshare labels demand different creating documents. A municipal permission and a private restriction operate on separate layers. Fair-housing duties reach communication and service, not only a final refusal.

Before moving on, be able to complete this sentence for any material statement: “The proposition is ___; its authority or evidence is ___; it proves only ___; the unresolved question is ___; the next authorized action is ___.” If you cannot fill every blank, the issue is not ready to become a promise in a contract.

Use the following final self-check:

1. Did I identify the exact parcel and exact interest?
2. Did I distinguish physical observation, record evidence, party statement, contract allocation, and legal conclusion?
3. Did I verify the owner and the signer's capacity instead of relying on control or family relationship?
4. Did I review the creating and governing documents for the ownership regime?
5. Did I run both public-law and private-instrument restrictions for the proposed use?
6. Did I use neutral, consistent service and property-focused advertising?
7. Did I label StateCourse workflows as provider methodology rather than law?
8. Did I escalate disputed title, ownership, authority, or drafting to the broker and qualified professionals?

The correct outcome is not always a completed deal. Sometimes it is a documented pause that prevents the parties from signing a promise unsupported by the property, the owner, or the law.
