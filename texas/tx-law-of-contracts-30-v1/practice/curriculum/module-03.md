## Default, remedies, and escrow disputes

Read paragraph 15 as alternatives. Where buyer default is established, the seller has a performance/other-lawful-relief route or may terminate and receive earnest money as liquidated damages under the printed provision. It does not promise the seller every remedy simultaneously. Where seller default is established, the buyer is not necessarily limited to a deposit refund. The form also describes a performance/other-relief route. Choosing a legal remedy and establishing entitlement require counsel’s analysis.

A claimed default is not established by an angry email. Build a chronology: executed terms, effective date, required act, actual performance, notice sent, recipient, delivery evidence and any written modification. Distinguish a default from a valid contractual termination. Missing evidence is a reason to investigate, not to backdate a record.

The escrow holder does not become the judge merely by holding the money. Paragraph 18 addresses releases and demands. An agent who believes the client is correct still cannot replace the contractual process with a personal order to disburse. An escrow release can have legal consequences, so the parties should obtain advice before surrendering disputed rights.

Paragraph 16 calls for unresolved contract disputes to go to a mutually acceptable mediation provider, with cost shared equally, while preserving access to equitable court relief. This is not the same as arbitration by the agents. Paragraph 17 separately addresses fees for prevailing parties in related proceedings. Do not guarantee a result merely because a contract contains a fee provision.

Worked file review: the seller alleges late earnest money; the buyer alleges timely delivery. Compare the effective date, the applicable delivery deadline, the escrow receipt and the seller’s notice chronology. Then provide the verified record for legal review. The correct role is accurate evidence and timely communication, not declaring a winner.
