## Title, closing, possession, and practical file review

A title commitment can contain an exception that affects the buyer’s intended use. A driveway or utility easement may matter to a planned garage even if the house itself looks suitable. Flag the exception, calendar the actual paragraph 6D objection period and encourage attorney review. A title policy is not a building permit, and a real estate license does not authorize a legal opinion that an easement is unenforceable.

Paragraph 9B describes closing performance, including a deed, good funds and satisfaction of liens except permitted assumed obligations. Paying the seller’s existing loan is a closing task; title insurance does not make that payoff unnecessary. Paragraph 10 distinguishes possession upon closing and funding from possession under a written lease. Signing all papers does not prove the loan funded. If the seller remains afterward, a written possession arrangement and insurance review are needed.

Seller disclosure and buyer inspection are complementary. Receiving a disclosure does not replace investigation. Likewise, the end of the option does not erase every other contract protection: paragraph 14 separately addresses casualty loss after the effective date, restoration and stated buyer choices if restoration fails for the specified reason.

Finish a contract review by resolving inconsistent checkboxes, blank negotiated periods, missing attachments and unsigned amendments with the parties. In a repair example, an unsigned draft does not prove an agreed repair duty. Confirm the actual written agreement and appropriate completion evidence before representing that a party complied.

The companion questions ask you to name the relevant provision, distinguish the trigger from the required response and explain why the incorrect choice overstates a right or skips a required act. A practice score measures this exercise only; it does not determine anyone’s legal rights or course completion.
