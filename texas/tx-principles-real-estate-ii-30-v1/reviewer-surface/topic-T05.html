Control of Land Use

Topic 5 of 9 · 110 prescribed minutes

Land-Use Controls

Land in Texas is controlled from two directions at once, and the two systems are written down in different places, enforced by different people, and answerable to different rules. The first is public control: regulation imposed by a governmental body under the police power. The second is private control: restrictions imposed by recorded instrument, running with the land, enforced by neighbors and property owners' associations. A license holder who checks only one of the two has done half the job.

The police power as the basis for public regulation. Public land-use regulation is not justified by the government owning anything. It is justified by the government's authority to regulate for the protection of the public, and the Texas Legislature says so in the first line of the municipal zoning chapter. Local Government Code § 211.001 states that the powers granted under that subchapter "are for the purpose of promoting the public health, safety, morals, or general welfare and protecting and preserving places and areas of historical, cultural, or architectural importance and significance." That is the whole theory of zoning in one clause: a municipality may tell an owner what to do with the owner's own land, without paying for it, because the regulation serves a public purpose. When governmental power stops being regulation and becomes an acquisition, the analysis changes entirely — the subject of the next unit.

What a Texas municipality may regulate. Local Government Code § 211.003(a) lists the tools. The governing body of a municipality may regulate "the height, number of stories, and size of buildings and other structures"; "the percentage of a lot that may be occupied"; "the size of yards, courts, and other open spaces"; "population density"; "the location and use of buildings, other structures, and land for business, industrial, residential, or other purposes"; and the pumping, extraction, and use of groundwater by persons other than retail public utilities where the groundwater "presents an actual or potential threat to human health." Section 211.003(b) adds authority over construction, reconstruction, alteration, or razing of buildings in designated places of historical, cultural, or architectural significance, and § 211.003(c) permits a home-rule municipality to regulate the bulk of buildings.

Notice the shape of that list. The use regulation — what activity may occur on the land — is one item among six; the rest are bulk, height, and area regulations. A buyer's plan can be defeated by either: a use may be permitted in the district and the project still fail because the building will not fit inside the required yards.

The comprehensive plan requirement. Zoning is not a free-floating power. Section 211.004(a) provides that "[z]oning regulations must be adopted in accordance with a comprehensive plan" and must be designed to serve listed ends — lessening street congestion, securing safety from fire and panic, promoting health and the general welfare, providing adequate light and air, preventing overcrowding, avoiding undue concentration of population, or facilitating adequate transportation, water, sewers, schools, and parks. Section 211.005(a) then lets the municipality divide itself into districts of the number, shape, and size the governing body considers best, and § 211.005(b) requires regulations to "be uniform for each class or kind of building in a district," though they "may vary from district to district."

How a zoning regulation is adopted. Section 211.006(a) provides that a regulation or district boundary "is not effective until after a public hearing on the matter at which parties in interest and citizens have an opportunity to be heard," with notice published before the 15th day before the hearing in an official newspaper or newspaper of general circulation and, if the municipality maintains a website, on that website. Section 211.006(a-1) adds a targeted written notice where a proposed change would turn a current conforming use into a nonconforming one, delivered to each owner and occupant not later than the 10th day before the hearing and containing, in bold 14-point type or larger, the warning that the municipality "IS HOLDING A HEARING THAT WILL DETERMINE WHETHER YOU MAY LOSE THE RIGHT TO CONTINUE USING YOUR PROPERTY FOR ITS CURRENT USE."

The zoning commission is the drafting body. Under § 211.007(a) the governing body of a home-rule municipality shall, and of a general-law municipality may, appoint one; it recommends the original district boundaries and the regulations for each district. Section 211.007(b) requires a preliminary report and at least one public hearing before a final report goes to the governing body, which "may not take action on the matter until it receives the final report." Section 211.007(c) requires written notice, before the 10th day before the hearing, to each owner of real property within 200 feet of the property whose classification is proposed to change. Neighbors also have a formal lever: § 211.0061(b) allows a written protest signed by the owners of at least 20 percent of the area covered by the change or of the adjoining area extending 200 feet from it, and under § 211.0061(d) a validly protested change then needs the affirmative vote of at least three-fourths of all members of the governing body.

The board of adjustment, variances, and special exceptions. Section 211.008(a) authorizes a municipality to appoint a board of adjustment and, in the regulations adopted under the subchapter, to authorize that board "in appropriate cases and subject to appropriate conditions and safeguards, to make special exceptions to the terms of the zoning ordinance that are consistent with the general purpose and intent of the ordinance and in accordance with any applicable rules contained in the ordinance." Read the whole grant: a special exception is not free-form relief. It is available only in the cases the ordinance contemplates, on the conditions and safeguards the ordinance sets, and it must square with both the ordinance's general purpose and its applicable rules. The board must have at least five members appointed for two-year terms (§ 211.008(b)), and each case "must be heard by at least 75 percent of the members" (§ 211.008(d)).

Section 211.009(a) sets out what the board may do: hear and decide an appeal alleging error by an administrative official enforcing the ordinance; hear and decide special exceptions when the ordinance requires it; and "authorize in specific cases a variance from the terms of a zoning ordinance if the variance is not contrary to the public interest and, due to special conditions, a literal enforcement of the ordinance would result in unnecessary hardship, and so that the spirit of the ordinance is observed and substantial justice is done." Section 211.009(b-1) lists grounds the board may consider on the hardship question for a structure, among them that the cost of compliance exceeds 50 percent of the structure's appraised value on the most recent certified appraisal roll, or that compliance would cost the lot at least 25 percent of its physically developable area. Under § 211.009(c), a concurring vote of 75 percent of the members is necessary to reverse an official's decision or to authorize a variance.

A common error is to treat a variance as a request to be excused from a rule the applicant dislikes, or to treat "I paid too much for the lot" as hardship. The standard is about special conditions and a result still faithful to the ordinance's spirit. A second common error confuses the remedies: a special exception is a use or condition the ordinance itself contemplates and delegates to the board, while a variance is a departure from the ordinance's terms. Deadlines matter too. Section 211.010(b) requires an appeal to the board not later than the 20th day after the decision, and § 211.010(d) requires the board to decide not later than the 60th day after filing. Under § 211.011 a person aggrieved, a taxpayer, or a municipal officer, department, board, or bureau may present a verified petition to a district court, county court, or county court at law, and § 211.011(b) requires it "within 10 days after the date the decision is filed in the board's office."

Nonconforming uses. When a district is created or changed, existing uses do not always fit. Section 211.019(b) provides that a person using property in a manner considered a nonconforming use as a result of the adoption of or change to a zoning regulation or boundary "may continue to use the property in the same manner unless required by a municipality to stop the nonconforming use." That is what "grandfathering" means in Texas: continuation as of right, not immunity forever. If the municipality does require the use to stop, § 211.019(d) gives the owner or lessee a choice between a payment from the municipality — costs directly attributable to ceasing the use, plus the diminution in market value — or continued nonconforming use until that amount is recovered through continued business activity. Section 211.019(o) removes the protection entirely for a use "intentionally abandoned for at least six months." Section 211.012 lets the municipality make a violation a misdemeanor, provide civil penalties, and sue to restrain, correct, or abate it, and § 211.013(a) resolves overlaps by a stricter-standard rule: where the zoning regulation imposes higher standards than another statute or local ordinance the zoning regulation controls, and where the other law is stricter the other law controls.

Texas reality: zoning is a municipal option, not a statewide mandate. Chapter 211 grants zoning power to "the governing body of a municipality," and it sits in a subtitle titled Municipal Regulatory Authority. Nothing in the chapter grants that power to a county. Section 211.006(a) speaks of "[t]he governing body of a municipality wishing to exercise the authority relating to zoning regulations" — permissive language, not a command. Section 211.015 lets the voters of a home-rule municipality repeal the municipality's zoning regulations in their entirety by charter election or, on initial adoption, by referendum. And the Legislature has written the possibility of a Texas city without zoning into another chapter: Local Government Code § 212.151 makes the municipal deed-restriction-enforcement subchapter available to a municipality with a population of 1.5 million or more that passes a qualifying ordinance and, separately, to a municipality "that does not have zoning ordinances and passes an ordinance that requires uniform application and enforcement of this subchapter with regard to all property and residents."

Houston is the well-known example of a very large Texas city that has not adopted conventional use-district zoning; that is common knowledge about how Texas cities have chosen to govern themselves, not a statutory rule, and no source read for this course states it. What the statutes do establish is the structure that makes it possible. Two consequences follow. First, land in the unincorporated portion of a Texas county is not zoned the way land inside a city is, because chapter 211 does not reach it. Second, in a municipality without zoning, recorded private restrictions do far more of the work of separating uses — which is exactly why § 212.151 exists.

Subdivision regulation and platting. Even a municipality that never adopts a use-district ordinance regulates how raw land becomes lots. Under § 212.002 a municipality may, after a public hearing, adopt rules governing plats and subdivisions within its jurisdiction. Section 212.004(a) requires a plat whenever an owner of a tract inside the limits or in the extraterritorial jurisdiction of a municipality divides it into two or more parts to lay out a subdivision, lots, streets, alleys, squares, parks, or other parts intended to be dedicated to public use — and the division counts however accomplished, "by using a metes and bounds description in a deed of conveyance or in a contract for a deed, by using a contract of sale or other executory contract to convey, or by using any other method." The subsection excludes "a division of land into parts greater than five acres, where each part has access and no public improvement is being dedicated." Section 212.004(d) requires the plat to be filed and recorded with the county clerk.

Approval is largely ministerial and is on a clock. Section 212.005(a) states that the responsible municipal authority "must approve a plat or replat that is required to be prepared under this subchapter and that satisfies the requirements of this subchapter." Section 212.006(a) identifies that authority as the municipal planning commission, or the governing body where there is none. Section 212.009(a) requires approval, conditional approval, or disapproval "within 30 days after the date the plat is filed," and a plat "is approved by the municipal authority unless it is disapproved within that period and in accordance with Section 212.0091". Both halves of that condition matter: a disapproval that is late, or that does not comply with § 212.0091, does not stop the clock, and the plat is approved by the authority's inaction. Section 212.0091 requires a written statement clearly articulating each condition or reason, directly related to the subchapter's requirements and citing the law relied on where applicable, and it "may not be arbitrary." Section 212.010(e) gives an owner whose conforming plat is refused an action for a writ of mandamus with reasonable attorney's fees. Section 212.012(a) then forbids listed utility providers to "serve or connect any land with water, sewer, electricity, gas, or other utility service" unless presented with or otherwise holding the approval certificate issued under § 212.0115. And a replat may be recorded without vacating the preceding plat under § 212.014 only if it "does not attempt to amend or remove any covenants or restrictions" — a statutory acknowledgement that the public plat process does not override private covenants.

Extraterritorial jurisdiction. A Texas municipality's reach extends past its city limits, but not evenly. Section 212.001(1) defines extraterritorial jurisdiction by reference to Chapter 42 of the Local Government Code. Section 212.003(a) lets a municipality extend its subdivision ordinances into the ETJ — and then flatly limits what it may do there: "unless otherwise authorized by state law, in its extraterritorial jurisdiction a municipality shall not regulate: (1) the use of any building or property for business, industrial, residential, or other purposes; (2) the bulk, height, or number of buildings constructed on a particular tract of land; (3) the size of a building that can be constructed on a particular tract of land, including without limitation any restriction on the ratio of building floor space to the land square footage; (4) the number of residential units that can be built per acre of land" — and, on the narrow terms stated there, (5) the size, type, or method of construction of certain water or wastewater facilities. Note the opening words: the prohibition yields where state law elsewhere authorizes the regulation, so it is a default, not an absolute bar. Section 212.049 adds that the development-plat subchapter "does not authorize the municipality to require municipal building permits or otherwise enforce the municipality's building code in its extraterritorial jurisdiction." The teaching point is precise: in the ETJ expect platting and subdivision regulation, and do not assume use, density, height, or bulk regulation. This is the most misdescribed piece of Texas land-use law in ordinary conversation.

Private controls: deed restrictions and restrictive covenants. Now the other half. Property Code § 202.001(4) defines a restrictive covenant as "any covenant, condition, or restriction contained in a dedicatory instrument, whether mandatory, prohibitive, permissive, or administrative." A dedicatory instrument, under § 202.001(1), is "each document governing the establishment, maintenance, or operation of a residential subdivision, planned unit development, condominium or townhouse regime, or any similar planned development," and expressly includes the declaration, the bylaws, the association's properly adopted rules, and all lawful amendments. Section 202.001(2) defines the property owners' association through which the owners or their board manage or regulate the development. Chapter 202 reaches back — § 202.002(a) applies it "to all restrictive covenants regardless of the date on which they were created" — and the interpretive rule cuts in favor of the restriction: § 202.003(a) provides that "[a] restrictive covenant shall be liberally construed to give effect to its purposes and intent," subject to the family-home limitation in § 202.003(b).

Enforcement powers are substantial. Section 202.004(a) provides that "[a]n exercise of discretionary authority by a property owners' association or other representative designated by an owner of real property concerning a restrictive covenant is presumed reasonable unless the court determines by a preponderance of the evidence that the exercise of discretionary authority was arbitrary, capricious, or discriminatory." Section 202.004(b) allows the association to initiate, defend, or intervene in litigation affecting enforcement, and § 202.004(c) permits a court to "assess civil damages for the violation of a restrictive covenant in an amount not to exceed $200 for each day of the violation." Property Code § 5.006(a) adds that in an action based on breach of a restrictive covenant the court "shall allow to a prevailing party who asserted the action reasonable attorney's fees in addition to the party's costs and claim."

A worked illustration, using figures chosen only to show the arithmetic and not drawn from any required schedule: an owner leaves a detached workshop that violates a recorded setback covenant in place for 90 days after judgment. At the § 202.004(c) ceiling of $200 per day, the maximum civil damages for that period would be 90 × $200 = $18,000, plus the prevailing claimant's reasonable attorney's fees under § 5.006(a). The point is not the number; it is that private restrictions in Texas carry a real, statutorily quantified downside on top of an injunction requiring removal.

Where private restrictions live, and the limits on them. Section 202.006(a) requires a property owners' association to file all dedicatory instruments in the real property records of each county where the property is located, and § 202.006(b) is the sentence to remember: "A dedicatory instrument has no effect until the instrument is filed in accordance with this section." Section 202.006(c) adds that an association may not collect a regular assessment if the instrument authorizing it was not filed. So the private control system is a county real property records system while the public control system is a city hall system; they are searched in different buildings.

The Legislature has also carved out categories no association may prohibit outright, each with detailed allowances letting the association regulate appearance and location instead: water-conserving landscaping and rainwater harvesting (§ 202.007); solar energy devices (§ 202.010); certain durable or solar-generating shingles that match the surrounding property (§ 202.011); display of the United States, Texas, and armed-forces flags (§ 202.012); permanently installed standby electric generators (§ 202.019); lawful possession of firearms and ammunition (§ 202.021); conforming swimming pool enclosures (§ 202.022); security measures including cameras and perimeter fencing (§ 202.023); and renting based on the tenant's method of payment, which § 202.024(a) defines to include a housing choice voucher (§ 202.024). When a client asks whether the HOA can stop a solar array, the answer is usually that it cannot prohibit one outright but can regulate how and where it is installed. Separately, Property Code § 5.026(a) provides that a restriction or deed provision prohibiting use by, or sale, lease, or transfer to, a person because of race, color, religion, or national origin "is void," and § 5.026(b) requires a court to dismiss a suit to enforce it.

Private restrictions can be stricter than public zoning — and a city can sometimes enforce them. Nothing requires a covenant to stop where the ordinance stops. A district may permit a 35-foot building while the declaration permits 28 feet; a district may permit a duplex while the covenant permits one single-family dwelling. The stricter of the two governs the owner's actual freedom, because each is independently enforceable by its own enforcer. Texas also lets certain municipalities enforce private restrictions directly. Under Local Government Code § 212.153(a) a covered municipality "may sue in any court of competent jurisdiction to enjoin or abate a violation of a restriction contained or incorporated by reference in a properly recorded plan, plat, or other instrument that affects a subdivision located inside the boundaries of the municipality," though § 212.153(b) bars that suit if a property owners' association with authority to enforce files its own. Section 212.152 defines the covered "restriction" broadly — use character, setbacks, lot and structure size, permitted activities, architectural features, fences, landscaping, noise, and required maintenance — which is, in substance, a list of the things zoning would otherwise do. Section 212.156(a) allows the governing body by ordinance to require compliance and to sue for a civil penalty.

The practical rule for a license holder. Two searches, two places, every time. The public side is found at the municipality: the zoning map and ordinance, the district's permitted uses and dimensional standards, the plat, whether the property is inside the city limits or in the ETJ, and whether the city has zoning at all. The private side is found in the county real property records: the recorded plat, the declaration and its amendments, and the association's dedicatory instruments, which under § 202.006(b) have no effect until filed there. Property Code § 5.012(a) requires a seller of residential property subject to POA membership to give the purchaser a written notice stating that the restrictive covenants and dedicatory instruments "have been or will be recorded in the Real Property Records of the county in which the property is located" and that copies "may be obtained from the county clerk" — the statute is telling the buyer exactly where to look. Reading the ordinance and stopping there, or reading the declaration and stopping there, is how a client ends up owning land that cannot be used the way the client bought it to be used.

Topic quiz

Question 1 of 4

A Texas home-rule city proposes to rezone a tract from single-family to retail. Before the hearing, owners of 22 percent of the area within 200 feet of the tract sign and file a written protest. What is the effect?

The protest right is a supermajority trigger, not a veto: reaching 20 percent of the area covered by the change, or of the adjoining area extending 200 feet from it, raises the vote needed under § 211.0061(d) to three-fourths of ALL members of the governing body — not three-fourths of those present. It sits alongside a separate notice duty in § 211.007(c), which requires written notice before the 10th day before the hearing to each owner of real property within 200 feet of the property whose classification is proposed to change.

Question 2 of 4

A lot owner asks a Texas board of adjustment for a variance, arguing that the setback is inconvenient and that he paid too much for the lot to build profitably within it. What must he actually establish, and what vote does the board need?

Section 211.009(a)(3) states all four elements, and none of them is 'the rule is inconvenient': the hardship must come from SPECIAL CONDITIONS, and the result must still be faithful to the ordinance. Section 211.009(c) requires a concurring vote of 75 percent of the members to authorize a variance or reverse an official's decision, and § 211.008(d) separately requires each case to be heard by at least 75 percent of the members of a board that must have at least five. Section 211.009(b-1) lists grounds the board MAY weigh on hardship, among them that compliance would cost more than 50 percent of the structure's appraised value on the most recent certified appraisal roll or cost the lot at least 25 percent of its physically developable area. And the last choice inverts the vocabulary: a special exception is what the ordinance itself contemplates and delegates; a variance is a departure from the ordinance's terms.

Question 3 of 4

A recorded subdivision plat shows a 2-acre tract labeled 'Park — dedicated to public use.' The city approved the plat three years ago and has never entered, used, or improved the tract; the developer still mows it. A buyer asks who owns and must maintain the park.

Texas platting splits dedication into two halves: an OFFER shown on the plat under § 212.004(a), and an ACCEPTANCE shown only by entry, use, or improvement under § 212.011(a). Section 212.011(b) completes the picture from the other direction — disapproval of the plat is treated as a refusal of the offered dedication. The practical consequence for a buyer is direct: a platted 'park' or street stub is not proof that the city owns it, maintains it, or ever will.

Question 4 of 4

A listing agent learns from the seller's contractor that the garage slab was cut and an old fuel tank pulled out after a leak. The seller marks Item 4 of the § 5.008 notice 'unknown.' The agent says nothing to buyers, reasoning that the notice is the seller's document. Analyse the agent's position.

Two duties run side by side and only one of them belongs to the seller. Section 5.008(d) does protect a seller who genuinely lacks knowledge — the notice is completed to the best of the seller's belief and knowledge, and indicating 'unknown' is compliance. It says nothing about the licence holder, whose exposure comes from § 1101.652(b)(3) and (4) for misrepresenting or failing to disclose a known significant defect, with § 1101.805(e) removing the shield exactly where the licence holder knew of the falsity and failed to disclose that knowledge. The fourth choice is the other half of the trap: characterizing the condition as safe is the confident wrong answer the disclosure rules are written to catch.
