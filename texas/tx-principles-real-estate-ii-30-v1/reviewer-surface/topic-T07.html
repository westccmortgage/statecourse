Real Estate Brokerage and Law of Agency

Topic 7 of 9 · 195 prescribed minutes

The Law of Agency

Agency is the legal relationship in which one person — the agent — is authorized to act for and

on behalf of another person — the principal — in dealings with third persons, and in which the

agent's authorized acts bind the principal as if the principal had acted personally. Every other idea in

this module rests on that sentence. A listing is an agency; a buyer representation agreement is an

agency; and the reason a license holder can lose a licence over a sentence said carelessly at a kitchen

table is that the license holder is not merely a helpful person in the room but someone else's legal

proxy.

Texas does not leave the existence of the relationship to inference. Occupations Code § 1101.557(a)

states it directly: "A broker who represents a party in a real estate transaction or who lists real

estate for sale under an exclusive agreement for a party is that party's agent." That confirms both that

representation creates agency and that taking an exclusive listing is agency in itself.

The vocabulary, precisely. The principal is the person the agent works for; in ordinary

brokerage speech the principal is the client. The person on the other side, whom the license holder

deals with but does not represent, is the customer. Neither word is defined in Chapter 1101, so do

not treat them as statutory terms. What Chapter 1101 does define, for the subchapter governing practice,

is party. Under § 1101.551(2), "'Party' means a prospective buyer, seller, landlord, or tenant or an

authorized representative of a buyer, seller, landlord, or tenant, including a trustee, guardian,

executor, administrator, receiver, or attorney-in-fact. The term does not include a license holder who

represents a party." That last clause matters: the license holder is never the party; the license holder

is the agent of a party.

Who is the agent — the broker, not the sales agent. This is the structural fact students most often

get backwards. Section 1101.002(7) defines a "sales agent" as "a person who is sponsored by a licensed

broker for the purpose of performing an act described by Subdivision (1)" — an act of brokerage. Section

1101.351(c) provides that "A licensed sales agent may not engage or attempt to engage in real estate

brokerage unless the sales agent is sponsored by a licensed broker and is acting for that broker." The

sales agent therefore has no independent brokerage authority. The agency runs between the broker and

the client, and the sales agent performs the broker's work. Section 1101.554 reflects this

administratively — the Commission delivers a copy of each sales agent licence to the sponsoring broker —

and § 1101.803 states the consequence: "A licensed broker is liable to the commission, the public, and

the broker's clients for any conduct engaged in under this chapter by the broker or by a sales agent

associated with or acting for the broker."

What acts require the licence. Section 1101.002(1) defines "broker" functionally: a person who, for

a commission or other valuable consideration or expecting one, performs for another person any of a

listed set of acts — selling, exchanging, purchasing or leasing real estate; offering to do so;

negotiating or attempting to negotiate a listing, sale, exchange, purchase or lease; listing or offering

to list; auctioning; dealing in options; aiding in locating or obtaining real estate; procuring a

prospect or procuring property to effect a transaction; controlling the acceptance or deposit of rent

from a resident of a single-family residential unit; and advising an owner about a short sale. Section

1101.004(a) restates that as a status, and § 1101.351(a) makes the licence a precondition: unless a

person holds a licence, the person may not act as or represent that the person is a broker or sales

agent, or act as a residential rental locator. Note what the definition turns on — acting *for another

person* with the expectation of compensation. Section 1101.004(b) draws the boundary from the other

side: constructing or repairing a building, participating as a principal in a real estate investment, or

entering into an obligation secured by an interest in real property is not, standing alone, brokerage.

Section 1101.005 lists persons to whom the chapter does not apply at all, including a Texas-licensed

attorney, an on-site manager of an apartment complex, and an owner or an owner's employee leasing the

owner's real estate.

How an agency is created. Agency law generally recognizes four routes, and it is worth being candid

about their source: Chapter 1101 does not enumerate them, so this module presents them as common-law

concepts rather than Texas statutory rules.

Express agency arises when the parties say so. A signed listing or buyer representation agreement is

express agency in writing; an oral appointment can be express too. That oral possibility is not

hypothetical here — § 1101.562(a)(1) permits a non-representation showing only where the broker "has not

agreed with the party, either orally or in writing, to represent the party," which tells you the

Legislature treats an oral agreement to represent as capable of creating the relationship. **Implied

agency** arises from conduct rather than words: the license holder advises on price, counsels on

strategy, behaves as the buyer's advocate, and the buyer reasonably understands the license holder to be

working for them, though nobody signed anything. That accident is what the 2026 amendments in the next

unit are built to prevent, and what § 1101.652(b)(7) punishes by making it a ground for discipline that a

license holder "fails to make clear to all parties to a real estate transaction the party for whom the

license holder is acting." Ratification occurs when a principal who did not authorize an act accepts

its benefit afterward with knowledge of the facts. Agency by estoppel arises when a principal's own

conduct leads a third person reasonably to believe someone is the principal's agent and that person

relies to their detriment. The last two are judicial doctrines; do not cite Chapter 1101 for them.

Actual and apparent authority. Actual authority is what the principal really gave the agent —

expressly in the words of the agreement, or by implication as reasonably necessary to carry the express

grant out. Apparent authority is what a third person is reasonably led by the principal's conduct to

believe the agent has, even where the agent in fact has less. Actual authority is the boundary the agent

must respect toward the principal; apparent authority is the boundary the principal may be held to toward

the outside world. These are common-law categories, but Texas has legislated at their edges in three ways

you must know.

On the power to bind: an ordinary Texas listing does not authorize the broker to sign the contract for

the seller. The broker markets, procures, communicates and negotiates; the party signs. Where a license

holder does hold power to bind, § 1101.557(c)(1) changes the license holder's status — "a license holder

who has the authority to bind a party to a lease or sale under a power of attorney or a property

management agreement is also a party to the lease or sale."

On liability for the agent's words: at common law a principal can be exposed for an agent's

misrepresentations, and Texas has narrowed that by statute. Section 1101.805(b) provides that the section

"prevails over any other law, including common law." Under § 1101.805(d), a party is not liable for a

license holder's misrepresentation or concealment of a material fact unless the party "knew of the falsity"

and "failed to disclose the party's knowledge of the falsity"; subsection (e) runs the same protection the

other way for the license holder, and subsection (c) preserves the broker's responsibility for a sponsored

sales agent. Knowledge plus silence destroys the protection either way.

On the outer limit of authority: no agency authorizes practising law. Section 1101.654(a) requires the

Commission to suspend or revoke the licence of a non-attorney license holder who, for consideration, drafts

an instrument other than a form described by § 1101.155 that transfers or affects an interest in real

property, or who advises on the validity or legal sufficiency of an instrument or the validity of title.

Subsection (b) protects the ordinary work: completing a contract form incidental to acting as a broker is

not unauthorized practice if the form was adopted by the Commission for that type of transaction, prepared

and approved by a Texas attorney for that type of transaction, or prepared by the owner or an attorney and

required by the owner.

How an agency ends. Agency relationships end in the ordinary ways — performance of the object,

expiration of the term, mutual agreement, revocation or renunciation, or an event making performance

impossible. Those are general agency principles, not Chapter 1101 rules. What Texas legislates is the term.

Section 1101.652(b)(12) makes it a ground for discipline to fail to specify "a definite termination date

that is not subject to prior notice" in a contract — other than a property management contract — in which

the license holder agrees to perform services requiring a licence. An open-ended listing is not merely poor

drafting in Texas; it is a disciplinary exposure. Section 1101.563(c)(1)(B), effective January 1, 2026,

carries the same instinct to the buyer side, and § 1101.563(e)(2) caps a showing-only agreement at fourteen

days.

A common error is to assume that when the agreement ends, everything ends. Two obligations in particular

outlive the paperwork — what you may never say about the former client, and what you must do with the

former client's money. Unit 4 takes them up.

Topic quiz

Question 1 of 5

A sales agent signs a buyer representation agreement in her own name and tells the buyer, "You're my client — I work for you, not for the brokerage." Correct her.

Section 1101.002(7) defines a sales agent as a person sponsored by a broker to perform brokerage acts, § 1101.351(c) forbids brokerage except while sponsored and acting for that broker, and § 1101.803 makes the broker liable to the Commission, the public and the broker's clients for the sponsored agent's conduct. The relationship runs between the broker and the client; the sales agent does the broker's work. And § 1101.551(2) says the opposite of the third choice: "party" expressly does not include a license holder who represents a party.

Question 2 of 5

A brokerage holds an exclusive listing and has just signed the buyer, who found the listing through the firm's own website. Both agreements carry a conspicuous bold paragraph setting out the conduct prohibited by § 1101.651(d) and authorizing the broker to act as an intermediary and to make appointments. What must happen before an associated license holder may give advice to each side?

Texas does not use dual agency. Chapter 1101, Subchapter L creates the intermediary: § 1101.559(a) requires written consent from each party plus a statement of the source of expected compensation; § 1101.560(a) and (b) permit appointment of one associated license holder to each side only where the written consent authorizes it and the broker gives written notice of the appointment to all parties; and § 1101.560(c) is the payoff — an appointed license holder may then provide opinions and advice during negotiations. Without appointments the brokerage is limited to fair and impartial facilitation under § 1101.559(c).

Question 3 of 5

At closing a grateful buyer hands the sponsored sales agent a personal cheque for $2,000, and the brokerage separately wants to pay $500 to a past client who sent the buyer over. Analyse both payments.

Chapter 1101 closes the loop in both directions. Section 1101.651(b) lets a sales agent accept compensation for a real estate transaction only from the sponsoring broker (or the broker sponsoring when it was earned), so the cheque must be routed through the broker. Section 1101.651(a) forbids a broker to compensate a person directly or indirectly for performing an act of a broker unless that person is a license holder, and § 1101.652(b)(11) makes paying or dividing a fee with a non-license holder a ground for discipline — procuring a prospect is one of the listed broker acts. Disclosure does not cure either.

Question 4 of 5

A license holder representing a seller arranges pre-listing repairs. The contractor bills $4,200; the license holder forwards the seller an invoice for $4,700 and keeps the difference as "coordination time," saying nothing. Which ground for discipline is implicated, and what single act would have changed the analysis?

Read what the loyalty provisions actually condemn. Subsection (b)(13) does not forbid the profit; it forbids the *undisclosed* profit on an expenditure made for a principal. The same shape appears in (b)(16), which reaches the *undisclosed* principal, and (b)(8), which reaches the second cheque taken *without* all parties' knowledge and consent. Disclosure converts a breach into a permissible arrangement — and § 1101.652(b)(24) separately reaches the doctored invoice itself.

Question 5 of 5

A Texas sales agent tells a buyer two things: "In my view this street will hold its value well," and "The seller replaced the sewer line in 2021" — which is false. How does the DTPA's license holder exemption in § 17.49(i) apply?

Section 17.49(i) exempts claims against a Chapter 1101 broker or salesperson arising from acts or omissions while acting as one, then removes three things from that protection: an express misrepresentation of material fact that cannot be characterized as advice, judgment or opinion; a failure to disclose in violation of § 17.46(b)(24); and an unconscionable action that cannot be so characterized. The exemption is drawn exactly along the opinion/fact line — and it is on the fact side that license holders get sued.
