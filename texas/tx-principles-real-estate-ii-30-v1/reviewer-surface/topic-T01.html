Real Estate Math

Topic 1 of 9 · 30 prescribed minutes

Calculators

Real estate arithmetic is not difficult mathematics. Almost everything a Texas license holder

computes in a working week — a commission, a seller's net, a tax proration, a price per square foot,

a loan-to-value figure — is multiplication, division, and the occasional subtraction. What makes real

estate math go wrong is almost never the mathematics. It is the machine, and the habit of reaching

for the machine before the problem has been stated.

So begin with the discipline that prevents most errors: **write the equation before you touch the

keys.** On paper or on screen, put down what you are solving for, what you know, and how they

connect — commission = sale price × commission rate, or daily amount = annual amount ÷ 365 —

before you enter a single digit. A calculator will faithfully compute the wrong thing. Writing the

equation first is what catches the wrong thing while it is still cheap to catch.

Order of operations. Arithmetic has a fixed precedence: parentheses first, then exponents, then

multiplication and division from left to right, then addition and subtraction from left to right. The

trap is that calculators disagree about whether to honor it. A scientific calculator applies

precedence: enter `412500 − 412500 × 0.06 =` and it multiplies first, returning 387,750. A basic

four-function calculator typically evaluates strictly left to right as you press keys: it

subtracts 412,500 from 412,500 to get zero, multiplies zero by 0.06, and returns 0. Both machines are

working correctly. Only one is answering the question you asked. The fix is not to memorize which

machine you own but to break the calculation into single operations you control: compute

412,500 × 0.06 = 24,750 first, then subtract that from 412,500 to reach a seller's figure of 387,750.

The percent key. The `%` key is the most misleading key on the keypad because it does different

things in different contexts, and often something other than what the user expects. On many

calculators `200 × 6 %` returns 12 — the key divided by 100 for you. On many of those same

calculators, `200 − 6 %` returns 188, because the key interpreted 6 as 6 percent of 200 and

subtracted 12. On others, the same keystrokes return 194, because the key simply converted 6 to 0.06

and subtracted it. A common error is to assume the key behaves consistently. The safe practice is to

never use it: convert the percentage to a decimal yourself by moving the decimal point two places

left — 6% becomes 0.06, 5.5% becomes 0.055, 0.5% becomes 0.005 — and multiply. You lose nothing, and

you always know what the machine did.

The memory keys. `M+` adds the display to memory, `M−` subtracts from it, `MR` (or `MRC`) recalls

it, and `MC` clears it. Their real value is not storage but precision: they let you carry a full

unrounded intermediate result into the next step instead of writing down a rounded one and keying it

back in. Clear memory before every new problem. A stale value in memory is a silent error, because

nothing on the display announces it.

Rounding discipline. Round once, at the end, to the precision the answer actually requires —

money to the cent, square footage to the whole foot, a rate to the hundredth of a percent. Rounding

an intermediate result and then multiplying it magnifies the error by the multiplier.

Illustration. Suppose an annual property tax bill of $4,382 is being prorated over 214 days.

The daily amount is 4,382 ÷ 365 = 12.005479…. Round that to $12.01 first and multiply:

12.01 × 214 = $2,570.14. Keep the full figure and multiply: 12.005479 × 214 = $2,569.17. The two

answers differ by $0.97 on a single line item, and every party at closing is entitled to the second

one. The half-cent you discarded was multiplied 214 times.

Sanity-check by estimation. Before you accept any number the calculator gives you, produce an

independent rough answer in your head and compare. The most useful tool for this is the 1% method:

1% of any figure is that figure with the decimal moved two places left, and every other percentage is

a small multiple or fraction of it. One percent of $412,500 is $4,125; six percent is six of those,

about $24,750 — so a displayed commission of $2,475 or $247,500 is wrong by a factor of ten, and you

know it in two seconds. Ten percent, one percent, and one-half are enough to bracket nearly any real

estate figure. A calculator answer that survives an estimate is usually right; one that contradicts

an estimate is a mis-keyed digit or a misplaced decimal until proven otherwise.

Topic quiz

Question 1 of 4

A license holder needs the seller's figure after a 6% commission on an illustrative $412,500 sale. She keys 412500 − 412500 × 0.06 = into a basic four-function calculator and the display reads 0. A colleague enters the identical keystrokes on a scientific calculator and gets 387,750. What is the correct figure, and what happened?

Arithmetic has a fixed precedence — parentheses, exponents, multiplication and division left to right, then addition and subtraction — but calculators disagree about honoring it. A scientific machine multiplies first and returns 387,750; a four-function machine evaluating strictly left to right subtracts 412,500 from 412,500, multiplies zero by 0.06, and returns 0. Both machines are working correctly and only one answered the question. The fix is not to memorize which machine you own but to break the calculation into single operations you control.

Question 2 of 4

An illustrative annual property tax bill of $5,200 is prorated over 255 days. One license holder rounds the daily amount to $14.25 and multiplies; another carries the full daily figure and rounds only the final product. What are the two answers, and which one is every party at closing entitled to?

The daily amount is $5,200 ÷ 365 = 14.246575…; rounded to $14.25 and multiplied by 255 it gives $3,633.75, and carried in full it gives $3,632.88. Rounding an intermediate result and then multiplying magnifies the error by the multiplier — here a half-cent discarded and then multiplied 255 times moved a single closing line item by $0.87. Round once, at the end: money to the cent, square footage to the whole foot, a rate to the hundredth of a percent.

Question 3 of 4

An illustrative Texas listing at $560,000 is reduced by 15%. The seller then wants to return to the original price. What is the reduced price, and what percentage increase restores $560,000?

A percentage always refers to a base, and when the quantity moves the base moves with it. The decrease of $84,000 was taken on the larger number, $560,000; the increase that undoes it must be taken on the smaller one, $476,000, so 84,000 ÷ 476,000 = 0.1765 = 17.65%. A decrease and an equal-sized increase never cancel, and the 11.11% figure belongs to a 10% reduction, not this one.

Question 4 of 4

An illustrative $438,000 sale carries a total commission of 5%, divided equally between the listing and buyer's brokerages. The listing sales agent's agreement with her sponsoring broker is a 60/40 split of the listing brokerage's share, in the agent's favor. What does the agent receive?

Commission splits are chained percentages, each taken on the output of the step before it: 438,000 × 0.05 = $21,900; ÷ 2 = $10,950 to the listing side; × 0.60 = $6,570 to the agent, with $4,380 retained by the broker. Notice that the agent did not receive 60% of the commission or 60% of anything the seller paid — she received 60% of one brokerage's half. Working the chain one link at a time, and naming the whole at each link, is the entire technique.
