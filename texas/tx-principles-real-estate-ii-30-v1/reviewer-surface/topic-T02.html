Sources of Real Estate Financing

Almost no one buys Texas real estate with cash. The overwhelming majority of residential transactions in this state close because a third party advances money against the property, and the license holder who does not understand where that money comes from cannot answer the two questions clients ask most often: who decides whether I get this loan, and who am I going to be dealing with for the next thirty years. Those are different questions with different answers, and the answers are different because the mortgage business has separated three functions that a student naturally assumes are one function.

Those three functions are origination, funding, and servicing. Origination is the work of taking an application, collecting documentation, ordering the appraisal and title work, underwriting the file against a set of credit standards, and preparing the loan for closing. Funding is the act of actually putting money on the table at closing — of being the party whose dollars are advanced and whose lien is created. Servicing is everything that happens afterward: collecting the monthly payment, allocating it between principal and interest, holding and disbursing escrowed taxes and insurance, sending statements, handling payoffs, and releasing the lien when the debt is satisfied. One institution may perform all three. Far more often, in current practice, three different institutions perform them, and the borrower's file is handed from one to the next.

Texas law itself recognizes that originators come in distinct legal flavors. Finance Code § 343.105(a) provides that "[a] lender, mortgage banker, or licensed mortgage broker shall provide to each applicant for a home loan a written notice at closing." That single sentence names the three categories of originator the Legislature expected to be present in a Texas home loan, and it names them separately because they are not the same thing.

Depository institutions.

A depository institution is one that takes deposits from the public and lends a portion of those deposits out. Commercial banks, savings institutions, and credit unions are depositories. The defining feature, for our purposes, is that a depository has its own balance sheet of customer deposits available as a funding source. It can originate a loan and fund it with money that is already sitting inside the institution, and if it chooses, it can keep that loan on its own books — hold it "in portfolio" — collect the payments itself, and never involve anyone else. When a depository holds a loan in portfolio it has originated, funded, and serviced the loan, and the borrower deals with one name from application through payoff.

Credit unions are depositories organized as member-owned cooperatives. A member's deposit is a share in the institution rather than a claim against a stockholder-owned company, and lending is made to the membership. That cooperative structure is a matter of how credit unions are chartered and organized rather than anything stated in the Texas or federal sources read for this course; treat the description as an account of how the institutions are built, not as a legal rule you can cite. What matters practically is that a credit union is a depository: it has deposits to lend, it can hold loans in portfolio, and it frequently does, which is why credit union borrowers often find that their loan never leaves the institution.

A depository's portfolio lending gives it a freedom the rest of the market does not have. Because the depository is going to keep the risk, it can write the loan to whatever terms it is willing to live with. It does not have to make the loan fit anyone else's rulebook. That flexibility is why unusual properties, unusual income, and unusual structures — a rural tract with a manufactured home, a self-employed borrower with volatile receipts, a small commercial building — are often financed by a local depository when the standardized market has no box that fits.

Non-depository lenders: mortgage bankers.

A mortgage banker is a lender that does not take deposits. It has no reservoir of customer money. Instead it funds loans out of its own capital plus a short-term revolving line of credit — commonly called a warehouse line in the trade — and then sells the closed loans, usually within weeks, to replenish the line and fund the next batch. The distinction that matters is this: the mortgage banker is the lender. The note is made payable to the mortgage banker, the deed of trust names the mortgage banker, and the mortgage banker's money funds the closing. It then transfers that note to a purchaser.

The consequence for the borrower is immediate and is the single most common source of client confusion in Texas closings. A borrower who has spent six weeks talking to one company's loan officer receives, a month after closing, a letter announcing that a company they have never heard of will be collecting the payment. Nothing has gone wrong. The mortgage banker did exactly what it always intended to do — it sold the loan — and the buyer of the loan, or a servicer the buyer hired, now performs the servicing function. A license holder who explains this at the beginning of the transaction rather than after it prevents a panicked phone call later.

Non-depository intermediaries: mortgage brokers.

A mortgage broker is not a lender at all. A broker does not fund. A broker takes the borrower's application, assembles the file, and places it with a lender that will fund it, earning compensation for that intermediary service. Regulation X captures the distinction cleanly in the rule governing the settlement-cost booklet: "If a borrower uses a mortgage broker, the mortgage broker shall distribute the special information booklet and the lender need not do so" (12 C.F.R. § 1024.6(a)(1)). The regulation treats broker and lender as two different persons in the same transaction and assigns the disclosure duty to whichever of them is standing in front of the applicant.

Regulation X draws the same line for the good faith estimate: "not later than 3 business days after a lender receives an application, or information sufficient to complete an application, the lender must provide the applicant with a GFE" (12 C.F.R. § 1024.7(a)(1)). And for the homeownership counseling list, the rule expressly allows either to deliver it while placing ultimate responsibility on one of them: "A mortgage broker or dealer may provide the list of homeownership counseling organizations required under this section to any loan applicant from whom it receives or for whom it prepares an application. If the mortgage broker or dealer has provided the required list of homeownership counseling organizations, the lender is not required to provide an additional list. The lender is responsible for ensuring that the list of homeownership counseling organizations is provided to a loan applicant in accordance with this section" (12 C.F.R. § 1024.20(a)(3)). Read those three provisions together and the architecture is plain: the broker is the applicant's point of contact and may discharge the disclosure duties, but the lender remains the party accountable for the loan and, in the counseling-list rule, for the disclosure itself.

A common error is to assume that "mortgage broker" and "mortgage banker" are two names for the same business because both appear on yard signs and both send loan officers to broker opens. They are not. The banker funds and the broker does not, and Finance Code § 343.105(a) lists them separately for exactly that reason. A second common error is to assume a mortgage broker is a real estate broker who happens to do loans. It is not; it is a distinct occupation with its own licensing scheme, and Finance Code § 343.105(a) refers to a "licensed mortgage broker" as a category separate from lenders and mortgage bankers.

The servicer.

RESPA and Regulation X treat the servicer as its own actor with its own duties. Regulation X defines an escrow account as "any account that a servicer establishes or controls on behalf of a borrower to pay taxes, insurance premiums (including flood insurance), or other charges with respect to a federally related mortgage loan" (12 C.F.R. § 1024.17(b)), and the whole apparatus of escrow analysis, initial and annual escrow statements, cushions, shortages, and surpluses in § 1024.17 is addressed to the servicer, not to the originator. Texas law also reaches the servicer directly. Finance Code § 343.106 requires the Finance Commission to adopt rules governing payoff statements and provides that a mortgage servicer who has furnished a compliant payoff statement "may not, on or before the proposed closing date, demand that a mortgagor pay an amount in excess of the payoff amount specified in the payoff statement" (Finance Code § 343.106(e)). Finance Code § 343.108(b) requires the servicer or mortgagee, within sixty days after receiving the correct payoff amount on a home loan, to deliver a release of lien to the mortgagor or file it with the county clerk. Finance Code § 343.103(b) requires a servicer, within thirty days after a qualifying request from a surviving spouse, to provide the loan information a monthly statement would have contained.

These are duties the servicer owes, and the servicer is often not the company that made the loan. When your Texas seller cannot get a payoff figure, the person to call is the servicer, and Finance Code § 343.106 is the reason the servicer has to produce one on a prescribed form within a time the Finance Commission sets — a period the statute requires to allow the servicer at least seven business days after receiving the request.

Seller financing and private lenders.

Not all mortgage money is institutional. A seller-financed transaction is one in which the seller, rather than a lender, extends the credit: the buyer pays part of the price at closing and signs a note to the seller for the balance, secured by a lien on the property being sold. Texas regulates one important form of seller financing — the executory contract, in which the seller keeps legal title while the buyer pays — extensively in Property Code chapter 5, subchapter D, and that regulation is the subject of a later unit in this module. Seller financing matters as a source because it is the source that exists when no institution will lend: on unimproved rural land, on properties with condition problems no lender will accept, and for buyers whose credit history closes the institutional door.

Private lenders — individuals, investor groups, and small lending companies advancing their own funds — occupy the same space. They fund from their own capital, they set their own terms, and they often lend on speed and collateral value rather than on documented borrower income. Two consequences follow, and both are business facts rather than legal rules, so treat them as description and cite nothing for them: private money is normally more expensive than institutional money, and it is normally shorter in term. Texas does define one product in this family. Finance Code § 343.001(1) defines a bridge loan as "temporary or short-term financing requiring payment of only interest until the entire unpaid balance is due" — the classic structure of a loan meant to carry a borrower from one permanent financing to the next.

Where private lending touches consumer home loans, Texas law reaches it. Finance Code § 343.001(2) defines a home loan as a loan "made to one or more individuals for personal, family, or household purposes" and secured in whole or part by a manufactured home used or to be used as the borrower's principal residence, or by "real property improved by a dwelling designed for occupancy by four or fewer families and used or to be used as the borrower's principal residence." Nothing in that definition asks whether the lender is a bank. If an individual investor makes a loan that meets the definition to a Texas borrower, chapter 343 applies, because Finance Code § 343.002(a) provides that the chapter "applies to a loan under this chapter that is extended to a person who is located in this state at the time the loan is made."

Why the license holder needs this.

Your client will ask you to recommend a lender. Before you answer, understand that you are making a referral in a transaction that federal law polices closely — a subject this module returns to under Financing Legislation — and that Occupations Code § 1101.652(b)(13) makes it a ground for disciplinary action if a license holder "accepts, receives, or charges an undisclosed commission, rebate, or direct profit on an expenditure made for a principal." Understand also that the recommendation has consequences your client will live with: whether the loan can be written outside a standard box, how fast it can close, and whether the servicing will move.

Question 1 of 6

A Houston buyer applies to a company whose loan officer takes the application, orders the appraisal and issues the approval on its own letterhead. At closing the note is payable to that company and its funds are wired. Five weeks later a different company writes directing all future payments to itself. What happened, and what does it tell you about the first company?

The residential mortgage business separates three functions a student naturally assumes are one: origination (taking the application and underwriting it), funding (advancing the money at closing), and servicing (collecting payments, handling escrow, issuing payoffs and releasing the lien). Funding is what distinguishes a mortgage banker from a mortgage broker — the banker is the lender and the note names it; the broker never funds. Finance Code § 343.105(a) lists a lender, a mortgage banker and a licensed mortgage broker separately for exactly that reason. A loan that is sold does not change: same principal, same rate, same schedule, same lien.

Question 2 of 6

A Fort Worth loan officer asks a mortgage applicant whether she is married, explaining that Texas marital property rules affect who must sign the deed of trust. Evaluate the inquiry under the Equal Credit Opportunity Act.

A common error is to read § 1691(a) as forbidding the question. The statute says the opposite: § 1691(b)(1) expressly permits a marital-status inquiry made to ascertain the creditor's rights and remedies — a live question in a community property state like Texas, where what the creditor's lien reaches may turn on the answer. What is forbidden is using the answer to discriminate. Note also that § 1691(a) reaches 'any aspect of a credit transaction,' not merely the yes-or-no decision: pricing, terms, the vigor with which a file is processed, and the encouragement or discouragement of an application are all aspects of it.

Question 3 of 6

Illustrative figures: a fully amortizing note of $312,000 at an annual rate of 5.4%, payable monthly, carries a payment of $1,751.98. Compute the interest and principal portions of the first payment, and say what the split shows.

The interest portion of any payment is the interest accrued on the outstanding balance since the last payment: the periodic rate is 0.054 ÷ 12 = 0.0045, and 312,000 × 0.0045 = $1,404.00. Whatever is left of the payment is principal: 1,751.98 − 1,404.00 = $347.98, leaving a balance of $311,652.02. Because the balance falls, month two's interest is 311,652.02 × 0.0045 = $1,402.43 and $349.55 goes to principal — the payment is level while its composition steadily shifts. That reversing mix is the shape of amortization and the fact clients find hardest to believe.

Question 4 of 6

An eligible veteran asks whether 'the VA will give me a loan,' and a buyer with a small down payment asks whether FHA will lend to her. Correct both premises and state the structural difference between the two programs.

The structural point to fix in memory is that FHA does not lend money and VA does not lend money. In both programs an approved private lender advances the funds and takes the application, and the government stands behind part of the lender's downside. In legal form the two devices differ: insurance is a policy under which the insurer pays the lender's covered loss, while a guaranty is a promise by a third party to answer for part of the borrower's debt, expressed as an amount or fraction of the loan. In both, the borrower pays for the protection and the protection runs to the lender. That is the entire economic logic — government-borne risk in exchange for expanded access.

Question 5 of 6

A Texas purchaser under an unrecorded executory contract for her residence has paid 44 percent of the amount due and then defaults. What remedy does Property Code § 5.066 leave the seller?

Section 5.066(a) switches the seller's remedy once the purchaser has paid 40 percent or more of the amount due, or the equivalent of 48 monthly payments, or — regardless of the amount paid — the contract has been recorded. The tests are alternatives, not cumulative requirements. In that circumstance the seller may not enforce rescission or forfeiture and acceleration; the seller gets a power of sale through a trustee, with at least sixty days to cure under § 5.066(b), notice of sale valid only after the cure period expires, and, under § 5.066(e), any surplus over the debt disbursed to the purchaser. That is the Legislature converting 'take the property back and keep everything paid' into 'sell it and hand the equity over.'

Question 6 of 6

A mortgage company proposes paying a Texas brokerage $400 for every buyer client its agents send who closes a loan with the company. The written agreement calls it a 'marketing services fee'; the brokerage would display the company's logo on its website; no agent would do any work beyond making the introduction; the buyers would be told nothing. Analyse under RESPA.

Section 2607(a) reaches both sides — giving and accepting — is not limited to money, and does not require a written agreement. The label on the payment does not create the 'services actually performed' that § 2607(b) requires, and 12 C.F.R. § 1024.14(b) forecloses the argument that the referral itself is the service. The cooperative brokerage exception does not rescue it: 12 C.F.R. § 1024.14(g)(1)(v) limits that exception to fee divisions within real estate brokerage arrangements where all parties are acting in a brokerage capacity, and gives it 'no applicability to any fee arrangements between real estate brokers and mortgage brokers.' Independently, Occupations Code § 1101.652(b)(13) makes it a ground for discipline to accept, receive or charge an undisclosed commission, rebate or direct profit on an expenditure made for a principal.
