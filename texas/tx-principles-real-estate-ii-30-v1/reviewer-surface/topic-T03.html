Requirements to Become an Appraiser

You are studying for a real estate license, not an appraiser credential. You still need this unit,

for two reasons. The first is that you will spend your career working alongside appraisers and

sometimes in tension with them — an appraisal that comes in below the contract price is one of the most

common ways a Texas transaction goes sideways — and you cannot deal intelligently with a professional

whose qualifications and constraints you do not understand. The second reason is sharper: the same

Texas statute that tells you who may perform an appraisal also tells you what you may not do. The

line between what a license holder produces for a seller and what an appraiser produces for a lender

is drawn in statute, and it is drawn in this chapter. Learn the appraiser's world and you will learn

the edge of your own.

The statute and its purpose. Appraisers in Texas are regulated under Chapter 1103 of the

Occupations Code, which § 1103.001 tells us "may be cited as the Texas Appraiser Licensing and

Certification Act." Section 1103.002 states the chapter's purpose in two clauses: to "conform state law

relating to the regulation of real estate appraisers to the requirements adopted under Title XI,

Financial Institutions Reform, Recovery, and Enforcement Act of 1989," and to "enforce standards for

the appraisal of real property." That first clause explains almost everything peculiar about appraiser

regulation. Texas did not decide on its own to license appraisers. It did so because Congress, in the

wake of the savings-and-loan collapse of the 1980s, conditioned federal recognition of appraisals on

state licensing systems that meet federal criteria. Chapter 1103 is a state statute built to satisfy a

federal design, and it therefore repeatedly hands the details to national bodies rather than fixing

them in the Texas code. That is not a drafting failure. It is the point.

The Board. Section 1103.051 provides that "[t]he Texas Appraiser Licensing and Certification Board

is an independent subdivision of the Texas Real Estate Commission." Read that sentence carefully,

because students routinely get it wrong in both directions. The **Texas Appraiser Licensing and

Certification Board** — TALCB — is not a wholly separate agency floating free of TREC, and it is not

simply a department of TREC that answers to the Commission on appraiser matters. It is an *independent

subdivision*: administratively housed within the Commission, substantively independent in the exercise

of its own authority. Section 1103.003(5) confirms that throughout Chapter 1103 the word "board" means

this body. TREC licenses brokers and sales agents; TALCB licenses and certifies appraisers. If a

consumer complains about an appraisal, the complaint belongs to TALCB.

Section 1103.052(a) sets the Board's composition: nine members, consisting of "the executive secretary

of the Veterans' Land Board or the executive secretary's designee" and "eight members appointed by the

governor with the advice and consent of the senate," of whom four must be "certified or licensed

appraisers actively engaged in the practice of appraising real property" and four must be "public

members who qualify for appointment based on their recognized business ability." Subsection (b) makes

the Veterans' Land Board representative a voting member. Subsection (c) directs the governor, in making

appointments, to ensure that no single appraisal organization is overrepresented, that the Board is

independent of any trade association, profession or industry, and that the Board represents both the

diverse geographic areas of the state and a cross-section of appraisal disciplines. Appointed members

serve staggered six-year terms under § 1103.055(a), must be qualified voters of this state under

§ 1103.053(a), and must take the constitutional oath of office not later than the fifteenth day after

appointment. Section 1103.054 makes a person ineligible to serve as a public member if the person or

the person's spouse is a certified or licensed appraiser, is licensed by an occupational regulatory

agency in real estate brokerage or appraisal, owns or controls a business whose primary purpose is real

estate sales, brokerage or appraisal, or is employed by or participates in the management of such a

business. The Legislature's evident concern in these provisions is capture: a regulator staffed

entirely by the regulated is a regulator with a conflict, and the statute answers that concern with a

half-public board and explicit anti-overrepresentation instructions.

The credential categories. Here is where the federal architecture shows. Section 1103.152 does not

list the credentials. It says the Board may "establish certificate and license categories that are

consistent with the categories recognized by the Appraiser Qualifications Board" and may "prescribe

qualifications for each category that are consistent with the qualifications established for that

category by the Appraiser Qualifications Board." The Appraiser Qualifications Board, defined at

§ 1103.003(4), is the Appraiser Qualifications Board of the Appraisal Foundation — a national private

body, not a Texas agency. Chapter 1103 delegates the content of the credentials to it and keeps the

authority to issue them in Texas.

The definitions section nonetheless names the tiers the chapter contemplates. Section 1103.003(5-a)

defines a certified appraiser as "a person who is certified under this chapter to practice as a

certified general or certified residential appraiser." Section 1103.003(7) defines a **licensed

appraiser** as "a person who is licensed under this chapter to practice as a residential real estate

appraiser." Section 1103.003(4-a) defines an appraiser trainee as "an appraiser trainee licensed

under this chapter." So the statutory ladder, in the chapter's own vocabulary, runs: appraiser trainee;

licensed residential appraiser; certified residential appraiser; certified general appraiser. The

certified general credential is the one associated with commercial and complex property work; the

residential credentials with dwellings. Chapter 1103 itself does not state the property-type or

transaction-value limits attached to each tier, because § 1103.152 sends those to Board rule tracking

the Appraiser Qualifications Board's categories.

Qualification: the three-legged structure. Section 1103.202 is the section to know. To be eligible

for a certificate or license under the chapter, an applicant must:

(1) pass the applicable examination required by Subchapter F; (2) successfully complete the number

and type of classroom hours or other educational qualifications required by the Appraiser

Qualifications Board; (3) provide evidence satisfactory to the board that the applicant has at least

the minimum number of hours of experience in performing appraisals over the specified number of

calendar years as required by the Appraiser Qualifications Board; (4) satisfy the board as to the

applicant's honesty, trustworthiness, and integrity; and (5) comply with the requirements of Sections

1103.203 and 1103.2031.

Notice what subdivisions (2) and (3) do not say. They do not say "150 hours" or "1,000 hours" or "two

years." They say "the number and type of classroom hours … required by the Appraiser Qualifications

Board" and "the minimum number of hours of experience in performing appraisals over the specified

number of calendar years as required by the Appraiser Qualifications Board." **Chapter 1103 does not

state the hour counts.** Any course that tells you Texas statute requires a specific number of

qualifying education hours or

experience hours for an appraiser credential is telling you something the statute does not contain.

The numbers exist, they change, and they live in the Appraiser Qualifications Board's criteria as

adopted by TALCB rule — which is exactly why the Legislature wrote the statute this way. If you need

the current figures, you go to the Board's rules and the AQB criteria, not to Chapter 1103. This course

holds neither of those documents, and so this course does not state the numbers.

The statute does say useful structural things about each leg. On education, § 1103.204(b) provides

that the chapter "does not limit the amount of time in which an applicant … is required to satisfy the

education requirements" — there is no statutory expiration on qualifying coursework. Subsection (c) is

directly relevant to you: the Board "shall give an applicant for a certificate or license credit"

toward the § 1103.202(2) education requirement "for classroom hours taken in the course of becoming

licensed as a real estate broker or sales agent or for professional development or continuing education

courses taken," provided those hours "satisfy the requirements established by the guidelines recognized

by the Appraiser Qualifications Board." A sales agent who later pursues an appraiser credential does

not automatically start from zero.

On experience, § 1103.205(a) likewise imposes no time limit for satisfying the requirement.

Subsection (c) provides that acceptable appraisal experience "includes any one or any combination of

the categories recognized by the Appraiser Qualifications Board" — again the delegation. Subsection (d)

is precise in a way worth noticing: "an hour of experience means 60 minutes spent in one or more of the

acceptable areas of appraisal experience recognized under this subchapter. Calculation of the hours of

experience must be based solely on actual hours of experience." Not billable hours, not estimated

hours; actual clock time. Section 1103.206(b) requires the Board to adopt a reliable verification

method that "must include the review of appraisal experience of all applicants for certification," and

gives an applicant at least sixty days after notice is sent to produce requested records. Section

1103.207 lets the Board demand more detail where a fraud, incompetency or malpractice complaint appears

reasonable or other just cause exists, including a listing of each claimed appraisal stating the

municipality or county, the type and description of the property, the approach to value used, and the

actual hours spent.

On examination, § 1103.251 requires the Board to prescribe an appraiser examination that must be

"written" and "approved by the Appraiser Qualifications Board." Section 1103.255 imposes a sequencing

rule students often reverse: "An applicant for the examination must fulfill the applicable experience

requirement for a certificate or license before taking the examination." Experience first, then exam.

Section 1103.256 sets the passing standard by reference — "the minimum score required by the Appraiser

Qualifications Board" — so again, no number in the Texas statute. Section 1103.253 requires the exam to

be offered at least once each month in Austin and at such other locations and times as the Board

determines. Section 1103.254(b) makes an examination application active for one year; an applicant who

has not passed by the first anniversary must reapply and pay again. Section 1103.257(a) requires notice

of results not later than the tenth day after the examination, and subsection (d) makes results

confidential. Under § 1103.258(b), an applicant who fails three consecutive times may not reapply

without evidence of additional education prescribed by the Board.

The trainee tier. Because experience must precede the examination, there must be a lawful way to

acquire experience before holding a credential, and Subchapter H supplies it. Section 1103.353 sets

trainee eligibility: at least eighteen years of age; a citizen of the United States or a lawfully

admitted alien; satisfying the Board as to honesty, trustworthiness and integrity; compliance with

§§ 1103.203 and 1103.2031; and meeting "all other qualifications established by the board." Section

1103.351 authorizes the Board to permit a certified appraiser meeting Board requirements to act as a

supervisory appraiser, and expressly allows a trainee to have more than one. Section 1103.354 is

the operative provision: a trainee "may perform an appraisal under the direction of a supervisory

appraiser," the supervisory appraiser "shall sign each report prepared by the appraiser trainee," and

the supervisory appraiser "is responsible to the public and to the board for the appraiser trainee's

reports and conduct." The trainee may co-sign. Responsibility, in other words, does not dilute as it

travels down; the supervisor owns the work.

Holding the credential. Section 1103.201(a) states the prohibition plainly: "A person may not

perform an appraisal of real estate unless the person is licensed or certified as an appraiser under

this chapter, registered as a temporary out-of-state appraiser under this chapter, or acting as an

appraiser trainee under the supervision of a supervisory appraiser." Subsection (b) adds a title

restriction — no using "certified real estate appraiser" or "licensed real estate appraiser," and no

calling one's own work a "certified appraisal" or "licensed appraisal," without the corresponding

credential. Section 1103.401 broadens that to any "title, designation, initials, or other insignia or

identification that would mislead the public" about one's credentials or competency. The teeth are

real: § 1103.5545 makes engaging in activity requiring a certificate or license without holding one a

Class A misdemeanor; § 1103.554 makes a knowing violation of § 1103.401 a Class B misdemeanor; and

§ 1103.5535 exposes an unlicensed person who received consideration to a civil penalty of not less than

the value of that consideration and not more than three times its value.

A credential expires "on the second anniversary of the date of issuance" under § 1103.211(a), and

renewal under subsection (b) requires the fee, evidence of continuing education complying with AQB

guidelines and Board rule, satisfaction of the Board as to honesty, trustworthiness and integrity, and

compliance with §§ 1103.203 and 1103.2031. Section 1103.2111 grades late renewal: expired ninety days

or less, one and one-half times the renewal fee; more than ninety days but less than six months, two

times the fee; six months or longer, no renewal at all — the person "may obtain a new certificate or

license by complying with the requirements and procedures for an original application." Subsection (c)

warns that no one may perform an appraisal in a federally related transaction while not actively

licensed or certified. Section 1103.213 provides for inactive status, during which the holder "may not

perform any activity regulated under this chapter" but must still pay annual renewal fees. Out-of-state

appraisers get Subchapter G: § 1103.301 lets an appraiser certified or licensed by another state

appraise here without a Texas credential if the person registers with the Board, and § 1103.303

conditions eligibility on the other state's program not having been disapproved by the Appraisal

Subcommittee and on the person's Texas appraisal business not exceeding six months. Section 1103.304

allows one ninety-day extension, and only one. Full reciprocity is separate: § 1103.209(a) requires the

Board to issue a reciprocal license or certificate where the other state's program complies with 12

U.S.C. § 3331 et seq., the applicant's home-state requirements meet or exceed Texas's, and the

honesty-and-integrity and application requirements are met.

Standards of practice. Section 1103.405(a) requires a person holding a certificate, license or

registration under the chapter to comply with "the most current edition of the Uniform Standards of

Professional Appraisal Practice adopted by the Appraisal Standards Board of the Appraisal Foundation"

or with other Board-rule standards "at least as stringent" as USPAP. Section 1103.154 authorizes the

Board to adopt rules incorporating USPAP and requiring compliance with its competency, conduct and

ethics standards. Two further practice rules matter to you as a license holder dealing with appraisers:

§ 1103.402(a) forbids a credentialed person to "sign or endorse an appraisal unless the appraisal is

substantially produced by that person," with subsection (b) defining substantial production as

contributing "in a material and verifiable manner to the research or analysis that results in the final

opinion of value"; and § 1103.404 requires retention of all business records relating to each appraisal

"until at least the fifth anniversary of the date of the appraisal." Section 1103.403(a) requires a

Texas-resident credentialed appraiser to maintain a fixed office in this state.

Question 1 of 5

A student is told that 'TREC issues the appraiser credential, and Chapter 1103 of the Occupations Code requires a certified residential appraiser to complete a fixed number of qualifying education hours.' Evaluate both halves.

Section 1103.051 makes TALCB 'an independent subdivision of the Texas Real Estate Commission' — administratively housed within the Commission, substantively independent in exercising its own authority. TREC licenses brokers and sales agents; TALCB licenses and certifies appraisers, and an appraisal complaint belongs to TALCB. Section 1103.202(2) and (3) each end by referring to what is 'required by the Appraiser Qualifications Board,' so any claim that Texas statute requires a specific number of qualifying education or experience hours is a claim the statute does not contain. The numbers exist, they change, and they live in the AQB criteria as adopted by Board rule.

Question 2 of 5

Three conditions affect a Texas house: a failing twenty-year-old composition roof; a bedroom that can be reached only by walking through another bedroom; and a six-lane arterial newly widened against the back fence. Classify each form of depreciation and say which the owner could cure.

In appraisal, depreciation means loss in value from any cause — not the accounting concept and not the tax concept — and it comes in three forms distinguished by where the cause sits. Physical deterioration is wear, tear and the elements acting on the improvement. Functional obsolescence is a loss caused by the property's own design or layout, which the market no longer wants. External obsolescence is caused by something outside the property boundary. The test to hold onto: physical and functional causes are inside the fence line, external causes are outside it, and only the owner's own causes are potentially curable.

Question 3 of 5

A lender has ordered the appraisal on your buyer's Bastrop County contract. The listing agent emails: 'Please forward these three comps to the appraiser, they're the strongest in the area.' Separately you are asked to relay that 'we need $472,000 to make this work.' What may you do?

Your role in the appraisal process is to supply accurate specific data and access, and nothing more. Appraisers routinely accept comparables from license holders — provide them because they are genuinely comparable, not because they are high, and supplying the four highest sales in the subdivision while omitting the three closest is advocacy dressed as assistance. Transmitting the contract is legitimate assignment information; transmitting a number the deal needs is an attempt to influence an opinion of value, which is exactly the conduct appraisal independence exists to prevent.

Question 4 of 5

Illustrative figures for a five-unit retail building: potential gross income of $120,000 (5 × $2,000 per month × 12); vacancy and collection loss at 8%, or $9,600, for effective gross income of $110,400; property taxes $19,200; insurance $6,800; management at 5% of effective gross income, $5,520; maintenance and reserves $10,400; utilities and common area $5,280. The owner's mortgage payments run $4,300 a month. Comparable sales indicate an overall capitalization rate of 8%. What is the indicated value?

Direct capitalization runs on the IRV relationship: V = I ÷ R, R = I ÷ V, and I = R × V. The income used is net operating income — income after vacancy and operating expenses, but before debt service and before income taxes. Here 110,400 − 47,200 = $63,200 of NOI, and 63,200 ÷ 0.08 = $790,000. Two errors are worth naming. Capitalizing effective gross income skips the expenses entirely. Multiplying rather than dividing inverts the relationship — a check is that a value must be far larger than the income that produces it.

Question 5 of 5

A Texas license holder prepares a written valuation of a Fort Worth warehouse for a bank deciding whether to make a commercial loan, titles the document 'Broker Price Opinion,' charges a flat fee, and has no listing or buyer relationship with anyone in the transaction. Which of the three statutory conditions is most doubtful?

Occupations Code § 1101.002(1)(A)(xi) and Occupations Code § 1103.004(a)(2) state the same three conditions, and all three must be satisfied: the analysis is not referred to as an appraisal; it is provided in the ordinary course of the person's business; and it relates to the actual or potential management, acquisition, disposition or encumbrance of an interest in real property. On these facts the naming condition is met and a transactional purpose exists, but the work sits outside any brokerage relationship. Section 1103.5545 makes engaging in activity requiring a credential without one a Class A misdemeanor, and § 1103.5535 exposes an unlicensed person who received consideration to a civil penalty of not less than the value of that consideration and not more than three times its value.
