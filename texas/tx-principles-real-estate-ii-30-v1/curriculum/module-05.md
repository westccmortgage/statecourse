# Texas Principles of Real Estate II — Module 05

## TREC Topic: Control of Land Use

**Prescribed time: 110 minutes.** This module carries the three units the Commission's PRINS2-1 course approval form places under *Control of Land Use*, whose scope note is *"Finance & regulations"*.

**Learning objectives.** After completing this module you will be able to: identify the statutory sources of public and private land-use control in Texas, distinguish a zoning restriction from a recorded restrictive covenant, and state where each is found; explain the framework by which government acquires and holds interests in private land, including condemnation, dedication, and the state's interest in water and navigable streams; and identify the environmental conditions a Texas seller's disclosure notice puts in issue, state what the federal lead-based paint disclosure section requires of a seller, a lessor, and the seller's own agent, and state the license holder's duty when a known condition affects a property.

**Every legal statement in this module comes from a source that was read for this course.** Where a statement is practice rather than law, the module says so and cites nothing for it.

## Unit: Land-Use Controls

Land in Texas is controlled from two directions at once, and the two systems are written down in different places, enforced by different people, and answerable to different rules. The first is **public control**: regulation imposed by a governmental body under the police power. The second is **private control**: restrictions imposed by recorded instrument, running with the land, enforced by neighbors and property owners' associations. A license holder who checks only one of the two has done half the job.

**The police power as the basis for public regulation.** Public land-use regulation is not justified by the government owning anything. It is justified by the government's authority to regulate for the protection of the public, and the Texas Legislature says so in the first line of the municipal zoning chapter. Local Government Code § 211.001 states that the powers granted under that subchapter "are for the purpose of promoting the public health, safety, morals, or general welfare and protecting and preserving places and areas of historical, cultural, or architectural importance and significance." That is the whole theory of zoning in one clause: a municipality may tell an owner what to do with the owner's own land, without paying for it, because the regulation serves a public purpose. When governmental power stops being regulation and becomes an acquisition, the analysis changes entirely — the subject of the next unit.

**What a Texas municipality may regulate.** Local Government Code § 211.003(a) lists the tools. The governing body of a municipality may regulate "the height, number of stories, and size of buildings and other structures"; "the percentage of a lot that may be occupied"; "the size of yards, courts, and other open spaces"; "population density"; "the location and use of buildings, other structures, and land for business, industrial, residential, or other purposes"; and the pumping, extraction, and use of groundwater by persons other than retail public utilities where the groundwater "presents an actual or potential threat to human health." Section 211.003(b) adds authority over construction, reconstruction, alteration, or razing of buildings in designated places of historical, cultural, or architectural significance, and § 211.003(c) permits a home-rule municipality to regulate the **bulk** of buildings.

Notice the shape of that list. The **use** regulation — what activity may occur on the land — is one item among six; the rest are **bulk, height, and area** regulations. A buyer's plan can be defeated by either: a use may be permitted in the district and the project still fail because the building will not fit inside the required yards.

**The comprehensive plan requirement.** Zoning is not a free-floating power. Section 211.004(a) provides that "[z]oning regulations must be adopted in accordance with a comprehensive plan" and must be designed to serve listed ends — lessening street congestion, securing safety from fire and panic, promoting health and the general welfare, providing adequate light and air, preventing overcrowding, avoiding undue concentration of population, or facilitating adequate transportation, water, sewers, schools, and parks. Section 211.005(a) then lets the municipality divide itself into **districts** of the number, shape, and size the governing body considers best, and § 211.005(b) requires regulations to "be uniform for each class or kind of building in a district," though they "may vary from district to district."

**How a zoning regulation is adopted.** Section 211.006(a) provides that a regulation or district boundary "is not effective until after a public hearing on the matter at which parties in interest and citizens have an opportunity to be heard," with notice published before the 15th day before the hearing in an official newspaper or newspaper of general circulation and, if the municipality maintains a website, on that website. Section 211.006(a-1) adds a targeted written notice where a proposed change would turn a current conforming use into a nonconforming one, delivered to each owner and occupant not later than the 10th day before the hearing and containing, in bold 14-point type or larger, the warning that the municipality "IS HOLDING A HEARING THAT WILL DETERMINE WHETHER YOU MAY LOSE THE RIGHT TO CONTINUE USING YOUR PROPERTY FOR ITS CURRENT USE."

The **zoning commission** is the drafting body. Under § 211.007(a) the governing body of a home-rule municipality *shall*, and of a general-law municipality *may*, appoint one; it recommends the original district boundaries and the regulations for each district. Section 211.007(b) requires a preliminary report and at least one public hearing before a final report goes to the governing body, which "may not take action on the matter until it receives the final report." Section 211.007(c) requires written notice, before the 10th day before the hearing, to each owner of real property within 200 feet of the property whose classification is proposed to change. Neighbors also have a formal lever: § 211.0061(b) allows a written protest signed by the owners of at least 20 percent of the area covered by the change or of the adjoining area extending 200 feet from it, and under § 211.0061(d) a validly protested change then needs the affirmative vote of at least three-fourths of all members of the governing body.

**The board of adjustment, variances, and special exceptions.** Section 211.008(a) authorizes a municipality to appoint a **board of adjustment** and to empower it "to make special exceptions to the terms of the zoning ordinance that are consistent with the general purpose and intent of the ordinance." The board must have at least five members appointed for two-year terms (§ 211.008(b)), and each case "must be heard by at least 75 percent of the members" (§ 211.008(d)).

Section 211.009(a) sets out what the board may do: hear and decide an appeal alleging error by an administrative official enforcing the ordinance; hear and decide special exceptions when the ordinance requires it; and "authorize in specific cases a **variance** from the terms of a zoning ordinance if the variance is not contrary to the public interest and, due to special conditions, a literal enforcement of the ordinance would result in unnecessary hardship, and so that the spirit of the ordinance is observed and substantial justice is done." Section 211.009(b-1) lists grounds the board *may* consider on the hardship question for a structure, among them that the cost of compliance exceeds 50 percent of the structure's appraised value on the most recent certified appraisal roll, or that compliance would cost the lot at least 25 percent of its physically developable area. Under § 211.009(c), a concurring vote of 75 percent of the members is necessary to reverse an official's decision or to authorize a variance.

A common error is to treat a variance as a request to be excused from a rule the applicant dislikes, or to treat "I paid too much for the lot" as hardship. The standard is about *special conditions* and a result still faithful to the ordinance's spirit. A second common error confuses the remedies: a **special exception** is a use or condition the ordinance itself contemplates and delegates to the board, while a **variance** is a departure from the ordinance's terms. Deadlines matter too. Section 211.010(b) requires an appeal to the board not later than the 20th day after the decision, and § 211.010(d) requires the board to decide not later than the 60th day after filing. Under § 211.011 a person aggrieved, a taxpayer, or a municipal officer, department, board, or bureau may present a verified petition to a district court, county court, or county court at law, and § 211.011(b) requires it "within 10 days after the date the decision is filed in the board's office."

**Nonconforming uses.** When a district is created or changed, existing uses do not always fit. Section 211.019(b) provides that a person using property in a manner considered a nonconforming use as a result of the adoption of or change to a zoning regulation or boundary "may continue to use the property in the same manner unless required by a municipality to stop the nonconforming use." That is what "grandfathering" means in Texas: continuation as of right, not immunity forever. If the municipality does require the use to stop, § 211.019(d) gives the owner or lessee a choice between a payment from the municipality — costs directly attributable to ceasing the use, plus the diminution in market value — or continued nonconforming use until that amount is recovered through continued business activity. Section 211.019(o) removes the protection entirely for a use "intentionally abandoned for at least six months." Section 211.012 lets the municipality make a violation a misdemeanor, provide civil penalties, and sue to restrain, correct, or abate it, and § 211.013(a) resolves overlaps by a **stricter-standard rule**: where the zoning regulation imposes higher standards than another statute or local ordinance the zoning regulation controls, and where the other law is stricter the other law controls.

**Texas reality: zoning is a municipal option, not a statewide mandate.** Chapter 211 grants zoning power to "the governing body of a municipality," and it sits in a subtitle titled *Municipal Regulatory Authority*. Nothing in the chapter grants that power to a county. Section 211.006(a) speaks of "[t]he governing body of a municipality **wishing to exercise** the authority relating to zoning regulations" — permissive language, not a command. Section 211.015 lets the voters of a home-rule municipality repeal the municipality's zoning regulations in their entirety by charter election or, on initial adoption, by referendum. And the Legislature has written the possibility of a Texas city without zoning into another chapter: Local Government Code § 212.151 makes the municipal deed-restriction-enforcement subchapter available to a municipality with a population of 1.5 million or more that passes a qualifying ordinance and, separately, to a municipality "that does not have zoning ordinances and passes an ordinance that requires uniform application and enforcement of this subchapter with regard to all property and residents."

Houston is the well-known example of a very large Texas city that has not adopted conventional use-district zoning; that is common knowledge about how Texas cities have chosen to govern themselves, not a statutory rule, and no source read for this course states it. What the statutes *do* establish is the structure that makes it possible. Two consequences follow. First, land in the unincorporated portion of a Texas county is not zoned the way land inside a city is, because chapter 211 does not reach it. Second, in a municipality without zoning, recorded private restrictions do far more of the work of separating uses — which is exactly why § 212.151 exists.

**Subdivision regulation and platting.** Even a municipality that never adopts a use-district ordinance regulates how raw land becomes lots. Under § 212.002 a municipality may, after a public hearing, adopt rules governing plats and subdivisions within its jurisdiction. Section 212.004(a) requires a plat whenever an owner of a tract inside the limits or in the extraterritorial jurisdiction of a municipality divides it into two or more parts to lay out a subdivision, lots, streets, alleys, squares, parks, or other parts intended to be dedicated to public use — and the division counts however accomplished, "by using a metes and bounds description in a deed of conveyance or in a contract for a deed, by using a contract of sale or other executory contract to convey, or by using any other method." The subsection excludes "a division of land into parts greater than five acres, where each part has access and no public improvement is being dedicated." Section 212.004(d) requires the plat to be filed and recorded with the county clerk.

Approval is largely ministerial and is on a clock. Section 212.005(a) states that the responsible municipal authority "must approve a plat or replat that is required to be prepared under this subchapter and that satisfies the requirements of this subchapter." Section 212.006(a) identifies that authority as the municipal planning commission, or the governing body where there is none. Section 212.009(a) requires approval, conditional approval, or disapproval "within 30 days after the date the plat is filed," and a plat "is approved by the municipal authority unless it is disapproved within that period." Section 212.0091 requires a written statement clearly articulating each condition or reason, directly related to the subchapter's requirements and citing the law relied on where applicable, and it "may not be arbitrary." Section 212.010(e) gives an owner whose conforming plat is refused an action for a writ of mandamus with reasonable attorney's fees. Section 212.012(a) then forbids listed utility providers to "serve or connect any land with water, sewer, electricity, gas, or other utility service" unless presented with or otherwise holding the approval certificate issued under § 212.0115. And a replat may be recorded without vacating the preceding plat under § 212.014 only if it "does not attempt to amend or remove any covenants or restrictions" — a statutory acknowledgement that the public plat process does not override private covenants.

**Extraterritorial jurisdiction.** A Texas municipality's reach extends past its city limits, but not evenly. Section 212.001(1) defines **extraterritorial jurisdiction** by reference to Chapter 42 of the Local Government Code. Section 212.003(a) lets a municipality extend its subdivision ordinances into the ETJ — and then flatly limits what it may do there: "unless otherwise authorized by state law, in its extraterritorial jurisdiction a municipality shall not regulate: (1) the use of any building or property for business, industrial, residential, or other purposes; (2) the bulk, height, or number of buildings constructed on a particular tract of land; (3) the size of a building that can be constructed on a particular tract of land...; [or] (4) the number of residential units that can be built per acre of land." Section 212.049 adds that the development-plat subchapter "does not authorize the municipality to require municipal building permits or otherwise enforce the municipality's building code in its extraterritorial jurisdiction." The teaching point is precise: in the ETJ expect **platting and subdivision** regulation, and do not assume **use, density, height, or bulk** regulation. This is the most misdescribed piece of Texas land-use law in ordinary conversation.

**Private controls: deed restrictions and restrictive covenants.** Now the other half. Property Code § 202.001(4) defines a **restrictive covenant** as "any covenant, condition, or restriction contained in a dedicatory instrument, whether mandatory, prohibitive, permissive, or administrative." A **dedicatory instrument**, under § 202.001(1), is "each document governing the establishment, maintenance, or operation of a residential subdivision, planned unit development, condominium or townhouse regime, or any similar planned development," and expressly includes the declaration, the bylaws, the association's properly adopted rules, and all lawful amendments. Section 202.001(2) defines the **property owners' association** through which the owners or their board manage or regulate the development. Chapter 202 reaches back — § 202.002(a) applies it "to all restrictive covenants regardless of the date on which they were created" — and the interpretive rule cuts in favor of the restriction: § 202.003(a) provides that "[a] restrictive covenant shall be liberally construed to give effect to its purposes and intent," subject to the family-home limitation in § 202.003(b).

Enforcement powers are substantial. Section 202.004(a) provides that "[a]n exercise of discretionary authority by a property owners' association or other representative designated by an owner of real property concerning a restrictive covenant is presumed reasonable unless the court determines by a preponderance of the evidence that the exercise of discretionary authority was arbitrary, capricious, or discriminatory." Section 202.004(b) allows the association to initiate, defend, or intervene in litigation affecting enforcement, and § 202.004(c) permits a court to "assess civil damages for the violation of a restrictive covenant in an amount not to exceed $200 for each day of the violation." Property Code § 5.006(a) adds that in an action based on breach of a restrictive covenant the court "shall allow to a prevailing party who asserted the action reasonable attorney's fees in addition to the party's costs and claim."

A worked illustration, using figures chosen only to show the arithmetic and not drawn from any required schedule: an owner leaves a detached workshop that violates a recorded setback covenant in place for 90 days after judgment. At the § 202.004(c) ceiling of $200 per day, the maximum civil damages for that period would be 90 × $200 = $18,000, plus the prevailing claimant's reasonable attorney's fees under § 5.006(a). The point is not the number; it is that private restrictions in Texas carry a real, statutorily quantified downside on top of an injunction requiring removal.

**Where private restrictions live, and the limits on them.** Section 202.006(a) requires a property owners' association to file all dedicatory instruments in the real property records of each county where the property is located, and § 202.006(b) is the sentence to remember: "A dedicatory instrument has no effect until the instrument is filed in accordance with this section." Section 202.006(c) adds that an association may not collect a regular assessment if the instrument authorizing it was not filed. So the private control system is a **county real property records** system while the public control system is a **city hall** system; they are searched in different buildings.

The Legislature has also carved out categories no association may prohibit outright, each with detailed allowances letting the association regulate appearance and location instead: water-conserving landscaping and rainwater harvesting (§ 202.007); **solar energy devices** (§ 202.010); certain durable or solar-generating shingles that match the surrounding property (§ 202.011); display of the United States, Texas, and armed-forces flags (§ 202.012); permanently installed **standby electric generators** (§ 202.019); lawful possession of firearms and ammunition (§ 202.021); conforming swimming pool enclosures (§ 202.022); security measures including cameras and perimeter fencing (§ 202.023); and renting based on the tenant's **method of payment**, which § 202.024(a) defines to include a housing choice voucher (§ 202.024). When a client asks whether the HOA can stop a solar array, the answer is usually "not outright, but it can regulate how and where." Separately, Property Code § 5.026(a) provides that a restriction or deed provision prohibiting use by, or sale, lease, or transfer to, a person because of race, color, religion, or national origin "is void," and § 5.026(b) requires a court to dismiss a suit to enforce it.

**Private restrictions can be stricter than public zoning — and a city can sometimes enforce them.** Nothing requires a covenant to stop where the ordinance stops. A district may permit a 35-foot building while the declaration permits 28 feet; a district may permit a duplex while the covenant permits one single-family dwelling. The stricter of the two governs the owner's actual freedom, because each is independently enforceable by its own enforcer. Texas also lets certain municipalities enforce private restrictions directly. Under Local Government Code § 212.153(a) a covered municipality "may sue in any court of competent jurisdiction to enjoin or abate a violation of a restriction contained or incorporated by reference in a properly recorded plan, plat, or other instrument that affects a subdivision located inside the boundaries of the municipality," though § 212.153(b) bars that suit if a property owners' association with authority to enforce files its own. Section 212.152 defines the covered "restriction" broadly — use character, setbacks, lot and structure size, permitted activities, architectural features, fences, landscaping, noise, and required maintenance — which is, in substance, a list of the things zoning would otherwise do. Section 212.156(a) allows the governing body by ordinance to require compliance and to sue for a civil penalty.

**The practical rule for a license holder.** Two searches, two places, every time. The public side is found at the municipality: the zoning map and ordinance, the district's permitted uses and dimensional standards, the plat, whether the property is inside the city limits or in the ETJ, and whether the city has zoning at all. The private side is found in the county real property records: the recorded plat, the declaration and its amendments, and the association's dedicatory instruments, which under § 202.006(b) have no effect until filed there. Property Code § 5.012(a) requires a seller of residential property subject to POA membership to give the purchaser a written notice stating that the restrictive covenants and dedicatory instruments "have been or will be recorded in the Real Property Records of the county in which the property is located" and that copies "may be obtained from the county clerk" — the statute is telling the buyer exactly where to look. Reading the ordinance and stopping there, or reading the declaration and stopping there, is how a client ends up owning land that cannot be used the way the client bought it to be used.

### Applied exercise: The buyer whose plan is blocked

A buyer contracts to purchase a platted lot in a Texas home-rule municipality, inside the city limits and inside a recorded subdivision governed by a property owners' association. The buyer intends to build a single-family residence plus a detached 900-square-foot structure to be used as a licensed accounting practice with two employees and client visits. The city's zoning ordinance places the lot in a single-family residential district. The subdivision's recorded declaration limits each lot to "one single-family dwelling and customary accessory structures, no lot to be used for any business, trade, or commercial purpose."

1. Which of the two controls would you check first, and where physically would you find each? Identify the provisions that tell you where the private instrument is recorded and when it takes effect.

4. Change one fact: the lot is not inside the city limits but in the municipality's extraterritorial jurisdiction. Under § 212.003(a), which control has changed, and which has not?

## Unit: Public Ownership

The previous unit dealt with government telling an owner what may be done with land the owner keeps. This unit deals with government acquiring, holding, and administering interests in land itself — and with the line between the two, because that line decides whether an owner is paid.

**Regulation versus acquisition.** When a municipality zones a tract, it exercises the police power described in Local Government Code § 211.001, and the owner keeps the land. When a governmental body takes an interest in the tract for public use, it must acquire that interest, and the owner must be compensated. The two elements ordinarily stated for a valid exercise of **eminent domain** — the power of a governmental entity to take private property — are that the taking be for a **public use** and that the owner receive **just compensation**. The Texas statutes read for this course confirm the compensation element without setting out the constitutional standard, and this module does not state constitutional text that was not read. Water Code § 11.035(b) provides that "[t]he party obtaining private property by condemnation shall cause damages to be assessed and paid for as provided by the statutes of this state relating to eminent domain." The word **condemnation** names the *procedure* by which the power of eminent domain is exercised; a common error is to use the two terms interchangeably.

**Who holds the power.** Eminent domain is not held by every public body simply because it is public. Water Code § 11.033 states the delegation for water-related purposes: "All political subdivisions of the state and constitutional governmental agencies exercising delegated legislative powers have the power of eminent domain to be exercised as provided by law for domestic, municipal, and manufacturing uses and for other purposes authorized by this code, including the irrigation of land for all requirements of agricultural employment." The same section declares that "[t]he right to take water necessary for domestic and municipal supply purposes is primary and fundamental." A condemning authority must therefore point to a delegation, and the taking must fall within it.

Texas has also delegated condemnation power to private water appropriators, under supervision, and the sequence shows the shape of the framework. Water Code § 11.035(a) provides that "[a]n appropriator may obtain rights-of-way over private land and may obtain the land necessary for pumping plants, intakes, headgates, and storage reservoirs by condemnation." Section 11.035(d) requires investigation, notice to the landowner, and a hearing; and § 11.035(e) provides that if the commission determines the condemnation is necessary, the executive director may institute proceedings "in the name of the State of Texas for the use and benefit of the party who applied." A delegated power, a statutory public purpose, a determination of necessity, notice and hearing, and assessed damages paid to the owner.

**Dedication.** Not every public interest in land is taken; much of it is given. **Dedication** is the owner's offer of land to public use, and the platting statutes carry the mechanics. Local Government Code § 212.004(a) speaks of laying out "streets, alleys, squares, parks, or other parts of the tract intended by the owner of the tract to be dedicated to public use," and § 212.004(b)(3) requires the plat to state the dimensions of each such part. The offer is not self-executing. Section 212.011(a) provides that "[t]he approval of a plat is not considered an acceptance of any proposed dedication and does not impose on the municipality any duty regarding the maintenance or improvement of any dedicated parts until the appropriate municipal authorities make an actual appropriation of the dedicated parts by entry, use, or improvement," and § 212.011(b) provides that disapproval "is considered a refusal by the municipality of the offered dedication indicated on the plat."

Dedication in Texas platting therefore has two sides: an **offer** shown on the plat and an **acceptance** shown by entry, use, or improvement. A buyer looking at a platted "park" or a platted street stub should not assume the city owns or maintains it. The Legislature has also limited what a city may demand: § 212.010(c) provides that the responsible authority "may not require the dedication of land within a subdivision for a future street or alley that is: (1) not intended by the owner of the tract; and (2) not included, funded, and approved in" an adopted capital improvement plan of the municipality, or a similar plan of the county or the state. And once a public corridor exists, private instruments cannot narrow it: Local Government Code § 212.153(e) provides that a municipality "may not enforce a deed restriction which purports to regulate or restrict the rights granted to public utilities to install, operate, maintain, replace, and remove facilities within easements and private or public rights-of-way."

**State ownership of water, and the surveying rule behind streambeds.** Some public ownership in Texas is very old and very broad. Water Code § 11.021(a) provides that "[t]he water of the ordinary flow, underflow, and tides of every flowing river, natural stream, and lake, and of every bay or arm of the Gulf of Mexico, and the storm water, floodwater, and rainwater of every river, natural stream, canyon, ravine, depression, and watershed in the state is the property of the state." Section 11.0235(a) adds that "[t]he waters of the state are held in trust for the public, and the right to use state water may be appropriated only as expressly authorized by law," and § 11.022 provides that the right to use state water "may be acquired by appropriation in the manner and for the purposes provided in this chapter" — the purposes § 11.023 lists, including domestic and municipal supply, agriculture, industry, mining, navigation, recreation, and "any other beneficial use." The consequence for a transaction is direct: a buyer of riverfront acreage does not buy the flowing water. If the buyer's plan requires diverting or impounding state water, the buyer needs a right acquired under Chapter 11, and a license holder should say so and refer the client rather than guess.

The public interest in streambeds has a surveying origin worth knowing. Natural Resources Code § 21.001(3) defines a **navigable stream** as "a stream which retains an average width of 30 feet from the mouth up." Section 21.012(a) provides that land surveyed for individuals lying on a navigable stream "shall front one-half of the square on the stream with the line running at right angles with the general course of the stream," where prior lines permit, and § 21.012(b) states the rule flatly: "A navigable stream may not be crossed by the lines of a survey." Section 21.011 requires each survey of public land to be made under authority of law by a surveyor duly appointed, elected, or licensed and qualified. The practical takeaway is that a boundary along a qualifying stream is not a matter of the seller's opinion, and a question near a stream of that size belongs to a licensed surveyor.

**The government as landowner.** Government does not merely regulate; it owns, and when it owns the regulatory picture inverts. Local Government Code § 211.013(c) provides that the municipal zoning subchapter "does not apply to a building, other structure, or land under the control, administration, or jurisdiction of a state or federal agency," while § 211.013(d) applies the subchapter to privately owned land and buildings "when leased to a state agency" — ownership, not occupancy, drives the exemption.

**Publicly financed improvements attached to private lots.** A buyer can acquire a private lot and, with it, an obligation to help pay for public improvements. Property Code § 5.014(a) requires a person who proposes to sell or convey real property located in a **public improvement district** to give the purchaser a prescribed written notice first. The notice text in § 5.014(a-1) tells the purchaser, in capital letters, that "AN ASSESSMENT HAS BEEN LEVIED AGAINST YOUR PROPERTY FOR THE AUTHORIZED IMPROVEMENTS, WHICH MAY BE PAID IN FULL AT ANY TIME," that unpaid balances come due in annual installments varying year to year, and that failure to pay "may result in penalties and interest being added to what you owe or in a lien on and the foreclosure of your property." A license holder working a subdivision built with district financing should expect this notice and never treat it as boilerplate.

**Escheat.** Where an owner dies leaving no person entitled to take the property and no valid disposition of it, the doctrine of **escheat** directs the property to the state. That is the general doctrine; no source read for this course sets out the Texas escheat procedure, waiting periods, or filing requirements, and this module therefore states no mechanics and cites nothing for them. A license holder who encounters a property with no locatable owner or heir should treat it as a title matter for the title company and counsel.

### Applied exercise: The offered park and the widened road

A developer records a plat for a 40-lot subdivision inside a Texas municipality. The plat shows a 2-acre tract labeled "Park — dedicated to public use" and a 60-foot strip along the north boundary labeled "future street." The city approves the plat. Three years later the city has never entered, used, or improved either area; the developer has mowed the park tract and the strip is vacant. The city now proposes to acquire an additional 15-foot strip of frontage from five lots to widen an existing arterial.

1. Under § 212.011(a), does approval of the plat make the city owner of the 2-acre park tract, or obligate it to maintain that tract? What event would change the answer?
2. The developer says the city forced the "future street" dedication onto the plat although the developer never wanted it and the street appears in no adopted capital improvement plan. What does § 212.010(c) say about that?
3. For the 15-foot frontage strip the city now wants, which mechanism from this unit applies — dedication or condemnation — and what does Water Code § 11.035(b) tell you about the owners' entitlement in a condemnation generally?
4. One of the five lots also fronts a stream averaging 45 feet in width. Under Natural Resources Code §§ 21.001(3) and 21.012(b), what should you tell the owner about how that boundary was originally laid out, and to whom should you refer the question?

## Unit: Environmental Protection Legislation

Environmental regulation reaches real estate three ways: it can forbid a use, it can define the parties exposed to cleanup costs in terms of who owns or operates rather than who caused the problem, and it can create a disclosure obligation that sits squarely in the license holder's path. A Texas license holder is not an environmental professional and must not act like one. What the license holder owes is accurate disclosure of what is known, an honest statement of what is not known, and a referral to someone qualified.

**Which federal sections were read, and which were not.** Four federal statutory sections were held and read for this course, and this unit teaches them from their own text: **42 U.S.C. § 4852d**, the disclosure section of the Residential Lead-Based Paint Hazard Reduction Act; **42 U.S.C. § 9601**, the definitions section of CERCLA; **42 U.S.C. § 4321**, the congressional declaration of purpose of the National Environmental Policy Act; and **16 U.S.C. § 1531**, the congressional findings, purposes, and policy of the Endangered Species Act. Every federal proposition stated below with a section number comes from one of those four sections. **What was not held is still not stated here: CERCLA's liability provisions in 42 U.S.C. § 9607, the Clean Water Act's wetlands and dredge-and-fill permitting provisions, the federal asbestos and underground storage tank regimes, the later operative sections of the Endangered Species Act and of the National Environmental Policy Act, and every EPA and HUD regulation promulgated under any of these statutes — including the lead disclosure rule that § 4852d itself directs the agencies to write — were not read for this course. This unit states no threshold, permit trigger, exemption, deadline, or form content from any of them, and those requirements must be verified against the current federal authority before being relied on in a transaction.**

**The federal lead-based paint disclosure: 42 U.S.C. § 4852d.** Of the four, this is the section that binds a residential license holder personally, on ordinary transactions, and it is the one to know cold. Section 4852d(a)(1) directed the Secretary of Housing and Urban Development and the Administrator of the Environmental Protection Agency to promulgate regulations "for the disclosure of lead-based paint hazards in target housing which is offered for sale or lease," and then told them what those regulations must contain. Read that structure honestly: the duties below are stated in the statute as the required content of a rule, and the rule itself was not a held source for this course. What § 4852d(a)(1) says the regulations "shall require" is that, **before the purchaser or lessee is obligated under any contract to purchase or lease the housing**, the seller or lessor shall (A) "provide the purchaser or lessee with a lead hazard information pamphlet, as prescribed by the Administrator of the Environmental Protection Agency under section 406 of the Toxic Substances Control Act"; (B) "disclose to the purchaser or lessee the presence of any known lead-based paint, or any known lead-based paint hazards, in such housing and provide to the purchaser or lessee any lead hazard evaluation report available to the seller or lessor"; and (C) "permit the purchaser a 10-day period (unless the parties mutually agree upon a different period of time) to conduct a risk assessment or inspection for the presence of lead-based paint hazards."

Four precise points sit in that sentence. First, the timing is fixed to a moment, not a date: the pamphlet, the disclosure, and the opportunity all come *before the purchaser or lessee is obligated under any contract* — not before closing, and not at closing. Second, the disclosure is of what is **known**, which is the same posture Texas takes in Property Code § 5.008: the section does not require the seller to test, and does not require a report to be created. Third, the records duty is delivery of what exists — "any lead hazard evaluation report available to the seller or lessor." Fourth, note whom subparagraph (C) names. Subparagraphs (A) and (B) say "purchaser or lessee"; subparagraph (C) gives the 10-day risk-assessment-or-inspection period to "the purchaser." The period is 10 days "unless the parties mutually agree upon a different period of time," so it is a default the parties may lengthen or shorten by agreement, not a fixed waiting period.

The contract itself is regulated. Section 4852d(a)(2) provides that the regulations "shall provide that every contract for the purchase and sale of any interest in target housing shall contain a Lead Warning Statement and a statement signed by the purchaser" that the purchaser has "read the Lead Warning Statement and understands its contents," has "received a lead hazard information pamphlet," and has "had a 10-day opportunity (unless the parties mutually agreed upon a different period of time) before becoming obligated under the contract to purchase the housing to conduct a risk assessment or inspection for the presence of lead-based paint hazards." Section 4852d(a)(3) then prints the Lead Warning Statement and specifies how it travels: the text is to be "printed in large type on a separate sheet of paper attached to the contract." Its opening words are the source of the date everyone quotes — "Every purchaser of any interest in residential real property on which a residential dwelling was built prior to 1978 is notified that such property may present exposure to lead from lead-based paint that may place young children at risk of developing lead poisoning" — and its closing words restate the duty in plain language for the buyer: "The seller of any interest in residential real property is required to provide the buyer with any information on lead-based paint hazards from risk assessments or inspections in the seller's possession and notify the buyer of any known lead-based paint hazards. A risk assessment or inspection for possible lead-based paint hazards is recommended prior to purchase."

A caution about the scope word. Section 4852d uses the defined term **target housing** and does not define it; the definition sits in another section of the same chapter that was not held. The pre-1978 line appears in § 4852d only inside the Lead Warning Statement's own text, quoted above. So state the 1978 date the way the warning statement states it — as the notice given to every purchaser of residential real property on which a dwelling was built prior to 1978 — and do not extend, narrow, or improvise the boundaries of "target housing" from memory. The section likewise does not define "lead-based paint hazard," "risk assessment," or "inspection."

**What § 4852d says about the agent.** This is the part of the section a license holder is most often surprised by, and it is short. Section 4852d(a)(4), headed "Compliance assurance," provides: "Whenever a seller or lessor has entered into a contract with an agent for the purpose of selling or leasing a unit of target housing, the regulations promulgated under this section shall require the agent, on behalf of the seller or lessor, to ensure compliance with the requirements of this section." Read the operative words. Not *inform the seller*. Not *if the seller asks*. **Ensure compliance** — an affirmative duty placed on the agent by the federal statute itself, on behalf of the seller or lessor, and triggered simply by having a listing or leasing engagement on a unit of target housing. Nothing in the section makes the duty conditional on the seller's cooperation, and nothing in it excuses the agent because the seller filled out the form. This is why a residential file for a pre-1978 property includes the signed disclosure, the attached Lead Warning Statement, evidence the pamphlet was delivered, any evaluation report the seller had, and either the 10-day opportunity or the parties' written agreement to a different period.

Consequences follow for a knowing violation. Section 4852d(b)(1) subjects "[a]ny person who knowingly violates any provision of this section" to civil money penalties "in accordance with the provisions of section 3545 of this title" — a cross-reference to a section of title 42 that was not read here, so no penalty amount is stated for it. Section 4852d(b)(2) authorizes the Secretary to act to enjoin a violation. Section 4852d(b)(3) is the one that reaches a private plaintiff: a person who knowingly violates the section "shall be jointly and severally liable to the purchaser or lessee in an amount equal to 3 times the amount of damages incurred by such individual," and under § 4852d(b)(4) a prevailing party in such an action may be awarded court costs, reasonable attorney fees, and expert witness fees. Section 4852d(b)(5) makes failure to comply a prohibited act under section 409 of the Toxic Substances Control Act and states that, for enforcement under that Act, "the penalty for each violation applicable under section 16 of that Act shall not be more than $10,000." Finally, § 4852d(c) draws a line that prevents a common misunderstanding: nothing in the section "shall affect the validity or enforceability of any sale or contract for the purchase and sale or lease of any interest in residential real property or any loan, loan agreement, mortgage, or lien made or arising in connection with a mortgage loan, nor shall anything in this section create a defect in title." A lead-paint failure is a penalty-and-damages problem, not a title problem, and telling a client the contract is void because the addendum was missed would be wrong.

**CERCLA vocabulary: 42 U.S.C. § 9601 — definitions, and only definitions.** Section 9601 opens "For purpose of this subchapter" and then defines terms. It is important to be exact about what that does and does not give us. **The CERCLA liability provisions — who is liable, to whom, for what, and with what defenses — are in 42 U.S.C. § 9607, which was not held and was not read. This course states no CERCLA liability rule, no defense, and no allocation standard.** What § 9601 does supply is the vocabulary those provisions run on, and the vocabulary alone explains why a buyer's lawyer worries.

Start with **"owner or operator."** Section 9601(20)(A) provides that the term means, "in the case of an onshore facility or an offshore facility, any person owning or operating such facility," with a parallel rule for vessels, and clause (iii) reaching back to "any person who owned, operated, or otherwise controlled activities at such facility immediately beforehand" where title or control was conveyed to a unit of State or local government "due to bankruptcy, foreclosure, tax delinquency, abandonment, or similar means." Notice what is absent: the definition says nothing about causing, contributing to, or knowing about contamination. It is a status defined by ownership or operation. The same paragraph carves out the lender: the term "does not include a person, who, without participating in the management of a vessel or facility, holds indicia of ownership primarily to protect his security interest in the vessel or facility" — the secured-creditor exclusion, elaborated in § 9601(20)(F) for lenders that are not participants in management. Subparagraph (D) excludes a unit of State or local government that acquired ownership or control through law-enforcement seizure, bankruptcy, tax delinquency, abandonment, or other circumstances in which government takes title "by virtue of its function as sovereign," except where it caused or contributed to the release.

Then **"facility."** Section 9601(9) defines it as "(A) any building, structure, installation, equipment, pipe or pipeline (including any pipe into a sewer or publicly owned treatment works), well, pit, pond, lagoon, impoundment, ditch, landfill, storage container, motor vehicle, rolling stock, or aircraft, or (B) any site or area where a hazardous substance has been deposited, stored, disposed of, or placed, or otherwise come to be located," excluding a consumer product in consumer use and any vessel. Subparagraph (B) is the one to sit with: an ordinary tract of Texas dirt becomes a "facility" because something came to be located on it — including, by the words used, coming to be located there without anyone putting it there on purpose.

Then **"release."** Section 9601(22) defines it as "any spilling, leaking, pumping, pouring, emitting, emptying, discharging, injecting, escaping, leaching, dumping, or disposing into the environment (including the abandonment or discarding of barrels, containers, and other closed receptacles containing any hazardous substance or pollutant or contaminant)," excluding four things: a release resulting in exposure to persons solely within a workplace, as to a claim those persons may assert against their employer; engine exhaust emissions from a motor vehicle, rolling stock, aircraft, vessel, or pipeline pumping station engine; certain releases of source, byproduct, or special nuclear material governed by the Atomic Energy Act; and "the normal application of fertilizer." An abandoned drum in a fence line is inside the definition by its own words.

Then **"hazardous substance."** Section 9601(14) does not list chemicals; it incorporates six other federal lists — substances designated under section 311(b)(2)(A) of the Federal Water Pollution Control Act, substances designated under 42 U.S.C. § 9602, hazardous wastes identified or listed under section 3001 of the Solid Waste Disposal Act, toxic pollutants listed under section 307(a) of the Federal Water Pollution Control Act, hazardous air pollutants listed under section 112 of the Clean Air Act, and imminently hazardous chemical substances or mixtures under section 7 of the Toxic Substances Control Act. Those six lists were not read for this course and no substance is named from them here. But the paragraph's closing exclusion is directly useful in Texas: the term "does not include petroleum, including crude oil or any fraction thereof which is not otherwise specifically listed or designated as a hazardous substance under subparagraphs (A) through (F) of this paragraph, and the term does not include natural gas, natural gas liquids, liquefied natural gas, or synthetic gas usable for fuel." That exclusion is narrower than it sounds — it turns on whether the material is "otherwise specifically listed or designated" — so it is not a license to tell a buyer that an old fuel tank or an oilfield tract raises no federal issue. It is a reason to route the question to an environmental professional and to counsel. Note also that the Texas notice in Property Code § 5.013 uses the phrase "hazardous substance" for a different purpose and takes its meaning from Health and Safety Code § 361.003, not from § 9601(14); the phrases are not interchangeable.

Finally, § 9601 gives the diligence vocabulary. Section 9601(35)(A) defines "contractual relationship" **"for the purpose of section 9607(b)(3) of this title"** — a section not held — and in doing so sets out what a defendant who acquired property after the disposal must establish, including that "[a]t the time the defendant acquired the facility the defendant did not know and had no reason to know that any hazardous substance which is the subject of the release or threatened release was disposed of on, in, or at the facility." Section 9601(35)(B)(i) explains "reason to know": the person must show that "on or before the date on which the defendant acquired the facility" it "carried out all appropriate inquiries... into the previous ownership and uses of the facility in accordance with generally accepted good commercial and customary standards and practices," and took reasonable steps to stop any continuing release, prevent any threatened future release, and prevent or limit exposure to any previously released hazardous substance. Section 9601(35)(B)(iii) then lists the criteria the Administrator must include in the standards, and the list reads like a due-diligence checklist written by Congress: the results of an inquiry by an environmental professional; interviews with past and present owners, operators, and occupants; reviews of historical sources "such as chain of title documents, aerial photographs, building department records, and land use records"; searches for recorded environmental cleanup liens; reviews of federal, state, and local government records, waste disposal records, underground storage tank records, and spill records; "[v]isual inspections of the facility and of adjoining properties"; the person's specialized knowledge or experience; "[t]he relationship of the purchase price to the value of the property, if the property was not contaminated"; commonly known or reasonably ascertainable information; and the obviousness of contamination. Section 9601(35)(B)(iv)(II) names the interim standard for property purchased on or after May 31, 1997 and until the Administrator's regulations issue: the ASTM procedures, "including the document known as 'Standard E1527–97', entitled 'Standard Practice for Environmental Site Assessment: Phase 1 Environmental Site Assessment Process.'" And § 9601(35)(B)(v) is the residential rule: for property in residential or similar use purchased by a nongovernmental or noncommercial entity, "a facility inspection and title search that reveal no basis for further investigation" satisfies the subparagraph.

That is why commercial and industrial purchasers commission environmental site assessments before closing. It is still true that **no source read for this course requires an environmental site assessment in any transaction** — the practice is driven by a defense located in § 9607, which was not read — but the practice is no longer merely custom in this course's telling: § 9601(35)(B) is where the phrase "all appropriate inquiries" and the Phase I standard come from, and § 9601(40)(B) repeats the same criteria for a **bona fide prospective purchaser**, defined in § 9601(40)(A) as a person who acquires ownership of a facility after January 11, 2002 (or, on the terms stated there, a leasehold interest after that date) and establishes by a preponderance of the evidence each of eight criteria — that all disposal occurred before acquisition, that all appropriate inquiries were made, that all legally required notices are given, that appropriate care is exercised by taking reasonable steps to stop continuing releases and limit exposure, that full cooperation and access are provided, that land use restrictions and institutional controls are respected, that information requests and subpoenas are answered, and that the person is not affiliated with a potentially responsible party. Section 9601(39)(A) supplies the term a redevelopment client will use: a **brownfield site** is "real property, the expansion, redevelopment, or reuse of which may be complicated by the presence or potential presence of a hazardous substance, pollutant, or contaminant," subject to the exclusions in (39)(B). Two honest limits close this out. Section 9601(32) says the terms "liable" and "liability" are "construed to be the standard of liability which obtains under section 311 of the Federal Water Pollution Control Act" — a cross-reference to a statute not held, so this course does not state what that standard is. And § 9601 contains **no** definition of "contiguous property"; that vocabulary lives elsewhere in the chapter, was not read, and is not stated here.

**NEPA: 42 U.S.C. § 4321.** The National Environmental Policy Act's declaration of purpose is a single sentence, and quoting it in full is the whole of what this course can say: "The purposes of this chapter are: To declare a national policy which will encourage productive and enjoyable harmony between man and his environment; to promote efforts which will prevent or eliminate damage to the environment and biosphere and stimulate the health and welfare of man; to enrich the understanding of the ecological systems and natural resources important to the Nation; and to establish a Council on Environmental Quality." That is a statement of national policy and the creation of a federal council. It imposes no duty on a Texas seller, no permit requirement on a private lot, and no disclosure obligation on a license holder. The environmental-review machinery associated with NEPA sits in later sections of chapter 55 that were not read, so this course states nothing about when a review or an impact statement is required, what it must contain, or how long it takes. The practical point is a referral point and is practice, not law: where a client's project depends on a federal permit, a federal approval, or federal money, federal environmental review may become part of that federal process, and the timing question belongs to counsel and to the agency, not to the license holder's memory.

**Endangered species: 16 U.S.C. § 1531.** Section 1531 is likewise a findings-and-purposes section, and it is directed at government. Section 1531(a) records the findings — that species have been rendered extinct "as a consequence of economic growth and development untempered by adequate concern and conservation," that others are depleted to the point of danger, that these species are "of esthetic, ecological, educational, historical, recreational, and scientific value to the Nation and its people," that the United States has pledged itself under named treaties and conventions, and that encouraging States and other interested parties through federal financial assistance and incentives is "a key to meeting the Nation's international commitments." Section 1531(b) states the purposes: "to provide a means whereby the ecosystems upon which endangered species and threatened species depend may be conserved, to provide a program for the conservation of such endangered species and threatened species, and to take such steps as may be appropriate to achieve the purposes of the treaties and conventions" listed in subsection (a). Section 1531(c)(1) declares it "the policy of Congress that all Federal departments and agencies shall seek to conserve endangered species and threatened species and shall utilize their authorities in furtherance of the purposes of this chapter," and § 1531(c)(2) adds the policy "that Federal agencies shall cooperate with State and local agencies to resolve water resource issues in concert with conservation of endangered species." Read carefully, this section commands *federal agencies*; the provisions that reach private conduct — listing, critical habitat, the prohibitions on taking, and the permit and conservation-plan mechanisms — are in later sections of the chapter that were not held. So a license holder can accurately say that federal law declares a policy of conserving endangered and threatened species and the ecosystems they depend on, and that a species question on a specific tract is one for counsel and a qualified biologist. A license holder cannot say from this course what is prohibited on a particular parcel, what a permit costs, or how long one takes.

**The conditions with no held federal source.** Several environmental conditions a Texas license holder meets constantly have no held federal source in this course, and are described here factually rather than legally, with no section cited and nothing asserted about permits, thresholds, or deadlines. **Wetlands** regulation restricts discharges of dredged or fill material into certain waters, and land that functions as wetland may be regulated even where it looks buildable and is dry much of the year; the Clean Water Act provisions and their implementing rules were not read and must be verified. **Asbestos** is a fibrous mineral used historically in insulation and floor, ceiling, and roofing materials; disturbing it is the hazard, and its presence is an inspection-and-abatement question for qualified professionals. **Radon** is a naturally occurring radioactive soil gas identified only by testing. **Underground storage tanks** create both a leak risk and a closure obligation and are often invisible from the surface; the federal tank rules were not read. Three of the four reach the license holder anyway through Texas law rather than federal law: "Asbestos Components" and "Radon Gas" are named items on the Texas seller's disclosure notice, and a buried tank will usually be reached, if at all, through that notice's "Subsurface Structure or Pits" and "Hazardous or Toxic Waste" items. That is the pattern to hold onto — for most environmental conditions in a Texas residential file, the operative instrument is the state disclosure notice, not a federal rule.

**Texas law: what the seller's disclosure notice actually puts in issue.** Texas does not leave environmental condition to the general law of misrepresentation. Property Code § 5.008(a) requires a seller of residential real property comprising not more than one dwelling unit to give the purchaser a written notice as prescribed by the section, or a substantially similar notice containing at a minimum all of the section's items. The statutory form in § 5.008(b) asks the seller directly whether the seller is aware of a long list of conditions, and the environmental content is unmistakable. Item 4 asks about "Hazardous or Toxic Waste," "Asbestos Components," "Urea formaldehyde Insulation," "Radon Gas," "Lead Based Paint," "Improper Drainage," "Water Damage Not Due to a Flood Event," "Landfill, Settling, Soil Movement, Fault Lines," "Subsurface Structure or Pits," and "Previous Use of Premises for Manufacture of Methamphetamine."

Item 6 asks whether the seller is aware of present flood insurance coverage, previous flooding due to a reservoir failure, breach, or controlled or emergency release, and previous water penetration into a structure from a natural flood event, and whether the property lies wholly or partly in a 100-year floodplain, a 500-year floodplain, a floodway, a flood pool, or a reservoir. The notice defines those terms itself: a "100-year floodplain" is an area identified on the flood insurance rate map as a special flood hazard area with "a one percent annual chance of flooding," and a "500-year floodplain" is a moderate hazard area with "a two-tenths of one percent annual chance of flooding." Items 7 and 8 ask whether the seller has ever filed a flood damage claim, including with the National Flood Insurance Program, and whether the seller has received FEMA or Small Business Administration assistance for flood damage. Item 9 reaches related ground, including "[a]ny notices of violations of deed restrictions or governmental ordinances affecting the condition or use of the Property," "[a]ny condition on the Property which materially affects the physical health or safety of an individual," and whether any portion of the property lies in a groundwater conservation district or a subsidence district. Item 10 tells a buyer of coastal property seaward of the Gulf Intracoastal Waterway or within 1,000 feet of the mean high tide bordering the Gulf of Mexico that the property "may be subject to the Open Beaches Act or the Dune Protection Act (Chapter 61 or 63, Natural Resources Code, respectively) and a beachfront construction certificate or dune protection permit may be required for repairs or improvements."

Two limits belong with the form. Section 5.008(d) provides that the notice "shall be completed to the best of seller's belief and knowledge as of the date the notice is completed and signed by the seller," and that if information is unknown the seller "shall indicate that fact on the notice, and by that act is in compliance with this section." Section 5.008(e) exempts listed transfers, including transfers pursuant to a court order or foreclosure sale, by a trustee in bankruptcy, by a fiduciary administering an estate or trust, between co-owners, and to a spouse or lineal relative. The notice's own heading states that it "IS NOT A SUBSTITUTE FOR ANY INSPECTIONS OR WARRANTIES THE PURCHASER MAY WISH TO OBTAIN." A common error is to treat a completed disclosure notice as a clean bill of health. It is a statement of the seller's knowledge, nothing more.

**How the federal lead duty and the Texas notice fit together.** On a pre-1978 Texas house both instruments are live at once, and neither one does the other's work. Property Code § 5.008 is a Texas statute; it reaches a **sale** of residential real property comprising not more than one dwelling unit; it asks whether the seller is *aware* of "Lead Based Paint" as one item among many in item 4; it is satisfied by the seller's honest "unknown" under § 5.008(d); and it does not apply at all to the transfers listed in § 5.008(e). Section 4852d reaches target housing offered for **sale or lease**; it requires the lead hazard information pamphlet, the disclosure of known lead-based paint and known lead-based paint hazards, delivery of any lead hazard evaluation report available to the seller or lessor, the purchaser's 10-day risk-assessment or inspection opportunity unless the parties mutually agree otherwise, and the Lead Warning Statement with the purchaser's signed acknowledgement, attached to the contract on a separate sheet. Three consequences. A "no" or "unknown" written on item 4 of the Texas notice does not satisfy § 4852d: the § 5.008 notice is not the pamphlet, is not the Lead Warning Statement, and carries no inspection period. A completed federal lead addendum does not satisfy § 5.008 either; it says nothing about drainage, fault lines, floodplain, or the rest of the Texas form. And the § 5.008(e) exemptions are exemptions from the **Texas** notice only — a transfer exempt from § 5.008 tells you nothing about whether § 4852d applies, and a lease is outside § 5.008 while target housing offered for lease is squarely inside § 4852d(a)(1). The two documents also allocate responsibility differently: § 5.008 places the notice duty on the seller, while § 4852d(a)(4) places on the seller's or lessor's **agent** the duty "to ensure compliance with the requirements of this section."

**Other Texas disclosures with environmental content.** Property Code § 5.013(a) requires a seller of unimproved real property to be used for residential purposes to give the purchaser written notice disclosing "the location of a transportation pipeline, including a pipeline for the transportation of natural gas, natural gas liquids, synthetic gas, liquefied petroleum gas, petroleum or a petroleum product, or a hazardous substance," with "hazardous substance" taking the meaning assigned by Health and Safety Code § 361.003 as § 5.013(e) provides. Under § 5.013(c), if the notice is not delivered on or before the effective date of a binding executory contract, "the purchaser may terminate the contract for any reason not later than the seventh day after the effective date of the contract." Property Code § 5.019 requires a **notice of water level fluctuations** for property adjoining an impoundment constructed and maintained under Chapter 11, Water Code, with a storage capacity of at least 5,000 acre-feet at normal operating level; § 5.019(e) preserves a misrepresentation action after conveyance where the seller failed to give the notice and had actual knowledge of the fluctuation.

Water Code § 11.086(a) is the Texas surface-water rule that most often becomes a neighbor dispute: "No person may divert or impound the natural flow of surface waters in this state, or permit a diversion or impounding by him to continue, in a manner that damages the property of another by the overflow of the water diverted or impounded." Section 11.086(b) gives the injured owner remedies at law and in equity and recovery of damages. A grading plan that solves one lot's drainage by moving water onto the lot next door is not merely a nuisance complaint waiting to happen; it is a statutory violation with a damages remedy. Texas has also placed an environmental tool inside the zoning power itself: Local Government Code § 211.003(a)(6) authorizes a municipality to regulate "the pumping, extraction, and use of groundwater by persons other than retail public utilities... for the purpose of preventing the use or contact with groundwater that presents an actual or potential threat to human health," and § 212.003(a) allows that kind of groundwater ordinance to be extended into the extraterritorial jurisdiction. Where such an ordinance covers a contaminated groundwater area, a buyer's plan to drill a well may be barred by the city even outside the city limits.

**The license holder's duty.** The obligation is specific and enforceable. Occupations Code § 1101.652(b)(3) makes it a ground for suspension or revocation if a license holder, while engaged in real estate brokerage, "makes a material misrepresentation to a potential buyer concerning a significant defect, including a latent structural defect, known to the license holder that would be a significant factor to a reasonable and prudent buyer in making a decision to purchase real property," and § 1101.652(b)(4) reaches the license holder who "fails to disclose to a potential buyer a defect described by Subdivision (3) that is known to the license holder." Section 1101.652(b)(1) separately reaches a license holder who "acts negligently or incompetently." Occupations Code § 1101.805(e) sets the boundary from the other side: a license holder "is not liable for a misrepresentation or a concealment of a material fact made by a party to a real estate transaction unless the license holder: (1) knew of the falsity of the misrepresentation or concealment; and (2) failed to disclose the license holder's knowledge of the falsity." You are not the guarantor of the seller's statements; you are responsible for what you know.

As a matter of practice and not of any statute read for this course, that translates into a short discipline. Disclose in writing what you know about the property's condition. Never characterize a stain, an odor, a slab crack, a fill area, an old tank, or a drainage pattern as harmless — you are not qualified to make that call, and a confident wrong answer is exactly the material misrepresentation § 1101.652(b)(3) describes. Refer the client to the right professional: an inspector, an environmental consultant, a licensed surveyor for boundary and stream questions, an engineer for drainage, and counsel for permitting and liability. Document the referral. On any pre-1978 residential listing or lease, work the § 4852d file as your own responsibility rather than the seller's, because § 4852d(a)(4) says it is: pamphlet delivered, known paint and hazards disclosed, available evaluation reports handed over, the 10-day opportunity given or a different period agreed, and the Lead Warning Statement signed and attached. And when the question is federal, separate the part you can state from a section you have actually read — § 4852d's disclosure requirements, § 9601's definitions, the declared purposes in § 4321 and § 1531 — from the far larger part you cannot: CERCLA's liability sections, the wetlands program, the asbestos and underground storage tank regimes, and every implementing regulation, including the lead rule itself. Those must be confirmed against the current federal authority rather than answered from memory.

### Module recap

This module established the architecture of land-use control in Texas and then filled it in with the statutes that actually operate.

The first unit showed that control comes from two independent systems. The public system rests on the police power stated in Local Government Code § 211.001 and is exercised through municipal zoning: use districts and dimensional standards under § 211.003, adopted in accordance with a comprehensive plan under § 211.004, through a zoning commission and noticed public hearings under §§ 211.006 and 211.007, with relief available from a board of adjustment on the variance and special-exception standards of § 211.009 and continuation rights for nonconforming uses under § 211.019. It also showed the Texas-specific structure: chapter 211 gives zoning power to municipalities, in permissive terms, subject to repeal by the voters under § 211.015 — so a Texas city may have no conventional zoning at all, and unincorporated county land is not zoned under this chapter. Even without zoning, subdivision and platting regulation under chapter 212 governs how land becomes lots, and § 212.003 sharply limits what a municipality may regulate in its extraterritorial jurisdiction. The private system runs on recorded restrictive covenants construed liberally under Property Code § 202.003, enforced by property owners' associations whose discretionary decisions are presumed reasonable under § 202.004, with instruments that have no effect until filed in the county real property records under § 202.006 — and private restrictions are frequently stricter than any ordinance.

The second unit drew the line between regulating land and acquiring it: dedication offered on a plat and accepted only by entry, use, or improvement under § 212.011; condemnation as the procedure for exercising a delegated eminent domain power, with damages assessed and paid under Water Code § 11.035(b); state ownership of water under Water Code § 11.021 held in trust for the public under § 11.0235; and the survey rules for navigable streams in Natural Resources Code §§ 21.001(3) and 21.012.

The third unit taught four federal sections from their own text and named the far larger federal territory it cannot teach. From 42 U.S.C. § 4852d: the pre-obligation delivery of the lead hazard information pamphlet, disclosure of known lead-based paint and known lead-based paint hazards, delivery of any lead hazard evaluation report available to the seller or lessor, the purchaser's 10-day risk-assessment or inspection period unless the parties mutually agree otherwise, the Lead Warning Statement attached to the contract on a separate sheet with the purchaser's signed acknowledgement, the treble-damages exposure for a knowing violation, the rule that a violation creates no defect in title — and, above all, § 4852d(a)(4), which requires the seller's or lessor's agent "to ensure compliance with the requirements of this section." From 42 U.S.C. § 9601: the definitions of "owner or operator," "facility," "release," and "hazardous substance," the secured-creditor exclusion, the petroleum and natural gas exclusion, and the "all appropriate inquiries" and bona fide prospective purchaser vocabulary — definitions only, since CERCLA's liability provisions in § 9607 were not read. From 42 U.S.C. § 4321 and 16 U.S.C. § 1531: declared national policy and purposes, directed at federal agencies, imposing no permit or disclosure duty on a Texas seller. Everything else federal — § 9607, wetlands, asbestos, underground storage tanks, and every EPA and HUD regulation — remains unstated here and must be verified against current federal authority. The unit then grounded the Texas duty in real text: the environmental items in the § 5.008 seller's disclosure notice and how they interlock with the federal lead duty, the pipeline and water-level notices in §§ 5.013 and 5.019, the surface-water rule of Water Code § 11.086, and the disclosure duty enforced through Occupations Code § 1101.652(b)(3) and (4).

You should now be able to open a Texas transaction and ask the right four questions in the right order: Is this land inside a municipality, in an ETJ, or unincorporated? Does that municipality zone, and what does the district permit? What is recorded against this lot in the county real property records? And what does the seller know, or refuse to say, about the condition of the ground?
