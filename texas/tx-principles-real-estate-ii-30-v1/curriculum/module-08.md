# Texas Principles of Real Estate II — Module 08

## TREC Topic: Fair Housing Laws

**Prescribed time: 170 minutes.** This module carries the five units the Commission's PRINS2-1 course
approval form places under *Fair Housing Laws*, whose scope note is *"Federal, state & local fair
housing laws."*

**Learning objectives.** After completing this module you will be able to: state the protected
classes listed in each subsection of 42 U.S.C. § 3604 and distinguish a reasonable accommodation from
a reasonable modification under § 3604(f)(3); identify the protected classes, prohibited practices,
exemptions and enforcement path of the Texas Fair Housing Act, Property Code ch. 301, and name the
agency that administers it; define steering, blockbusting and discriminatory advertising and identify
the statutory provision each violates, including the licence-discipline ground in Occupations Code
§ 1101.652(b)(32); state the prohibited bases and permitted inquiries under the Equal Credit
Opportunity Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1691(a) and (b), and explain the adverse-action notification a creditor
owes an applicant under § 1691(d); and explain the congressional findings and purpose of the
Community Reinvestment Act, 12 U.S.C. § 2901, and distinguish that affirmative community-credit
obligation from an individual anti-discrimination claim.

**Every legal statement in this module comes from a source that was read for this course.** Where a
statement is practice rather than law, the module says so and cites nothing for it. Four federal
provisions this topic ordinarily touches were **not** among the sources held for this course: the
Fair Housing Act's definitions section (42 U.S.C. § 3602), its exemptions (§ 3603(b) and § 3607), its
residential real estate-related transactions section (§ 3605), and its brokerage-services section
(§ 3606); likewise the Community Reinvestment Act's examination and rating sections (12 U.S.C.
§§ 2902–2906) and the Equal Credit Opportunity Act's definitions and remedies sections (15 U.S.C.
§§ 1691a–1691f). Where the module reaches those subjects it says so plainly, teaches the parallel
Texas provision that *was* read, and tells you to read the federal text yourself rather than
paraphrasing text nobody opened. Every name, address and dollar figure in the illustrations is
invented to make a rule visible; none is a required or standard figure.

## Unit: Equal Opportunity in Housing

Federal fair housing law begins with a single sentence. 42 U.S.C. § 3601 says: "It is the policy of
the United States to provide, within constitutional limitations, for fair housing throughout the
United States." That is the whole section. It states an objective and nothing else — no prohibition,
no penalty, no protected class. Everything operative comes later, and a license holder who wants to
know what is actually forbidden has to read § 3604.

Still, the sentence is worth pausing over, because it tells you what kind of statute this is. It does
not say the government will build housing or set prices. It says the *policy* is fair housing
"throughout the United States," and the phrase "within constitutional limitations" is an
acknowledgment that Congress was legislating at the edge of its power over private conduct. The Fair
Housing Act reaches what private owners, landlords, brokers and lenders do with housing, and it does
so nationally. A rural Texas landlord with four units and a Houston property-management company with
four thousand are answering to the same federal statute.

**Where the Act came from, and when each protected class arrived.** The dates matter because they
explain why the statute's own lists are ragged. The source credit under § 3601 is Pub. L. 90–284,
title VIII, § 801, Apr. 11, 1968. The statutory note under § 3601 records that Pub. L. 90–284 "may be
cited as the 'Civil Rights Act of 1968,'" and that its title VIII "may be cited as the 'Fair Housing
Act.'" So the Act is title VIII of the 1968 Civil Rights Act, passed in April 1968, and you will still
hear practitioners call fair housing complaints "title VIII" matters.

As enacted, § 3604 did not protect sex. The amendments note under § 3604 records that in 1974,
Pub. L. 93–383 "inserted ', sex' after 'religion' wherever appearing in cls. (a) to (e)." Fourteen
years later came the change that produced the statute we read today: the Fair Housing Amendments Act
of 1988, Pub. L. 100–430. The amendments note under § 3604 is precise about what it did. In
subsections (a) and (b), § 6(b)(2) "inserted 'familial status,' after 'sex,'". In subsections (c)
through (e), § 6(b)(1) "inserted 'handicap, familial status,' after 'sex,'". And § 6(a) "added
subsec. (f)" — the entire disability regime. The note under § 3601 states that the 1988 Act took
effect "on the 180th day beginning after" September 13, 1988. One further amendment is recorded in
the § 3601 notes: Pub. L. 104–76 (1995) "may be cited as the 'Housing for Older Persons Act of
1995'" and amended § 3607.

Read those notes together and the shape of the statute becomes legible. **Race, color, religion and
national origin** are original 1968 classes. **Sex** was added in 1974. **Familial status** and
**handicap** were added in 1988 — and because Congress inserted them by amendment rather than
rewriting the section, they landed in different subsections in different ways. That is why the lists
in § 3604 are not identical from one subsection to the next, and why a student who memorizes "the
seven protected classes" as a single list will misread the statute.

**The statute's protected classes, exactly as § 3604 lists them.** The seven bases the section names
somewhere in its text are race, color, religion, sex, familial status, national origin, and handicap.
The statute uses the word **handicap**, not "disability." Texas law, as the next unit shows, uses
"disability." When you are reading or citing the federal Act, use its word.

**The opening clause is a limit, and you must read it first.** Section 3604 begins: "As made
applicable by section 3603 of this title and except as exempted by sections 3603(b) and 3607 of this
title, it shall be unlawful—". Two things follow. First, the reach of § 3604 is set by § 3603, not by
§ 3604 itself. Second, two exemption provisions sit outside the section. The texts of § 3603 and
§ 3607 were not among the sources held for this course, so this module does not tell you what they
say; it tells you they exist, that § 3604's own opening words make them controlling, and that you
should read them in the United States Code before you rely on an exemption. Texas's parallel
exemptions *were* read, and they are taught in the next unit and previewed below.

**§ 3604(a) — refusal to deal.** It is unlawful "[t]o refuse to sell or rent after the making of a
bona fide offer, or to refuse to negotiate for the sale or rental of, or otherwise make unavailable
or deny, a dwelling to any person because of race, color, religion, sex, familial status, or national
origin." Note three separate wrongs: refusing after a bona fide offer, refusing to negotiate at all,
and the catch-all "otherwise make unavailable or deny." That catch-all is the workhorse of the
section — it is where steering, discriminatory tenant-screening criteria, and quiet
never-return-the-call practices live. *Illustration:* a Fort Worth landlord takes an application from
a family, reviews it, and tells them the unit is spoken for; the same afternoon the landlord accepts
a materially identical application from a different family. Nothing was said out loud about a
protected class. If the reason was a protected class, "otherwise make unavailable or deny" reaches it
anyway.

**§ 3604(b) — terms, conditions, privileges, services and facilities.** It is unlawful "[t]o
discriminate against any person in the terms, conditions, or privileges of sale or rental of a
dwelling, or in the provision of services or facilities in connection therewith," on the same six
bases listed in (a). Subsection (a) is about getting in the door; (b) is about what happens after.
*Illustration:* an El Paso apartment community rents to everyone who applies but charges one group a
larger security deposit, or restricts one group's pool hours, or takes three weeks to answer their
maintenance tickets and three days to answer everyone else's. The lease was signed; the violation is
in the terms and in the services.

**§ 3604(c) — advertising and statements.** It is unlawful "[t]o make, print, or publish, or cause to
be made, printed, or published any notice, statement, or advertisement, with respect to the sale or
rental of a dwelling that indicates any preference, limitation, or discrimination based on race,
color, religion, sex, handicap, familial status, or national origin, or an intention to make any such
preference, limitation, or discrimination." Here the list is seven bases: handicap is present. Read
the verbs: *make*, *print*, *publish*, or *cause to be* made, printed or published. A license holder
who drafts the copy, and the publisher who runs it, are both inside the section. Read the object
too — "any notice, statement, or advertisement." A spoken statement at a showing counts. And read the
test: the notice must *indicate* a preference, limitation or discrimination, or an intention to make
one. There is no requirement that anyone was actually turned away. *Illustration:* a listing
description for a San Antonio duplex reading "quiet building, perfect for a mature professional
couple, not suited to children" indicates a limitation based on familial status on the face of the
words, whether or not a single family with children ever inquired.

**§ 3604(d) — false representation that a dwelling is unavailable.** It is unlawful "[t]o represent
to any person because of race, color, religion, sex, handicap, familial status, or national origin
that any dwelling is not available for inspection, sale, or rental when such dwelling is in fact so
available." This is the "sorry, it's already leased" lie, and it is stated as its own violation
precisely because it is the most common way the refusal in (a) is delivered. *Illustration:* a
Lubbock leasing agent tells one caller the two-bedroom is gone and tells the next caller, an hour
later, to come see it.

**§ 3604(e) — blockbusting.** It is unlawful "[f]or profit, to induce or attempt to induce any person
to sell or rent any dwelling by representations regarding the entry or prospective entry into the
neighborhood of a person or persons of a particular race, color, religion, sex, handicap, familial
status, or national origin." Three elements do the work. **For profit** — the inducement must be
commercially motivated, which is nearly automatic for a license holder soliciting listings.
**Induce or attempt to induce** — the attempt is enough; no one has to list. And **representations
regarding entry or prospective entry** — the statement is about who is moving in, and "prospective"
means a prediction counts. Unit 3 works this practice in detail.

**§ 3604(f) — handicap.** Subsection (f) is the longest part of the section and the part license
holders most often get wrong. Paragraph (f)(1) makes it unlawful "[t]o discriminate in the sale or
rental, or to otherwise make unavailable or deny, a dwelling to any buyer or renter because of a
handicap of— (A) that buyer or renter, (B) a person residing in or intending to reside in that
dwelling after it is so sold, rented, or made available; or (C) any person associated with that buyer
or renter." Paragraph (f)(2) does the same for "the terms, conditions, or privileges of sale or
rental" and "the provision of services or facilities," with the same three-part reach. So (f)(1) and
(f)(2) are the disability analogues of (a) and (b) — which is exactly why handicap is missing from
the lists in (a) and (b) themselves. Notice how far the protection extends: it covers the buyer or
renter, anyone who will live there, and **any person associated with** them. A Plano seller who
refuses an offer because the buyer's adult son who will live in the home uses a wheelchair, or
because the buyer's caregiver has a disability, is inside (f)(1) even though the applicant is not the
person with the disability.

Paragraph (f)(3) is the definitional heart: "For purposes of this subsection, discrimination
includes—" and then three things. Two of them are the pair students confuse constantly, so learn them
as a contrast rather than as two definitions.

A **reasonable modification** is subparagraph (A): "a refusal to permit, at the expense of the
handicapped person, reasonable modifications of existing premises occupied or to be occupied by such
person if such modifications may be necessary to afford such person full enjoyment of the premises
except that, in the case of a rental, the landlord may where it is reasonable to do so condition
permission for a modification on the renter agreeing to restore the interior of the premises to the
condition that existed before the modification, reasonable wear and tear excepted."

A **reasonable accommodation** is subparagraph (B): "a refusal to make reasonable accommodations in
rules, policies, practices, or services, when such accommodations may be necessary to afford such
person equal opportunity to use and enjoy a dwelling."

Put the two texts side by side and four differences fall out of the words themselves. First, the
**object**: a modification changes the *premises* — physical structure. An accommodation changes
*rules, policies, practices, or services* — how the housing provider operates. Second, **who pays**:
the statute says modifications are "at the expense of the handicapped person." Subparagraph (B) says
nothing about expense, because changing a rule ordinarily costs nothing. Third, the **standard**: a
modification must be one that "may be necessary to afford such person full enjoyment of the
premises"; an accommodation must be one that "may be necessary to afford such person equal
opportunity to use and enjoy a dwelling." Fourth, **restoration**: only the modification clause has a
restoration proviso, and it is narrow — a *rental*, a landlord acting "where it is reasonable to do
so," restoration of "the interior" only, and "reasonable wear and tear excepted."

A common error is to assume the landlord must pay for a ramp because "it's a fair housing
requirement." Under (f)(3)(A) the ramp is a modification of the premises, and the statute puts the
expense on the person with the handicap; the landlord's duty is not to *refuse permission*
unreasonably. The mirror-image error is to assume a tenant must pay a fee to have a rule waived.
Under (f)(3)(B) waiving a no-pets policy for an assistance animal, or assigning a parking space
closer to the door, or accepting rent from a third-party payer, is a change to rules, policies,
practices or services — the text attaches no expense to it. The exam-proof phrasing: **modification =
premises, tenant's expense, possible restoration; accommodation = rules, no expense clause, no
restoration clause.**

The third item in (f)(3) is subparagraph (C), the **design and construction requirements**. For
"covered multifamily dwellings for first occupancy after the date that is 30 months after
September 13, 1988," it is discrimination to fail to design and construct them so that "(i) the
public use and common use portions of such dwellings are readily accessible to and usable by
handicapped persons; (ii) all the doors designed to allow passage into and within all premises within
such dwellings are sufficiently wide to allow passage by handicapped persons in wheelchairs; and
(iii) all premises within such dwellings contain the following features of adaptive design: (I) an
accessible route into and through the dwelling; (II) light switches, electrical outlets, thermostats,
and other environmental controls in accessible locations; (III) reinforcements in bathroom walls to
allow later installation of grab bars; and (IV) usable kitchens and bathrooms such that an individual
in a wheelchair can maneuver about the space." Paragraph (f)(7) defines **covered multifamily
dwellings** as "(A) buildings consisting of 4 or more units if such buildings have one or more
elevators; and (B) ground floor units in other buildings consisting of 4 or more units." So a
walk-up fourplex is covered as to its ground-floor units; a four-unit building with an elevator is
covered throughout. Paragraph (f)(4) provides that compliance with "ANSI A117.1" suffices for
(3)(C)(iii), and (f)(5)(A) provides that where a State or local government has incorporated the
(3)(C) requirements into its laws, compliance with those laws "shall be deemed to satisfy" the
federal paragraph — though (f)(6)(B) adds that such state or local determinations "shall not be
conclusive in enforcement proceedings" under the Act. Paragraph (f)(8) preserves any state or local
law "that requires dwellings to be designed and constructed in a manner that affords handicapped
persons greater access than is required by this subchapter."

Finally, (f)(9): "Nothing in this subsection requires that a dwelling be made available to an
individual whose tenancy would constitute a direct threat to the health or safety of other
individuals or whose tenancy would result in substantial physical damage to the property of others."
This is a narrow, evidence-based escape hatch about *this* individual's tenancy, not a licence to act
on assumptions about a category of people.

**Familial status and housing for older persons.** The Fair Housing Act's definitions of "dwelling"
and "familial status" live in 42 U.S.C. § 3602, and its housing-for-older-persons provisions live in
§ 3607; neither was held for this course, so this module does not state their contents. What can be
said from what was read: § 3604's opening clause makes § 3607 an express exception to the entire
section, and the § 3601 notes record that Congress revisited § 3607 in the Housing for Older Persons
Act of 1995. Texas's parallel provisions were read in full and are taught in the next unit —
Property Code § 301.004 defines familial status for Texas purposes, and § 301.043 exempts certain
elderly housing from the familial-status provisions. Because Property Code § 301.062 requires the
Texas agency's substantive rules to "impose obligations, rights, and remedies that are the same as
are provided in federal fair housing regulations," the Texas provisions are a sound place to learn
the concepts — but if you need the federal rule for a federal filing, read § 3602 and § 3607
directly.

**Exemptions, and the one that never applies to advertising.** Section 3604's opening clause points
to § 3603(b) and § 3607 for exemptions. The Texas analogue, Property Code § 301.041, was read, and it
shows the structure clearly: it exempts certain owner sales and rentals from **§§ 301.021, 301.023,
301.024 and 301.025** — and conspicuously *not* from § 301.022, the publication and advertising
prohibition. Texas makes the point twice over, because § 301.041(a)(1)(B)(ii) conditions the
single-family owner exemption on the house being sold or rented without "the publication, posting, or
mailing of a notice, statement, or advertisement prohibited by Section 301.022." The practical rule,
stated for Texas from Texas text: **an owner who qualifies for the sale-or-rental exemption still may
not advertise a discriminatory preference, and advertising one can cost the exemption itself.** The
same clause of § 301.041(a)(1)(B)(i) removes the exemption if the owner uses "the sales or rental
facilities or services of a broker, agent, or salesperson licensed under Chapter 1101, Occupations
Code." That sentence is why exemptions are of limited interest to a license holder: the moment you
are engaged, the owner is not exempt, and you were never exempt.

### Statute reading exercise: the six subsections of § 3604

Open 42 U.S.C. § 3604 and read straight through subsections (a) to (f)(3), with the section's opening
clause. Then answer from the text quoted in this unit:

1. Which subsections of § 3604 list "handicap" among the bases, and which do not? Why does the
   omission not mean handicap discrimination in a rental refusal is lawful?
2. Section 3604(e) makes blockbusting unlawful "[f]or profit, to induce **or attempt to induce**."
   What does the phrase "attempt to induce" add that "induce" alone would not cover?
3. Under § 3604(f)(3)(A) and (B), who bears the expense of a reasonable modification, and what does
   the statute say about the expense of a reasonable accommodation?
4. Section 3604(f)(1)(C) reaches discrimination because of the handicap of "any person associated
   with that buyer or renter." Name one Texas transaction fact pattern that would fall under (C) but
   not under (A).

### Applied exercise: the ramp and the parking space

Marisol leases a ground-floor unit in a twelve-unit Austin apartment building that was first occupied
in 2004. She uses a wheelchair. She makes two written requests to the property manager. First, she
asks permission to have a contractor build a wooden ramp over the two steps at her private entrance
and to widen the bathroom doorway, and she offers to pay for the work herself. Second, she asks that
the complex assign her the unreserved parking space nearest the building entrance, which the complex
currently allocates on a first-come basis under a written policy. The manager refuses both, saying
the building "isn't required to be accessible" and that changing the parking policy "wouldn't be fair
to the other residents."

1. Which of Marisol's two requests is a **reasonable modification** under § 3604(f)(3)(A) and which
   is a **reasonable accommodation** under § 3604(f)(3)(B)? Point to the words in each subparagraph
   that decide it.
2. Marisol offered to pay for the ramp and the doorway. Does § 3604(f)(3)(A) require her to? Does
   § 3604(f)(3)(B) say anything about who pays for the parking change?
3. The manager wants Marisol to agree in writing to remove the ramp and restore the doorway when she
   leaves. Which of the two requests, if either, does the restoration proviso in (f)(3)(A) reach, and
   what three limits does that proviso place on the landlord's condition?
4. The manager's first answer was that the building "isn't required to be accessible." Assume the
   building is a covered multifamily dwelling first occupied in 2004. Explain why the design and
   construction requirements in § 3604(f)(3)(C) are a separate question from Marisol's two requests,
   and why a "no" on one does not answer the other.

## Unit: Texas Fair Housing Laws

Texas has its own fair housing statute, and a license holder practising in Texas is answerable under
it as well as under the federal Act. It is Property Code ch. 301, and § 301.001 gives it its name:
"This chapter may be cited as the Texas Fair Housing Act."

**Purpose, and the design goal of substantial equivalence.** Section 301.002 states three purposes:
"(1) provide for fair housing practices in this state; (2) create a procedure for investigating and
settling complaints of discriminatory housing practices; and (3) provide rights and remedies
substantially equivalent to those granted under federal law." That third purpose is the key to the
whole chapter. Texas did not set out to build a different fair housing law; it set out to build a
state forum that mirrors the federal one. Section 301.062 carries the idea into rulemaking: the
commission "may adopt rules necessary to implement this chapter, but substantive rules adopted by the
commission shall impose obligations, rights, and remedies that are the same as are provided in
federal fair housing regulations." A student who expects Texas to be a materially different code will
be looking for a difference that the legislature deliberately did not create.

**Who administers it.** Section 301.0015 answers this and is easy to misstate: "The powers and duties
exercised by the Commission on Human Rights under this chapter are transferred to the Texas Workforce
Commission. A reference in this chapter to the 'commission' means the Texas Workforce Commission."
So every time ch. 301 says "the commission," it means the **Texas Workforce Commission**. It does not
mean TREC. TREC's role in fair housing is licence discipline under Occupations Code ch. 1101, which
unit 3 covers; the fair housing complaint itself goes to the Workforce Commission.

**The protected classes Texas lists.** Read the prohibition sections and the list is: **race, color,
religion, sex, disability, familial status, and national origin.** Two observations, both from the
text. First, Texas says **disability** where the federal Act says handicap, and § 301.003(6) defines
it: "a mental or physical impairment that substantially limits at least one major life activity, a
record of the impairment, or being regarded as having the impairment. The term does not include
current illegal use or addiction to any drug or illegal or federally controlled substance and does
not apply to an individual because of an individual's sexual orientation or because that individual
is a transvestite." Second, the classes are distributed across sections exactly as the federal Act
distributes them across subsections: § 301.021, on sale and rental, lists "race, color, religion,
sex, familial status, or national origin" without disability, because § 301.025 is the disability
section. Do not read the omission in § 301.021 as permission.

**Does Texas add protected classes?** On the face of ch. 301, no — the chapter lists the same seven
bases the federal Act names, using "disability" for "handicap." It does not list sexual orientation,
gender identity, marital status, age, source of income, or military or veteran status, and
§ 301.005 forecloses reading them in: "The statutory civil remedies or theories of recovery created
by this chapter may not be expanded beyond their express statutory terms." Do not tell a client that
the Texas Fair Housing Act protects a class it does not name.

Texas has, however, **broadened one class by definition**. Section 301.0045, added in 2023, provides
that "protective hairstyle" includes braids, locks, and twists, and that a provision of the chapter
"referring to discrimination because of race or on the basis of race includes discrimination because
of or on the basis of a person's hair texture or protective hairstyle commonly or historically
associated with race." A Dallas landlord who turns an applicant away over locs is, under Texas law,
discriminating on the basis of race. This is coverage added inside an existing class, and that is the
accurate way to describe it.

Section 301.004 defines **familial status** for Texas: an act is committed because of familial status
if it is committed because the person is "(1) pregnant; (2) domiciled with an individual younger than
18 years of age" as parent or legal custodian or with the written permission of one, "or (3) in the
process of obtaining legal custody of an individual younger than 18 years of age." Pregnancy is
expressly inside familial status, and so is the adoptive or custodial parent mid-process. Section
301.003(9) adds that "'Family' includes a single individual," and § 301.003(8) defines **dwelling** to
include not only a structure or part of a structure occupied or intended for occupancy as a residence
"by one or more families" but also "vacant land that is offered for sale or lease for the
construction or location of" such a structure. Vacant residential lots are inside the Texas Act.

**The prohibited practices.** Subchapter B tracks § 3604 section by section. Section 301.021(a)
prohibits refusing to sell or rent after a bona fide offer, refusing to negotiate, or "in any other
manner mak[ing] unavailable or deny[ing] a dwelling"; § 301.021(b) prohibits discrimination in terms,
conditions, privileges, services and facilities. Section 301.021(c) adds a Texas sentence with no
federal analogue in the text read here: "This section does not prohibit discrimination against a
person because the person has been convicted under federal law or the law of any state of the illegal
manufacture or distribution of a controlled substance." Section 301.022 is the publication and
advertising prohibition, § 301.023 the false-unavailability prohibition, § 301.024 the blockbusting
prohibition ("A person may not, for profit, induce or attempt to induce another to sell or rent a
dwelling by representations regarding the entry or prospective entry into a neighborhood…"), and
§ 301.025 the disability section, including at § 301.025(c) the same three-part definition of
discrimination — refusal to permit a reasonable modification "at the expense of the person having a
disability," refusal to make a reasonable accommodation "in rules, policies, practices, or services,"
and failure to meet the design and construction requirements. Texas fixes the design-and-construction
cut-off by date rather than by a 30-month formula: § 301.025(e) provides that the requirement "does
not apply to a building the first occupancy of which occurred on or before March 13, 1991."

Two Texas sections reach beyond sale and rental. Section 301.026 covers the **residential real estate
related transaction**, defined in subsection (b) as "(1) the making or purchasing of loans or the
provision of other financial assistance: (A) to purchase, construct, improve, repair, or maintain a
dwelling; or (B) to secure residential real estate; or (2) the selling, brokering, or appraising of
residential real property." A person whose business includes such transactions "may not discriminate
against another in making a real estate related transaction available or in the terms or conditions
of a real estate related transaction" on the seven bases. Read that definition twice: **brokering and
appraising are named in it**, so a Texas license holder's own services are a residential real estate
related transaction. Section 301.027 covers **brokerage services** — no one may deny another access
to or participation in "a multiple-listing service, real estate brokers' organization, or other
service, organization, or facility relating to the business of selling or renting dwellings," or
discriminate in the terms of that access, on the seven bases.

**Exemptions.** Section 301.041(a) exempts from §§ 301.021, 301.023, 301.024 and 301.025 the sale or
rental of a single-family house by an owner who does not own, or hold an interest in, "more than
three single-family houses at any one time," provided the house is sold or rented without a licensed
broker, agent or salesperson and without prohibited advertising; and the sale or rental of rooms or
units in a dwelling for "not more than four families living independently of each other, if the owner
maintains and occupies one of the living quarters as the owner's residence." Subsection (b) limits
the single-family exemption to "one sale in a 24-month period if the owner was not the most recent
resident of the house at the time of the sale." Subsection (c) preserves the use of "attorneys,
escrow agents, abstractors, title companies, and other professional assistants necessary to transfer
title," and subsection (d) defines when a person is "in the business of selling or renting a
dwelling" — three or more sale or rental transactions as seller or landlord in the preceding year,
two or more as an agent providing sales or rental services, or ownership of a dwelling for five or
more families.

Section 301.042 exempts religious organizations and nonprofits they control from limiting sale,
rental or occupancy of dwellings they own or operate "for other than a commercial purpose" to persons
of the same religion, or giving preference to them, "unless membership in the religion is restricted
because of race, color, or national origin" — with a 2025 carve-back at § 301.042(a-1) removing the
exemption for a single-family house, duplex, triplex or quadruplex "located on a subdivided lot in a
parcel of land 25 acres or greater" owned by such an organization. Subsection (b) exempts a bona fide
private club not open to the public that provides lodging incident to its primary purpose, and
subsection (c) provides that the chapter does not prohibit an appraiser "from considering in those
appraisals factors other than race, color, religion, sex, disability, familial status, or national
origin." Section 301.043 exempts from the familial-status provisions housing the commission
determines "is specifically designed and operated to assist elderly individuals under a federal or
state program," housing "intended for, and solely occupied by, individuals 62 years of age or older,"
and housing "intended and operated for occupancy by at least one individual 55 years of age or older
for each unit as determined by commission rules." Section 301.044 preserves reasonable occupancy
limits and health and safety standards, and provides that the chapter "does not affect a requirement
of nondiscrimination in any other state or federal law."

**Local law.** The topic's scope note names local fair housing laws, and ch. 301 acknowledges them in
two places without cataloguing them. Section 301.068 lets the commission "defer proceedings under
this chapter and refer a complaint to a municipality that has been certified by the federal
Department of Housing and Urban Development as a substantially equivalent fair housing agency" — so
Texas contemplates certified municipal fair housing agencies handling complaints. And § 301.044(b)
preserves nondiscrimination requirements in other law. Whether a particular Texas city's ordinance
adds bases beyond the seven in ch. 301 is a question of that city's ordinance. No municipal ordinance
was held as a source for this course, so this module names none and states none. Before you advertise
or screen in a specific Texas city, read that city's ordinance.

**The Texas enforcement path, step by step.** Section 301.063 makes the commission the body that
"shall receive, investigate, seek to conciliate, and act on complaints." Under § 301.081 a complaint
must be "in writing," "under oath," and "in the form prescribed by the commission," and must be filed
"on or before the first anniversary of the date the alleged discriminatory housing practice occurs or
terminates, whichever is later." An aggrieved person may file; the commission may file. Within 20
days the commission serves each respondent with notice and a copy of the complaint. The respondent
may answer within 10 days (§ 301.082), in writing and under oath. The commission investigates, and
§ 301.083(b) requires it to complete the investigation "not later than the 100th day after the date
the complaint is filed" or, failing that, to dispose of all related administrative proceedings within
one year, with written notice of the reasons for delay. Throughout, § 301.085 requires the commission
"to the extent feasible" to engage in **conciliation**; conciliation statements may not be made
public or used as evidence without written consent.

Section 301.088 requires a **reasonable cause determination**, also targeted at 100 days; if
reasonable cause is found the commission "shall… immediately issue a charge on behalf of the
aggrieved person," and if it is not found the commission "shall promptly dismiss the complaint"
(§ 301.091) and disclose the dismissal publicly. Section 301.090 carves out land use: if the matter
involves the legality of a zoning or other land use law, the commission may not issue a charge and
must refer the matter to the attorney general.

Then comes the fork every Texas complainant and respondent must understand. Under § 301.093, "[a]
complainant, a respondent, or an aggrieved person on whose behalf a complaint was filed may elect to
have the claims asserted in the charge decided in a civil action," and the election must be made
"not later than the 20th day after" service of the charge. **If no timely election is made**, the
commission provides an administrative hearing under § 301.111, governed by Government Code ch. 2001.
There the commission may order "actual damages, reasonable attorney fees, court costs, and other
injunctive or equitable relief," and § 301.112(b) allows a civil penalty "[t]o vindicate the public's
interest" not exceeding "$10,000 if the respondent has been found by order of the commission or a
court to have committed a prior discriminatory housing practice," "$25,000 if the respondent has been
found… to have committed one other discriminatory housing practice during the five-year period ending
on the date of the filing of the charges," or "$50,000 if the respondent has been found… to have
committed two or more discriminatory housing practices during the seven-year period ending on the
date of filing of the charge." **If a timely election is made**, § 301.131 directs the attorney
general, within 30 days, to file a civil action in district court on the aggrieved person's behalf,
with venue where the practice occurred.

Two further routes exist. Section 301.132 lets the attorney general, at the commission's request,
bring a **pattern or practice** action, with civil penalties not exceeding "$50,000 for a first
violation" and "$100,000 for a second or subsequent violation." And § 301.151 preserves a **private
civil action**: an aggrieved person may sue in district court "not later than the second year after
the date of the occurrence or the termination of an alleged discriminatory housing practice or the
breach of a conciliation agreement… whichever occurs last," and may do so "whether a complaint has
been filed under Section 301.081 and without regard to the status of any complaint filed under that
section." Section 301.153 lets the court award "actual and punitive damages," attorney fees, court
costs and injunctive relief; § 301.156 allows attorney fees to the prevailing party in either forum.
Note the two limits: § 301.151(d) bars a private action where the commission obtained a conciliation
agreement with the aggrieved person's consent, except to enforce it, and § 301.151(e) bars one once
the commission has begun a hearing on the record on the charge.

Finally, two provisions a license holder should know by name. Section 301.114 provides that when the
commission issues an order about a discriminatory housing practice "that occurs in the course of a
business subject to a licensing or regulation by a governmental agency," the commission shall, within
30 days, send the findings and order to that agency and "recommend to the governmental agency
appropriate disciplinary action." For a Texas license holder, that agency is TREC. And § 301.171
makes it a **Class A misdemeanor** to intimidate or interfere with a person by force or threat of
force because of the person's protected class and because that person is buying, selling, renting,
financing or occupying a dwelling, or is aiding others in doing so.

**How the Texas and federal options relate.** From what was read: ch. 301 is designed to be
substantially equivalent, not exclusive. Section 301.044(b) says the chapter "does not affect a
requirement of nondiscrimination in any other state or federal law." Section 301.083(a) contemplates
the federal government referring a complaint to the commission or deferring jurisdiction to it.
Section 301.068 contemplates referral down to a HUD-certified municipal agency. And § 301.092 and
§ 301.111(c) both stop the state administrative track once a civil action on the same practice — "under
federal or state law" — reaches trial. The picture is one of parallel forums coordinated to avoid
duplication, with the complainant holding a meaningful choice at the § 301.093 election. What this
module cannot tell you, because the federal enforcement sections were not held, is the federal filing
deadline or the federal election procedure. If a client is deciding between forums, that is a
decision for the client's lawyer on the current federal text, not for a license holder.

## Unit: Housing Discrimination

The previous two units set out the statutes. This unit names the practices those statutes reach, in
the vocabulary the industry actually uses, and then does the thing that matters most to your career:
it shows where a Texas license holder's personal exposure sits.

**Refusal to deal.** The plainest violation. Refusing to sell or rent after a bona fide offer,
refusing to negotiate, or otherwise making a dwelling unavailable, because of a protected class:
42 U.S.C. § 3604(a); Property Code § 301.021(a). The catch-all matters more than the obvious refusal,
because outright refusals are rare and functional refusals are not. Screening criteria applied to one
applicant and waived for another, a "we're not showing it this weekend" that becomes a showing for
the next caller, a listing agent who forwards five offers to the seller and sets one aside — all of
these "otherwise make unavailable or deny."

**Discriminatory terms and conditions.** Section 3604(b) and § 301.021(b). Different deposits,
different fees, different lease durations, different rules, different maintenance response, different
access to amenities. The transaction closed or the lease was signed; the discrimination is in the
terms. A Texas property manager who imposes a larger deposit on families with children has committed
a familial-status violation in the terms of the rental even though nobody was turned away.

**Discriminatory advertising.** Section 3604(c) and § 301.022. Recall the four features from unit 1:
it covers notices, statements and advertisements; it covers making, printing, publishing, or causing
those; the test is whether the item *indicates* a preference, limitation or discrimination, or an
intention to make one; and — critically, on the Texas text — the exemption in § 301.041 does not
reach § 301.022 at all, and a discriminatory advertisement by an otherwise-exempt owner destroys the
exemption under § 301.041(a)(1)(B)(ii). Advertising is where fair housing failures are easiest to
prove, because the evidence is a document with your name on it. As a matter of practice and not of
statute, the safest habit is to describe the **property** and never the **people** — square footage,
finishes, schools by name and distance, and no adjectives about who would fit in.

**Steering.** Steering is the practice of channelling a prospect toward or away from particular
areas, buildings or neighborhoods on the basis of a protected class, whether or not any property is
ever refused. Two points about where it lives in the law.

First, the federal Act does not use the word. Steering is unlawful under § 3604(a) because it
"otherwise make[s] unavailable or den[ies]" a dwelling, and under § 3604(b) and § 3604(d) when it
takes the form of differential service or a false statement of availability; in Texas it violates
§ 301.021 in the same way. Second — and this is the provision that should be on the wall of every
Texas brokerage — **Texas licence law names it expressly.** Occupations Code § 1101.652(b)(32)
authorizes TREC to suspend or revoke a licence, or take other disciplinary action, if the license
holder, while engaged in real estate brokerage, "discriminates against an owner, potential buyer,
landlord, or potential tenant on the basis of race, color, religion, sex, disability, familial status,
national origin, or ancestry, including directing a prospective buyer or tenant interested in
equivalent properties to a different area based on the race, color, religion, sex, disability,
familial status, national origin, or ancestry of the potential owner or tenant."

Read that subdivision closely, because three details are doing work. It lists **ancestry** in
addition to the seven fair housing bases — a basis that appears in the licence statute and not in
Property Code ch. 301's prohibition sections. It protects the **owner or landlord** as well as the
buyer or tenant. And its steering clause turns on the phrase "interested in **equivalent
properties**" — the wrong is sending the prospect somewhere else when the properties are comparable,
which is exactly the fact pattern in which an agent will tell themselves they were being helpful.

**Blockbusting.** Section 3604(e) and Property Code § 301.024. The elements are "for profit," "induce
or attempt to induce" a sale or rental, and "representations regarding the entry or prospective entry
into the neighborhood" of persons of a protected class. Historically this was door-knocking and
panic-selling; today it can be a targeted mailer or a social-media post. A Texas agent who farms a
neighborhood with the message "the area is changing, list now before values drop" and whose surrounding
messaging makes clear what "changing" refers to has made a representation about prospective entry, is
plainly acting for profit, and has attempted to induce sales. That is the whole offence. No one has
to list; nothing has to sell; the values do not have to fall.

**Redlining.** Redlining is the refusal or disfavouring of loans, insurance, appraisal or other
housing-related services in a geographic area, historically drawn on race. The federal Fair Housing
Act's financing-discrimination section is 42 U.S.C. § 3605, which was not held for this course; this
module therefore does not paraphrase it. What was read, and what governs in Texas, is Property Code
§ 301.026: a person "whose business includes engaging in residential real estate related
transactions may not discriminate against another in making a real estate related transaction
available or in the terms or conditions of a real estate related transaction" on the seven bases, and
"residential real estate related transaction" is defined to include "the making or purchasing of
loans or the provision of other financial assistance… to purchase, construct, improve, repair, or
maintain a dwelling" and "the selling, brokering, or appraising of residential real property." Unit 4
takes up credit discrimination directly, and unit 5 takes up the Community Reinvestment Act, which
addresses the affirmative side of the same geography.

**A license holder's personal exposure, in three layers.** Do not think of fair housing as something
the broker or the seller worries about. Three separate consequences can attach to you individually.

*Layer one — the fair housing statutes themselves.* Section 3604 and § 301.021 through § 301.027 say
"a person may not," and § 301.003(12) defines "person" to include an individual. Under Property Code
§ 301.153 a court may award "actual and punitive damages," attorney fees and costs; under § 301.112
the commission may order actual damages and assess civil penalties in the tiers quoted in unit 2.

*Layer two — your licence.* Section 1101.652(b)(32) is a ground for suspension or revocation. Section
1101.652(b)(2) independently reaches conduct "that is dishonest or in bad faith or that demonstrates
untrustworthiness," and § 1101.652(b)(1) reaches acting "negligently or incompetently." Under
Occupations Code § 1101.701 TREC may impose an administrative penalty on a person who violates the
chapter, and § 1101.702(a) caps that penalty at "$5,000 for each violation," with each day a
violation continues countable as a separate violation. Section 1101.701(c) permits TREC to combine a
penalty proceeding with a proceeding to suspend or revoke.

*Layer three — the bridge between them.* Property Code § 301.114 requires the Texas Workforce
Commission, within 30 days of issuing an order about a discriminatory housing practice occurring in a
licensed business, to send the findings and order to the licensing agency and "recommend… appropriate
disciplinary action." So a state fair housing order against a license holder arrives at TREC by
operation of statute. And Occupations Code § 1101.801 closes the loop from the other direction:
"Disciplinary action taken against a person under Section 1101.652 does not relieve the person from
civil or criminal liability." The layers stack; they do not substitute.

**The question you will actually be asked.** Sooner or later a buyer will ask you some version of:
"Is this a good area for people like me?" or "What kind of people live here?" or "How are the
schools — really?" The question is usually innocent. The answer is where careers end.

The rule is short. **Do not characterize a neighborhood by the demographic characteristics of the
people in it — not favourably, not unfavourably, not vaguely, not in response to a direct request,
and not because the client volunteered their own group first.** Answering "yes, you'll fit in here"
or "you might be happier in the northwest part of town" is steering under § 3604(a) and § 301.021 and
is squarely inside the licence-discipline language of § 1101.652(b)(32) if it directs a prospect
"interested in equivalent properties to a different area." The client's consent does not cure it, and
the client's own framing of the question does not cure it. Neither the Fair Housing Act nor ch. 301
contains a consent exception.

What to say instead is practice guidance, not a statutory script, and this module cites nothing for
it: acknowledge the question, explain briefly that you are not permitted to describe neighborhoods by
the characteristics of the people who live in them and that this is the same rule protecting the
client, and then redirect to **objective published sources the client can read for themselves** —
school district and campus accountability reports, published crime statistics from the relevant law
enforcement agency, census data, municipal and appraisal district records, HOA documents, and the
property's own disclosures. Then show the client every property meeting their stated objective
criteria, in every area those criteria cover, and let them draw the conclusions. A useful habit, again
practice and not law, is to write down the client's objective criteria — price, bedrooms, square
footage, commute time, school district, lot size — at the outset, and to run every showing decision
back through that written list. It keeps your service consistent and it leaves a record that your
service was consistent.

## Unit: Housing Credit Discrimination

Discrimination does not stop at the front door of the house; it also happens at the desk where the
loan is decided. Two bodies of law meet there, and a Texas license holder should be able to tell them
apart.

**The Equal Credit Opportunity Act.** ECOA is 15 U.S.C. § 1691 and following, enacted as title VII of
the Consumer Credit Protection Act. Section 1691's own catchline is "Scope of prohibition," and the
statutory notes under it record that the subchapter "known as the 'Equal Credit Opportunity Act.'"
The Act's origin is narrower than its present text, and the history explains the list. The 1974
congressional findings printed in the notes to § 1691 say Congress found "a need to insure that the
various financial institutions and other firms engaged in the extensions of credit exercise their
responsibility to make credit available with fairness, impartiality, and without discrimination on
the basis of **sex or marital status**," and stated the purpose "to require that financial
institutions and other firms engaged in the extension of credit make that credit equally available to
all credit-worthy customers without regard to sex or marital status." Two years later the list grew:
the amendments note under § 1691 records that in 1976 Pub. L. 94–239 "expanded prohibition against
discrimination to include race, color, religion, national origin and age, and added cls. (2) and
(3)."

**§ 1691(a) — the prohibited bases.** "It shall be unlawful for any creditor to discriminate against
any applicant, with respect to any aspect of a credit transaction— (1) on the basis of race, color,
religion, national origin, sex or marital status, or age (provided the applicant has the capacity to
contract); (2) because all or part of the applicant's income derives from any public assistance
program; or (3) because the applicant has in good faith exercised any right under this chapter."

Three features of that text repay attention. The phrase **"any aspect of a credit transaction"** is
broad: it covers the application, the terms offered, the pricing, servicing decisions and
termination, not merely the yes-or-no. The **age** basis carries its own qualifier — "provided the
applicant has the capacity to contract" — so ECOA does not turn a minor's incapacity into
discrimination. And paragraph (3) is an **anti-retaliation** provision: penalizing an applicant for
having in good faith exercised a right under the chapter is itself a violation.

Now compare the lists, because this is the single most useful thing a license holder can carry out of
this unit. ECOA protects **marital status, age, and public-assistance income**; the Fair Housing Act
and Property Code ch. 301 do not name any of those three. The Fair Housing Act and ch. 301 protect
**familial status and handicap/disability**; § 1691(a) does not name either. The overlap is race,
color, religion, national origin and sex. So a mortgage application on a Texas home can be governed by
both statutes at once, and a fact pattern that fails one may not fail the other. A lender who prices
a loan worse because the applicant's income is a housing voucher or disability benefit is inside
§ 1691(a)(2) on the face of the words; a lender who declines an applicant because she is pregnant is
outside § 1691(a)'s list but inside familial status under Property Code § 301.004(1) — and, if the
lending is a "residential real estate related transaction," inside § 301.026.

**§ 1691(b) — what does not constitute discrimination.** The statute anticipates that lenders must ask
questions, and it protects five kinds of inquiry. It is not discrimination for a creditor: "(1) to
make an inquiry of marital status if such inquiry is for the purpose of ascertaining the creditor's
rights and remedies applicable to the particular extension of credit and not to discriminate in a
determination of credit-worthiness"; "(2) to make an inquiry of the applicant's age or of whether the
applicant's income derives from any public assistance program if such inquiry is for the purpose of
determining the amount and probable continuance of income levels, credit history, or other pertinent
element of credit-worthiness as provided in regulations of the Bureau"; "(3) to use any empirically
derived credit system which considers age if such system is demonstrably and statistically sound in
accordance with regulations of the Bureau, except that in the operation of such system the age of an
elderly applicant may not be assigned a negative factor or value"; "(4) to make an inquiry or to
consider the age of an elderly applicant when the age of such applicant is to be used by the creditor
in the extension of credit in favor of such applicant"; or "(5) to make an inquiry under section
1691c–2 of this title, in accordance with the requirements of that section."

The through-line in (b)(1) and (b)(2) is **purpose**. Marital status may be asked about to determine
the creditor's rights and remedies — in Texas, community property and spousal-joinder questions make
that a real need — but "not to discriminate in a determination of credit-worthiness." Age and
public-assistance income may be asked about to assess "the amount and probable continuance of income
levels." The question is lawful; using the answer as a proxy for creditworthiness is not. Paragraph
(3) draws the line in the other direction: an age-considering scoring model is permitted if
"demonstrably and statistically sound," but an elderly applicant's age "may not be assigned a
negative factor or value." Note that § 1691(b) refers throughout to "regulations of the Bureau"; the
Bureau is defined elsewhere in the Consumer Credit Protection Act, which was not held for this
course, and its implementing regulation is likewise not a held source, so this module states only
what § 1691 itself says.

Section 1691(c) adds that it is not a violation to refuse credit offered under "any credit assistance
program expressly authorized by law for an economically disadvantaged class of persons," a nonprofit
program for its members or such a class, or a **special purpose credit program** offered by a
for-profit organization "to meet special social needs" meeting the Bureau's standards, "if such
refusal is required by or made pursuant to such program." That is the provision that lets targeted
down-payment-assistance and similar programs restrict eligibility without the restriction itself
being ECOA discrimination.

**§ 1691(d) — adverse action notification.** This is the part of ECOA that produces the letter your
client will bring to you, and the numbers in it are worth knowing exactly.

Paragraph (1): "Within thirty days (or such longer reasonable time as specified in regulations of the
Bureau for any class of credit transaction) after receipt of a **completed** application for credit, a
creditor shall notify the applicant of its action on the application." The word "completed" is doing
real work: the clock runs from a completed application.

Paragraph (2): "Each applicant against whom adverse action is taken shall be entitled to a statement
of reasons for such action from the creditor," and the creditor satisfies that duty either by
"providing statements of reasons in writing as a matter of course," or by "giving written
notification of adverse action which discloses (i) the applicant's right to a statement of reasons
within thirty days after receipt by the creditor of a request made within sixty days after such
notification, and (ii) the identity of the person or office from which such statement may be
obtained." So under the second route there are two periods: the applicant has **sixty days** from the
notification to request reasons, and the creditor then has **thirty days** from receiving the request
to supply them.

Paragraph (3): "A statement of reasons meets the requirements of this section only if it contains the
**specific reasons** for the adverse action taken." A letter saying "you did not meet our credit
standards" is not a statement of reasons under paragraph (3).

Paragraph (4) handles the indirect case — where a third party asked the creditor to extend credit,
notification may run directly or through the third party, "provided in either case that the identity
of the creditor is disclosed." Paragraph (5) allows verbal notification by a creditor "who did not
act on more than one hundred and fifty applications during the calendar year preceding."

Paragraph (6) defines the term: "'adverse action' means a denial or revocation of credit, a change in
the terms of an existing credit arrangement, or a refusal to grant credit in substantially the amount
or on substantially the terms requested. Such term does not include a refusal to extend additional
credit under an existing credit arrangement where the applicant is delinquent or otherwise in default,
or where such additional credit would exceed a previously established credit limit." A common error is
to think adverse action means only a flat denial. It also covers an approval for materially less than
was requested, or on materially different terms.

**§ 1691(e) — the appraisal copy.** Section 1691(e)(1) requires each creditor to furnish the applicant
"a copy of any and all written appraisals and valuations developed in connection with the applicant's
application for a loan that is secured or would have been secured by a first lien on a dwelling
promptly upon completion, but in no case later than 3 days prior to the closing of the loan, whether
the creditor grants or denies the applicant's request for credit or the application is incomplete or
withdrawn." The applicant may waive the three-day timing (e)(2) and may be charged a reasonable fee
for the appraisal itself (e)(3), but the **copy** must be provided "at no additional cost" (e)(4), and
(e)(5) requires the creditor to notify the applicant in writing of this right at the time of
application. Subsection (e)(6) defines "valuation" broadly to include "any estimate of the value of a
dwelling developed in connection with a creditor's decision to provide credit, including those values
developed pursuant to a policy of a government sponsored enterprise or by an automated valuation
model, a broker price opinion, or other methodology or mechanism."

**The Fair Housing Act side, and the Texas provision that was read.** The Fair Housing Act has its own
financing provision at 42 U.S.C. § 3605, which was not held for this course and is therefore not
paraphrased here. The Texas parallel *was* read: Property Code § 301.026 prohibits a person whose
business includes residential real estate related transactions from discriminating "in making a real
estate related transaction available or in the terms or conditions of a real estate related
transaction" on the basis of race, color, religion, sex, disability, familial status or national
origin, and defines those transactions to include making or purchasing dwelling loans, providing
other financial assistance to purchase, construct, improve, repair or maintain a dwelling or to
secure residential real estate, and "the selling, brokering, or appraising of residential real
property." Practically, then, a Texas mortgage denial can implicate three regimes at once: ECOA on the
bases in § 1691(a); Property Code § 301.026 on the seven fair housing bases; and, where a license
holder's own conduct contributed, Occupations Code § 1101.652(b)(32).

**What a license holder should and should not do when a client is denied.** This paragraph is
practice, not law, and cites nothing. **Do**: make sure the client actually reads the notice, and
point out that § 1691(d)(2) entitles them to a statement of specific reasons and that the notice
itself must tell them how to request one and from whom. **Do** note the timing — the sixty-day window
to request and the thirty-day window to receive — and tell the client to calendar it. **Do** remind
them of the § 1691(e) appraisal-copy right, since a valuation problem is a common reason for an
adverse action on substantially different terms. **Do** encourage them to speak to the creditor, to a
different creditor, or to a lawyer. **Do not** offer your own theory of why the denial happened,
**do not** tell the client whether they have a claim, **do not** steer them toward a particular lender
because you believe that lender is friendlier to people in the client's group — that is itself
differential service — and **do not** repeat the client's protected characteristics to a lender or
underwriter as context. Your job is to make sure the client knows the rights the statute already gives
them, and to keep your own conduct out of the fact pattern.

## Unit: Community Reinvestment

The four preceding units have all been about prohibition: do not refuse, do not vary terms, do not
advertise a preference, do not steer, do not discriminate in credit. The Community Reinvestment Act
is a different kind of statute, and understanding the difference is the point of this unit.

**The findings.** The Community Reinvestment Act of 1977 — Pub. L. 95–128, title VIII, and by its
own short-title note "may be cited as the 'Community Reinvestment Act of 1977'" — opens at 12 U.S.C.
§ 2901 with three congressional findings:

"(1) regulated financial institutions are required by law to demonstrate that their deposit
facilities serve the convenience and needs of the communities in which they are chartered to do
business; (2) the convenience and needs of communities include the need for credit services as well
as deposit services; and (3) regulated financial institutions have continuing and affirmative
obligation to help meet the credit needs of the local communities in which they are chartered."

Read them as a three-step argument, because that is how they are built. Step one is a premise Congress
treats as already settled: a chartered depository institution must serve "the convenience and needs"
of its community. Step two supplies the move the whole Act turns on: *needs* include **credit**, not
only deposits. The observation sounds obvious now; in 1977 it was the crux. An institution could
gather deposits from a neighborhood and lend them somewhere else entirely, and nothing in the
convenience-and-needs framework plainly said otherwise. Step three states the conclusion: institutions
have a "**continuing and affirmative obligation** to help meet the credit needs of the local
communities in which they are chartered."

Weigh those two adjectives. **Continuing** means the obligation is not discharged by a single
transaction or a single good year; it runs for as long as the charter does. **Affirmative** means the
duty is to *do* something, not merely to refrain. That is the word that separates the CRA from every
other statute in this module.

**The purpose, and the mechanism.** Section 2901(b) states it: "It is the purpose of this chapter to
require each appropriate Federal financial supervisory agency to use its authority when examining
financial institutions, to encourage such institutions to help meet the credit needs of the local
communities in which they are chartered consistent with the safe and sound operation of such
institutions."

Take that sentence apart. The party the subsection **requires** to do something is not the bank; it is
"each appropriate Federal financial supervisory agency." What that agency must do is "use its
authority **when examining** financial institutions." What it is using that authority for is to
"**encourage**" institutions to help meet local credit needs. And the whole thing is bounded by
"**consistent with the safe and sound operation of such institutions**."

Four consequences follow, and each is a place students go wrong.

First, **the CRA works through examination.** Its lever is supervisory: the regulator evaluates the
institution's record as part of the examination process. Section 2901 does not create a licence
requirement, a loan quota, or a transaction-by-transaction rule.

Second, **the CRA does not command a specific loan.** The statute says "encourage… to help meet the
credit needs," not "require to make." No borrower can point at § 2901 and say a bank was obliged to
lend to *them*.

Third, **safety and soundness is a stated limit inside the purpose itself.** The CRA does not ask an
institution to make loans it judges unsound. The obligation is to serve the whole community with the
same seriousness it brings to any market, not to abandon underwriting.

Fourth, **§ 2901 is addressed to regulated financial institutions and their supervisors — not to real
estate brokerages.** No provision of § 2901 imposes a duty on a Texas license holder.

**The examination and rating framework, and what this course can and cannot tell you.** The evaluation
mechanics — how an institution's assessment area is drawn, what lending, investment and service
activity is measured, what the written public evaluation contains, and what performance ratings are
assigned — are set out in later sections of chapter 30 of title 12, at 12 U.S.C. §§ 2902 through 2906.
**Those sections were not among the sources held for this course, and this module does not state their
contents.** What can be said from § 2901 alone is the concept: the supervisory agency assesses an
institution's record of helping meet local credit needs as part of examining it, and that assessment
is meant to influence the institution's conduct. If you need the rating categories, the assessment-area
rules, or the circumstances in which a CRA record is considered in an application for a merger or a
branch, read §§ 2902–2906 in the United States Code. Do not accept a summary — including a confident
one — from anyone who has not.

**Low- and moderate-income communities.** The phrase you will hear attached to the CRA does not appear
in § 2901's findings; § 2901 speaks of "the local communities in which they are chartered." The phrase
appears in the statutory notes printed under § 2901, and those notes were read. The note reproducing
§ 715 of Pub. L. 106–102 (1999) directed the Secretary of the Treasury, with the Federal banking
agencies, to study "the extent to which adequate services are being provided as intended by the
Community Reinvestment Act of 1977, including **services in low- and moderate-income neighborhoods
and for persons of modest means**." The note reproducing § 910 of Pub. L. 102–550 (1992) directed the
Federal Reserve Board to report to Congress "comparing residential, small business, and commercial
lending by insured depository institutions in **low-income, minority, and distressed neighborhoods**
to such lending in other neighborhoods, with the report to contain comparisons of relevant risks and
reasons for different lending practices." So the low- and moderate-income framing is genuinely part of
this statutory scheme, and this is where the held text puts it: in Congress's own directions to study
and report on how the Act is working, rather than in the § 2901 findings themselves. Be precise about
that when you teach it to a client or repeat it on an exam.

**How the CRA relates to — and differs from — the anti-discrimination statutes.** Put § 2901 next to
§ 3604, Property Code § 301.021 and § 1691(a) and the contrast is structural, not merely emphatic.

The anti-discrimination statutes are **prohibitions running to individuals**. Each says a person may
not do a thing to another person because of a protected characteristic. Each identifies protected
classes. Each supplies a remedy to a person who was injured: Property Code § 301.153 lets a court
award actual and punitive damages to a plaintiff; § 301.081 lets an aggrieved person file a complaint;
§ 1691(d) entitles an applicant to a statement of specific reasons.

The CRA is an **affirmative obligation running to communities**, supervised by examination. Section
2901 names no protected class. It does not say a person may not do a thing. Nothing in the section
held for this course gives an individual borrower a claim against a bank for failing to lend to them,
and nothing in it identifies an injured person at all — its subject is "the local communities in which
they are chartered." A common and consequential error is to describe the CRA as an anti-redlining
*prohibition*. Redlining as an unlawful practice is reached by the fair housing and credit statutes —
in Texas, by Property Code § 301.026, and federally by 42 U.S.C. § 3605, which was not held here and
so is not summarized. The CRA comes at the same geography from the opposite direction: instead of
forbidding an institution to disfavour a neighborhood, it obliges the institution to affirmatively
help meet that neighborhood's credit needs, and it charges a federal supervisor with checking whether
it did.

The two are complements. An institution can comply perfectly with the Fair Housing Act and ECOA by
treating every applicant who walks in identically and still have a thin record of serving parts of its
community — because those statutes speak to how you treat the people who apply, and the CRA speaks to
whether you are serving the whole community you are chartered in. That is the entire reason Congress
wrote a separate statute in 1977, nine years after the Fair Housing Act and three years after ECOA.

**What this means for a Texas license holder.** The following is practice and observation, not a legal
duty, and cites nothing. You will not be examined under the CRA and you owe no CRA obligation. What
the Act gives you is context: it is one reason lenders operating in Texas markets fund community
development programs, participate in first-time-buyer and down-payment-assistance products, and open
branches and loan production offices in neighborhoods a purely transactional analysis might skip. When
a buyer in a modest Texas neighborhood is told by one lender that "nobody lends on houses in that
part of town," the useful response is not a lecture on § 2901 but the simple fact that other regulated
institutions serve that community and it is worth shopping the loan. Keep the boundary clean: you may
tell a client that community lending programs exist and that lenders differ; you may not tell them
what a given institution's CRA record is, and you may not steer them to or from a neighborhood on any
basis this module has spent five units warning you about.

### Module recap

This module traced fair housing from a one-sentence federal policy to the licence you hold.

42 U.S.C. § 3601 declares the policy; 42 U.S.C. § 3604 supplies the prohibitions. Its six subsections
each forbid a different thing — refusal to deal, discriminatory terms, discriminatory advertising and
statements, false representations of unavailability, blockbusting, and the entire disability regime in
(f) — and their protected-class lists differ because Congress added sex in 1974 and handicap and
familial status in 1988 by insertion rather than rewriting. Inside § 3604(f)(3), the two provisions
students most often merge stay separate if you hold the texts side by side: a **reasonable
modification** changes the premises, at the expense of the person with the handicap, and in a rental
may be conditioned on restoring the interior; a **reasonable accommodation** changes rules, policies,
practices or services, and the statute attaches no expense and no restoration to it.

Texas legislated in parallel. Property Code ch. 301 protects race, color, religion, sex, disability,
familial status and national origin, is administered by the Texas Workforce Commission, and by
§ 301.002(3) is built to provide rights and remedies "substantially equivalent to those granted under
federal law." It adds no new protected class, and § 301.005 forbids expanding its remedies beyond
their express terms — but § 301.0045 folds hair texture and protective hairstyles into race. Its
enforcement path is concrete and datable: a sworn written complaint within one year, a 20-day notice,
a 10-day answer, a 100-day investigation and reasonable-cause determination, conciliation throughout,
a charge, a 20-day election between an administrative hearing and a district court action prosecuted
by the attorney general, and a private suit available within two years regardless.

Those statutes name practices, and the practices carry personal consequences for a license holder.
Steering, blockbusting, discriminatory advertising and redlining are not abstractions: steering is
named expressly in Occupations Code § 1101.652(b)(32) — which adds ancestry to the list and turns on
directing a prospect "interested in equivalent properties to a different area" — Property Code
§ 301.114 sends a state fair housing order to TREC with a recommendation of discipline within 30 days,
and Occupations Code § 1101.801 confirms that TREC discipline does not relieve you of civil or
criminal liability.

At the lending desk, ECOA's § 1691(a) adds marital status, age and public-assistance income to the
bases, § 1691(b) permits purpose-limited inquiries, and § 1691(d) gives your client a right to notice
within thirty days of a completed application and to a statement of *specific* reasons for any adverse
action. And 12 U.S.C. § 2901 stands apart: a continuing and affirmative obligation on regulated
institutions to help meet their communities' credit needs, enforced by supervisory examination and
bounded by safe and sound operation — a complement to the anti-discrimination statutes, not another
one of them.

You should now be able to state each § 3604 prohibition and its class list, distinguish accommodation
from modification, walk a Texas complainant through ch. 301's forum choice, name the statute a steering
complaint violates in both the fair housing and the licensing codes, read an adverse-action notice
against § 1691(d), and explain why the CRA is an obligation to a community rather than a claim for an
individual.
