# Texas Principles of Real Estate II — Module 08

## TREC Topic: Fair Housing Laws

**Prescribed time: 170 minutes.** This module carries the five units the Commission's PRINS2-1 course
approval form places under *Fair Housing Laws*, whose scope note is *"Federal, state & local fair
housing laws."*

**Learning objectives.** After completing this module you will be able to: state the protected classes
listed in each subsection of 42 U.S.C. § 3604 and distinguish a reasonable accommodation from a
reasonable modification under § 3604(f)(3); apply the Fair Housing Act's own definitions of
"dwelling," "person," "handicap" and "familial status" in 42 U.S.C. § 3602; state the two exemptions
in § 3603(b), the conditions and limits on each, and explain why neither ever reaches § 3604(c);
identify housing for older persons under § 3607(b) and distinguish its 62-and-over structure from its
55-and-over structure; identify the protected classes, prohibited practices, exemptions and
enforcement path of the Texas Fair Housing Act, Property Code ch. 301, and name the agency that
administers it; define steering, blockbusting and discriminatory advertising and identify the
provision each violates, including the licence-discipline ground in Occupations Code
§ 1101.652(b)(32); state what a "residential real estate-related transaction" is under 42 U.S.C.
§ 3605(b) and who § 3605(a) covers; state what 42 U.S.C. § 3606 reaches in the provision of brokerage
services and compare it with Property Code § 301.027; state what 42 U.S.C. § 3617 forbids and who it
protects; describe the administrative complaint route in 42 U.S.C. § 3610 — who may file, with whom,
within what period, and how conciliation and referral to a certified state or local agency work — and
set it beside the Texas Workforce Commission route in Property Code ch. 301; state the prohibited
bases and permitted inquiries under the Equal Credit Opportunity Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1691(a) and (b), and
explain the adverse-action notification owed under § 1691(d); apply the Regulation B rules that
implement ECOA — the definitions in 12 C.F.R. § 1002.2, the general prohibition in § 1002.4, the
limits on requests for information in § 1002.5, and the notification requirements and timing in
§ 1002.9; and explain the congressional findings and purpose of the Community Reinvestment Act,
12 U.S.C. § 2901, describe the assessment and written-evaluation framework in 12 U.S.C. §§ 2902, 2903
and 2906 including the four statutory rating levels, and distinguish that affirmative community-credit
obligation from an individual anti-discrimination claim.

**Every legal statement in this module comes from a source that was read for this course.** Where a
statement is practice rather than law, the module says so and cites nothing for it. On the federal
fair housing side the sources held and read are 42 U.S.C. §§ 3601, 3602, 3603, 3604, 3605, 3606, 3607,
3610 and 3617, and this module teaches each of them from its own text. On the credit side the sources
held and read are 15 U.S.C. §§ 1691, 1691a and 1691c together with four sections of Regulation B, the
Bureau's implementing regulation — 12 C.F.R. §§ 1002.2, 1002.4, 1002.5 and 1002.9. On the community
reinvestment side the sources held and read are 12 U.S.C. §§ 2901, 2902, 2903, 2904, 2905 and 2906.
A few provisions this topic can touch are still **not** among the sources held: the Fair Housing Act's
remaining enforcement sections (42 U.S.C. §§ 3611–3614, which carry the federal election of forum, the
administrative hearing, judicial relief and the Attorney General's pattern-or-practice action); ECOA's
rulemaking, state-law and civil-liability sections (15 U.S.C. §§ 1691b, 1691d, 1691e and 1691f); the
remaining sections of Regulation B; and any HUD rule, Texas Workforce Commission rule or Texas
municipal ordinance. Where the module reaches those subjects it says so plainly, teaches the parallel
provision that *was* read, and tells you to read the text yourself rather than
paraphrasing text nobody opened. Texas Property Code ch. 301 is taught throughout alongside the
federal Act, not as a substitute for it: a Texas license holder is governed by both, and the places
where the two texts align and the places where they do not are worth knowing precisely. Every name,
address and dollar figure in the illustrations is invented to make a rule visible.

## Unit: Equal Opportunity in Housing

Federal fair housing law begins with one sentence. 42 U.S.C. § 3601 says: "It is the policy of the
United States to provide, within constitutional limitations, for fair housing throughout the United
States." That is the whole section — an objective, with no prohibition, no penalty and no protected
class. Everything operative comes later, and a license holder who wants to know what is actually
forbidden must read § 3604. Still, the sentence tells you what kind of statute this is: it reaches
what private owners, landlords, brokers and lenders do with housing, and it does so nationally. A
rural Texas landlord with four units and a Houston management company with four thousand answer to
the same Act.

**Where the Act came from, and when each class arrived.** The dates explain why the statute's own
lists are ragged. The source credit under § 3601 is Pub. L. 90–284, title VIII, § 801, Apr. 11, 1968,
and the statutory notes record that Pub. L. 90–284 "may be cited as the 'Civil Rights Act of 1968,'"
and that its title VIII "may be cited as the 'Fair Housing Act.'" You will still hear practitioners
call fair housing matters "title VIII" cases. As enacted, § 3604 did not protect sex: the amendments
note under § 3604 records that in 1974 Pub. L. 93–383 "inserted ', sex' after 'religion' wherever
appearing in cls. (a) to (e)." The Fair Housing Amendments Act of 1988, Pub. L. 100–430, then did
three separate things, each recorded in that note — in subsections (a) and (b) it "inserted 'familial
status,' after 'sex,'"; in subsections (c) through (e) it "inserted 'handicap, familial status,' after
'sex,'"; and § 6(a) "added subsec. (f)," the entire disability regime, effective "on the 180th day
beginning after" September 13, 1988. The § 3601 notes also record that Pub. L. 104–76 (1995) "may be
cited as the 'Housing for Older Persons Act of 1995'" and amended § 3607.

So **race, color, religion and national origin** are original 1968 classes, **sex** was added in 1974,
and **familial status** and **handicap** were added in 1988 by insertion rather than by rewriting the
section. That is why the lists differ from subsection to subsection, and why a student who memorizes
"the seven protected classes" as one uniform list will misread the statute. The seven bases § 3604
names somewhere in its text are race, color, religion, sex, familial status, national origin and
handicap. The federal statute says **handicap**, not "disability"; Texas says disability. Use each
statute's own word when you cite it.

**The words the Act defines for itself.** Section 3602 supplies the definitions used throughout the
subchapter, and several of them decide cases before any prohibition is reached.

**"Dwelling"** (§ 3602(b)) means "any building, structure, or portion thereof which is occupied as, or
designed or intended for occupancy as, a residence by one or more families, and any vacant land which
is offered for sale or lease for the construction or location thereon of any such building, structure,
or portion thereof." Two consequences. A *portion* of a building is a dwelling, so a single rented room
counts. And **vacant land offered for sale or lease for residential construction is a dwelling** — a
raw lot listed for a house is inside the Act, which matters in a state with as much lot inventory as
Texas. Property Code § 301.003(8) defines dwelling to the same effect, land included.

**"Family"** (§ 3602(c)) "includes a single individual," matching Property Code § 301.003(9). So a
statute written in terms of "families" is not a statute about households with children; do not confuse
this definition with familial status.

**"Person"** (§ 3602(d)) "includes one or more individuals, corporations, partnerships, associations,
labor organizations, legal representatives, mutual companies, joint-stock companies, trusts,
unincorporated organizations, trustees, trustees in cases under title 11, receivers, and fiduciaries."
The list opens with *individuals*. That single word is why a sales agent is personally a "person" who
may not do what § 3604 forbids — the brokerage's liability is not a shield, and Property Code
§ 301.003(12) makes the identical point on the Texas side.

**"To rent"** (§ 3602(e)) "includes to lease, to sublease, to let and otherwise to grant for a
consideration the right to occupy premises not owned by the occupant," so a sublease is inside the Act.

**"Handicap"** (§ 3602(h)) means, "with respect to a person— (1) a physical or mental impairment which
substantially limits one or more of such person's major life activities, (2) a record of having such an
impairment, or (3) being regarded as having such an impairment," and "such term does not include
current, illegal use of or addiction to a controlled substance." Learn all three prongs. The third —
**regarded as** — means a landlord who acts on an impairment he wrongly believes the applicant has is
inside the definition, so being wrong about the facts is no defence. Texas Property Code § 301.003(6)
tracks the same three prongs and the same drug exclusion, then adds two exclusions the federal
definition does not contain, for sexual orientation and for a person who is a transvestite. On the
federal side the transvestism point is not in § 3602 at all; it appears in a statutory note printed
under the section, § 6(b)(3) of Pub. L. 100–430, which provides that "neither the term 'individual with
handicaps' nor the term 'handicap' shall apply to an individual solely because that individual is a
transvestite." Different placement, and worth noting when you are comparing the two texts.

**"Aggrieved person"** (§ 3602(i)) "includes any person who— (1) claims to have been injured by a
discriminatory housing practice; or (2) believes that such person will be injured by a discriminatory
housing practice that is about to occur." The second clause reaches an anticipated injury.

Finally, § 3602(f) defines a **"discriminatory housing practice"** as "an act that is unlawful under
section 3604, 3605, 3606, or 3617 of this title" — the phrase that carries the substantive prohibitions
into the enforcement machinery. All four of those sections were held and read for this course.
Sections 3604 and 3605 are taught in this unit and in unit 4. The other two are taught in unit 3, and
it is worth knowing now what they are, because the definition you just read makes each of them a
"discriminatory housing practice" on the same footing as § 3604. **Section 3606** forbids denying a
person access to or membership or participation in "any multiple-listing service, real estate brokers'
organization or other service, organization, or facility relating to the business of selling or
renting dwellings" — the section that reaches your own trade organizations and your own MLS.
**Section 3617** makes it unlawful "to coerce, intimidate, threaten, or interfere with any person" in
the exercise of, or on account of having exercised or having aided another in exercising, rights
granted or protected by §§ 3603, 3604, 3605 or 3606. Note what § 3602(f) does *not* list: § 3607, the
exemptions section, is not among the sections whose violation is a discriminatory housing practice,
because § 3607 creates no prohibition to violate. Familial status, § 3602(k), is treated later in this
unit alongside § 3607.

**Read the opening clause first, then read § 3603.** Section 3604 begins: "As made applicable by
section 3603 of this title and except as exempted by sections 3603(b) and 3607 of this title, it shall
be unlawful—". Both cross-referenced sections were read for this course, so we can follow the chain
rather than stopping at it. Section 3603(a) sets the reach. "Subject to the provisions of subsection
(b) and section 3607 of this title," the § 3604 prohibitions applied on enactment to four categories of
federally connected housing — dwellings "owned or operated by the Federal Government"; dwellings aided
by federal "loans, advances, grants, or contributions" under agreements entered into after November 20,
1962; dwellings "provided in whole or in part by loans insured, guaranteed, or otherwise secured by the
credit of the Federal Government" under such agreements; and dwellings provided by development or
redevelopment of property obtained from a public agency receiving federal slum-clearance or urban
renewal assistance. Then § 3603(a)(2): "After December 31, 1968, to all dwellings covered by paragraph
(1) and to all other dwellings except as exempted by subsection (b)."

That staged 1968–69 phase-in is spent history, and the only operative sentence today is the second one.
State it in the form you will use: **§ 3604 applies to all dwellings, except those § 3603(b) or § 3607
excepts.** The federally connected categories in § 3603(a)(1) are not a limit on the modern statute;
they are a record of which housing was covered first. The two exemptions are set out at the end of this
unit, and they are narrower than most people assume.

**§ 3604(a) — refusal to deal.** It is unlawful "[t]o refuse to sell or rent after the making of a
bona fide offer, or to refuse to negotiate for the sale or rental of, or otherwise make unavailable or
deny, a dwelling to any person because of race, color, religion, sex, familial status, or national
origin." Three wrongs: refusing after a bona fide offer, refusing to negotiate at all, and the
catch-all "otherwise make unavailable or deny." That catch-all is the workhorse — steering,
selectively applied screening criteria and never-returned calls all live there. *Illustration:* a Fort
Worth landlord tells one family the unit is spoken for and that afternoon accepts a materially
identical application from another. Nothing was said out loud about a protected class; if the reason
was one, the catch-all reaches it.

**§ 3604(b) — terms, conditions, services.** It is unlawful "[t]o discriminate against any person in
the terms, conditions, or privileges of sale or rental of a dwelling, or in the provision of services
or facilities in connection therewith," on the same six bases. Subsection (a) is about getting in the
door; (b) is about what happens after. *Illustration:* an El Paso community rents to all applicants
but charges one group a larger deposit, restricts their pool hours, and answers their maintenance
tickets in three weeks rather than three days.

**§ 3604(c) — advertising and statements.** It is unlawful "[t]o make, print, or publish, or cause to
be made, printed, or published any notice, statement, or advertisement, with respect to the sale or
rental of a dwelling that indicates any preference, limitation, or discrimination based on race,
color, religion, sex, handicap, familial status, or national origin, or an intention to make any such
preference, limitation, or discrimination." Here the list is seven bases — handicap is present. Note
the verbs (*make, print, publish,* or *cause to be*), which capture the agent who drafts copy and the
publisher who runs it; the objects ("any notice, statement, or advertisement," so a spoken remark at a
showing counts); and the test — the item must *indicate* a preference, limitation or discrimination,
or an intention to make one, and nobody need have been turned away. *Illustration:* a San Antonio
duplex advertised as "quiet building, perfect for a mature professional couple, not suited to
children" indicates a familial-status limitation on the face of the words.

**§ 3604(d) — false unavailability.** It is unlawful "[t]o represent to any person because of race,
color, religion, sex, handicap, familial status, or national origin that any dwelling is not available
for inspection, sale, or rental when such dwelling is in fact so available." This is the "sorry, it's
already leased" lie, given its own subsection because it is the usual way a refusal under (a) is
delivered.

**§ 3604(e) — blockbusting.** It is unlawful "[f]or profit, to induce or attempt to induce any person
to sell or rent any dwelling by representations regarding the entry or prospective entry into the
neighborhood of a person or persons of a particular race, color, religion, sex, handicap, familial
status, or national origin." Three elements: **for profit**; **induce or attempt to induce**, so the
attempt alone completes the violation; and **representations regarding entry or prospective entry**,
so a prediction counts. Unit 3 works this practice in detail.

**§ 3604(f) — handicap.** Paragraph (f)(1) makes it unlawful "[t]o discriminate in the sale or rental,
or to otherwise make unavailable or deny, a dwelling to any buyer or renter because of a handicap
of— (A) that buyer or renter, (B) a person residing in or intending to reside in that dwelling after
it is so sold, rented, or made available; or (C) any person associated with that buyer or renter."
Paragraph (f)(2) does the same for "the terms, conditions, or privileges of sale or rental" and "the
provision of services or facilities," with the same three-part reach. So (f)(1) and (f)(2) are the
disability analogues of (a) and (b) — which is precisely why handicap is absent from those lists.
Notice how far the protection runs: a Plano seller who rejects an offer because the buyer's adult son
who will live in the home uses a wheelchair, or because the buyer's caregiver has a disability, is
inside (f)(1) even though the applicant is not the person with the handicap.

Paragraph (f)(3) says "discrimination includes" three things. Two of them are the pair students
confuse constantly, so learn them as a contrast.

A **reasonable modification**, (f)(3)(A): "a refusal to permit, at the expense of the handicapped
person, reasonable modifications of existing premises occupied or to be occupied by such person if
such modifications may be necessary to afford such person full enjoyment of the premises except that,
in the case of a rental, the landlord may where it is reasonable to do so condition permission for a
modification on the renter agreeing to restore the interior of the premises to the condition that
existed before the modification, reasonable wear and tear excepted."

A **reasonable accommodation**, (f)(3)(B): "a refusal to make reasonable accommodations in rules,
policies, practices, or services, when such accommodations may be necessary to afford such person
equal opportunity to use and enjoy a dwelling."

Four differences fall out of the words. **Object:** a modification changes the *premises*; an
accommodation changes *rules, policies, practices, or services*. **Expense:** modifications are "at
the expense of the handicapped person"; (B) says nothing about expense, because changing a rule
ordinarily costs nothing. **Standard:** "full enjoyment of the premises" versus "equal opportunity to
use and enjoy a dwelling." **Restoration:** only (A) has a restoration proviso, and it is narrow — a
*rental*, a landlord acting "where it is reasonable to do so," "the interior" only, "reasonable wear
and tear excepted."

A common error is to assume the landlord must pay for a ramp because "fair housing requires it."
Under (f)(3)(A) a ramp is a modification of the premises and the statute puts the expense on the
person with the handicap; the landlord's duty is not to *refuse permission* unreasonably. The
mirror-image error is to assume a tenant may be charged for waiving a rule. Waiving a no-pets policy
for an assistance animal, assigning a closer parking space, or accepting rent from a third-party payer
are changes to rules, policies, practices or services, and (B) attaches no expense to them. Memorize
the contrast: **modification = premises, occupant's expense, possible restoration; accommodation =
rules, no expense clause, no restoration clause.**

The third item, (f)(3)(C), is the **design and construction requirement**. For "covered multifamily
dwellings for first occupancy after the date that is 30 months after September 13, 1988," it is
discrimination to fail to design and construct them so that public and common use portions are
"readily accessible to and usable by handicapped persons"; doors are "sufficiently wide to allow
passage by handicapped persons in wheelchairs"; and all premises contain four "features of adaptive
design" — an accessible route through the dwelling, environmental controls in accessible locations,
"reinforcements in bathroom walls to allow later installation of grab bars," and kitchens and
bathrooms an individual in a wheelchair "can maneuver about." Paragraph (f)(7) defines **covered
multifamily dwellings** as "(A) buildings consisting of 4 or more units if such buildings have one or
more elevators; and (B) ground floor units in other buildings consisting of 4 or more units" — so a
walk-up fourplex is covered as to its ground-floor units. Compliance with "ANSI A117.1" satisfies the
adaptive-design features (f)(4), and (f)(8) preserves state or local law requiring "greater access
than is required by this subchapter." Finally, (f)(9): "Nothing in this
subsection requires that a dwelling be made available to an individual whose tenancy would constitute
a direct threat to the health or safety of other individuals or whose tenancy would result in
substantial physical damage to the property of others" — a narrow, evidence-based provision about
*this* individual's tenancy, not a licence to act on assumptions about a category of people.

**Familial status, defined.** Section 3602(k) defines **familial status** as "one or more individuals
(who have not attained the age of 18 years) being domiciled with— (1) a parent or another person having
legal custody of such individual or individuals; or (2) the designee of such parent or other person
having such custody, with the written permission of such parent or other person." A closing sentence
extends it: "The protections afforded against discrimination on the basis of familial status shall
apply to any person who is pregnant or is in the process of securing legal custody of any individual
who has not attained the age of 18 years."

Read that carefully, because the class is narrower and stranger than the phrase "families with
children" suggests. The trigger is **domicile with a person under 18** — not marriage, not household
size, not being a parent in the abstract. A grandmother raising a ten-year-old is protected; so is a
designee caring for a child with the custodial parent's *written* permission. And the closing sentence
protects two people who are not yet domiciled with any minor at all: a pregnant applicant, and a person
"in the process of securing legal custody." Property Code § 301.004 states the same three routes —
pregnant, domiciled with an individual younger than 18 as parent or legal custodian or with that
person's written permission, or in the process of obtaining legal custody. Federal and Texas familial
status are the same class described in the same terms.

**Housing for older persons — § 3607(b).** This is where the familial-status class stops, and it is
the exemption a Texas license holder is most likely to meet in practice, because active-adult
communities are everywhere in this state. Section 3607(b)(1) does two things in two sentences: nothing
in the subchapter "limits the applicability of any reasonable local, State, or Federal restrictions
regarding the maximum number of occupants permitted to occupy a dwelling," and "[n]or does any
provision in this subchapter regarding familial status apply with respect to housing for older
persons."

Note the width of that second sentence, and then note its limit. It removes **the familial-status
provisions and nothing else**. Race, color, religion, sex, national origin and handicap all continue to
apply in full inside a seniors community. A 55-and-over community that qualifies under § 3607(b) may
lawfully decline to rent to a household with a twelve-year-old; it may not decline to rent to a
62-year-old applicant on the basis of national origin, and it owes the same § 3604(f)(3) accommodations
and modifications as any other housing provider. "It's senior housing" answers exactly one question.

Section 3607(b)(2) defines **housing for older persons** as housing in any of three alternatives:

- **(A) Program housing** — housing "provided under any State or Federal program that the Secretary
  determines is specifically designed and operated to assist elderly persons (as defined in the State
  or Federal program)." The Secretary is the Secretary of Housing and Urban Development, per
  § 3602(a). Note that qualification here runs through a determination by the Secretary, not through
  the operator's own view of its program.
- **(B) 62-and-over** — housing "intended for, and solely occupied by, persons 62 years of age or
  older."
- **(C) 55-and-over** — housing "intended and operated for occupancy by persons 55 years of age or
  older," *and* meeting three further requirements: "(i) at least 80 percent of the occupied units are
  occupied by at least one person who is 55 years of age or older"; "(ii) the housing facility or
  community publishes and adheres to policies and procedures that demonstrate the intent required
  under this subparagraph"; and "(iii) the housing facility or community complies with rules issued by
  the Secretary for verification of occupancy," which rules must "provide for verification by reliable
  surveys and affidavits" and "include examples of the types of policies and procedures relevant to a
  determination of compliance," with those surveys and affidavits "admissible in administrative and
  judicial proceedings for the purposes of such verification."

**Contrast (B) and (C) precisely; the difference is the whole point.** Sixty-two-and-over is a
*solely occupied* standard — every occupant meets the age, and the statute adds no conditions beyond
intent and sole occupancy. Fifty-five-and-over is a *threshold* standard with paperwork: only one
person aged 55 or older is needed in each qualifying unit, only 80 percent of the **occupied** units
must qualify (unoccupied units are excluded from the denominator by the word "occupied"), and the
community must additionally publish and adhere to age policies and comply with the Secretary's
occupancy-verification rules. A community that calls itself "55+" in its marketing, has never published
an age policy and cannot document its 80 percent is not, on this text, housing for older persons — and
every familial-status exclusion it has made is unprotected.

Two savings provisions follow, and both are practical. Under § 3607(b)(3), housing "shall not fail to
meet the requirements for housing for older persons" by reason of persons who were residing there as of
September 13, 1988 and do not meet the (B) or (C) ages, "Provided, That new occupants of such housing
meet the age requirements," or by reason of "unoccupied units: Provided, That such units are reserved
for occupancy by persons who meet the age requirements." So legacy residents and vacant units do not
break the exemption.

Under § 3607(b)(5)(A), "[a] person shall not be held personally liable for monetary damages for a
violation of this subchapter if such person reasonably relied, in good faith, on the application of the
exemption under this subsection relating to housing for older persons." Subparagraph (B) then says how
that reliance may be shown — and the word to underline is *only*: "a person may only show good faith
reliance… by showing that— (i) such person has no actual knowledge that the facility or community is
not, or will not be, eligible for such exemption; and (ii) the facility or community has stated
formally, in writing, that the facility or community complies with the requirements for such
exemption." Both elements, and the second is documentary. This provision is aimed squarely at the
agent, manager or advertiser who takes a community's word for its status: **get the written statement
of compliance, or the good-faith defence is unavailable on the statute's own terms.** That is a
statutory point, not a practice tip.

Section 3607(b)(4) rounds out the section: "Nothing in this subchapter prohibits conduct against a
person because such person has been convicted by any court of competent jurisdiction of the illegal
manufacture or distribution of a controlled substance as defined in section 802 of title 21." Note how
narrow it is — *manufacture or distribution*, convictions only, and only of a controlled substance. It
is not a general permission to screen on criminal history.

**Where Texas lands.** Property Code § 301.043 exempts from the Texas familial-status provisions the
same three categories: housing "intended for, and solely occupied by, individuals 62 years of age or
older"; housing "intended and operated for occupancy by at least one individual 55 years of age or
older for each unit as determined by commission rules"; and housing the commission determines is
designed and operated to assist elderly individuals under a federal or state program. The structures
match, with one drafting difference worth knowing: **Texas routes the 55-and-over detail through
commission rules, while the federal 80 percent figure sits in the statute itself** at
§ 3607(b)(2)(C)(i). No Texas Workforce Commission rule was held for this course, so this module states
the federal percentage from § 3607 and does not state what the Texas rules require. Property Code
§ 301.062 requires the commission's substantive rules to impose obligations "the same as are provided
in federal fair housing regulations," which tells you the direction Texas points, but a rule you have
not read is not a rule you can quote to a client.

**The § 3603(b) exemptions, and the one thing they never cover.** Start with the opening words of
§ 3603(b), because everything turns on a five-word parenthesis: "**Nothing in section 3604 of this
title (other than subsection (c)) shall apply to**—". Read it twice. Section 3603(b) exempts qualifying
transactions from all of § 3604 *except subsection (c)*, and subsection (c) is the advertising and
statements prohibition. So a fully exempt owner may still refuse to sell for a discriminatory reason
without violating § 3604(a) — and may **not** make, print or publish a notice, statement or
advertisement indicating a preference, limitation or discrimination, or an intention to make one.
**There is no exemption from federal fair housing advertising law.** That is the single most useful
sentence in § 3603, it comes from the section's own first line, and it is the answer to the client who
says "I'm exempt, so I can put whatever I want in the ad."

Two transactions are exempted.

**(1) A single-family house sold or rented by its owner** — § 3603(b)(1) exempts "any single-family
house sold or rented by an owner," subject to four provisos, each of which narrows it:

- **The three-house ceiling.** The exemption applies "Provided, That such private individual owner does
  not own more than three such single-family houses at any one time."
- **The anti-evasion ceiling.** It applies further only if "such bona fide private individual owner does
  not own any interest in, nor is there owned or reserved on his behalf, under any express or voluntary
  agreement, title to or any right to all or a portion of the proceeds from the sale or rental of, more
  than three such single-family houses at any one time." This closes the obvious dodge of holding the
  fourth and fifth houses through an entity or a proceeds arrangement.
- **The one-sale-in-24-months limit for an absent owner.** "[I]n the case of the sale of any such
  single-family house by a private individual owner not residing in such house at the time of such sale
  or who was not the most recent resident of such house prior to such sale, the exemption granted by
  this subsection shall apply only with respect to one such sale within any twenty-four month period."
  Note who this catches: the owner who did not live in the house. An owner selling the home she has
  been living in is outside this limit.
- **The no-broker, no-advertisement condition.** "[A]fter December 31, 1969, the sale or rental of any
  such single-family house shall be excepted from the application of this subchapter only if such house
  is sold or rented (A) without the use in any manner of the sales or rental facilities or the sales or
  rental services of any real estate broker, agent, or salesman, or of such facilities or services of
  any person in the business of selling or renting dwellings, or of any employee or agent of any such
  broker, agent, salesman, or person and (B) without the publication, posting or mailing, after notice,
  of any advertisement or written notice in violation of section 3604(c) of this title." A closing
  clause preserves ordinary closing help: "nothing in this proviso shall prohibit the use of attorneys,
  escrow agents, abstractors, title companies, and other such professional assistance as necessary to
  perfect or transfer the title."

Clause (A) is the one that matters to you, and it is why this whole exemption is of limited practical
interest to a license holder. **The moment the owner uses your services, the exemption is gone.** The
statute does not say "unless the broker discriminates"; it says the sale is excepted *only if* it is
made without the use "in any manner" of a broker's, agent's or salesman's facilities or services. And
clause (B) makes the advertising point a second time, from the other direction: a § 3604(c) violation
does not merely go unexcused, it destroys the exemption for the transaction as a whole.

**(2) Rooms or units in an owner-occupied dwelling** — § 3603(b)(2) exempts "rooms or units in
dwellings containing living quarters occupied or intended to be occupied by no more than four families
living independently of each other, if the owner actually maintains and occupies one of such living
quarters as his residence." Four elements: living quarters for **no more than four** families; the
families living **independently of each other**; the **owner** occupying one of them; and occupying it
as the owner's **residence**, which the statute reinforces with "actually maintains and occupies." An
owner living in one unit of a fourplex and renting the other three is inside it. An owner who lives
elsewhere and rents all four is not. You will hear this called the "Mrs. Murphy" exemption in the
industry; the statute does not use that name and this module cites nothing for it.

**Who is "in the business of selling or renting dwellings."** Section 3603(b)(1)(A) denies the
exemption to an owner who uses the facilities or services of "any person in the business of selling or
renting dwellings," and § 3603(c) defines that phrase for the subsection. A person is in the business
if "(1) he has, within the preceding twelve months, participated as principal in three or more
transactions involving the sale or rental of any dwelling or any interest therein"; or "(2) he has,
within the preceding twelve months, participated as agent, other than in the sale of his own personal
residence in providing sales or rental facilities or sales or rental services in two or more
transactions involving the sale or rental of any dwelling or any interest therein"; or "(3) he is the
owner of any dwelling designed or intended for occupancy by, or occupied by, five or more families."
Three transactions as principal, two as agent, or a five-family building — thresholds low enough that
almost anyone working in this field clears them.

**How Texas does the same job.** Property Code § 301.041 was read in full and is built on the same
plan, which is worth seeing side by side. Texas exempts qualifying owner sales and rentals from
**§§ 301.021, 301.023, 301.024 and 301.025** — and conspicuously *not* from § 301.022, the advertising
prohibition. Where the federal Act makes that point with a parenthesis ("other than subsection (c)"),
Texas makes it by listing the sections the exemption reaches and leaving the advertising section off
the list. Both then repeat the point as a condition: § 301.041(a)(1)(B)(ii) requires the house to be
sold or rented without "the publication, posting, or mailing of a notice, statement, or advertisement
prohibited by Section 301.022," just as § 3603(b)(1)(B) requires it to be sold without an advertisement
"in violation of section 3604(c)." Texas likewise removes the exemption where the owner uses "the sales
or rental facilities or services of a broker, agent, or salesperson licensed under Chapter 1101,
Occupations Code" (§ 301.041(a)(1)(B)(i)) — the state version of the federal no-broker condition,
pinned to a Texas licence. Texas caps ownership at "more than three single-family houses at any one
time," and § 301.041(b) limits the exemption to "one sale in a 24-month period if the owner was not the
most recent resident of the house at the time of the sale." The Texas owner-occupied exemption uses the
same "not more than four families living independently of each other" formula and requires that "the
owner maintains and occupies one of the living quarters as the owner's residence."

So on both texts the rule for a license holder is identical, and it has two halves. **An exempt owner
still may not advertise a discriminatory preference, and advertising one costs the exemption itself.
And the moment you are engaged, the owner is not exempt — and you were never exempt.** Neither § 3603(b)
nor § 301.041 exempts brokers, agents or salespersons at all; they exempt certain owners, on conditions
that your involvement defeats.

**The other federal exemption: § 3607(a).** Alongside housing for older persons, § 3607(a) exempts two
kinds of provider. First, nothing in the subchapter prohibits "a religious organization, association,
or society, or any nonprofit institution or organization operated, supervised or controlled by or in
conjunction with a religious organization, association, or society, from limiting the sale, rental or
occupancy of dwellings which it owns or operates for other than a commercial purpose to persons of the
same religion, or from giving preference to such persons, unless membership in such religion is
restricted on account of race, color, or national origin." Three limits are built in: the dwellings must
be **owned or operated by** the organization, operated "**for other than a commercial purpose**," and
the preference collapses if religious membership is itself "restricted on account of race, color, or
national origin" — a proviso that stops the exemption being used as a proxy for a racial one. Second,
nothing prohibits "a private club not in fact open to the public, which as an incident to its primary
purpose or purposes provides lodgings which it owns or operates for other than a commercial purpose,
from limiting the rental or occupancy of such lodgings to its members or from giving preference to its
members." Read "not **in fact** open to the public" and "as an **incident** to its primary purpose" as
the working limits: a club whose primary business is lodging, or whose exclusivity is nominal, is not
described. Property Code § 301.042 mirrors both exemptions in nearly the same words, subject to a 2025
carve-back at § 301.042(a-1); unit 2 takes up the Texas version.

## Unit: Texas Fair Housing Laws

Texas has its own fair housing statute, and a license holder practising here answers under it as well
as under the federal Act. It is Property Code ch. 301, and § 301.001 names it: "This chapter may be
cited as the Texas Fair Housing Act."

**Purpose, and who administers it.** Section 301.002 states three purposes: "(1) provide for fair
housing practices in this state; (2) create a procedure for investigating and settling complaints of
discriminatory housing practices; and (3) provide rights and remedies substantially equivalent to
those granted under federal law." The third is the key to the chapter: Texas did not build a different
code, it built a state forum mirroring the federal one. Section 301.062 carries that into rulemaking —
substantive rules "shall impose obligations, rights, and remedies that are the same as are provided in
federal fair housing regulations." Section 301.0015 supplies the administering agency: the powers of
the former Commission on Human Rights "are transferred to the Texas Workforce Commission," and "[a]
reference in this chapter to the 'commission' means the Texas Workforce Commission." Every
"commission" in ch. 301 is the **Texas Workforce Commission** — not TREC, whose fair housing role is
licence discipline under Occupations Code ch. 1101, taken up in unit 3.

**The classes ch. 301 lists.** Reading the prohibition sections of this chapter — and of this chapter
only — the bases are **race, color, religion,
sex, disability, familial status and national origin**. Texas says **disability** where the federal Act
says handicap, and § 301.003(6) defines it as "a mental or physical impairment that substantially
limits at least one major life activity, a record of the impairment, or being regarded as having the
impairment," expressly excluding "current illegal use or addiction to any drug or illegal or federally
controlled substance" and providing that the term "does not apply to an individual because of an
individual's sexual orientation or because that individual is a transvestite." As in the federal Act,
the classes are distributed across sections: § 301.021, on sale and rental, omits disability because
§ 301.025 is the disability section. Do not read that omission as permission.

**Does the statewide Act add classes?** Say this one precisely, because the loose version of it is
wrong in a way that can cost a client. **The prohibition sections of the current statewide Texas Fair
Housing Act, Property Code ch. 301, as read for this course, name the same seven bases the federal Act
names — race, color, religion, sex, disability, familial status and national origin — and name no
eighth.** Chapter 301 does not list sexual orientation, gender identity, marital status, age, source
of income, or military or veteran status, and § 301.005 forecloses reading them into the chapter's own
remedies: "The statutory civil remedies or theories of recovery created by this chapter may not be
expanded beyond their express statutory terms." So do not tell a client that **ch. 301** protects a
class ch. 301 does not name.

That statement is about one statute and nothing else. It is **not** a statement that a Texas renter,
buyer or applicant enjoys no protection beyond those seven. A Texas municipality or county may adopt a
fair housing or nondiscrimination ordinance of its own, and some do; other state and federal law may
also apply to the same conduct, and § 301.044(b) says so expressly — the chapter "does not affect a
requirement of nondiscrimination in any other state or federal law." **No Texas municipal or county
ordinance was held for this course, so this module surveys none, names none, and takes no position on
what any of them cover.** The absence of an ordinance from these pages is a gap in this course's
sources, not a finding that the ordinance does not exist or does not reach further than ch. 301. The
only safe practice is the one unit 2 states again below: read the ordinance of the city and county you
actually work in before you advertise, screen or advise.

Inside ch. 301 itself, one class is **broadened by definition**, and the difference between broadening
a class and adding one is the whole point. Section 301.0045, added in 2023, is captioned "RACIAL
DISCRIMINATION BASED ON HAIR TEXTURE OR PROTECTIVE HAIRSTYLE." Subsection (a) provides that in that
section "'protective hairstyle' includes braids, locks, and twists." Subsection (b) then provides that
"[a] provision in this chapter, other than a provision in Subchapter I, referring to discrimination
because of race or on the basis of race includes discrimination because of or on the basis of a
person's hair texture or protective hairstyle commonly or historically associated with race." Read the
operative verb: the section says race **includes** these things. It does not create a new protected
category, it does not amend the list in § 301.021 or § 301.022, and it is not an eighth statewide
protected class — it tells you what the existing category of race already reaches. So a Dallas
landlord who turns an applicant away over locs has discriminated **because of race** under ch. 301,
and the claim is a race claim. Note the carve-out too: § 301.0045(b) reaches every provision of the
chapter *other than* a provision in Subchapter I, which is the criminal penalty subchapter containing
§ 301.171. Section 301.004 likewise fills out **familial status**: the act must be committed
because the person is "(1) pregnant; (2) domiciled with an individual younger than 18 years of age" as
parent or legal custodian or with that person's written permission, "or (3) in the process of
obtaining legal custody" of such an individual. Pregnancy is expressly inside the class. And
§ 301.003(8) defines **dwelling** to include "vacant land that is offered for sale or lease for the
construction or location of" a residence, so residential lots are inside the Act.

**Prohibited practices.** Subchapter B tracks § 3604. Section 301.021(a) prohibits refusing to sell or
rent after a bona fide offer, refusing to negotiate, or "in any other manner mak[ing] unavailable or
deny[ing] a dwelling"; § 301.021(b) covers terms, conditions, privileges, services and facilities; and
§ 301.021(c) provides that the section "does not prohibit discrimination against a person because the
person has been convicted under federal law or the law of any state of the illegal manufacture or
distribution of a controlled substance." That one does have a federal counterpart, though Texas puts it
in a different place: the same rule appears federally at 42 U.S.C. § 3607(b)(4), which provides that
"[n]othing in this subchapter prohibits conduct against a person because such person has been convicted
by any court of competent jurisdiction of the illegal manufacture or distribution of a controlled
substance." Texas locates it inside the sale-and-rental prohibition; Congress locates it in the
exemptions section, where it reaches the whole subchapter. Both are equally narrow in substance —
manufacture or distribution, by conviction, of a controlled substance — and neither is a general
licence to screen on criminal history. Section 301.022 is the advertising prohibition, § 301.023 the false-unavailability
prohibition, § 301.024 the blockbusting prohibition, and § 301.025 the disability section — including
at § 301.025(c) the same three-part definition of discrimination: refusing to permit a reasonable
modification "at the expense of the person having a disability," refusing to make a reasonable
accommodation "in rules, policies, practices, or services," and failing to meet the design and
construction requirements, which by § 301.025(e) do "not apply to a building the first occupancy of
which occurred on or before March 13, 1991."

Two sections reach past sale and rental. Section 301.026 covers the **residential real estate related
transaction**, defined to include "the making or purchasing of loans or the provision of other
financial assistance… to purchase, construct, improve, repair, or maintain a dwelling" or "to secure
residential real estate," and "the selling, brokering, or appraising of residential real property."
Read that twice: **brokering and appraising are named in it**, so a license holder's own services are
a residential real estate related transaction. This is one of the closest alignments in the two
statutes: the federal counterpart, 42 U.S.C. § 3605, defines "residential real estate-related
transaction" in materially the same terms and is taken up in unit 4. Section 301.027 covers **brokerage
services** — no one may deny another access to or participation in "a multiple-listing service, real
estate brokers' organization, or other service, organization, or facility relating to the business of
selling or renting dwellings," or discriminate in the terms of that access, on the seven bases. The
federal Act has a brokerage-services section of its own at 42 U.S.C. § 3606, and it was held and read
for this course; the two texts are close enough to set side by side. Section 3606 provides: "After
December 31, 1968, it shall be unlawful to deny any person access to or membership or participation in
any multiple-listing service, real estate brokers' organization or other service, organization, or
facility relating to the business of selling or renting dwellings, or to discriminate against him in
the terms or conditions of such access, membership, or participation, on account of race, color,
religion, sex, handicap, familial status, or national origin." Section 301.027 uses the same list of
covered bodies — "a multiple-listing service, real estate brokers' organization, or other service,
organization, or facility relating to the business of selling or renting dwellings" — and the same
two-stage prohibition of denying access and of discriminating "in the terms or conditions of access,
membership, or participation." Three differences and no more: Texas says **disability** where § 3606
says handicap; Texas opens with "A person may not," where § 3606 opens with the spent 1968 commencement
date; and Texas omits the federal pronoun "him." Unit 3 works § 3606 as a practice.

**Exemptions.** Section 301.041(a) exempts from §§ 301.021, 301.023, 301.024 and 301.025 the sale or
rental of a single-family house by an owner who does not own or hold an interest in "more than three
single-family houses at any one time," provided it is sold or rented without a licensed broker, agent
or salesperson and without advertising prohibited by § 301.022; and the sale or rental of rooms or
units in a dwelling for "not more than four families living independently of each other, if the owner
maintains and occupies one of the living quarters as the owner's residence." Subsection (b) limits the
single-family exemption to "one sale in a 24-month period if the owner was not the most recent
resident of the house at the time of the sale." Section 301.042 exempts religious organizations and
affiliated nonprofits preferring persons of the same religion in dwellings they own or operate "for
other than a commercial purpose," "unless membership in the religion is restricted because of race,
color, or national origin," subject to a 2025 carve-back at § 301.042(a-1), and exempts a private club
not open to the public providing lodging incident to its primary purpose. Section 301.043 exempts from
the familial-status provisions housing "intended for, and solely occupied by, individuals 62 years of
age or older," housing "intended and operated for occupancy by at least one individual 55 years of age
or older for each unit as determined by commission rules," and housing the commission determines is
designed and operated to assist elderly individuals under a federal or state program.

**Local law.** The topic's scope note names local fair housing laws, and ch. 301 acknowledges them
without cataloguing them: § 301.068 lets the commission "defer proceedings under this chapter and
refer a complaint to a municipality that has been certified by the federal Department of Housing and
Urban Development as a substantially equivalent fair housing agency," and § 301.044(b) provides that
the chapter "does not affect a requirement of nondiscrimination in any other state or federal law."
The federal Act builds in the same referral structure from the other end: 42 U.S.C. § 3610(f), read
below, requires the Secretary to refer a complaint to a state or local agency certified under that
subsection before taking any action on it, and certification requires the Secretary to find that the
agency's substantive rights, procedures, remedies and judicial review are "substantially equivalent to
those created by and under this subchapter." Whether a particular Texas city or county ordinance adds
bases beyond the seven that ch. 301 names is a question of that ordinance, and it is a live question:
a local ordinance may protect people ch. 301 does not, and nothing in this module should be read to
suggest otherwise. **No municipal or county ordinance was held for this course, so this module names
none and describes none.** Before you advertise, screen or advise in a specific Texas city, read that
city's ordinance and your county's — and do not answer the question from this module, which does not
contain the answer.

**The Texas enforcement path.** Under § 301.081 a complaint must be "in writing," "under oath," and
"in the form prescribed by the commission," and filed "on or before the first anniversary of the date
the alleged discriminatory housing practice occurs or terminates, whichever is later"; an aggrieved
person may file, and so may the commission. Each respondent is served with notice and a copy within 20
days and may answer, in writing and under oath, within 10 days (§ 301.082). Section 301.083(b)
requires the investigation to be completed "not later than the 100th day after the date the complaint
is filed" or, failing that, all related administrative proceedings disposed of within one year.
Throughout, § 301.085 requires the commission "to the extent feasible" to engage in **conciliation**,
and conciliation statements may not be made public or used as evidence without written consent.
Section 301.088 requires a **reasonable cause determination**, also targeted at 100 days: if cause is
found the commission "shall… immediately issue a charge on behalf of the aggrieved person"; if not, it
"shall promptly dismiss the complaint" (§ 301.091). Where the matter involves the legality of a zoning
or other land use law, § 301.090 bars a charge and requires referral to the attorney general.

Then comes the fork. Under § 301.093 a complainant, a respondent, or an aggrieved person "may elect to
have the claims asserted in the charge decided in a civil action," and must elect "not later than the
20th day after" service of the charge. **If no timely election is made**, § 301.111 provides an
administrative hearing governed by Government Code ch. 2001, where the commission may order "actual
damages, reasonable attorney fees, court costs, and other injunctive or equitable relief" and, under
§ 301.112(b), assess a civil penalty "[t]o vindicate the public's interest" capped at $10,000 where
the respondent has been found by commission or court order "to have committed a prior discriminatory
housing practice," $25,000 for one other such practice "during the five-year period ending on the date
of the filing of the charges," and $50,000 for two or more "during the seven-year period ending on the
date of filing of the charge." **If a timely election is made**, § 301.131 directs the attorney
general within 30 days to file a civil action in district court on the aggrieved person's behalf, with
venue where the practice occurred. Separately, § 301.132 lets the attorney general bring a **pattern
or practice** action with penalties capped at "$50,000 for a first violation" and "$100,000 for a
second or subsequent violation," and § 301.151 preserves a **private civil action** in district court
"not later than the second year after the date of the occurrence or the termination of an alleged
discriminatory housing practice or the breach of a conciliation agreement… whichever occurs last,"
available regardless of whether a complaint was filed — though barred by § 301.151(d) where the
commission obtained a conciliation agreement with the aggrieved person's consent, and by § 301.151(e)
once the commission has begun a hearing on the record. Section 301.153 allows "actual and punitive
damages," attorney fees, costs and injunctive relief; § 301.156 allows attorney fees to the prevailing
party in either forum; and § 301.171 makes it a **Class A misdemeanor** to intimidate or interfere
with a person by force or threat of force because of a protected class and because that person is
buying, selling, renting, financing or occupying a dwelling.

**How the Texas and federal options relate.** Chapter 301 is substantially equivalent, not exclusive.
Section 301.044(b) preserves other law; § 301.083(a) contemplates the federal government referring a
complaint to the commission or deferring jurisdiction to it; § 301.068 contemplates referral to a
HUD-certified municipal agency; and §§ 301.092 and 301.111(c) both stop the state administrative track
once a civil action on the same practice, "under federal or state law," reaches trial. These are
parallel forums coordinated to avoid duplication, with the complainant holding a real choice at the
§ 301.093 election.

**The federal administrative route — 42 U.S.C. § 3610.** The federal counterpart to the ch. 301
complaint path was held and read for this course, so the comparison can be made from both texts rather
than from one. Section 3610 is titled "Administrative enforcement; preliminary matters," and it is the
front half of the federal machinery: it takes a complaint from filing through investigation and
conciliation to a reasonable cause determination and a charge.

*Who may file, with whom, and when.* Section 3610(a)(1)(A)(i): "An aggrieved person may, not later
than one year after an alleged discriminatory housing practice has occurred or terminated, file a
complaint with the Secretary alleging such discriminatory housing practice. The Secretary, on the
Secretary's own initiative, may also file such a complaint." The Secretary is the Secretary of Housing
and Urban Development (§ 3602(a)); "aggrieved person" is the § 3602(i) definition you read in unit 1,
which reaches a person who believes an injury "is about to occur." Clause (iii) adds that the
Secretary "may also investigate housing practices to determine whether a complaint should be brought."
Complaints "shall be in writing and shall contain such information and be in such form as the
Secretary requires" (clause (ii)), and under § 3610(a)(1)(D) both "[c]omplaints and answers shall be
under oath or affirmation, and may be reasonably and fairly amended at any time." **One year, in
writing, sworn** — put that beside § 301.081's one-year, written, sworn complaint to the Texas
Workforce Commission and the two front doors look almost identical, which is what "substantially
equivalent" in § 301.002(3) was built to produce.

*What happens next, and how fast.* Under § 3610(a)(1)(B) the Secretary must serve notice on the
aggrieved person "acknowledging such filing and advising the aggrieved person of the time limits and
choice of forums provided under this subchapter"; must serve the respondent "not later than 10 days
after such filing" with a notice identifying the alleged practice, advising of the respondent's
procedural rights and obligations, and enclosing a copy of the complaint; each respondent "may file,
not later than 10 days after receipt of notice," an answer; and the Secretary must investigate and
"complete such investigation within 100 days after the filing of the complaint… unless it is
impracticable to do so," with written notice of the reasons to both parties if that deadline is missed
(§ 3610(a)(1)(C)). Under § 3610(a)(2) a person not named in the complaint but identified during the
investigation may be joined as an additional or substitute respondent on written notice explaining the
basis for the joinder. Compare ch. 301: service on respondents within 20 days and a 10-day answer
(§ 301.082), and the same 100-day investigation target (§ 301.083(b)).

*Conciliation.* Section 3610(b)(1): "During the period beginning with the filing of such complaint and
ending with the filing of a charge or a dismissal by the Secretary, the Secretary shall, to the extent
feasible, engage in conciliation with respect to such complaint." Learn the structure, because it is
not quite the Texas one. A conciliation agreement "shall be an agreement between the respondent and
the complainant, and shall be subject to approval by the Secretary" (b)(2); it "may provide for binding
arbitration of the dispute," and such arbitration "may award appropriate relief, including monetary
relief" (b)(3); and — the point where federal and Texas practice diverge — "[e]ach conciliation
agreement shall be made public unless the complainant and respondent otherwise agree and the Secretary
determines that disclosure is not required to further the purposes of this subchapter" (b)(4). The
*agreement* is presumptively public. The *negotiation* is not: § 3610(d)(1) provides that "[n]othing
said or done in the course of conciliation under this subchapter may be made public or used as
evidence in a subsequent proceeding under this subchapter without the written consent of the persons
concerned," which is § 301.085's rule in almost the same words. Section 3610(b)(5) requires a final
investigative report at the end of each investigation, listing the five contents the paragraph names,
and § 3610(d)(2) makes that report and the information derived from the investigation available to
both the aggrieved person and the respondent on request once the investigation is complete. If the
Secretary has reasonable cause to believe a respondent has breached a conciliation agreement,
§ 3610(c) requires referral to the Attorney General with a recommendation that a civil action be filed
under § 3614 to enforce it.

*Referral to a certified state or local agency — where the Texas Workforce Commission comes in.* This
is the subsection that makes the two systems one system. Under § 3610(f)(1), whenever a complaint
alleges a practice "within the jurisdiction of a State or local public agency" that "has been certified
by the Secretary under this subsection," the Secretary "shall refer such complaint to that certified
agency before taking any action with respect to such complaint." After referral, and except with the
certified agency's consent, the Secretary "shall take no further action" unless the agency fails to
commence proceedings "before the end of the 30th day after the date of such referral," fails to carry
proceedings forward "with reasonable promptness," or no longer qualifies for certification
(§ 3610(f)(2)). Certification itself is available only where the Secretary determines that the agency's
substantive rights, its procedures, its remedies and the availability of judicial review of its action
"are substantially equivalent to those created by and under this subchapter" (§ 3610(f)(3)(A)), taking
into account the agency's "current practices and past performance"; and the Secretary must revisit each
certification "[n]ot less frequently than every 5 years" (§ 3610(f)(5)). Now read Property Code
§ 301.002(3) again — "rights and remedies substantially equivalent to those granted under federal law"
— and § 301.083(a), which contemplates the federal government referring a complaint to the commission
or deferring jurisdiction to it. The Texas statute is written to the federal certification standard,
and § 3610(f) is the reason it is.

*Cause, charge, and the end of § 3610.* Section 3610(g)(1) requires the Secretary, "within 100 days
after the filing of the complaint," to determine "whether reasonable cause exists to believe that a
discriminatory housing practice has occurred or is about to occur," unless that is impracticable or a
conciliation agreement has been approved, again with written notice of the reasons for any delay. On a
finding of cause the Secretary "shall… immediately issue a charge on behalf of the aggrieved person"
(g)(2)(A); the charge is a "short and plain statement of the facts," must be "based on the final
investigative report," and "need not be limited to the facts or grounds alleged in the complaint"
(g)(2)(B). Where the matter "involves the legality of any State or local zoning or other land use law
or ordinance," the Secretary refers it to the Attorney General instead of charging (g)(2)(C). On a
finding of no cause the Secretary "shall promptly dismiss the complaint" and "shall make public
disclosure of each such dismissal" (g)(3). And § 3610(g)(4) bars a charge "after the beginning of the
trial of a civil action commenced by the aggrieved party under an Act of Congress or a State law,
seeking relief with respect to that discriminatory housing practice." Every one of those five moves has
a ch. 301 twin: § 301.088 (cause and immediate charge), § 301.090 (land use referred to the attorney
general), § 301.091 (prompt dismissal, publicly disclosed) and § 301.092 (no charge after trial has
begun). Finally, § 3610(h) requires the Secretary, after issuing a charge, to serve a copy on each
respondent and each aggrieved person "together with information as to how to make an election under
section 3612(a) of this title and the effect of such an election," with a notice of opportunity for a
hearing unless the election is made.

**What is still not held, and what that means for the exercise below.** Section 3610 ends at the
charge. The federal election of forum itself lives in § 3612, and the federal administrative hearing,
the private civil action and the Attorney General's pattern-or-practice suit live in §§ 3612–3614.
**None of those sections was held for this course, and this module states no federal election deadline,
no federal damages or penalty figure, and no federal limitations period for a civil action.** What
§ 3610 alone establishes is real and usable: the one-year sworn complaint to the Secretary, the 10-day
service and answer, the 100-day investigation and 100-day cause determination, conciliation running
from filing to charge or dismissal, mandatory referral to a certified substantially equivalent state or
local agency, and the fact that an election of forum exists and must be explained to the parties both
at filing (§ 3610(a)(1)(B)(i), "the time limits and choice of forums") and at the charge (§ 3610(h)).
A client weighing the federal election against the § 301.093 election still needs a lawyer working from
§ 3612 and the current federal text; this course can put the front half of the federal route beside the
Texas route accurately, and stops where its sources stop.

## Unit: Housing Discrimination

The last two units set out the statutes. This unit names the practices in the vocabulary the industry
actually uses, then shows where a Texas license holder's personal exposure sits.

**Refusal to deal.** The plainest violation: refusing to sell or rent after a bona fide offer,
refusing to negotiate, or otherwise making a dwelling unavailable because of a protected class —
42 U.S.C. § 3604(a); Property Code § 301.021(a). The catch-all matters more than the flat refusal,
because outright refusals are rare and functional ones are not: screening criteria applied to one
applicant and waived for another, a "we're not showing it this weekend" that becomes a showing for the
next caller, a listing agent who forwards five offers to the seller and sets one aside.

**Discriminatory terms and conditions.** Section 3604(b) and § 301.021(b): different deposits, fees,
lease terms, rules, maintenance response or amenity access. A Texas property manager who imposes a
larger deposit on families with children has committed a familial-status violation in the terms of the
rental even though nobody was turned away.

**Discriminatory advertising.** Section 3604(c) and § 301.022. Recall the four features: notices,
statements and advertisements are covered; making, printing, publishing and *causing* those are
covered; the test is whether the item *indicates* a preference, limitation or discrimination or an
intention to make one; and on the Texas text the exemption in § 301.041 does not reach § 301.022 at
all, while a discriminatory advertisement destroys an otherwise-available exemption under
§ 301.041(a)(1)(B)(ii). Advertising failures are the easiest to prove, because the evidence is a
document with your name on it. As a matter of practice and not of statute, the safe habit is to
describe the **property** and never the **people**: square footage, finishes, schools by name and
distance, and no adjectives about who would fit in.

**Steering.** Steering is channelling a prospect toward or away from particular areas, buildings or
neighborhoods on the basis of a protected class, whether or not any property is ever refused. Two
points about where it lives in the law. First, the federal Act does not use the word: steering is
unlawful under § 3604(a) because it "otherwise make[s] unavailable or den[ies]" a dwelling, and under
§ 3604(b) and (d) when it takes the form of differential service or a false statement of availability;
in Texas it violates § 301.021 the same way. Second — and this belongs on the wall of every Texas
brokerage — **Texas licence law names it expressly.** Occupations Code § 1101.652(b)(32) authorizes
TREC to suspend or revoke a licence or take other disciplinary action if a license holder, while
engaged in real estate brokerage, "discriminates against an owner, potential buyer, landlord, or
potential tenant on the basis of race, color, religion, sex, disability, familial status, national
origin, or ancestry, including directing a prospective buyer or tenant interested in equivalent
properties to a different area based on the race, color, religion, sex, disability, familial status,
national origin, or ancestry of the potential owner or tenant."

Three details in that subdivision do real work. It lists **ancestry** in addition to the seven fair
housing bases — a basis that appears in the licence statute and not in ch. 301's prohibition sections.
It protects the **owner or landlord** as well as the buyer or tenant. And its steering clause turns on
"interested in **equivalent properties**": the wrong is sending a prospect elsewhere when the
properties are comparable, which is exactly the fact pattern in which an agent tells themselves they
were being helpful.

**Blockbusting.** Section 3604(e) and § 301.024. The elements are "for profit," "induce or attempt to
induce" a sale or rental, and "representations regarding the entry or prospective entry into a
neighborhood" of persons of a protected class. Historically this was door-knocking and panic selling;
today it can be a targeted mailer or a social post. A Texas agent farming a neighborhood with "the
area is changing — list now before values drop," whose surrounding messaging makes clear what
"changing" refers to, has made a representation about prospective entry, is plainly acting for profit,
and has attempted to induce sales. That is the whole offence: no one has to list, nothing has to sell,
values need not fall.

**Redlining.** Redlining is refusing or disfavouring loans, insurance, appraisal or other
housing-related services in a geographic area, historically drawn on race. Two statutes reach it
directly, one federal and one Texas, and they are close enough that you should learn them as a pair.

Federally, it is 42 U.S.C. § 3605(a): "It shall be unlawful for any person or other entity whose
business includes engaging in residential real estate-related transactions to discriminate against any
person in making available such a transaction, or in the terms or conditions of such a transaction,
because of race, color, religion, sex, handicap, familial status, or national origin." Section 3605(b)
then defines a **residential real estate-related transaction** to mean "(1) [t]he making or purchasing
of loans or providing other financial assistance— (A) for purchasing, constructing, improving,
repairing, or maintaining a dwelling; or (B) secured by residential real estate," and "(2) [t]he
selling, brokering, or appraising of residential real property."

In Texas, Property Code § 301.026 forbids a person "whose business includes engaging in residential
real estate related transactions" to discriminate on the seven Texas bases "in making a real estate
related transaction available or in the terms or conditions of" one, and defines those transactions to
include dwelling lending and "the selling, brokering, or appraising of residential real property." The
two texts are near-identical in structure and nearly so in wording — Texas says "disability" where the
federal Act says "handicap," and drops the hyphen — which is exactly what Property Code § 301.002(3)
promised when it set out to provide rights "substantially equivalent to those granted under federal
law."

Take one point out of both. **Selling, brokering and appraising are named in the definition**, so a
license holder's own services are a residential real estate-related transaction under § 3605(b)(2) and
§ 301.026 alike. Redlining is not only a lender's offence. An agent who will not take listings in a
neighborhood, or who tells a seller there that the office "doesn't work over there," is disfavouring a
geographic area in the *brokering* of residential real property — inside the definition on its face.
Unit 4 takes up credit discrimination directly and works § 3605 in detail; unit 5 takes up the Community
Reinvestment Act, the affirmative side of the same geography.

**Denial of brokerage services — 42 U.S.C. § 3606 and Property Code § 301.027.** Every practice so far
in this unit is something done to a consumer. This one is done to *you*, or by your organization to a
colleague, and it is the fair housing provision that speaks most directly to the machinery of the
brokerage business. Section 3606 reads in full: "After December 31, 1968, it shall be unlawful to deny
any person access to or membership or participation in any multiple-listing service, real estate
brokers' organization or other service, organization, or facility relating to the business of selling
or renting dwellings, or to discriminate against him in the terms or conditions of such access,
membership, or participation, on account of race, color, religion, sex, handicap, familial status, or
national origin." Property Code § 301.027 says the same thing on the seven Texas bases, "disability"
for handicap.

Four features of that sentence do the work. **The covered bodies** are named and then opened out: a
multiple-listing service, a real estate brokers' organization, or "other service, organization, or
facility relating to the business of selling or renting dwellings." The closing phrase is not
decoration — it is a functional test, and it is what carries the section past the two named examples
to whatever else is organized around the business of selling or renting dwellings. **The prohibited
conduct is two-stage,** exactly as in § 3604(a) and (b): denying "access to or membership or
participation in," and discriminating "in the terms or conditions of such access, membership, or
participation." So conditioning participation, pricing it differently, or curtailing it after
admission is inside the section without any denial at all. **The bases are seven**, the full
§ 3604(c) list, handicap and familial status included; § 3606 does not split disability off into a
subsection of its own the way § 3604 does. And **the exemptions you learned in unit 1 do not answer a
§ 3606 problem.** Section 3603(b) by its own opening words exempts qualifying transactions from
"section 3604 of this title (other than subsection (c))" and from nothing else, so it cannot reach
§ 3606 at all. Section 3607's exemptions are phrased "[n]othing in this subchapter," so they are
subchapter-wide in form — but read what each of them is *about*: limiting "the sale, rental or
occupancy of dwellings" a religious organization or private club owns or operates (§ 3607(a)), and the
familial-status provisions "with respect to housing for older persons" (§ 3607(b)(1)). None of that
describes who may join a multiple-listing service or a brokers' organization. Do not answer a
brokerage-access question with a housing exemption.

Why this belongs in a licensing course rather than in a footnote: § 3606 is the fair housing section
whose subject matter is a license holder's own working life. Access to the MLS, to a local board or
association, to a referral network, to a lockbox system, to a co-op arrangement, to an office's
floor-time rotation — each is at least arguably a "service, organization, or facility relating to the
business of selling or renting dwellings," and a discriminatory condition on any of them is a
discriminatory housing practice under § 3602(f), not merely an internal governance dispute.
*Illustration:* a Texas real estate brokers' association admits applicants on stated criteria but
routes one applicant's file through an additional interview and a sponsorship requirement nobody else
is asked for, and the record shows national origin is why. No consumer was refused a dwelling and no listing
was affected; § 3606 and § 301.027 are violated on their own terms. The mirror-image point matters
too, and it is the one to carry into practice: **a broker or association that excludes on a protected
basis is not protected by the fact that the exclusion is aimed at a competitor rather than a
consumer.**

**Interference, coercion or intimidation — 42 U.S.C. § 3617.** The last of the four sections
§ 3602(f) names is the one that protects the people who assert fair housing rights and the people who
help them. It is one sentence: "It shall be unlawful to coerce, intimidate, threaten, or interfere
with any person in the exercise or enjoyment of, or on account of his having exercised or enjoyed, or
on account of his having aided or encouraged any other person in the exercise or enjoyment of, any
right granted or protected by section 3603, 3604, 3605, or 3606 of this title."

Take it in three pieces. **Four verbs** — coerce, intimidate, threaten, *interfere with* — and the
fourth is the widest and the one most often overlooked; interference needs no threat and no force.
**Three timings**, which is the structural point: the section reaches conduct against a person *in the
exercise or enjoyment* of a right (during), *on account of having exercised or enjoyed* it (after), and
*on account of having aided or encouraged any other person* in exercising or enjoying it. **A closed
list of underlying rights** — those granted or protected by § 3603, § 3604, § 3605 or § 3606. Note who
is missing from that list: § 3607, again, because it grants no right, and § 3617 itself.

The third timing is the one to write down, because **it protects you.** A license holder who documents
a client's reasonable-accommodation request, who tells a seller the instruction to exclude a group
cannot be followed, who refuses to run copy that indicates a familial-status preference, or who
testifies in a complaint, is a person who has "aided or encouraged any other person in the exercise or
enjoyment of" a § 3604 right. Retaliation against that license holder — a terminated listing agreement
is not it, but threats, a campaign to have them removed from a board or an MLS, or intimidation aimed
at making them stop — is reached by § 3617 in its own right, and reached whether or not the client's
underlying claim ever succeeds, because the section turns on the aiding and not on the outcome. It
runs the other way as well: an agent, manager or owner who pressures a tenant to drop a fair housing
complaint, or who makes life harder for a neighbour who supported one, has interfered on account of
the exercise of a § 3604 right. Both directions are discriminatory housing practices under § 3602(f).

Texas has no section numbered as § 3617's counterpart in ch. 301's prohibition subchapter, and this
module does not manufacture one. What Texas does have, in a different register, is Property Code
§ 301.171, the Class A misdemeanor treated in unit 2, which reaches intimidating or interfering with a
person **by force or threat of force** because of a protected class and because that person is buying,
selling, renting, financing or occupying a dwelling. Set the two beside each other and the difference
is the whole lesson: § 301.171 is a criminal provision requiring force or the threat of force, while
§ 3617 is a civil prohibition whose widest verb is "interfere with" and which requires no force at all.
A course of conduct far short of the Texas criminal threshold can still be a federal discriminatory
housing practice.

**Your personal exposure, in three layers.** Fair housing is not something only the broker or the
seller worries about.

*Layer one — the fair housing statutes.* Section 3604 and §§ 301.021 through 301.027 say "a person may
not," and § 301.003(12) defines "person" to include an individual. Under § 301.153 a court may award
"actual and punitive damages," attorney fees and costs; under § 301.112 the commission may order actual
damages and assess the civil penalties quoted in unit 2.

*Layer two — your licence.* Section 1101.652(b)(32) is a ground for suspension or revocation.
Section 1101.652(b)(2) independently reaches conduct "that is dishonest or in bad faith or that
demonstrates untrustworthiness," and § 1101.652(b)(1) reaches acting "negligently or incompetently."
Under Occupations Code § 1101.701 TREC may impose an administrative penalty on a person who violates
the chapter; § 1101.702(a) caps it at "$5,000 for each violation," with each day a violation continues
countable separately, and § 1101.701(c) permits combining a penalty proceeding with a proceeding to
suspend or revoke.

*Layer three — the bridge.* Property Code § 301.114 requires the Texas Workforce Commission, within 30
days of issuing an order about a discriminatory housing practice occurring in a licensed business, to
send the findings and order to the licensing agency and "recommend… appropriate disciplinary action."
For a Texas license holder, that agency is TREC. Occupations Code § 1101.801 closes the loop from the
other side: "Disciplinary action taken against a person under Section 1101.652 does not relieve the
person from civil or criminal liability." The layers stack; they do not substitute.

**The question you will actually be asked.** Sooner or later a buyer asks some version of "Is this a
good area for people like me?" or "What kind of people live here?" The question is usually innocent.
The answer is where careers end. The rule is short: **do not characterize a neighborhood by the
demographic characteristics of the people in it — not favourably, not unfavourably, not vaguely, not
on direct request, and not because the client volunteered their own group first.** "You'll fit in
here" or "you might be happier in the northwest part of town" is steering under § 3604(a) and
§ 301.021 and sits squarely inside § 1101.652(b)(32) if it directs a prospect "interested in equivalent
properties to a different area." Neither the Fair Housing Act nor ch. 301 contains a consent
exception, so the client's request does not cure it.

What to say instead is practice guidance, not a statutory script, and this module cites nothing for it:
acknowledge the question, say plainly that you are not permitted to describe neighborhoods by the
characteristics of the people who live in them and that the same rule protects the client, then
redirect to **objective published sources the client can read themselves** — school district and
campus accountability reports, published crime statistics from the relevant law enforcement agency,
census data, municipal and appraisal district records, HOA documents, and the property's own
disclosures. Then show every property meeting their stated objective criteria, in every area those
criteria cover, and let them draw the conclusions. A useful habit, again practice and not law, is to
write down the client's objective criteria — price, bedrooms, square footage, commute, school district,
lot size — at intake and run every showing decision back through that written list. It keeps your
service consistent and leaves a record that it was.

### Applied exercise: the "equivalent properties" problem

Devin is a Texas sales agent. A buyer couple gives him their criteria: three bedrooms, under $385,000,
within a twenty-minute commute of downtown, in a named school district. His MLS search returns eleven
properties meeting every criterion — six in the Eastside neighborhood and five elsewhere. During
intake the buyers mention their national origin. Devin shows them the five outside Eastside, never
mentions the six, and later tells his broker, "They'd have been more comfortable on the west side — I
was doing them a favour." No property was refused and they bought one of the five.

1. Identify the fair housing violation. Which subsection of § 3604 and which section of ch. 301 reach
   conduct that never refuses anyone a dwelling? Quote the operative phrase.
2. Identify the licence consequence. Quote the steering clause of § 1101.652(b)(32) and apply each of
   its elements to Devin's facts, including "interested in equivalent properties."
3. Devin says the buyers raised their national origin first and that he never said anything negative
   about Eastside. Does either fact matter under § 3604(a), § 301.021 or § 1101.652(b)(32)? Point to
   language, not intuition.
4. Assume the Texas Workforce Commission later issues an order against Devin's brokerage. Which section
   of ch. 301 sends that order to TREC, and within how many days? Which section of Occupations Code
   ch. 1101 tells you TREC discipline will not end Devin's civil exposure?
5. Rewrite Devin's showing plan so it complies. What does he do with the eleven listings, and what does
   he say if the buyers ask directly which neighborhood "people like us" prefer?

## Unit: Housing Credit Discrimination

Discrimination does not stop at the front door; it also happens at the desk where the loan is decided.
Two bodies of law meet there, and a license holder should be able to tell them apart.

**The Equal Credit Opportunity Act.** ECOA is 15 U.S.C. § 1691 and following, enacted as title VII of
the Consumer Credit Protection Act; § 1691's catchline is "Scope of prohibition," and the statutory
notes record that the subchapter is "known as the 'Equal Credit Opportunity Act.'" Its origin was
narrower than its present text. The 1974 congressional findings printed in the notes to § 1691 say
Congress found a need to make credit available "with fairness, impartiality, and without
discrimination on the basis of **sex or marital status**," and stated the purpose of making credit
"equally available to all credit-worthy customers without regard to sex or marital status." Two years
later the list grew: the amendments note records that in 1976 Pub. L. 94–239 "expanded prohibition
against discrimination to include race, color, religion, national origin and age, and added cls. (2)
and (3)."

**§ 1691(a) — the prohibited bases.** "It shall be unlawful for any creditor to discriminate against
any applicant, with respect to any aspect of a credit transaction— (1) on the basis of race, color,
religion, national origin, sex or marital status, or age (provided the applicant has the capacity to
contract); (2) because all or part of the applicant's income derives from any public assistance
program; or (3) because the applicant has in good faith exercised any right under this chapter."

Three features repay attention. **"Any aspect of a credit transaction"** is broad — application,
terms, pricing, servicing and termination, not merely the yes-or-no. **Age** carries its own qualifier,
"provided the applicant has the capacity to contract," so a minor's incapacity is not discrimination.
And paragraph (3) is an **anti-retaliation** clause: penalizing an applicant for having in good faith
exercised a right under the chapter is itself a violation.

Now compare the lists, which is the most useful thing to carry out of this unit. ECOA protects
**marital status, age and public-assistance income**; the Fair Housing Act and ch. 301 name none of
those. The Fair Housing Act and ch. 301 protect **familial status and handicap/disability**; § 1691(a)
names neither. The overlap is race, color, religion, national origin and sex. A mortgage application on
a Texas home can be governed by both regimes at once, and a fact pattern failing one may not fail the
other. A lender who prices a loan worse because part of the applicant's income is a public assistance
payment is inside § 1691(a)(2) on the face of the words; a lender who declines an applicant
because she is pregnant is outside § 1691(a)'s list but inside familial status under Property Code
§ 301.004(1) and, if the lending is a "residential real estate related transaction," inside § 301.026.

**§ 1691(b) — what does not constitute discrimination.** Lenders must ask questions, and the statute
protects five kinds of inquiry. It is not discrimination for a creditor "(1) to make an inquiry of
marital status if such inquiry is for the purpose of ascertaining the creditor's rights and remedies
applicable to the particular extension of credit and not to discriminate in a determination of
credit-worthiness"; "(2) to make an inquiry of the applicant's age or of whether the applicant's income
derives from any public assistance program if such inquiry is for the purpose of determining the amount
and probable continuance of income levels, credit history, or other pertinent element of
credit-worthiness as provided in regulations of the Bureau"; or "(3) to use any empirically derived
credit system which considers age if such system is demonstrably and statistically sound in accordance
with regulations of the Bureau, except that in the operation of such system the age of an elderly
applicant may not be assigned a negative factor or value." Paragraph (4) likewise permits considering
an elderly applicant's age where it "is to be used by the creditor in the extension of credit in favor
of such applicant," and (5) permits an inquiry under 15 U.S.C. § 1691c–2.

The through-line in (b)(1) and (b)(2) is **purpose**. Marital status may be asked about to determine
the creditor's rights and remedies — in Texas, community property and spousal-joinder questions make
that a real need — but "not to discriminate in a determination of credit-worthiness." Age and public
assistance income may be asked about to assess "the amount and probable continuance of income levels."
The question is lawful; using the answer as a proxy for creditworthiness is not. Section 1691(b) refers
throughout to "regulations of the Bureau," and both halves of that phrase were held and read for this
course: **15 U.S.C. § 1691a(c) supplies the definition — "[t]he term 'Bureau' means the Bureau of
Consumer Financial Protection"** — and the Bureau's regulations under ECOA are Regulation B, 12 C.F.R.
part 1002, four sections of which were held and are taught below. Section 1691(c) adds that refusing credit offered under a legally authorized
assistance program for an economically disadvantaged class, a nonprofit program, or a **special purpose
credit program** "offered by a profit-making organization to meet special social needs" is not a
violation "if such refusal is required by or made pursuant to such program" — the provision that lets
targeted assistance programs restrict eligibility.

**§ 1691(d) — adverse action notification.** This produces the letter your client brings to you.
Paragraph (1): "Within thirty days (or such longer reasonable time as specified in regulations of the
Bureau for any class of credit transaction) after receipt of a **completed** application for credit, a
creditor shall notify the applicant of its action on the application." The word "completed" is doing
real work. Paragraph (2): every applicant against whom adverse action is taken "shall be entitled to a
statement of reasons," which the creditor satisfies either by "providing statements of reasons in
writing as a matter of course," or by written notification disclosing "(i) the applicant's right to a
statement of reasons within thirty days after receipt by the creditor of a request made within sixty
days after such notification, and (ii) the identity of the person or office from which such statement
may be obtained." Under that second route there are two clocks: the applicant has **sixty days** from
notification to request reasons; the creditor then has **thirty days** from the request to supply them.
Paragraph (3): a statement of reasons complies "only if it contains the **specific reasons** for the
adverse action taken" — "you did not meet our credit standards" is not a statement of reasons.
Paragraph (6) defines the term: "'adverse action' means a denial or revocation of credit, a change in
the terms of an existing credit arrangement, or a refusal to grant credit in substantially the amount
or on substantially the terms requested," excluding refusals of additional credit where the applicant
is delinquent or in default or where the credit would exceed an established limit. A common error is to
think adverse action means only a flat denial; it also covers approval for materially less, or on
materially different terms.

**§ 1691(e) — the appraisal copy.** Each creditor must furnish the applicant "a copy of any and all
written appraisals and valuations developed in connection with the applicant's application for a loan
that is secured or would have been secured by a first lien on a dwelling promptly upon completion, but
in no case later than 3 days prior to the closing of the loan, whether the creditor grants or denies
the applicant's request for credit or the application is incomplete or withdrawn." The applicant may
waive the three-day timing and may be charged a reasonable fee for the appraisal itself, but the copy
must be "at no additional cost," and the creditor must give written notice of the right at
application. Subsection (e)(6) defines "valuation" broadly, reaching values from "an automated
valuation model, a broker price opinion, or other methodology or mechanism."

**§ 1691a — the words ECOA defines for itself.** Section 1691a supplies the definitions and rules of
construction "applicable for the purposes of this subchapter," and four of them decide who the statute
reaches. An **"applicant"** is "any person who applies to a creditor directly for an extension,
renewal, or continuation of credit, or applies to a creditor indirectly by use of an existing credit
plan for an amount exceeding a previously established credit limit" (§ 1691a(b)). A **"creditor"** is
"any person who regularly extends, renews, or continues credit; any person who regularly arranges for
the extension, renewal, or continuation of credit; or any assignee of an original creditor who
participates in the decision to extend, renew, or continue credit" (§ 1691a(e)). Read the middle
clause slowly: **"any person who regularly arranges for" credit is a creditor**, which is a wider net
than "lender," and § 1691a(f) defines "person" to mean "a natural person, a corporation, government or
governmental subdivision or agency, trust, estate, partnership, cooperative, or association" — so an
individual is a person here, as under 42 U.S.C. § 3602(d). **"Credit"** is "the right granted by a
creditor to a debtor to defer payment of debt or to incur debts and defer its payment or to purchase
property or services and defer payment therefor" (§ 1691a(d)). And § 1691a(g) is the hinge into
Regulation B: "[a]ny reference to any requirement imposed under this subchapter or any provision
thereof includes reference to the regulations of the Bureau under this subchapter or the provision
thereof in question." That sentence is why the regulation below is not a gloss on the statute but part
of what the statute requires.

**§ 1691c — who enforces ECOA.** Section 1691c is titled "Administrative enforcement," and it answers
the question a client will ask when the letter arrives: who does this go to? Its structure is a list
of enforcing agencies, each paired with the institutions it supervises. Under § 1691c(a)(1) the
requirements are enforced under section 8 of the Federal Deposit Insurance Act "by the appropriate
Federal banking agency" as to national banks, Federal savings associations and Federal branches and
agencies of foreign banks; member banks of the Federal Reserve System; and banks and State savings
associations insured by the FDIC. Paragraph (a)(2) assigns Federal credit unions to the National
Credit Union Administration; (a)(6) assigns Farm Credit System institutions to the Farm Credit
Administration; (a)(7) assigns brokers and dealers to the Securities and Exchange Commission; (a)(8)
assigns small business investment companies to the Small Business Administration; and (a)(9) assigns,
under subtitle E of the Consumer Financial Protection Act of 2010, "any person subject to this
subchapter" to the Bureau. Section 1691c(c) then supplies the residual: except where enforcement "is
specifically committed to some other Government agency" under paragraphs (1) through (8), "the Federal
Trade Commission shall be authorized to enforce such requirements," with all its Federal Trade
Commission Act powers available "irrespective of whether that person is engaged in commerce or meets
any other jurisdictional tests." Section 1691c(b) makes a violation of an ECOA requirement "a
violation of a requirement imposed under" whichever Act the supervising agency enforces, which is how
a fair lending failure becomes an ordinary supervisory violation. Two practical takeaways. First,
**there is an agency for every creditor** — the list plus the FTC residual leaves no gap, which is why
Regulation B requires the adverse-action notice to name one. Second, § 1691c is about *administrative*
enforcement; ECOA's civil liability section, 15 U.S.C. § 1691e, was **not** held for this course, so
this module states no ECOA damages figure, no statutory penalty and no limitations period.

**Regulation B — 12 C.F.R. part 1002.** Regulation B is the Bureau's regulation implementing ECOA, and
by § 1691a(g) its requirements are ECOA's requirements. Four sections were held and read, and together
they are the operational form of everything in § 1691.

**§ 1002.2 — definitions, and the one that changes your reading of § 1691(d).** Section 1002.2(z)
gathers the statutory bases into a single defined term: **"Prohibited basis** means race, color,
religion, national origin, sex, marital status, or age (provided that the applicant has the capacity to
enter into a binding contract); the fact that all or part of the applicant's income derives from any
public assistance program; or the fact that the applicant has in good faith exercised any right under
the Consumer Credit Protection Act or any state law upon which an exemption has been granted by the
Bureau." That is § 1691(a)'s list, restated as a noun the rest of the regulation can use.
Section 1002.2(n) defines the verb to match: **"Discriminate against an applicant** means to treat an
applicant less favorably than other applicants" — no intent word, and the comparison is with other
applicants. Section 1002.2(m) defines a **"credit transaction"** as "every aspect of an applicant's
dealings with a creditor regarding an application for credit or an existing extension of credit,"
naming information requirements, investigation procedures, standards of creditworthiness, terms of
credit, furnishing of credit information, revocation, alteration or termination of credit, and
collection procedures — which is what § 1691(a)'s "any aspect of a credit transaction" means in
practice, itemized. Section 1002.2(o) defines **"Elderly"** as "age 62 or older," the age § 1691(b)(3)
leaves unquantified. Section 1002.2(l) widens "creditor" for two purposes: for §§ 1002.4(a) and (b),
the term "also includes a person who, in the ordinary course of business, regularly refers applicants
or prospective applicants to creditors, or selects or offers to select creditors to whom requests for
credit may be made." **Read that one twice.** A license holder who, in the ordinary course of
business, regularly refers buyers to lenders is inside that clause for the purposes of the
anti-discrimination and anti-discouragement rules in § 1002.4 — the single most important sentence in
this unit for your own conduct.

Now the definition the exercise turns on. Section 1002.2(c)(1) defines **adverse action** as (i) "[a]
refusal to grant credit in substantially the amount or on substantially the terms requested in an
application unless the creditor makes a counteroffer (to grant credit in a different amount or on other
terms) and the applicant uses or expressly accepts the credit offered"; (ii) "[a] termination of an
account or an unfavorable change in the terms of an account that does not affect all or substantially
all of a class of the creditor's accounts"; or (iii) "[a] refusal to increase the amount of credit
available to an applicant who has made an application for an increase." Paragraph (c)(2) then excludes
five things, including "[a] change in the terms of an account expressly agreed to by an applicant";
action taken "in connection with inactivity, default, or delinquency as to that account"; "[a] refusal
to extend credit because applicable law prohibits the creditor from extending the credit requested";
and "[a] refusal to extend credit because the creditor does not offer the type of credit or credit plan
requested." Paragraph (c)(3) supplies the tie-breaker: an action within both lists "is governed by
paragraph (c)(2)," so the exclusions win.

Compare that with § 1691(d)(6), which you read above. The statute and the regulation agree on the
core — an approval for substantially less than was requested is adverse action — but the regulation
adds the **counteroffer carve-out** the statute does not spell out: where the creditor makes a
counteroffer and the applicant "uses or expressly accepts" it, the original refusal is not adverse
action. The client who applied for $310,000, was offered $240,000, and *took it* is in a different
position from the client who was offered $240,000 and walked away. Section 1002.2(f) also supplies the
definition the § 1691(d)(1) clock depends on: a **"completed application"** is one "in connection with
which a creditor has received all the information that the creditor regularly obtains and considers in
evaluating applications for the amount and type of credit requested," including credit reports,
additional information requested from the applicant, and any necessary approvals or reports — and
"[t]he creditor shall exercise reasonable diligence in obtaining such information," so a creditor may
not stall the clock by declining to gather what it needs.

**§ 1002.4 — the general rules.** Two sentences carry the section. Paragraph (a), **Discrimination:**
"A creditor shall not discriminate against an applicant on a prohibited basis regarding any aspect of a
credit transaction." Paragraph (b), **Discouragement:** "A creditor shall not make any oral or written
statement, in advertising or otherwise, directed at applicants or prospective applicants that the
creditor knows or should know would cause a reasonable person to believe that the creditor would deny,
or would grant on less favorable terms, a credit application by the applicant or prospective applicant
because of the applicant or prospective applicant's prohibited basis characteristic(s)," and for that
paragraph "oral or written statements are spoken or written words, or visual images such as symbols,
photographs, or videos."

Paragraph (b) has no counterpart in § 1691 and it is the paragraph closest to a license holder's daily
speech. Notice its features. **No application is required** — it protects "prospective applicants," so
the violation can be complete before anyone applies. **The standard is objective and constructive:**
what the creditor "knows or should know" would cause "a reasonable person" to believe, not what the
speaker intended. **The belief that matters is a discouraging one** — that credit "would" be denied or
granted on less favourable terms. And the coverage extends to **images**, so marketing photography is
inside the rule. Set that beside § 1002.2(l)'s extension of "creditor" for the purposes of § 1002.4(a)
and (b) to a person who regularly refers applicants to creditors, and the practical instruction writes
itself: telling a prospective buyer some version of "people in your situation don't usually get
approved here" is exactly the statement § 1002.4(b) describes. The rest of the section is mechanical
but worth knowing: § 1002.4(c) requires written applications "for the dwelling-related types of credit
covered by § 1002.13(a)"; § 1002.4(d)(1) requires written disclosures to be "clear and conspicuous" and
generally in a retainable form; § 1002.4(d)(2) permits electronic delivery subject to the E-Sign Act;
and § 1002.4(e) permits disclosures in other languages "provided they are available in English upon
request."

**§ 1002.5 — what a creditor may and may not ask.** This is the section that turns § 1691(b)'s general
permission into workable rules, and it is the answer to a client who asks why a lender wanted to know
something. The default is permissive: under § 1002.5(a)(1), "[e]xcept as provided in paragraphs (b)
through (d) of this section, a creditor may request any information in connection with a credit
transaction." The exceptions are where the content is.

*Race, color, religion, national origin, sex.* Section 1002.5(b) is a flat prohibition with two
exceptions: "A creditor shall not inquire about the race, color, religion, national origin, or sex of
an applicant or any other person in connection with a credit transaction, except as provided in
paragraphs (b)(1) and (b)(2)." The first exception is a **self-test** meeting § 1002.15's requirements,
and a creditor making such an inquiry must disclose, orally or in writing and at the time of the
request, that the applicant need not provide the information, that the creditor is requesting it "to
monitor its compliance with the Federal Equal Credit Opportunity Act," that federal law prohibits
discriminating on the basis of that information "or on the basis of an applicant's decision not to
furnish the information," and, if applicable, that some information will be collected by visual
observation or surname. The second is narrow and formal: an applicant "may be requested to designate a
title on an application form (such as Ms., Miss, Mr., or Mrs.) if the form discloses that the
designation of a title is optional," and the form "shall otherwise use only terms that are neutral as
to sex."

That prohibition has an important counterweight in the same section, and it explains a form your
clients will meet. Section 1002.5(a)(2) provides that **notwithstanding** paragraphs (b) through (d),
"a creditor shall request information for monitoring purposes as required by § 1002.13 for credit
secured by the applicant's dwelling," and may obtain information required by a regulation, order or
agreement of a court or enforcement agency to monitor or enforce compliance. So for credit secured by the applicant's
dwelling the demographic questions are not merely permitted, they are **required** for monitoring —
which is why a mortgage application asks what a car loan application may not. Section 1002.13, which
fixes exactly which credit that monitoring requirement covers and how the information is to be
collected, was **not** held for this course, so this module states the cross-reference and does not
describe its contents. Section 1002.5(a)(3) allows
otherwise-restricted information to be obtained to determine eligibility for a special purpose credit
program under § 1002.8, the regulatory partner of § 1691(c)(3), and § 1002.5(a)(4) sets out a detailed
list of further permitted collections tied to HMDA reporting under 12 C.F.R. part 1003 and to the
small-business lending data in subpart B of part 1002.

*Spouses and marital status.* Section 1002.5(c)(1): "[e]xcept as permitted in this paragraph, a
creditor may not request any information concerning the spouse or former spouse of an applicant."
Paragraph (c)(2) then lists five permissible situations — the spouse will be permitted to use the
account; will be contractually liable on it; the applicant is relying on the spouse's income for
repayment; **"[t]he applicant resides in a community property state or is relying on property located
in such a state as a basis for repayment of the credit requested"**; or the applicant is relying on
alimony, child support or separate maintenance from a spouse or former spouse. The fourth is the Texas
one, and it is the concrete form of the point unit 4 already makes about § 1691(b)(1): Texas is a
community property state, so the spousal inquiry a Texas lender makes is the inquiry § 1002.5(c)(2)(iv)
expressly permits, and it is permitted for that reason and not because lenders may ask what they like.
Section 1002.5(d)(1) completes the rule: on an application for **individual unsecured credit** a
creditor "shall not inquire about the applicant's marital status unless the applicant resides in a
community property state or is relying on property located in such a state as a basis for repayment,"
and on any other application the creditor may inquire "but shall use only the terms married,
unmarried, and separated," with an optional explanation that "unmarried" includes single, divorced and
widowed persons. Section 1002.2(u) defines marital status to the same three states.

*Alimony and children.* Section 1002.5(d)(2) forbids asking whether stated income derives from
alimony, child support or separate maintenance "unless the creditor discloses to the applicant that
such income need not be revealed if the applicant does not want the creditor to consider it." And
§ 1002.5(d)(3), **Childbearing, childrearing**, is the one to remember: "A creditor shall not inquire
about birth control practices, intentions concerning the bearing or rearing of children, or capability
to bear children. A creditor may inquire about the number and ages of an applicant's dependents or
about dependent-related financial obligations or expenditures, provided such information is requested
without regard to sex, marital status, or any other prohibited basis." Dependants as a budget line,
yes; family plans, never. Section 1002.5(e) provides that a creditor "may inquire about the permanent
residency and immigration status of an applicant or any other person in connection with a credit
transaction."

**§ 1002.9 — the notification requirement, which is § 1691(d) with the details filled in.** Start with
timing. Section 1002.9(a)(1) requires a creditor to notify an applicant of action taken within: "(i) 30
days after receiving a completed application concerning the creditor's approval of, counteroffer to, or
adverse action on the application"; "(ii) 30 days after taking adverse action on an incomplete
application," unless notice of incompleteness is given under paragraph (c); "(iii) 30 days after taking
adverse action on an existing account"; or "(iv) 90 days after notifying the applicant of a counteroffer
if the applicant does not expressly accept or use the credit offered." Four clocks, not one. The
statute's thirty days runs from a *completed* application; the regulation adds thirty days for adverse
action on an *incomplete* application and on an *existing account*, and ninety days for an unaccepted
counteroffer.

Then content. Section 1002.9(a)(2): a notification given when adverse action is taken "shall be in
writing" and shall contain a statement of the action taken; the name and address of the creditor; a
statement of the provisions of section 701(a) of the Act; the name and address of the Federal agency
that administers compliance with respect to the creditor — this is where § 1691c's list of agencies
surfaces on the page your client is holding — and **either** "(i) [a] statement of specific reasons for
the action taken" **or** "(ii) [a] disclosure of the applicant's right to a statement of specific
reasons within 30 days, if the statement is requested within 60 days of the creditor's notification,"
including "the name, address, and telephone number of the person or office from which the statement of
reasons can be obtained." Those are § 1691(d)(2)'s two routes and its 60-day and 30-day clocks, in the
same shape; the regulation adds that if the creditor gives the reasons orally it must also disclose the
applicant's right to have them confirmed in writing within 30 days of a written request for
confirmation.

Section 1002.9(b)(1) prints the **ECOA notice** the creditor must provide "substantially similar" to
the regulation's text, reciting the prohibited bases and directing the applicant to "[t]he Federal
agency that administers compliance with this law concerning this creditor." And § 1002.9(b)(2) puts
teeth on § 1691(d)(3)'s "specific reasons": the statement "must be specific and indicate the principal
reason(s) for the adverse action," and — the sentence to quote to a client holding a vague letter —
"[s]tatements that the adverse action was based on the creditor's internal standards or policies or
that the applicant, joint applicant, or similar party failed to achieve a qualifying score on the
creditor's credit scoring system are insufficient."

Four more paragraphs matter to a real file. **Incomplete applications** (§ 1002.9(c)): within 30 days
of receiving an application incomplete "regarding matters that an applicant can complete," the creditor
must either notify of action taken or send a written notice of incompleteness "specifying the
information needed, designating a reasonable period of time for the applicant to provide the
information, and informing the applicant that failure to provide the information requested will result
in no further consideration being given to the application"; if the applicant supplies it in time the
creditor must act and notify under paragraph (a). **Small-volume creditors** (§ 1002.9(d)): a creditor
that "did not receive more than 150 applications during the preceding calendar year" may satisfy the
section, statements of specific reasons included, by oral notification. **Withdrawal**
(§ 1002.9(e)): where the parties contemplate that the applicant will inquire about the application's
status, a creditor that approves it may treat it as withdrawn if the applicant has not inquired within
30 days of applying. **Multiple applicants and third parties** (§ 1002.9(f) and (g)): notice need only
go to one applicant "but must be given to the primary applicant where one is readily apparent"; and
where an application is made to more than one creditor and the applicant accepts or uses one
creditor's offer, the others need not notify — otherwise each creditor taking adverse action must
comply "directly or through a third party," and a third-party notice "shall disclose the identity of
each creditor on whose behalf the notice is given." That last rule is the one that explains a client
receiving several denial letters from lenders they never contacted.

**The fair housing side — 42 U.S.C. § 3605.** ECOA is not the only statute at the lending desk. The
Fair Housing Act has its own financing provision, and it is short enough to learn whole.

**§ 3605(a) — the prohibition.** "It shall be unlawful for any person or other entity whose business
includes engaging in residential real estate-related transactions to discriminate against any person in
making available such a transaction, or in the terms or conditions of such a transaction, because of
race, color, religion, sex, handicap, familial status, or national origin."

Four things to take from that sentence. **Who is covered:** "any person or other entity **whose business
includes** engaging in residential real estate-related transactions." Not "banks," not "lenders" — the
test is what your business *includes*, and § 3602(d) defines "person" to include individuals. That
wording is deliberate. Until 1988 this section reached only "any bank, building and loan association,
insurance company or other corporation, association, firm or enterprise whose business consists in whole
or in part in the making of commercial real estate loans"; the amendments note under § 3605 records that
Pub. L. 100–430 "amended section generally" and prints the narrower predecessor, so you can see the
widening for yourself. **What is prohibited:** discrimination "in making available such a transaction,
or in the terms or conditions of such a transaction" — the same two-stage structure as § 3604(a) and
(b), access and terms. **The bases:** seven, "race, color, religion, sex, handicap, familial status, or
national origin," which is the full § 3604(c) list. Unlike § 3604(a), this section does **not** drop
handicap and familial status; there is no separate disability subsection here, so the seven sit
together. **The verb:** "shall be unlawful," with no intent word, no "knowingly," and no requirement
that a loan actually be denied — worse terms are enough.

**§ 3605(b) — what a "residential real estate-related transaction" is.** "As used in this section, the
term 'residential real estate-related transaction' means any of the following: (1) The making or
purchasing of loans or providing other financial assistance— (A) for purchasing, constructing,
improving, repairing, or maintaining a dwelling; or (B) secured by residential real estate. (2) The
selling, brokering, or appraising of residential real property."

Work through the reach. Paragraph (1) covers not only *making* a loan but **purchasing** one, so the
secondary market is inside the section, and "other financial assistance" reaches beyond loans. The
purposes in (1)(A) run past acquisition to construction, improvement, repair and maintenance, so a home
improvement or repair loan is covered; and (1)(B) supplies an independent route — any financial
assistance "secured by residential real estate," whatever it was borrowed for. Then paragraph (2) is the
one to underline in your own copy: **"[t]he selling, brokering, or appraising of residential real
property."** Brokering is named. A Texas license holder's own services are a residential real
estate-related transaction under the federal Act, not merely under Texas law, and § 3605 binds you
directly and by name. Property Code § 301.026 defines the Texas term in materially the same way,
including the same "selling, brokering, or appraising" clause, so on this point the two statutes travel
together.

**§ 3605(c) — the appraisal provision.** "Nothing in this subchapter prohibits a person engaged in the
business of furnishing appraisals of real property to take into consideration factors other than race,
color, religion, national origin, sex, handicap, or familial status." Read what this does and does not
say. It is permissive and it is bounded: an appraiser may consider **factors other than** the protected
characteristics. It is not an exemption for appraisers, and it does not remove appraising from
§ 3605(b)(2). Its whole work is to confirm that valuing property on legitimate factors — condition,
size, comparable sales, location as a market rather than a demographic fact — is not made unlawful by
this subchapter. An appraisal that discounts a property because of the race of the owner or of the
neighborhood is not "factors other than," and is squarely inside § 3605(a). Texas carries the identical
provision at Property Code § 301.042(c): the chapter "does not prohibit a person engaged in the business
of furnishing appraisals of real property from considering in those appraisals factors other than race,
color, religion, sex, disability, familial status, or national origin." Same permission, same bounds,
"disability" for "handicap."

So a Texas mortgage denial can implicate several regimes at once, and they do not have the same
protected classes: ECOA on the § 1691(a) bases, including marital status, age and public-assistance
income; 42 U.S.C. § 3605 on the seven federal fair housing bases; Property Code § 301.026 on the seven
Texas bases; and, where a license holder's conduct contributed, Occupations Code § 1101.652(b)(32) as a
licence matter. Run a fact pattern against each list rather than against a general sense of fairness.
The applicant declined because she is pregnant is outside § 1691(a) and inside familial status under
§ 3602(k) and § 301.004, and so inside § 3605(a) and § 301.026; the applicant declined because part of
her income is a public assistance payment is inside § 1691(a)(2) and outside every fair housing list.

**What to do when a client is denied.** This paragraph is practice, not law, and cites nothing. **Do**
make sure the client reads the notice, and point out that § 1691(d)(2) and 12 C.F.R. § 1002.9(a)(2)
entitle them to a statement of specific reasons and that the notice must say how to request one and
from whom. **Do** note the timing
— sixty days to request, thirty days to receive — and tell them to calendar it. **Do** point out that
the notice must name "the Federal agency that administers compliance with respect to the creditor"
(§ 1002.9(a)(2)), which is the agency § 1691c assigns, and that under § 1002.9(b)(2) a letter saying
only that they missed the creditor's internal standards or failed to make a qualifying score is
"insufficient" on the regulation's own word. **Do** remind them of
the § 1691(e) appraisal-copy right, since a valuation problem is a common reason for adverse action on
different terms. **Do** encourage them to speak with the creditor, another creditor, or a lawyer.
**Do not** offer your own theory of the denial, tell the client whether they have a claim, steer them
toward a lender you believe is friendlier to people in the client's group — that is itself
differential service — or repeat the client's protected characteristics to an underwriter as context.
Your job is to make sure the client knows the rights the statute gives them, and to keep your own
conduct out of the fact pattern.

## Unit: Community Reinvestment

The four preceding units were about prohibition: do not refuse, do not vary terms, do not advertise a
preference, do not steer, do not discriminate in credit. The Community Reinvestment Act is a different
kind of statute, and the difference is the point of this unit.

**The findings.** The Community Reinvestment Act of 1977 — Pub. L. 95–128, title VIII, and by its own
short-title note it "may be cited as the 'Community Reinvestment Act of 1977'" — opens at 12 U.S.C.
§ 2901 with three congressional findings: "(1) regulated financial institutions are required by law to
demonstrate that their deposit facilities serve the convenience and needs of the communities in which
they are chartered to do business; (2) the convenience and needs of communities include the need for
credit services as well as deposit services; and (3) regulated financial institutions have continuing
and affirmative obligation to help meet the credit needs of the local communities in which they are
chartered."

Read them as a three-step argument, because that is how they are built. Step one is a premise Congress
treats as settled: a chartered depository institution must serve "the convenience and needs" of its
community. Step two supplies the move the Act turns on — *needs* include **credit**, not only deposits.
That sounds obvious now; in 1977 it was the crux, because an institution could gather deposits from a
neighborhood and lend them somewhere else. Step three states the conclusion: a "**continuing and
affirmative obligation** to help meet the credit needs of the local communities in which they are
chartered." Weigh those adjectives. **Continuing** means the obligation is not
discharged by one transaction or one good year; it runs as long as the charter does. **Affirmative**
means the duty is to *do* something, not merely to refrain — the word that separates the CRA from every
other statute in this module.

**The purpose and the mechanism.** Section 2901(b): "It is the purpose of this chapter to require each
appropriate Federal financial supervisory agency to use its authority when examining financial
institutions, to encourage such institutions to help meet the credit needs of the local communities in
which they are chartered consistent with the safe and sound operation of such institutions."

Take that apart. The party **required** to act is not the bank; it is "each appropriate Federal
financial supervisory agency." What it must do is "use its authority **when examining** financial
institutions." The object is to "**encourage**" institutions to help meet local credit needs. And the
whole is bounded by "**consistent with the safe and sound operation of such institutions**." Four
consequences follow, and each is a place students go wrong. First, the CRA works through
**examination**: its lever is supervisory, not a licence condition, a quota, or a
transaction-by-transaction rule. Second, it **does not command a specific loan** — the statute says
"encourage… to help meet," so no borrower can point at § 2901 and say a bank was obliged to lend to
*them*. Third, **safety and soundness is a limit inside the purpose itself**; the Act does not ask an
institution to make loans it judges unsound, only to serve its whole community with the seriousness it
brings to any market. Fourth, § 2901 is addressed to **regulated financial institutions and their
supervisors**, not to real estate brokerages; no provision of it imposes a duty on a Texas license
holder.

**Who the Act is about — § 2902's definitions.** Section 2901 states findings and a purpose; § 2902
supplies the nouns those sentences run on, and all three matter. First, the **"appropriate Federal
financial supervisory agency"** is defined institution by institution: the **Comptroller of the
Currency** as to national banks and Federal savings associations whose deposits are FDIC-insured; the
**Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System** as to State chartered banks that are members of
the Federal Reserve System, bank holding companies, and savings and loan holding companies; and the
**Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation** as to State chartered banks and savings banks that are not
Federal Reserve members and whose deposits the Corporation insures, and State savings associations
whose deposits are FDIC-insured (§ 2902(1)(A)–(C)). Three agencies, no more, and none of them is HUD,
the Bureau or TREC. Second — and this is the definition that settles who owes a CRA obligation at all
— "the term **'regulated financial institution'** means an insured depository institution (as defined
in section 1813 of this title)" (§ 2902(2)). Note how narrow that is. A mortgage broker is not one; a
mortgage banker is not one; a credit union is not one; a real estate brokerage is emphatically not one.
The CRA reaches **insured depository institutions**, which is to say banks and savings institutions
whose deposits carry federal insurance. Third, an **"application for a deposit facility"** is defined
in § 2902(3) as an application to the appropriate agency, otherwise required by federal law, for a
national bank or Federal savings and loan association charter; deposit insurance for a newly chartered
institution; "the establishment of a domestic branch or other facility with the ability to accept
deposits"; "the relocation of the home office or a branch office"; a merger, consolidation, asset
acquisition or liability assumption requiring approval under 12 U.S.C. § 1828(c); or the acquisition of
shares or assets requiring approval under 12 U.S.C. § 1842. Hold that list — it is the list of moments
at which a CRA record has consequences. Section 2902(4) adds a special rule: an institution whose
business "predominately consists of serving the needs of military personnel who are not located within
a defined geographic area may define its 'entire community' to include its entire deposit customer base
without regard to geographic proximity," which in a state with as many military installations as Texas
is worth knowing exists.

**The assessment — § 2903.** This is the section that converts § 2901's purpose into a duty. Section
2903(a): "In connection with its examination of a financial institution, the appropriate Federal
financial supervisory agency shall— (1) assess the institution's record of meeting the credit needs of
its entire community, including low- and moderate-income neighborhoods, consistent with the safe and
sound operation of such institution; and (2) take such record into account in its evaluation of an
application for a deposit facility by such institution."

Read the two paragraphs as a pair, because neither works alone. Paragraph (1) is the **assessment**:
mandatory ("shall"), performed "[i]n connection with its examination," and measured against the
institution's **"entire community, including low- and moderate-income neighborhoods."** Paragraph (2)
is the **consequence**: the agency must "take such record into account" when it evaluates an
application for a deposit facility — the § 2902(3) list of charters, deposit insurance, new branches,
relocations, mergers and acquisitions. That is the whole enforcement design of the CRA, and it explains
why the Act has force despite creating no prohibition and no private claim: the record is assessed on
examination and cashed at the moment the institution wants to do something that needs federal approval.
Two further subsections add factors. Section 2903(b) permits an agency, in assessing a nonminority-owned
and nonwomen-owned institution, to "consider as a factor capital investment, loan participation, and
other ventures undertaken by the institution in cooperation with minority- and women-owned financial
institutions and low-income credit unions," provided those activities help meet local credit needs; and
§ 2903(d) requires the agency to consider, as a factor, "low-cost education loans provided by the
financial institution to low-income borrowers." Section 2903(c) attaches a CRA condition to a corporate
election: a bank holding company's election to become a financial holding company under 12 U.S.C.
§ 1843 is not effective if the Board finds that, as of the filing date, "not all of the subsidiary
insured depository institutions of the bank holding company had achieved a rating of 'satisfactory
record of meeting community credit needs', or better, at the most recent examination of each such
institution," subject to a limited 12-month exclusion for newly acquired institutions covered by an
accepted remediation plan.

**Reporting and rulemaking — §§ 2904 and 2905.** Section 2904 is one sentence: "Each appropriate
Federal financial supervisory agency shall include in its annual report to the Congress a section
outlining the actions it has taken to carry out its responsibilities under this chapter." Section 2905
provides that regulations to carry out the chapter's purposes "shall be published by each appropriate
Federal financial supervisory agency," except that the Comptroller of the Currency prescribes those
applicable to savings associations and the Board of Governors those applicable to insured State member
banks, bank holding companies and savings and loan holding companies, and that they "shall take effect
no later than 390 days after October 12, 1977." Two things follow. **The CRA is agency-by-agency, not
one uniform code** — each supervisor writes its own regulations under § 2905, which is why the assessment
factors § 2906 refers to are found in regulations rather than in the statute. And **no CRA regulation
was held for this course.** This module therefore states what §§ 2902–2906 say and does not describe
any agency's assessment-area rules, performance tests or examination procedures, which live in those
regulations.

**The written evaluation and the four ratings — § 2906.** Section 2906 is the section that makes a CRA
record public, and it is the one most often misremembered, so read the words. Section 2906(a)(1): "Upon
the conclusion of each examination of an insured depository institution under section 2903 of this
title, the appropriate Federal financial supervisory agency shall prepare a written evaluation of the
institution's record of meeting the credit needs of its entire community, including low- and
moderate-income neighborhoods." Section 2906(a)(2): "Each written evaluation required under paragraph
(1) shall have a **public section and a confidential section**."

*The public section* (§ 2906(b)(1)(A)) must "(i) state the appropriate Federal financial supervisory
agency's conclusions for each assessment factor identified in the regulations prescribed by the Federal
financial supervisory agencies to implement this chapter; (ii) discuss the facts and data supporting
such conclusions; and (iii) contain the institution's rating and a statement describing the basis for
the rating." Under § 2906(b)(1)(B) the information required by clauses (i) and (ii) "shall be presented
separately for each metropolitan area in which a regulated depository institution maintains one or more
domestic branch offices."

*The rating.* Section 2906(b)(2) provides that the institution's rating "shall be 1 of the following,"
and these are the statute's own words, in the statute's own order:

- **"Outstanding record of meeting community credit needs"** (§ 2906(b)(2)(A));
- **"Satisfactory record of meeting community credit needs"** (§ 2906(b)(2)(B));
- **"Needs to improve record of meeting community credit needs"** (§ 2906(b)(2)(C));
- **"Substantial noncompliance in meeting community credit needs"** (§ 2906(b)(2)(D)).

The paragraph closes: "Such ratings shall be disclosed to the public on and after July 1, 1990." Four
levels, no more; and note that the fourth is worded as *substantial noncompliance*, not "unsatisfactory"
or "failing." Use the statutory phrases. When § 2903(c) conditions a financial holding company election
on a rating of "satisfactory record of meeting community credit needs, or better," it is pointing at
subparagraph (B) of this list, so "or better" means (B) or (A).

*The confidential section* (§ 2906(c)) "shall contain all references that identify any customer of the
institution, any employee or officer of the institution, or any person or organization that has
provided information in confidence to a Federal or State financial supervisory agency," and "any
statements obtained or made by the appropriate Federal financial supervisory agency in the course of an
examination which, in the judgment of the agency, are too sensitive or speculative in nature to disclose
to the institution or the public." It may be disclosed to the institution in whole or part where the
agency determines that doing so "will promote the objectives of this chapter," but never in a way that
identifies a confidential informant.

*Multi-state institutions.* Section 2906(d) requires, for an institution with domestic branches in two
or more States, both a written evaluation of the entire institution and "for each State in which the
institution maintains 1 or more domestic branches, a separate written evaluation" of its performance
within that State; a separate evaluation is likewise required for each multistate metropolitan area in
which it branches. A state-level evaluation must present the (b)(1)(A) and (B) information separately
for each metropolitan area in which the institution has branch offices and separately for the remainder
of the State's nonmetropolitan area, and must "describe how the Federal financial supervisory agency has
performed the examination of the institution, including a list of the individual branches examined."
Section 2906(e) defines a **"domestic branch"** as "any branch office or other facility of a regulated
financial institution that accepts deposits, located in any State," and a **"metropolitan area"** as a
primary, ordinary or consolidated metropolitan statistical area as defined by the Director of the Office
of Management and Budget "with a population of 250,000 or more," plus any other area the agency
designates. For a large bank operating in Texas and elsewhere, this is why a Texas-specific CRA
evaluation exists at all, and why it breaks out by metropolitan area.

**Low- and moderate-income communities — where the phrase actually sits.** The phrase you will hear
attached to the CRA does not appear in § 2901's findings, which speak of "the local communities in
which they are chartered." It appears twice in the operative text you have just read: § 2903(a)(1)
requires the agency to assess the institution's record of meeting the credit needs of "its entire
community, **including low- and moderate-income neighborhoods**," and § 2906(a)(1) uses the identical
phrase for the written evaluation. So the framing is statutory, and it enters through the assessment
and evaluation sections rather than through the findings. The statutory notes printed under § 2901
carry it further still, and those notes were read. The note reproducing § 715 of
Pub. L. 106–102 (1999) directed the Secretary of the Treasury, with the Federal banking agencies, to
study "the extent to which adequate services are being provided as intended by the Community
Reinvestment Act of 1977, including **services in low- and moderate-income neighborhoods and for
persons of modest means**." The note reproducing § 910 of Pub. L. 102–550 (1992) directed the Federal
Reserve Board to report to Congress "comparing residential, small business, and commercial lending by
insured depository institutions in **low-income, minority, and distressed neighborhoods** to such
lending in other neighborhoods." Be precise about which text says which: the findings in § 2901 do not
use the phrase, §§ 2903 and 2906 do, and the notes extend it to studies and reports.

**How the CRA relates to, and differs from, the anti-discrimination statutes.** Put § 2901 beside
§ 3604, § 301.021 and § 1691(a) and the contrast is structural. The anti-discrimination statutes are
**prohibitions running to individuals**: each says a person may not do a thing to another person
because of a protected characteristic, each identifies protected classes, and each supplies a remedy to
an injured person — § 301.153 lets a court award actual and punitive damages, § 301.081 lets an
aggrieved person file a complaint, § 1691(d) entitles an applicant to specific reasons.

The CRA is an **affirmative obligation running to communities**, supervised by examination. Sections
2901 through 2906 name no protected class between them. None of them says a person may not do a thing.
Nothing in the six sections held for this course gives an individual borrower a claim against a bank
for failing to lend to them, and nothing in them identifies an injured person at all; the subject
throughout is the institution's "entire community." Where the anti-discrimination statutes give the
injured person a complaint and a suit, the CRA gives the **agency** an assessment (§ 2903(a)(1)), a
public written evaluation with a rating (§ 2906), and a moment to use them — the § 2903(a)(2)
evaluation of an application for a deposit facility. A common and consequential error is to describe the CRA as an anti-redlining
*prohibition*. Redlining as an unlawful practice is reached by the fair housing and credit statutes —
federally by 42 U.S.C. § 3605, which makes it unlawful for a person "whose business includes engaging in
residential real estate-related transactions" to discriminate in making such a transaction available or
in its terms on the seven fair housing bases, and in Texas by Property Code § 301.026 in nearly the same
words. Notice the difference in address. Sections 3605 and 301.026 speak to a *person whose business
includes* those transactions and forbid that person to discriminate; § 2901 speaks to *regulated
financial institutions and their federal supervisors* and creates no prohibition at all. The CRA comes at
the same geography from the opposite direction: instead of
forbidding an institution to disfavour a neighborhood, it obliges the institution to affirmatively help
meet that neighborhood's credit needs, and charges a federal supervisor with checking whether it did.

The two are complements. An institution can comply perfectly with the Fair Housing Act and ECOA by
treating identically every applicant who walks in, and still have a thin record of serving parts of its
community — because those statutes speak to how you treat the people who apply, and the CRA speaks to
whether you are serving the whole community you are chartered in. That is why Congress wrote a separate
statute in 1977, nine years after the Fair Housing Act and three after ECOA.

**What this means for a Texas license holder.** The following is practice and observation, not a legal
duty, and cites nothing. You will not be examined under the CRA and you owe no CRA obligation. What the
Act gives you is context: it is one reason lenders operating in Texas fund community development
programs, participate in first-time-buyer and down-payment-assistance products, and open branches in
neighborhoods a purely transactional analysis might skip. When a buyer in a modest Texas neighborhood
is told by one lender that "nobody lends on houses in that part of town," the useful response is not a
lecture on § 2901 but the fact that other regulated institutions serve that community and the loan is
worth shopping. One thing the newly read sections let you say accurately: under § 2906(a)(2) and
(b)(1) an insured depository institution's CRA evaluation has a **public section** containing its
rating and the basis for it, and § 2906(b)(2) provides that those ratings "shall be disclosed to the
public on and after July 1, 1990." So a rating is a published fact a client can look up. Keep the
boundary clean: you may say that community lending programs exist, that lenders differ, and that CRA
evaluations are public documents; you may not characterize a given institution's CRA record yourself
from anything other than the published evaluation, you may not present a rating as a recommendation,
and you may not steer a client to or from a neighborhood on any basis this module has spent five units
warning you about.

### Module recap

This module traced fair housing from a one-sentence federal policy to the licence you hold.
42 U.S.C. § 3601 declares the policy; § 3604 supplies the prohibitions — refusal to deal,
discriminatory terms, discriminatory advertising, false representations of unavailability,
blockbusting, and the disability regime in (f) — whose class lists differ because Congress added sex
in 1974 and handicap and familial status in 1988 by insertion. Inside § 3604(f)(3) the pair students
most often merge stays separate: a **reasonable modification** changes the premises, at the occupant's
expense, and in a rental may be conditioned on restoring the interior; a **reasonable accommodation**
changes rules, policies, practices or services, with no expense and no restoration clause attached.

The sections around § 3604 do the defining and the limiting. Section 3602 supplies the vocabulary:
a **dwelling** includes a portion of a building and vacant land offered for residential construction; a
**person** includes an individual, which is how the Act reaches you personally; **handicap** has three
prongs, the third being "regarded as" having an impairment; and **familial status** turns on domicile
with someone under 18, extended by the closing sentence to a pregnant person and to one securing legal
custody. Section 3603(a)(2) sets the reach — after December 31, 1968, all dwellings except as exempted
by § 3603(b). Section 3603(b) then exempts two transactions, a single-family house sold or rented by an
owner who does not hold more than three (limited to one sale in twenty-four months where the owner was
not the resident, and lost entirely if a broker's services are used "in any manner"), and rooms or units
in a dwelling for no more than four families where the owner "actually maintains and occupies" one as a
residence — both **"other than subsection (c)," so no exemption ever reaches discriminatory
advertising.** Section 3607(a) exempts religious organizations and genuine private clubs on stated
conditions, and § 3607(b) takes housing for older persons out of the familial-status provisions only:
62-and-over must be "intended for, and solely occupied by," while 55-and-over needs one qualifying
person in at least 80 percent of *occupied* units plus published policies and Secretary-verified
occupancy, with a good-faith defence available under § 3607(b)(5) only to someone holding the
community's written statement of compliance.

Texas legislated in parallel. Property Code ch. 301 protects the same seven bases, is administered by
the **Texas Workforce Commission** under § 301.0015 and not by TREC, and by § 301.002(3) provides
rights and remedies "substantially equivalent to those granted under federal law." State its class
list precisely: **the prohibition sections of the current statewide Texas Fair Housing Act, ch. 301, as
read for this course, name those seven bases and name no eighth, and § 301.005 forbids expanding the
chapter's remedies beyond their express terms.** That is a statement about ch. 301 only. It is not a
statement that Texans have no further protection: a municipality or county may adopt its own fair
housing ordinance, § 301.044(b) preserves nondiscrimination requirements in other state and federal
law, and **no local ordinance was held for this course, so this module surveys none** — read the
ordinance where you work. Inside ch. 301, § 301.0045 broadens a class rather than adding one: it
provides that a provision of the chapter (other than one in Subchapter I, the criminal penalty
subchapter) referring to discrimination because of race "includes" discrimination because of hair
texture or a protective hairstyle "commonly or historically associated with race," so a locs case is a
race case. Its enforcement path is datable: a sworn complaint within one year, a 100-day investigation
and reasonable-cause determination, conciliation, a charge, a 20-day election between an
administrative hearing and an attorney general suit, and a private action within two years. The
federal administrative route in 42 U.S.C. § 3610 runs alongside it and was read here too: a sworn
complaint to the Secretary within one year, 10-day service and answer, a 100-day investigation and
100-day cause determination, conciliation from filing to charge or dismissal, and mandatory referral
under § 3610(f) to a state or local agency the Secretary has certified as "substantially equivalent" —
the standard ch. 301 was written to meet. The federal election of forum itself is in § 3612, which was
not held, so no federal election deadline appears in this module.

The practices carry personal consequences. Steering is named expressly in Occupations Code
§ 1101.652(b)(32) — which adds ancestry and turns on directing a prospect "interested in equivalent
properties to a different area"; § 301.114 sends a state fair housing order to TREC within 30 days
with a recommendation of discipline; and § 1101.801 confirms TREC discipline does not relieve you of
civil or criminal liability. At the lending desk two regimes overlap without matching: 42 U.S.C.
§ 3605 forbids anyone "whose business includes engaging in residential real estate-related transactions"
to discriminate in making one available or in its terms on the seven fair housing bases, and its
§ 3605(b) definition names "[t]he selling, brokering, or appraising of residential real property," so
your own services are inside it — Property Code § 301.026 says the same in Texas — while ECOA's
§ 1691(a) adds marital status, age and public-assistance income to the bases and names neither familial
status nor handicap, and § 1691(d) gives your client notice within thirty days of a completed
application and a statement of *specific* reasons for any adverse action. Regulation B supplies the
working detail behind that statute: § 1002.2(z) gathers the bases as a "prohibited basis," § 1002.2(c)
defines adverse action with its counteroffer carve-out, § 1002.4(a) and (b) forbid discrimination and
**discouragement** — reaching prospective applicants, images as well as words, and, through
§ 1002.2(l), a person who regularly refers applicants to creditors — § 1002.5 sets out what may and may
not be asked, including the community-property clause that makes a Texas spousal inquiry lawful and the
flat bar on childbearing questions, and § 1002.9 fixes the notice clocks and content and declares an
"internal standards" explanation insufficient.

Two sections of the Fair Housing Act speak to your own side of the business. Section 3606 makes it
unlawful to deny anyone access to or membership or participation in "any multiple-listing service, real
estate brokers' organization or other service, organization, or facility relating to the business of
selling or renting dwellings," or to discriminate in the terms of that access, on the seven bases —
Property Code § 301.027 in nearly identical words — and no § 3603(b) or § 3607 exemption reaches it.
Section 3617 makes it unlawful to "coerce, intimidate, threaten, or interfere with" a person exercising
or enjoying a right under §§ 3603, 3604, 3605 or 3606, or on account of having exercised it, **or on
account of having aided or encouraged any other person** in exercising it — which is why it protects
the license holder who backs a client's request, and not only the client.

And the Community Reinvestment Act stands apart: 12 U.S.C. § 2901 declares an affirmative,
continuing obligation on regulated institutions to help meet their communities' credit needs, enforced
by examination and bounded by safe and sound operation; § 2902 confines that obligation to insured
depository institutions supervised by the Comptroller, the Federal Reserve Board or the FDIC; § 2903(a)
requires the agency to assess the institution's record of meeting the credit needs of "its entire
community, including low- and moderate-income neighborhoods" and to take that record into account on an
application for a deposit facility; and § 2906 requires a written evaluation with a public and a
confidential section, the public section carrying a rating that must be one of four — "Outstanding
record of meeting community credit needs," "Satisfactory record of meeting community credit needs,"
"Needs to improve record of meeting community credit needs," or "Substantial noncompliance in meeting
community credit needs."

statutes a steering complaint violates, identify a residential real estate-related transaction under
