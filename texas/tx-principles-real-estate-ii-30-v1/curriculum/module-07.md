# Texas Principles of Real Estate II — Module 07

## TREC Topic: Real Estate Brokerage and Law of Agency

**Prescribed time: 195 minutes.** This module carries the five units the Commission's PRINS2-1 course
approval form places under *Real Estate Brokerage and Law of Agency*. The form gives this topic no
scope note; none is stated here, and none has been invented to fill the space.

**Learning objectives.** After completing this module you will be able to: define agency and identify
the principal, agent, client and customer in a Texas transaction, distinguish express, implied,
ratified and estoppel-based agency, and distinguish actual from apparent authority; identify which
agency position a Texas license holder occupies in a given transaction — seller representation, buyer
representation, intermediary, or a non-representation showing — and state the written consent,
appointment, notice and written-agreement requirements that attach to each; explain how a Texas
brokerage is organized, how a sales agent's authority flows through a sponsoring broker, and how
compensation is earned, paid and disclosed; state each fiduciary duty and identify the subsection of
Occupations Code § 1101.652 that turns a breach of it into a ground for discipline; and apply the
Texas Deceptive Trade Practices–Consumer Protection Act to a real estate misrepresentation by
identifying whether the complainant is a "consumer," which enumerated practice is implicated, and
what the "knowingly" and "intentionally" findings do to the damages available.

**Every legal statement in this module comes from a source that was read for this course.** Where a
statement is practice rather than law, the module says so and cites nothing for it. This course is not
approved by the Texas Real Estate Commission and no Texas credit is offered for it.

## Unit: The Law of Agency

**Agency** is the legal relationship in which one person — the **agent** — is authorized to act for and
on behalf of another person — the **principal** — in dealings with third persons, and in which the
agent's authorized acts bind the principal as if the principal had acted personally. Every other idea
in this module rests on that sentence. A listing is an agency. A buyer representation agreement is an
agency. The reason a license holder can lose a licence over a sentence said carelessly at a kitchen
table is that the license holder is not merely a helpful person in the room; the license holder is
someone else's legal proxy.

Texas does not leave the existence of the relationship to inference. Occupations Code § 1101.557(a)
states it directly: "A broker who represents a party in a real estate transaction or who lists real
estate for sale under an exclusive agreement for a party is that party's agent." That does two things.
It confirms that representation creates agency, and it makes taking an exclusive listing agency in
itself — you do not have to argue about it.

**The vocabulary, precisely.** The **principal** is the person the agent works for; in ordinary
brokerage speech the principal is the **client**. The person on the other side, whom the license holder
deals with but does not represent, is the **customer**. Neither word is defined in Chapter 1101, and
you should not treat them as statutory terms. What Chapter 1101 does define, for the subchapter
governing practice, is **party**. Under § 1101.551(2), "'Party' means a prospective buyer, seller,
landlord, or tenant or an authorized representative of a buyer, seller, landlord, or tenant, including
a trustee, guardian, executor, administrator, receiver, or attorney-in-fact. The term does not include
a license holder who represents a party." That last clause matters: the license holder is never the
party; the license holder is the agent *of* a party.

**Who is the agent — the broker, not the sales agent.** This is the structural fact students most often
get backwards. Section 1101.002(7) defines a "sales agent" as "a person who is sponsored by a licensed
broker for the purpose of performing an act described by Subdivision (1)" — an act of brokerage. Section
1101.351(c) provides that "A licensed sales agent may not engage or attempt to engage in real estate
brokerage unless the sales agent is sponsored by a licensed broker and is acting for that broker." The
sales agent therefore has no independent brokerage authority. The agency runs between the *broker* and
the client, and the sales agent performs the broker's work. Section 1101.554 reflects this
administratively — the Commission delivers a copy of each sales agent licence to the sponsoring broker —
and § 1101.803 states the consequence: "A licensed broker is liable to the commission, the public, and
the broker's clients for any conduct engaged in under this chapter by the broker or by a sales agent
associated with or acting for the broker."

**What acts require the licence.** Section 1101.002(1) defines "broker" functionally: a person who, for
a commission or other valuable consideration or expecting one, performs for another person any of a
listed set of acts — selling, exchanging, purchasing or leasing real estate; offering to do so;
negotiating or attempting to negotiate a listing, sale, exchange, purchase or lease; listing or offering
to list; auctioning; dealing in options; aiding in locating or obtaining real estate for purchase or
lease; procuring a prospect or procuring property to effect a transaction; controlling the acceptance or
deposit of rent from a resident of a single-family residential unit; providing a written analysis or
opinion of estimated price in the circumstances described in subparagraph (xi); and advising an owner
about a short sale. Section 1101.004(a) restates that as a status, and § 1101.351(a) makes the licence a
precondition: unless a person holds a licence, the person may not act as or represent that the person is
a broker or sales agent, or act as a residential rental locator. Note what the definition turns on —
acting *for another person* with the *expectation of compensation*. Section 1101.004(b) draws the
boundary from the other side: constructing or repairing a building, participating as a principal or
financial manager in a real estate investment, or entering into an obligation secured by an interest in
real property is not, standing alone, brokerage. Section 1101.005 then lists persons and transactions to
which the chapter does not apply at all, including a Texas-licensed attorney, an attorney-in-fact
authorized under a power of attorney to conduct not more than three real estate transactions annually,
an on-site manager of an apartment complex, and an owner or an owner's employee leasing the owner's real
estate.

**How an agency is created.** Agency law generally recognizes four routes, and it is worth being candid
about their source: Chapter 1101 does not enumerate them, so this module presents them as common-law
concepts rather than Texas statutory rules.

**Express agency** arises when the parties say so. A signed listing or buyer representation agreement is
express agency in writing; an oral appointment can be express too. That oral possibility is not
hypothetical here — § 1101.562(a)(1) permits a non-representation showing only where the broker "has not
agreed with the party, either orally or in writing, to represent the party," which tells you the
Legislature treats an oral agreement to represent as capable of creating the relationship. **Implied
agency** arises from conduct rather than words: the license holder advises on price, counsels on
strategy, behaves as the buyer's advocate, and the buyer reasonably understands the license holder to be
working for them. Nobody signed anything. That accident is what the 2026 amendments in the next unit are
built to prevent, and it is what § 1101.652(b)(7) punishes when it makes it a ground for discipline that
a license holder "fails to make clear to all parties to a real estate transaction the party for whom the
license holder is acting." **Ratification** occurs when a principal who did not authorize an act accepts
its benefit afterward with knowledge of the facts. **Agency by estoppel** arises when a principal's own
conduct leads a third person reasonably to believe someone is the principal's agent and that person
relies to their detriment. The last two are judicial doctrines; do not cite Chapter 1101 for them.

**Actual and apparent authority.** **Actual authority** is what the principal really gave the agent —
expressly in the words of the agreement, or by implication as reasonably necessary to carry the express
grant out. **Apparent authority** is what a third person is reasonably led by the principal's conduct to
believe the agent has, even where the agent in fact has less. Actual authority is the boundary the agent
must respect toward the principal; apparent authority is the boundary the principal may be held to toward
the outside world. These are common-law categories, but Texas has legislated at their edges in three ways
you must know.

On the **power to bind**: an ordinary Texas listing does not authorize the broker to sign the contract for
the seller. The broker markets, procures, communicates and negotiates; the party signs. Where a license
holder does hold power to bind, § 1101.557(c)(1) changes the license holder's status — "a license holder
who has the authority to bind a party to a lease or sale under a power of attorney or a property
management agreement is also a party to the lease or sale."

On **liability for the agent's words**: at common law a principal can be exposed for an agent's
misrepresentations, and Texas has narrowed that by statute. Section 1101.805(b) provides that the section
"prevails over any other law, including common law." Under § 1101.805(d), a party is not liable for a
license holder's misrepresentation or concealment of a material fact unless the party "knew of the falsity"
and "failed to disclose the party's knowledge of the falsity." Subsection (e) runs the protection the other
way for the license holder as to a party's misrepresentation, and subsection (c) preserves the broker's
responsibility for a sponsored sales agent's acts and omissions. The seller does not automatically own the
agent's careless sentence, and the agent does not automatically own the seller's lie — but knowledge plus
silence destroys the protection either way.

On the **outer limit of authority**: no agency authorizes practising law. Section 1101.654(a) requires the
Commission to suspend or revoke the licence of a non-attorney license holder who, for consideration, drafts
an instrument other than a form described by § 1101.155 that transfers or affects an interest in real
property, or who advises on the validity or legal sufficiency of an instrument or the validity of title.
Subsection (b) protects the ordinary work: completing a contract form incidental to acting as a broker is
not unauthorized practice if the form was adopted by the Commission for that type of transaction, prepared
and approved by a Texas-licensed attorney for that type of transaction, or prepared by the owner or by an
attorney and required by the owner.

**How an agency ends.** Agency relationships end in the ordinary ways — performance of the object,
expiration of the term, mutual agreement, revocation or renunciation, or an event making performance
impossible, such as death or incapacity of a party or destruction of the property. Those are general agency
principles, not Chapter 1101 rules. What Texas legislates is the term. Section 1101.652(b)(12) makes it a
ground for discipline to fail to specify "a definite termination date that is not subject to prior notice"
in a contract — other than a property management contract — in which the license holder agrees to perform
services requiring a licence. An open-ended listing is not merely poor drafting in Texas; it is a
disciplinary exposure. Section 1101.563(c)(1)(B), effective January 1, 2026, carries the same instinct to
the buyer side by requiring the written agreement with a prospective residential buyer to state a
termination date, and § 1101.563(e)(2) caps a showing-only agreement at fourteen days.

A common error is to assume that when the agreement ends, everything ends. Two obligations in particular
outlive the paperwork — what you may never say about the former client, and what you must do with the
former client's money. Unit 4 takes them up.

## Unit: Agency Positions and Disclosure

There is a small, closed set of positions a Texas license holder can occupy toward a person in a
transaction. Learn them as positions, not as feelings about whom you are helping. In every deal you must be
able to name yours in one sentence and prove it on paper.

**Position one: representing the seller or landlord.** The broker takes a listing and, under § 1101.557(a),
is that party's agent. Section 1101.557(b) then sets a floor of conduct that cannot be bargained away: the
broker "must inform the party if the broker receives material information related to a transaction to list,
buy, sell, or lease the party's real estate, including the receipt of an offer by the broker," and "shall,
at a minimum, answer the party's questions and present any offer to or from the party." A listing broker who
sits on an offer because it looks weak is not exercising judgment; the broker is violating a statutory
minimum.

**Position two: representing the buyer or tenant.** The mirror image, with the same statutory floor and the
fiduciary duties taken up in Unit 4. On the residential side, as of January 1, 2026, this position can no
longer drift into existence — see § 1101.563 below.

**Position three: intermediary.** **Texas does not use "dual agency" the way some other states do.** Do not
carry that phrase into Texas practice. Some states let a broker be the full agent of both buyer and seller
at once, owing each the complete set of fiduciary duties and asking the broker to hold two irreconcilable
loyalties. Texas's answer differs in name and in substance. Chapter 1101, Subchapter L, creates the
**intermediary**, defined in § 1101.551(1): "'Intermediary' means a broker who is employed to negotiate a
transaction between the parties to a transaction and for that purpose may act as an agent of the parties."
The intermediary is not two agents. It is one broker in a defined, statutorily constrained middle position,
and the constraints are the point.

*Getting there requires written consent.* Section 1101.559(a) permits a broker to act as an intermediary
"if: (1) the broker obtains written consent from each party for the broker to act as an intermediary in the
transaction; and (2) the written consent of the parties states the source of any expected compensation to
the broker." Two requirements, both mandatory, neither satisfiable orally. Section 1101.559(b) says where
that consent normally lives: a written listing agreement or a written buyer or tenant representation
agreement "that authorizes a broker to act as an intermediary in a real estate transaction is sufficient to
establish written consent of the party to the transaction if the written agreement specifies in conspicuous
bold or underlined print the conduct that is prohibited under Section 1101.651(d)." That is why Texas
listing and representation agreements carry a conspicuous intermediary paragraph — the consent is captured
at the front end, long before anyone knows whether an in-house match will happen.

*How the intermediary must behave.* Section 1101.559(c) states the standard in five words — "An intermediary
shall act fairly and impartially" — and then answers the question students ask next: "Appointment by a broker
acting as an intermediary of an associated license holder under Section 1101.560 to communicate with, carry
out the instructions of, and provide opinions and advice to the parties to whom that associated license
holder is appointed is a fair and impartial act." Appointing associates to advise each side separately is not
a breach of impartiality; the statute pre-approves it.

*Appointments.* Section 1101.560(a) provides that a broker who complies with § 1101.559's written consent
requirements "may appoint: (1) a license holder associated with the broker to communicate with and carry out
instructions of one party to a real estate transaction; and (2) another license holder associated with the
broker to communicate with and carry out instructions of any other party to the transaction." Section
1101.560(b) sets two conditions: the parties' written consent must authorize the broker to make the
appointment, and "the broker provides written notice of the appointment to all parties involved in the real
estate transaction." Then the payoff, in § 1101.560(c): "A license holder appointed under this section may
provide opinions and advice during negotiations to the party to whom the license holder is appointed."
Without appointments, the intermediary brokerage is limited to fair and impartial facilitation. With them,
each side gets someone who may actually counsel them.

*What an intermediary may never do.* Section 1101.651(d) binds the broker and any broker or sales agent
appointed under § 1101.560. Such a person may not:

- "disclose to the buyer or tenant that the seller or landlord will accept a price less than the asking
  price, unless otherwise instructed in a separate writing by the seller or landlord";
- "disclose to the seller or landlord that the buyer or tenant will pay a price greater than the price
  submitted in a written offer to the seller or landlord, unless otherwise instructed in a separate writing
  by the buyer or tenant";
- "disclose any confidential information or any information a party specifically instructs the broker or
  sales agent in writing not to disclose," unless instructed otherwise in a separate writing by that party,
  unless disclosure is required by Chapter 1101 or a court order, or unless "the information materially
  relates to the condition of the property";
- "treat a party to a transaction dishonestly"; or
- "violate this chapter."

Notice the shape. Permission to disclose price flexibility runs only from the party whose flexibility it is,
and only in a *separate writing* — a clause buried in the listing will not do. And one carve-out runs the
other way: confidentiality never covers information that "materially relates to the condition of the
property." A buyer's confidence about how high they will go is protected. A seller's confidence about a
foundation problem is not.

*The intermediary is not optional once you hold both sides.* Section 1101.561(b): "A broker must agree to act
as an intermediary under this subchapter if the broker agrees to represent in a transaction: (1) a buyer or
tenant; and (2) a seller or landlord." And § 1101.561(a) resolves the conflict-of-laws problem: "The duties
of a license holder acting as an intermediary under this subchapter supersede the duties of a license holder
established under any other law, including common law."

**Position four: showing property without representing anyone.** Effective January 1, 2026, § 1101.562(a)
permits a broker to "show real property available for sale or lease to a party without representing the party
if the broker: (1) has not agreed with the party, either orally or in writing, to represent the party; (2) is
not otherwise acting as the party's agent at the time of showing the real property; (3) does not provide to
the party opinions or advice regarding the real property or real estate transactions in general; and (4) does
not perform any other act of real estate brokerage for the party." Before such a showing, § 1101.562(b)
requires the broker, as applicable, to make the § 1101.558(b) disclosure if the broker represents the owner,
or to provide the written notice described by § 1101.558(b-1) if the broker does not. Section 1101.562(c)
preserves a narrow but essential permission: notwithstanding the bans on opinions, advice and other brokerage
acts, "a broker showing real property under this section may confirm information to a party regarding the
size, price, and terms of the real property available for sale or lease." You may confirm the square footage
and the asking price. You may not tell them it is a good deal.

**The IABS written notice.** Section 1101.558 carries two distinct obligations that students routinely merge.
Subsection (b) is the *representation* disclosure: a license holder who represents a party "shall disclose,
orally or in writing, that representation at the time of the license holder's first contact with: (1) another
party to the transaction; or (2) another license holder who represents another party to the transaction."
Oral suffices; the trigger is first contact.

Subsection (b-1) is the *written notice* the business calls the Information About Brokerage Services form. "At
the time of a license holder's first substantive communication with a party relating to a proposed transaction
regarding specific real property, the license holder shall provide to the party written notice in at least a
10-point font that: (1) describes the ways in which a broker can represent a party to a real estate
transaction, including as an intermediary; (2) describes the basic duties and obligations a broker has to a
party to a real estate transaction that the broker represents; (3) describes the basic obligations a broker
has to a party to a real estate transaction that the broker does not represent; and (4) provides the name,
license number, and contact information for the license holder and the license holder's supervisor and broker,
if applicable." Under subsection (b-2) the Commission by rule prescribes the text required by (b-1)(1) and (2)
and establishes the delivery methods, so exact wording and delivery are matters of Commission rule rather than
of the statute. TREC's article on the 2026 changes states that the changes effective January 1, 2026 to the
IABS reflect the new notice requirements.

Section 1101.558(c) excuses the (b-1) notice in three situations: "(1) the proposed transaction is for a
residential lease for less than one year and a sale is not being considered; (2) the license holder meets with
a party who the license holder knows is represented by another license holder; or (3) the communication occurs
at a property that is held open for any prospective buyer or tenant and the communication concerns that
property." The third is the open-house exception, and it is narrower than its reputation: it excuses the
*notice*, at that property, about that property. It excuses nothing else — and after January 1, 2026 something
else is required.

**The § 1101.563 written agreement, effective January 1, 2026.** Section 1101.563(a) defines "residential real
property" for the section as a single-family house; a duplex, triplex or quadraplex; or a unit in a multiunit
residential structure in which title to an individual unit is transferred to the unit owner under a condominium
or cooperative system. Subsection (b) states the rule: "A license holder who performs any act of real estate
brokerage for a prospective buyer of residential real property must enter into a written agreement with the
prospective buyer before: (1) showing any residential real property to the prospective buyer; or (2) if no
residential real property will be shown, presenting an offer to purchase residential real property on behalf of
the prospective buyer."

Subsection (c) prescribes the contents: the services to be provided; the termination date; whether the
agreement is exclusive or non-exclusive; as applicable, that the license holder either represents the
prospective buyer as the buyer's agent or does not represent the buyer if the only brokerage act is showing
property under § 1101.562; and the amount or rate of compensation the broker will receive and how it will be
determined. It must also "disclose in conspicuous language that broker compensation is not set by law and is
fully negotiable." Subsection (d) requires a separate agreement if additional brokerage acts follow a
showing-only agreement. Subsection (e) caps the showing-only agreement twice over: it "may not: (1) be an
exclusive agreement with the license holder; or (2) state a termination date more than fourteen days from the
date the agreement is entered into."

Skipping it is not merely a lost deal. Section 1101.652(b)(34) makes it a ground for suspension or revocation
that a license holder "fails to enter into a written agreement with a prospective buyer as required by Section
1101.563."

**The open house, worked through.** TREC's article on the 2026 changes gives two scenarios that show how
§ 1101.558(c)(3), § 1101.562 and § 1101.563 interact. Where the agent hosting the open house is a member of
the listing broker's office, the article states the agent does not need to provide the IABS or enter into a
written agreement with prospective buyers, but will need to disclose that he or she represents the owner,
orally or in writing, as § 1101.558(b) requires. Where the hosting agent is not a member of the listing
broker's office and does not represent the seller — a showing agent, for instance — the article states the
agent must provide the IABS and enter into a written agreement as described by § 1101.563(c) with prospective
buyers, and that this is so even when a prospective buyer already has a written representation agreement with
another broker. The article adds that by law the IABS must be provided and the agreement entered into before
the showing, so ideally the agreement is presented and signed before the buyer enters the property, or at
least before the buyer starts viewing it. It also states the uncomfortable consequence: if a buyer refuses to
sign, the agent cannot show the property, and license holders can face disciplinary action for failing to
enter into the agreement § 1101.563 requires. Whether a broker permits its agents to host open houses for
another brokerage is, the article notes, a matter for brokerage policy rather than a statutory rule.

**Subagency, handled accurately.** TREC's article states that two references to subagency were removed from
TRELA, so the circumstances in which subagency could arise in practice are limited; it also states that
subagency has not been eliminated generally as a legal concept, and advises a license holder who thinks
subagency may be involved to contact the broker or an attorney. Both halves matter. Do not tell a Texas
consumer that subagency "no longer exists." Say that TRELA no longer references it, that its practical field
has narrowed, and that whether it arises in an unusual arrangement is a question for the broker and counsel.

**Two provisions police all of this.** Section 1101.652(b)(7) reaches a license holder who "fails to make clear
to all parties to a real estate transaction the party for whom the license holder is acting." Section
1101.652(b)(8) reaches one who "receives compensation from more than one party to a real estate transaction
without the full knowledge and consent of all parties to the transaction." Read with § 1101.559(a)(2)'s
requirement that intermediary consent state the source of expected compensation, they express a single Texas
instinct: whom you serve and who pays you must both be on the table before the money moves.

## Unit: Brokerage & Nature of the Brokerage Business

A brokerage is a business built on a licence, and nearly everything distinctive about how it is organized traces
back to the fact that the licence — not the salesperson's reputation, not the office lease, not the sign — is
what the law regulates. This unit describes the legal architecture and the way the business actually runs, and
labels which is which.

**The broker is the licensed enterprise.** Section 1101.002(4) defines "license holder" as a broker or sales
agent licensed under Chapter 1101. A broker may be an individual or a business entity. Section 1101.351(a-1)
provides that unless a business entity holds a licence, the entity may not act as a broker. Section 1101.355(a)
adds two eligibility requirements for an entity: it must "designate one of its managing officers as its broker
for purposes of this chapter," and it must "provide proof that the entity maintains errors and omissions
insurance with a minimum annual limit of $1 million for each occurrence if the designated broker owns less than
10 percent of the business entity." Subsection (b) bars an entity from acting as a broker unless its designated
broker is a licensed individual broker in active status and good standing according to Commission records, and
subsection (b-1) lets the Commission weigh, in judging good standing, the disciplinary history of the broker or
of entities the broker serves or served as designated broker, and amounts owed to the Commission.

There is a narrow registration alternative for the entity through which many individual license holders take
their pay. Section 1101.355(c) requires a business entity that receives compensation on behalf of a license
holder to be licensed as a broker. But § 1101.355(d) excuses licensure where the entity receives compensation on
behalf of a licensed broker or sales agent that the license holder earned while engaged in brokerage, performs
no other acts of a broker, is a limited liability company as defined by § 101.001 of the Business Organizations
Code or an S corporation as defined by 26 U.S.C. § 1361, is registered with the Commission, and is at least
51 percent owned by the license holder on whose behalf it receives compensation. Subsection (e) directs the
Commission to adopt registration rules.

**Becoming a broker.** Section 1101.356(a) requires an applicant for a broker licence to show at least four years
of active experience as a license holder during the 60 months preceding the application, the qualifying and
related coursework required by Commission rule (not to exceed 60 semester hours or equivalent classroom hours),
and attendance at the broker responsibility course approved under § 1101.458, which may count toward the
related-course hours. Subsection (b) exempts an applicant already licensed as a broker in a state with comparable
requirements; subsection (b-1) leaves "active experience" to Commission rule. That four-year requirement is why a
Texas brokerage is not a flat organization of equals: the person carrying the licence has, by statutory design,
done the work longer.

**Where the brokerage sits.** Section 1101.552(a) requires a resident broker to maintain a fixed office in Texas,
with the office address designated on the licence; subsection (b) requires an application designating a new
location not later than the tenth day after moving. Subsection (d) provides that a nonresident licensed broker
need not maintain a Texas place of business. Subsection (e) requires every license holder to give the Commission
a current mailing address, business address, business telephone number and business e-mail address, plus the name
of the associated broker if the license holder is an associated broker; subsection (f) requires notice of changes.
Subsection (g) defines "associated broker" as "a broker who associates with and is paid through another broker
under a relationship that is intended to be a continuous relationship, including as an employee or an ongoing
independent contractor."

**Sponsorship: the spine of the organization.** Everything a sales agent does is done for the broker. Section
1101.351(c) forbids a sales agent from engaging or attempting to engage in brokerage unless sponsored by a
licensed broker and acting for that broker; § 1101.554 has the Commission deliver a copy of the sales agent
licence to the sponsoring broker; and § 1101.803 makes the broker liable to the Commission, the public and the
broker's clients for the conduct of the broker and of any sales agent associated with or acting for the broker.
Section 1101.652(b)(26) makes it a ground for discipline to establish "an association by employment or otherwise
with a person other than a license holder if the person is expected or required to act as a license holder" — the
provision that stops a brokerage from staffing production with unlicensed assistants quietly doing licensed work.

As a matter of business practice rather than law, most Texas sales agents are engaged as independent contractors
rather than employees, are paid by commission split rather than salary, and pay some combination of desk,
transaction or franchise fees. None of that is prescribed by Chapter 1101, and nothing is cited for it here. What
Chapter 1101 insists on is that whatever the internal economics, the *authority* is the broker's.

**Supervision.** Section 1101.458(a) provides that during the term of the current licence, a broker responsibility
course approved by the Commission must be attended by a licensed broker and by a sales agent "who supervises
another license holder." Subsection (b) has the Commission prescribe the course's title, content and duration by
rule and caps it at six classroom hours; subsection (c) lets those hours count toward the hours described by
§ 1101.455(f); subsection (d) exempts a broker exempt from continuing education under § 1101.456. Read with
§ 1101.803, the design is plain: the statute assigns the liability to the broker and then requires the broker —
and any supervising sales agent — to be trained in carrying it.

**Listing and buyer representation agreements.** The business often calls these **employment contracts**: the
client engages the brokerage to perform services and agrees what it will be paid if it performs. That is a
teaching convention, not a statutory label, and it does not make the sales agent an employee of the client. What
the law adds is specific.

First, a **definite termination date** under § 1101.652(b)(12). Second, on the residential buyer side, the
**§ 1101.563 written agreement** described in the previous unit, with its required contents, its separate-agreement
rule in subsection (d), and its non-exclusivity and fourteen-day cap for showing-only agreements in subsection (e).
Third, the **writing requirement for suing over a commission**: § 1101.806(c) provides that "A person may not
maintain an action in this state to recover a commission for the sale or purchase of real estate unless the promise
or agreement on which the action is based, or a memorandum, is in writing and signed by the party against whom the
action is brought or by a person authorized by that party to sign the document." Section 1101.806(b) adds that no
one may maintain an action to collect compensation for a brokerage act performed in Texas without alleging and
proving that the person was a license holder when the act was commenced, or an attorney licensed in any state.
Subsection (a) carves out agreements to share compensation among license holders and preserves causes of action
among brokers for interference with business relationships. Subsection (d) attaches a striking forfeiture: a license
holder who fails to advise a buyer as provided by § 1101.555 "may not receive payment of or recover any commission
agreed to be paid on the sale." Section 1101.555 requires that when an offer to purchase Texas real estate is
signed, the license holder shall advise each buyer in writing that the buyer should either have the abstract
examined by an attorney of the buyer's choosing or be provided with or obtain a title insurance policy. The same
failure is independently a ground for discipline under § 1101.652(b)(29).

**Compensation: how it flows, and what you must say about it.** Three rules control the flow. Section 1101.651(a):
a licensed broker may not pay a commission to or otherwise compensate a person directly or indirectly for
performing an act of a broker unless the person is a license holder, or a broker licensed in another state who
conducts none of the negotiations in Texas. Section 1101.651(b): a sales agent may not accept compensation for a
real estate transaction from anyone other than the sponsoring broker, or the broker who was sponsoring the agent
when the compensation was earned. Section 1101.651(c): a sales agent may not pay a commission to a person except
through the sponsoring broker. Section 1101.652(b)(11) makes paying or dividing a fee with someone who is not a
license holder or a broker or sales agent licensed in another state a ground for discipline. The effect is a closed
loop: brokerage money moves broker-to-broker and broker-to-sponsored-agent, and a grateful buyer cannot hand a
cheque directly to the sales agent who found the house.

Now the sentence that must be said out loud. **Broker compensation is not set by law and is fully negotiable.**
That is a statutory disclosure, not a slogan: § 1101.563(c)(2) requires the written agreement with a prospective
residential buyer to "disclose in conspicuous language that broker compensation is not set by law and is fully
negotiable," and § 1101.563(c)(1)(E) requires the agreement to state "the amount or rate of compensation the broker
will receive and how this amount will be determined." Chapter 1101 contains no commission rate and recognizes no
customary rate. Telling a consumer that a rate is standard or required is false, and if it misleads it is exposed
under § 1101.652(b)(23)(A) and under the DTPA in Unit 5. Related money duties: § 1101.652(b)(13) reaches accepting,
receiving or charging "an undisclosed commission, rebate, or direct profit on an expenditure made for a principal,"
and § 1101.652(b)(8) reaches compensation from more than one party without all parties' full knowledge and consent.

*Illustration, for arithmetic only.* Suppose a Texas seller and a listing broker negotiate a total fee of 5.5% on a
$400,000 sale, and the listing broker's written agreement with a cooperating broker allocates 2.5 percentage points
to the cooperating side. Total fee: $400,000 × 0.055 = $22,000. Cooperating side: $400,000 × 0.025 = $10,000.
Listing side: $22,000 − $10,000 = $12,000. If the listing brokerage's internal split with its sponsored sales agent
is 70/30 in the agent's favour, the agent's gross share is $12,000 × 0.70 = $8,400 and the brokerage retains $3,600
— and under § 1101.651(b) that $8,400 must reach the agent through the sponsoring broker, not from the title company
or the seller. Every number here is invented for the arithmetic. None is standard, customary or required, and none
should be repeated to a consumer as though it were.

**Advertising, names and teams.** Section 1101.652(b)(23) makes it a ground for discipline to publish or cause to be
published an advertisement that "(A) misleads or is likely to deceive the public; (B) tends to create a misleading
impression; (C) implies that a sales agent is responsible for the operation of the broker's real estate brokerage
business; or (D) fails to include the name of the broker for whom the license holder acts, which name may be the
licensed name, assumed name, or trade name of the broker as authorized by a law of this state and registered with
the commission." Subparagraphs (C) and (D) together are the statutory basis for how Texas treats **team names**: a
team may market under a name, but the advertising must carry the broker's name and must not imply that the team or
the sales agent runs the brokerage. The detailed requirements for registering assumed names, alternate names and
team names are matters of Commission rule; those rules are not among the sources read for this module, so nothing
further about them is stated here.

Section 1101.652(b)(18) forbids placing a sign offering property for sale or lease without the owner's or authorized
agent's written consent; (b)(19) and (b)(20) forbid offering to sell or lease property without the owner's knowledge
and consent or on unauthorized terms. Section 1101.804 protects anyone assembling market data: a license holder or
nonprofit real estate board or association that provides information about sales prices or terms of sale to
facilitate listing, selling, leasing, financing or appraisal "is not liable to another person for providing that
information unless the disclosure of that information is specifically prohibited by statute."

**Poaching and interference.** Section 1101.652(b)(22) makes it a ground for discipline to negotiate or attempt to
negotiate a sale, exchange or lease with an owner, landlord, buyer or tenant "with knowledge that that person is a
party to an outstanding written contract that grants exclusive agency to another broker in connection with the
transaction," and (b)(21) reaches inducing a party to break a contract to substitute a new one. Section
1101.557(b)(1) forbids a representing broker from instructing another broker to violate (b)(22). But § 1101.557(c)(3)
creates a careful safe harbour: sole delivery of an offer to a party does not violate (b)(22) if the party's broker
consents, a copy is sent to the party's broker unless a sealed-bid governmental process forbids it, and the person
delivering engages in no other conduct violating (b)(22).

**Two structural cautions.** Section 1101.652(b)(16) makes it a ground for discipline to act "in a dual capacity as
broker and undisclosed principal in a real estate transaction," and § 1101.652(a-1)(1) reaches misrepresentation,
dishonesty or fraud when a license holder buys, sells, trades or leases property in the license holder's own name, a
spouse's name, or the name of a person related within the first degree by consanguinity. Buying for your own account
is lawful; doing it without saying so is not. And § 1101.801 removes any comfort from surviving a Commission
proceeding: "Disciplinary action taken against a person under Section 1101.652 does not relieve the person from civil
or criminal liability." The licence and the lawsuit are separate exposures.

## Unit: Fiduciary Duties

An agent occupies a position of trust, and the duties flowing from it are **fiduciary duties**. They require the agent
to subordinate the agent's own interest to the client's to a degree ordinary arm's-length dealing never demands. The
classical catalogue has six entries. Chapter 1101 does not set them out as a list under that heading, but nearly every
one has a Texas disciplinary provision behind it. This unit pairs each duty with that provision and is explicit where
no held source supplies one.

One Texas override first. Section 1101.561(a) provides that the duties of a license holder acting as an intermediary
under Subchapter L "supersede the duties of a license holder established under any other law, including common law."
When you are an intermediary, the governing description of your obligations is the Subchapter L framework from Unit 2
— written consent, fair and impartial conduct, the appointment mechanics, and the five prohibitions of § 1101.651(d) —
not the single-agency catalogue below.

**Loyalty.** The agent acts in the client's interest, not the agent's own, and does not profit secretly from the
agency. Three provisions enforce it. Section 1101.652(b)(13) reaches accepting, receiving or charging "an undisclosed
commission, rebate, or direct profit on an expenditure made for a principal" — the repair invoice with a quiet markup,
the vendor kickback. Section 1101.652(b)(16) reaches acting "in a dual capacity as broker and undisclosed principal in
a real estate transaction" — buying the client's listing, directly or through a controlled entity, without saying the
buyer is you. Section 1101.652(b)(8) reaches "compensation from more than one party to a real estate transaction
without the full knowledge and consent of all parties," which is also why § 1101.559(a)(2) requires intermediary
consent to state the source of expected compensation. Notice what each condemns: not the profit, not the second
cheque, not the purchase — the *undisclosed* profit, the *unconsented* second cheque, the *undisclosed* self-dealing.
Disclosure converts a breach into a permissible arrangement.

**Obedience to lawful instructions.** The agent follows the client's instructions within the scope of the agency —
lawful ones only. Sections 1101.652(b)(19) and (20) supply the enforcement: it is a ground for discipline to offer to
sell or lease property "without the knowledge and consent of the owner of the real property or the owner's authorized
agent," or "on terms other than those authorized by the owner." Section 1101.652(b)(18) adds the sign. The limiting
word is *lawful*. A seller's instruction not to present offers from buyers of a particular race is not one the agent
may obey: § 1101.652(b)(32) makes discrimination against an owner, potential buyer, landlord or potential tenant on
the basis of race, color, religion, sex, disability, familial status, national origin or ancestry — including steering
a prospect interested in equivalent properties to a different area on those bases — an independent ground for
discipline.

**Disclosure.** The agent tells the client what the client needs to know. Section 1101.557(b)(2) requires a representing
broker to inform the party of "material information related to a transaction to list, buy, sell, or lease the party's
real estate, including the receipt of an offer by the broker," and (b)(3) requires the broker at a minimum to answer
the party's questions and present any offer to or from the party. Toward the other side of the table the duty is sharper
than students expect. Section 1101.652(b)(3) reaches "a material misrepresentation to a potential buyer concerning a
significant defect, including a latent structural defect, known to the license holder that would be a significant factor
to a reasonable and prudent buyer in making a decision to purchase real property," and (b)(4) reaches failing to
disclose such a known defect to a potential buyer. That duty runs to a *potential buyer* — a customer, not a client.
Representing the seller does not license you to conceal a known significant defect from the person across the table.

Two boundaries deserve equal emphasis. Section 1101.556 provides that notwithstanding other law, a license holder is not
required to inquire about, disclose or release information relating to whether a previous or current occupant had, may
have had, has or may have AIDS, an HIV-related illness or an HIV infection as defined by the Centers for Disease Control
and Prevention, or whether "a death occurred on a property by natural causes, suicide, or accident unrelated to the
condition of the property"; § 1101.802 adds that a person is not civilly or criminally liable for failing to inquire
about or disclose the HIV/AIDS matters described. Running the other way, § 1101.555 imposes the affirmative written
advisory about an abstract examination or title policy, with discipline under § 1101.652(b)(29) and loss of commission
under § 1101.806(d) if it is skipped.

**Confidentiality.** The agent does not disclose the client's confidences, above all those that weaken the client's
bargaining position. In the intermediary setting the duty is statutory: § 1101.651(d)(1) and (2) forbid disclosing that
the seller will take less or that the buyer will pay more absent a separate written instruction from that party, and
(d)(3) forbids disclosing "any confidential information or any information a party specifically instructs the broker or
sales agent in writing not to disclose," subject to three exceptions — a separate written instruction, a requirement to
disclose under Chapter 1101 or a court order, and information that "materially relates to the condition of the property."
Outside the intermediary setting Chapter 1101 contains no general confidentiality section, and this module does not
pretend otherwise; in ordinary single agency the duty is a common-law fiduciary duty. But the property-condition
carve-out in (d)(3) shows how Texas resolves the tension whenever it arises: negotiating posture is confidential,
defects in the house are not.

**Reasonable care and diligence.** Section 1101.652(b)(1) reaches a license holder who "acts negligently or
incompetently." Section 1101.652(a)(6) reaches one who "fails to consider market conditions for the specific geographic
area in which the license holder is providing a service." Section 1101.652(b)(2) reaches conduct "that is dishonest or
in bad faith or that demonstrates untrustworthiness," and (b)(5) reaches "a false promise that is likely to influence a
person to enter into an agreement when the license holder is unable or does not intend to keep the promise." Section
1101.652(b)(28) requires providing, on request, a copy of a transaction document to a person who signed it, and
§ 1101.652(a)(5) reaches failing to surrender to the owner, without just cause, a requested document in the license
holder's possession. Care is also where competence has an edge: § 1101.654 draws the line at practising law, and the
diligent answer to a legal question is a referral, not a guess.

**Accounting.** Section 1101.652(b)(9) reaches failing "within a reasonable time to properly account for or remit money
that is received by the license holder and that belongs to another person." Section 1101.652(b)(10) reaches commingling
another person's money with the license holder's own. Section 1101.652(b)(30) requires depositing escrow or trust funds
within a reasonable time, either in trust with a title company authorized to do business in Texas or in a custodial,
trust or escrow account maintained for that purpose in a Texas-authorized banking institution. Section 1101.652(b)(31)
forbids disbursing from such an account "before the completion or termination of the real estate transaction." Section
1101.652(b)(24) adds the paperwork: withholding from or inserting into a statement of account or invoice a statement the
license holder knows makes it materially inaccurate.

**Which duties survive termination.** The classical teaching is that the forward-looking duties end with the agency —
loyalty in future dealings, obedience, the duty to keep working — while two continue: **confidentiality**, because the
client's secrets do not become yours when the listing expires, and **accounting**, because money belonging to another
does not become yours when the relationship ends. Be precise about authority. No source read for this module states a
survival rule in so many words. What the text supports is this: the accounting provisions in § 1101.652(b)(9), (10),
(30) and (31) speak of money "that belongs to another person" and of the transaction's completion or termination, not of
a live agency, so an expired listing does not release the license holder from them; and the confidentiality prohibition
in § 1101.651(d)(3) contains no expiration and no exception for a concluded transaction. Beyond that textual
observation, the survival of confidentiality in ordinary single agency is a common-law proposition, identified here as
one rather than cited to a statute.

Finally, hold on to § 1101.801: "Disciplinary action taken against a person under Section 1101.652 does not relieve the
person from civil or criminal liability." A fiduciary breach in Texas can cost the licence and cost a judgment, and
neither proceeding substitutes for the other.

## Unit: Texas Deceptive Trade Practices - Consumer Protection Act

Business & Commerce Code Chapter 17, Subchapter E — cited under its own short title in § 17.41 as the **Deceptive Trade
Practices-Consumer Protection Act** — is the statute that most often turns a careless sentence about a property into
personal liability for the person who said it. It is not a real estate statute. It is a general consumer statute, which
is precisely why it reaches so far into this business.

**How it is read.** Section 17.44(a) instructs that the subchapter "shall be liberally construed and applied to promote
its underlying purposes, which are to protect consumers against false, misleading, and deceptive business practices,
unconscionable actions, and breaches of warranty and to provide efficient and economical procedures to secure such
protection." Section 17.44(b) provides that Chapter 27, Property Code, prevails over the subchapter to the extent of any
conflict. Section 17.43 makes the remedies cumulative rather than exclusive, while cautioning that a violation of another
law is not by itself a DTPA violation and that no recovery of both damages and penalties under the DTPA and another law
is permitted for the same act.

**Waiver is presumptively void.** Section 17.42(a): "Any waiver by a consumer of the provisions of this subchapter is
contrary to public policy and is unenforceable and void" — valid only if it is in writing and signed by the consumer, the
consumer is not in a significantly disparate bargaining position, and the consumer is represented by legal counsel in
seeking or acquiring the goods or services. Section 17.42(b) voids it if that counsel was directly or indirectly
identified, suggested or selected by a defendant or a defendant's agent, and § 17.42(c) requires the waiver to be
conspicuous, in boldface type of at least 10 points, headed "Waiver of Consumer Rights" or words of similar meaning, and
substantially in the statutory form. The inference for a license holder is obvious: you cannot paper your way out of the
DTPA with a clause in your own agreement.

**Who is a "consumer."** This is the threshold question and the one students skip. Section 17.45(4): "'Consumer' means an
individual, partnership, corporation, this state, or a subdivision or agency of this state who seeks or acquires by
purchase or lease, any goods or services, except that the term does not include a business consumer that has assets of
$25 million or more, or that is owned or controlled by a corporation or entity with assets of $25 million or more." Two
elements: the person must *seek or acquire* — an actual or prospective purchaser or lessee, not a bystander — and what is
sought must be *goods or services*.

Both terms reach real estate. Section 17.45(1): "'Goods' means tangible chattels or real property purchased or leased for
use." Section 17.45(2): "'Services' means work, labor, or service purchased or leased for use, including services
furnished in connection with the sale or repair of goods." Section 17.45(6) defines "trade" and "commerce" as "the
advertising, offering for sale, sale, lease, or distribution of any good or service, of any property, tangible or
intangible, real, personal, or mixed," including trade or commerce directly or indirectly affecting the people of Texas.
So a Texas buyer of a house is a consumer seeking goods, and a client engaging a brokerage is a consumer seeking services.
Section 17.45(10) defines "business consumer" — one seeking goods or services for commercial or business use, excluding
the state and its subdivisions — the category the $25 million ceiling operates on. Section 17.45(12) defines "residence"
as a single-family house, duplex, triplex, quadruplex, or condominium or cooperative unit occupied or to be occupied as
the consumer's residence; that definition matters for the exemptions below.

**What is unlawful.** Section 17.46(a) declares that "False, misleading, or deceptive acts or practices in the conduct of
any trade or commerce are hereby declared unlawful," subject to action by the consumer protection division of the attorney
general's office under §§ 17.47, 17.58, 17.60 and 17.61. Section 17.46(b) then supplies the **laundry list**, introduced by
the words "includes, but is not limited to, the following acts." That open-ended phrase governs enforcement by the attorney
general. For a *private consumer suit* the list is closed: § 17.46(d) provides that for the relief authorized in
§ 17.50(a)(1), the term "is limited to the acts enumerated in specific subdivisions of Subsection (b)." A private plaintiff
must point at a numbered item.

The items a license holder is most likely to meet, in the statute's words:

- **§ 17.46(b)(5)** — "representing that goods or services have sponsorship, approval, characteristics, ingredients, uses,
  benefits, or quantities which they do not have or that a person has a sponsorship, approval, status, affiliation, or
  connection which the person does not." This is the workhorse. "The addition was permitted." "It's zoned for a duplex."
  "That's a new roof." Each is a representation about a characteristic, use or benefit.
- **§ 17.46(b)(7)** — "representing that goods or services are of a particular standard, quality, or grade, or that goods
  are of a particular style or model, if they are of another."
- **§ 17.46(b)(9)** — "advertising goods or services with intent not to sell them as advertised."
- **§ 17.46(b)(12)** — "representing that an agreement confers or involves rights, remedies, or obligations which it does
  not have or involve, or which are prohibited by law."
- **§ 17.46(b)(14)** — "misrepresenting the authority of a salesman, representative or agent to negotiate the final terms of
  a consumer transaction." Overstating your own authority to bind is itself an enumerated deceptive act.
- **§ 17.46(b)(20)** — "representing that a guaranty or warranty confers or involves rights or remedies which it does not
  have or involve."
- **§ 17.46(b)(24)** — "failing to disclose information concerning goods or services which was known at the time of the
  transaction if such failure to disclose such information was intended to induce the consumer into a transaction into which
  the consumer would not have entered had the information been disclosed." Read every element: the information must have been
  *known*, at the time, and the failure must have been *intended to induce*. Silence alone is not (b)(24); silence deployed to
  get the deal signed is.

Section 17.46(c)(1) adds that in construing subsection (a) in suits under § 17.47, courts are to be guided so far as possible
by subsection (b) and by the interpretations the Federal Trade Commission and the federal courts have given section 5(a)(1)
of the Federal Trade Commission Act.

**The consumer's causes of action.** Section 17.50(a): "A consumer may maintain an action where any of the following
constitute a producing cause of economic damages or damages for mental anguish": (1) use of a false, misleading or deceptive
act "specifically enumerated in a subdivision of Subsection (b) of Section 17.46" *and* "relied on by a consumer to the
consumer's detriment"; (2) breach of an express or implied warranty; (3) "any unconscionable action or course of action by any
person"; or (4) an act in violation of Chapter 541, Insurance Code. Two features of (a)(1) matter: the standard is **producing
cause**, not proximate cause, and the laundry-list route requires **detrimental reliance**. "Unconscionable action or course of
action" is defined in § 17.45(5) as "an act or practice which, to a consumer's detriment, takes advantage of the lack of
knowledge, ability, experience, or capacity of the consumer to a grossly unfair degree" — a high bar, but a route that depends
on no numbered item.

**Damages, and what "knowingly" and "intentionally" do to them.** Section 17.50(b)(1) is worth knowing verbatim. A prevailing
consumer may obtain "the amount of economic damages found by the trier of fact. If the trier of fact finds that the conduct of
the defendant was committed knowingly, the consumer may also recover damages for mental anguish, as found by the trier of fact,
and the trier of fact may award not more than three times the amount of economic damages; or if the trier of fact finds the
conduct was committed intentionally, the consumer may recover damages for mental anguish, as found by the trier of fact, and
the trier of fact may award not more than three times the amount of damages for mental anguish and economic damages."

Parse the tiers. **Knowingly** unlocks mental anguish damages plus additional damages of up to three times *economic* damages.
**Intentionally** unlocks mental anguish damages plus additional damages of up to three times the *combined* mental anguish and
economic damages. The base is defined in § 17.45(11): "'Economic damages' means compensatory damages for pecuniary loss,
including costs of repair and replacement," excluding exemplary damages and damages for physical pain and mental anguish, loss
of consortium, disfigurement, physical impairment, or loss of companionship and society.

The mental states are the practical heart of DTPA risk management. Section 17.45(9): "'Knowingly' means actual awareness, at the
time of the act or practice complained of, of the falsity, deception, or unfairness of the act or practice giving rise to the
consumer's claim... but actual awareness may be inferred where objective manifestations indicate that a person acted with actual
awareness." Section 17.45(13): "'Intentionally' means actual awareness of the falsity, deception, or unfairness of the act or
practice... coupled with the specific intent that the consumer act in detrimental reliance on the falsity or deception or in
detrimental ignorance of the unfairness," and intention "may be inferred from objective manifestations that indicate that the
person acted intentionally or from facts showing that a defendant acted with flagrant disregard of prudent and fair business
practices." Note both inference clauses: neither state requires a confession.

Section 17.50(b) also authorizes injunctive relief, orders restoring money or property acquired in violation of the subchapter,
and other relief the court deems proper. Section 17.50(d) provides that "Each consumer who prevails shall be awarded court costs
and reasonable and necessary attorneys' fees" — mandatory, and often the largest number in a small case; § 17.50(c) balances it
by requiring an award of fees and costs to the defendant on a finding that the action was groundless in fact or law, brought in
bad faith, or brought for harassment. Section 17.50(e) excludes attorneys' fees, costs and prejudgment interest from the
computation of additional damages, and § 17.50(g) provides that Chapter 41, Civil Practice and Remedies Code, does not apply.

**Pre-suit notice and the settlement window.** Section 17.505(a) makes notice a prerequisite: "As a prerequisite to filing a suit
seeking damages under Subdivision (1) of Subsection (b) of Section 17.50 of this subchapter against any person, a consumer shall
give written notice to the person at least 60 days before filing the suit advising the person in reasonable detail of the
consumer's specific complaint and the amount of economic damages, damages for mental anguish, and expenses, including attorneys'
fees, if any, reasonably incurred by the consumer in asserting the claim." Section 17.505(b) excuses notice where 60 days is
impracticable because suit must be filed to prevent limitations from running, or where the claim is a counterclaim. Section
17.505(c) lets a defendant who received no notice file a plea in abatement not later than the 30th day after filing an original
answer; under (d) the court shall abate after a hearing, and the suit abates automatically on the 11th day after a verified,
uncontroverted plea; under (e) the abatement continues until the 60th day after notice is properly served.

That period is an opportunity, not a formality. Section 17.5052(a) permits a person who receives § 17.505 notice to tender an
offer of settlement during the 60 days after receipt. Under § 17.5052(d) the offer must state separately an amount settling the
damages claim and an amount for the consumer's reasonable and necessary attorneys' fees incurred as of the offer date, and under
(e) it is rejected unless both parts are accepted within 30 days. Subsections (g) and (h) supply the incentive: if the court finds
the damages amount tendered was the same as, substantially the same as, or more than the damages found by the trier of fact, the
consumer may not recover damages exceeding the lesser of the amount tendered or the amount found, and fees may be capped at the
amount tendered on the same test. Subsection (k) provides that an offer is not an admission of liability. Section 17.5051 allows a
party to move to compel mediation within 90 days after service of a pleading seeking DTPA relief.

**Defenses.** Section 17.506(a) gives a defense to any damages or attorneys' fees on proof that "before consummation of the
transaction he gave reasonable and timely written notice to the plaintiff of the defendant's reliance on" written information
about the particular goods or service obtained from official government records or another source, or written information about a
test required or prescribed by a government agency — in each case where the information was false or inaccurate and "the defendant
did not know and could not reasonably have known of the falsity or inaccuracy." Section 17.506(b) requires the defendant to prove
that information was a producing cause of the damage, and provides that one producing cause does not bar recovery if other conduct
of the defendant was also a producing cause; § 17.506(c) permits suit against the third party who supplied it, without privity,
where that party knew or should reasonably have foreseen it would reach a consumer. Section 17.506(d) supplies a second, complete
defense: a defendant who proves he received the consumer's notice and, "within 30 days after the day on which the defendant
received the notice," tendered the economic and mental anguish damages claimed plus the consumer's reasonably incurred expenses
including attorneys' fees, has a defense to the cause of action. Section 17.565 sets the limitations period — two years after the
act or practice occurred, or two years after the consumer discovered or should reasonably have discovered it, extendable by 180
days where the plaintiff proves the delay was caused by the defendant knowingly engaging in conduct solely calculated to induce
postponement of suit.

**The exemptions — including one written for license holders.** Section 17.49(c) is the **professional services exemption**:
"Nothing in this subchapter shall apply to a claim for damages based on the rendering of a professional service, the essence of
which is the providing of advice, judgment, opinion, or similar professional skill." It then removes five things from that
protection: an express misrepresentation of a material fact that cannot be characterized as advice, judgment or opinion; a failure
to disclose in violation of § 17.46(b)(24); an unconscionable action or course of action that cannot be so characterized; breach of
an express warranty that cannot be so characterized; and a violation of § 17.46(b)(26).

Subsection (i) is written for this profession, and every Texas license holder should be able to recite its shape: "Nothing in this
subchapter shall apply to a claim against a person licensed as a broker or salesperson under Chapter 1101, Occupations Code,
arising from an act or omission by the person while acting as a broker or salesperson. This exemption does not apply to: (1) an
express misrepresentation of a material fact that cannot be characterized as advice, judgment, or opinion; (2) a failure to
disclose information in violation of Section 17.46(b)(24); or (3) an unconscionable action or course of action that cannot be
characterized as advice, judgment, or opinion."

Understand what that gives you and what it does not. It protects the *advisory* part of the job — the market opinion, the pricing
judgment, the strategic counsel. It gives you nothing for a flat factual assertion. "I think this will appraise" is advice. "It
appraised at $412,000 last month" is an express misrepresentation of material fact if it did not. The exemption is drawn exactly
along the line between opinion and fact, and it is on the fact side that license holders get sued.

Two further exemptions turn on transaction size. Section 17.49(f) exempts a claim arising out of a written contract where the
contract relates to a transaction or set of related transactions involving total consideration by the consumer of more than
$100,000, the consumer was represented in negotiating it by legal counsel not identified, suggested or selected by the defendant
or the defendant's agent, and "the contract does not involve the consumer's residence." Section 17.49(g) exempts a cause of action
arising from a transaction or set of related transactions involving total consideration by the consumer of more than $500,000,
"other than a cause of action involving a consumer's residence." Note the residence carve-out in both: the statute is most
protective exactly where an individual's home is concerned. Section 17.49(e) provides that except as specifically provided by
§ 17.50(b) and (h), nothing in the subchapter applies to a cause of action for bodily injury or death or for the infliction of
mental anguish.

**The practical lesson.** Put the pieces together. Your Texas buyer or seller is almost certainly a "consumer" under § 17.45(4),
because a house is "goods" under § 17.45(1) and brokerage is "services" under § 17.45(2). The sentence that hurts you is the
confident factual one: it lands in § 17.46(b)(5) or (7), it falls outside the § 17.49(i) exemption because it cannot be
characterized as advice or opinion, and under § 17.50(a)(1) it need only be a producing cause of economic damages and have been
relied on. If the trier of fact concludes you had actual awareness of its falsity — inferable under § 17.45(9) from objective
manifestations — mental anguish damages and up to treble economic damages come into play under § 17.50(b)(1), along with the
consumer's mandatory attorneys' fees under § 17.50(d). And § 1101.801 reminds you that none of this is instead of a Commission
proceeding.

The defensive habits follow from the statute rather than from folklore. Attribute every fact to its source — the survey, the
appraisal district record, the inspection report, the seller's disclosure — and say the source out loud, in writing, which is also
the posture § 17.506(a) rewards. Say "I don't know; let's verify" instead of answering from memory. Disclose what you know about
the property, particularly anything meeting the "known at the time" element of § 17.46(b)(24), remembering that Chapter 1101
independently requires disclosure of known significant defects under § 1101.652(b)(3) and (4). And when a demand letter arrives,
read § 17.505 and § 17.5052 before responding: the 60-day window is the cheapest moment the dispute will ever have.

### Applied exercise: the flooded garage in Katy

A married couple buys a house in Katy for $365,000 as their residence. During a showing, the sales agent — who represents the
seller — is asked whether the garage has ever taken water. The agent, who two months earlier read an email from the seller
describing water in the garage after a storm, replies, "No, this place has never had any water intrusion." The buyers rely on the
answer, buy the house, and after the first heavy rain spend $18,000 on drainage and remediation. They also testify to sleeplessness
and distress. The agent was sponsored by a broker at all relevant times.

1. Are the buyers "consumers" under § 17.45(4)? Identify the goods or services and confirm that no part of the definition's
   exclusion applies on these facts.
2. Which subdivision of § 17.46(b) does the statement most directly implicate, and could § 17.46(b)(24) also be in play? What
   element of (b)(24) would have to be proved?
3. Does the § 17.49(i) exemption protect the agent here? Identify the words of the exemption and of its exception that decide it.
4. Assume the trier of fact finds $18,000 of economic damages and finds the conduct was committed knowingly. Under § 17.50(b)(1),
   what becomes available that was not available without that finding, and what is the maximum multiple of economic damages the
   trier of fact may award? Now assume the finding is "intentionally" — what changes about the multiplier's base?
5. Under § 17.505(a), what must the buyers do at least 60 days before filing suit, and under § 17.5052 and § 17.506(d), what two

### Module recap

This module traced one relationship from creation to consequences. It began with agency itself — the arrangement in which one
person acts for another and binds that other, declared for Texas brokerage by Occupations Code § 1101.557(a) and organized so that
the agency belongs to the broker while the sales agent works through the broker under § 1101.351(c) and § 1101.803. It
distinguished how an agency comes into being, and the difference between the authority the principal actually gave and the
authority a third person is reasonably led to perceive, marking where Texas has legislated over the common law: § 1101.805 on
liability for misrepresentation, § 1101.557(c)(1) on the power to bind, § 1101.654 on the boundary at the practice of law.

It then named the positions a Texas license holder may occupy. Seller and buyer representation are familiar. The two that make this
a Texas course are the **intermediary** — the mechanism Chapter 1101 supplies in place of the dual agency other states use,
requiring written consent under § 1101.559, permitting appointments under § 1101.560, and constrained by the five prohibitions of
§ 1101.651(d) — and the **non-representation showing** under § 1101.562, effective January 1, 2026, which arrives paired with the
written-agreement requirement of § 1101.563 and its fourteen-day, non-exclusive limits on showing-only agreements. Around both sits
the disclosure architecture of § 1101.558: an oral-or-written representation disclosure at first contact, a written notice at first
substantive communication about specific property, and three exceptions including the narrow open-house exception.

The brokerage unit set those positions inside a business — licensed entities and designated brokers under § 1101.355, sponsorship
and supervision under § 1101.351(c), § 1101.458 and § 1101.803, agreements with definite termination dates under § 1101.652(b)(12),
a closed compensation loop under § 1101.651(a)–(c), and the disclosure required by § 1101.563(c)(2) that broker compensation is not
set by law and is fully negotiable. The fiduciary unit gave the relationship its moral content, attaching each duty — loyalty,
obedience, disclosure, confidentiality, care, accounting — to the subsection of § 1101.652 that makes its breach a disciplinary
matter, while being candid about which propositions rest on common law rather than on a statute read for this course. The DTPA unit
then showed what happens when accurate speech fails: a consumer under § 17.45(4), an enumerated act under § 17.46(b), a claim under
§ 17.50 that the § 17.49(i) exemption will not cover because it was fact rather than opinion, and damages that escalate on findings
of knowing or intentional conduct.

You should now be able to name your position in any Texas transaction in one sentence, produce the writing that proves it, state
which duties you owe and what a breach costs under § 1101.652, and recognize before you speak the difference between an opinion the
law protects and a fact the law will hold you to.
