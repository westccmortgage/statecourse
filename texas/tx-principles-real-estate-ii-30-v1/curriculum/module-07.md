# Texas Principles of Real Estate II — Module 07

## TREC Topic: Real Estate Brokerage and Law of Agency

**Prescribed time: 195 minutes.** This module carries the five units the Commission's PRINS2-1 course
approval form places under *Real Estate Brokerage and Law of Agency*. The form gives this topic no
scope note; none is stated here, and none has been invented to fill the space.

**Learning objectives.** After completing this module you will be able to: define agency and identify
the principal, agent, client and customer in a Texas transaction, distinguish express, implied,
ratified and estoppel-based agency, and distinguish actual from apparent authority; identify which
agency position a Texas license holder occupies in a given transaction — seller representation, buyer
representation, intermediary, or a non-representation showing — and state the written consent,
appointment, notice and written-agreement requirements that attach to each; explain how a Texas
brokerage is organized, how a sales agent's authority flows through a sponsoring broker, and how
compensation is earned, paid and disclosed; state each fiduciary duty and identify the subsection of
Occupations Code § 1101.652 that turns a breach of it into a ground for discipline; and apply the
Texas Deceptive Trade Practices–Consumer Protection Act to a real estate misrepresentation by
identifying whether the complainant is a "consumer," which enumerated practice is implicated, and
what the "knowingly" and "intentionally" findings do to the damages available.

## Unit: The Law of Agency

**Agency** is the legal relationship in which one person — the **agent** — is authorized to act for
and on behalf of another person — the **principal** — in dealings with third persons, and in which
the agent's authorized acts bind the principal as if the principal had done them personally. Every
other idea in this module rests on that single sentence. A listing is an agency. A buyer
representation agreement is an agency. The reason a Texas seller is bound when a broker presents an
offer, the reason a buyer's agent's promise matters, and the reason a license holder can lose a
license for a sentence said carelessly at a kitchen table all trace back to the fact that the license
holder is not merely a helpful person in the room — the license holder is *someone else's legal
proxy*.

Texas does not leave the existence of that relationship to inference. Occupations Code § 1101.557(a)
states the rule directly: "A broker who represents a party in a real estate transaction or who lists
real estate for sale under an exclusive agreement for a party is that party's agent." Read that
carefully, because it does two things. It confirms that representation creates agency, and it also
provides that taking an exclusive listing *is itself* agency — the statute does not make you argue
about it.

**The vocabulary, precisely.** The **principal** is the person the agent works for. In everyday Texas
brokerage speech the principal is called the **client**. The person on the other side of the
transaction, whom the license holder deals with but does not represent, is called the **customer**.
Neither "client" nor "customer" is defined in Chapter 1101, and you should not treat them as statutory
terms; they are the working vocabulary of the business. What Chapter 1101 does define, for the
subchapter governing practice, is **party**: under § 1101.551(2), "'Party' means a prospective buyer,
seller, landlord, or tenant or an authorized representative of a buyer, seller, landlord, or tenant,
including a trustee, guardian, executor, administrator, receiver, or attorney-in-fact. The term does
not include a license holder who represents a party." That last clause matters. The license holder is
never the "party." The license holder is the agent *of* a party.

**Who is the agent — the broker, not the sales agent.** This is the structural fact Texas students
most often get backwards. Under § 1101.002(7), a "sales agent" is "a person who is sponsored by a
licensed broker for the purpose of performing an act described by Subdivision (1)" — that is, an act
of brokerage. Under § 1101.351(c), "A licensed sales agent may not engage or attempt to engage in real
estate brokerage unless the sales agent is sponsored by a licensed broker and is acting for that
broker." A sales agent therefore has no independent brokerage authority at all. The agency runs
between the *broker* and the client; the sales agent performs the broker's work. Section 1101.554
reinforces the relationship administratively — the Commission delivers a copy of each sales agent
license to the sponsoring broker — and § 1101.803 makes the consequence explicit: "A licensed broker
is liable to the commission, the public, and the broker's clients for any conduct engaged in under
this chapter by the broker or by a sales agent associated with or acting for the broker."

**What acts require the licence.** Section 1101.002(1) defines "broker" functionally, by a list of
acts performed for another person in exchange for, or with the expectation of, a commission or other
valuable consideration: selling, exchanging, purchasing or leasing real estate; offering to do so;
negotiating or attempting to negotiate a listing, sale, exchange, purchase or lease; listing or
offering to list; auctioning; dealing in options; aiding or attempting to aid in locating or obtaining
real estate for purchase or lease; procuring a prospect or procuring property to effect a sale,
exchange or lease; controlling the acceptance or deposit of rent from a resident of a single-family
residential unit; providing a written analysis, opinion or conclusion about estimated price in the
circumstances described in subparagraph (xi); and advising an owner about the negotiation or
completion of a short sale. Section 1101.004(a) restates the same idea as a status: a person "is
engaged in real estate brokerage" if, expecting valuable consideration, the person performs or offers
to perform any act described by § 1101.002(1) for another person, whether as part of a transaction or
as the entire transaction. Section 1101.351(a) then makes the licence a precondition: unless a person
holds a licence, the person may not act as or represent that the person is a broker or sales agent, or
act as a residential rental locator. Note what the definition turns on — acting *for another person*
with the *expectation of compensation*. Section 1101.004(b) confirms the boundary from the other
direction: a person is not engaged in real estate brokerage based solely on constructing, remodeling
or repairing a building, on participating as a principal or financial manager in a real estate
investment, or on entering into an obligation to pay another that is secured by an interest in real
property. And § 1101.005 lists persons and transactions to which the chapter simply does not apply,
including a Texas-licensed attorney, an attorney-in-fact authorized under a power of attorney to
conduct not more than three real estate transactions annually, an on-site manager of an apartment
complex, and an owner or an owner's employee leasing the owner's real estate.

**How an agency is created.** Four routes are taught in agency law generally, and it is worth being
candid about their sources. Chapter 1101 does not enumerate them; they are common-law concepts, and
this module presents them as such rather than as Texas statutory rules.

The first is **express agency** — the parties say so, in words. A signed listing agreement or a signed
buyer representation agreement is express agency in writing. An oral appointment can also be express.
That oral possibility is not hypothetical in Texas: § 1101.562(a)(1) permits a non-representation
showing only where the broker "has not agreed with the party, either orally or in writing, to
represent the party," which tells you plainly that the Legislature understands an oral agreement to
represent as a real thing capable of creating the relationship.

The second is **implied agency**, which arises from conduct rather than words — the license holder
behaves like the buyer's advocate, advises on price, counsels on strategy, and the buyer reasonably
understands the license holder to be working for the buyer. Nobody signed anything. That is exactly
the accident the 2026 amendments discussed in the next unit are designed to prevent, and it is exactly
what § 1101.652(b)(7) punishes when it makes it a ground for discipline that a license holder "fails
to make clear to all parties to a real estate transaction the party for whom the license holder is
acting."

The third is **ratification**: a principal who did not authorize an act in advance accepts its benefit
afterward with knowledge of the facts, and is treated as having authorized it. The fourth is **agency
by estoppel**: a principal whose own conduct led a third person reasonably to believe someone was the
principal's agent may be prevented from denying it, where the third person relied to their detriment.
Both are judicial doctrines; neither appears in Chapter 1101, and you should not cite Chapter 1101 for
them.

**Actual authority and apparent authority.** **Actual authority** is what the principal has really
given the agent — expressly, in the words of the agreement, or by implication as reasonably necessary
to carry out the express grant. **Apparent authority** is what a third person is reasonably led by the
principal's conduct to believe the agent has, even where the agent in fact has less. The distinction
is not academic. Actual authority is the boundary the agent must respect toward the principal;
apparent authority is the boundary the principal may be held to toward the outside world. Again, these
are common-law categories rather than statutory ones, but Texas has legislated at the edges of them
in ways you must know.

First, on the **power to bind**. In Texas real estate brokerage the ordinary listing does not give the
broker power to sign a contract for the seller; the broker's authority is to market, to procure, to
communicate and to negotiate, while the party signs. But where a license holder does hold the power to
bind, § 1101.557(c)(1) treats that fact as changing the license holder's status: "a license holder who
has the authority to bind a party to a lease or sale under a power of attorney or a property
management agreement is also a party to the lease or sale."

Second, on **liability for the agent's words**. At common law, a principal can be exposed for an
agent's misrepresentations. Texas has narrowed that by statute, and § 1101.805(b) says the section
"prevails over any other law, including common law." Under § 1101.805(d), a party is not liable for a
misrepresentation or concealment of a material fact made by a license holder unless the party "knew of
the falsity of the misrepresentation or concealment" and "failed to disclose the party's knowledge of
the falsity." Subsection (e) runs the same protection the other way: a license holder is not liable for
a party's misrepresentation or concealment unless the license holder knew of the falsity and failed to
disclose that knowledge. Subsection (c) preserves the broker's responsibility for the acts and
omissions of a sponsored sales agent. So the seller does not automatically own the agent's careless
sentence, and the agent does not automatically own the seller's lie — but knowledge plus silence
destroys the protection for either one.

Third, on the **outer limit of the agent's authority**. No amount of agency authorizes practising law.
Section 1101.654(a) requires the Commission to suspend or revoke the licence of a non-attorney license
holder who, for consideration, drafts an instrument other than a form described by § 1101.155 that
transfers or otherwise affects an interest in real property, or who advises a person regarding the
validity or legal sufficiency of an instrument or the validity of title. Subsection (b) then protects
the ordinary work of the business: completing a contract form incidental to acting as a broker is not
the unauthorized practice of law if the form was adopted by the Commission for that type of
transaction, prepared and approved by a Texas-licensed attorney for that type of transaction, or
prepared by the property owner or by an attorney and required by the owner.

**How an agency ends.** Agency relationships end in the ordinary ways any personal-services
relationship ends — by performance of the object, by expiration of the stated term, by mutual
agreement, by one side's revocation or renunciation, or by an event that makes performance impossible
such as death or incapacity of a party or destruction of the subject property. Those are general
agency principles, not Chapter 1101 rules, and this module states them as principles. What Texas
*does* legislate is the term itself. Section 1101.652(b)(12) makes it a ground for discipline that a
license holder "fails to specify a definite termination date that is not subject to prior notice in a
contract, other than a contract to perform property management services, in which the license holder
agrees to perform services for which a license is required." An open-ended listing is not merely poor
drafting in Texas; it is a disciplinary exposure. Section 1101.563(c)(1)(B), effective January 1,
2026, carries the same instinct into buyer-side agreements by requiring the written agreement with a
prospective residential buyer to state the termination date of the agreement.

A common error is to assume that when the agreement ends, everything ends. It does not. Two obligations
in particular outlive the paperwork, and Unit 4 takes them up: what the license holder must never say
about the former client, and what the license holder must do with the former client's money.

## Unit: Agency Positions and Disclosure

There are, in Texas, a small and closed set of positions a license holder can occupy toward a person
in a transaction. Learn them as positions, not as feelings about who you are helping. In every deal
you must be able to name yours in one sentence and prove it on paper.

**Position one: representing the seller or landlord.** The broker takes a listing, and under
§ 1101.557(a) is that party's agent. Section 1101.557(b) then imposes a floor of conduct that cannot be
bargained away: the broker "must inform the party if the broker receives material information related
to a transaction to list, buy, sell, or lease the party's real estate, including the receipt of an
offer by the broker," and "shall, at a minimum, answer the party's questions and present any offer to
or from the party." A listing broker who sits on an offer because it looks weak, or who declines to
answer the seller's questions, is not exercising judgment; the broker is violating a statutory
minimum.

**Position two: representing the buyer or tenant.** The mirror image. The broker is the buyer's agent
and owes the buyer the same statutory floor and the fiduciary duties taken up in Unit 4. As of January
1, 2026, this position on the residential side is no longer something that can drift into existence —
see § 1101.563 below.

**Position three: intermediary.** This is the position that most distinguishes Texas, and it deserves
a paragraph of its own before the mechanics.

**Texas does not use "dual agency" the way some other states do.** Do not carry the phrase into Texas
practice, and be sceptical of any course, form or colleague who does. Some states permit a broker to
be simultaneously the full agent of both the buyer and the seller, owing each the complete set of
fiduciary duties and asking the broker to hold two irreconcilable loyalties at once. Texas's answer is
different in name and in substance. Chapter 1101, Subchapter L, creates the **intermediary**. Section
1101.551(1) defines the term: "'Intermediary' means a broker who is employed to negotiate a
transaction between the parties to a transaction and for that purpose may act as an agent of the
parties." The intermediary is not two agents; it is one broker occupying a defined, statutorily
constrained middle position, and the constraints are the point.

**Getting there requires written consent.** Section 1101.559(a) permits a broker to act as an
intermediary "if: (1) the broker obtains written consent from each party for the broker to act as an
intermediary in the transaction; and (2) the written consent of the parties states the source of any
expected compensation to the broker." Two requirements, both mandatory, neither satisfiable orally.
Section 1101.559(b) then tells you where that consent normally lives: "A written listing agreement to
represent a seller or landlord or a written agreement to represent a buyer or tenant that authorizes a
broker to act as an intermediary in a real estate transaction is sufficient to establish written
consent of the party to the transaction if the written agreement specifies in conspicuous bold or
underlined print the conduct that is prohibited under Section 1101.651(d)." In practice this is why
Texas listing and buyer representation agreements contain a conspicuous intermediary paragraph: the
consent is captured at the front end, long before anyone knows whether an in-house match will happen.

**How the intermediary must behave.** Section 1101.559(c) states the standard in five words —
"An intermediary shall act fairly and impartially" — and then adds a clarification that resolves the
question students always ask next: "Appointment by a broker acting as an intermediary of an associated
license holder under Section 1101.560 to communicate with, carry out the instructions of, and provide
opinions and advice to the parties to whom that associated license holder is appointed is a fair and
impartial act." In other words, appointing associates to advise each side separately is not a breach
of impartiality; the statute pre-approves it.

**Appointments.** Section 1101.560(a) provides that a broker who complies with § 1101.559's written
consent requirements "may appoint: (1) a license holder associated with the broker to communicate with
and carry out instructions of one party to a real estate transaction; and (2) another license holder
associated with the broker to communicate with and carry out instructions of any other party to the
transaction." Two conditions govern the appointment, both in § 1101.560(b): the parties' written
consent under § 1101.559 must authorize the broker to make the appointment, and "the broker provides
written notice of the appointment to all parties involved in the real estate transaction." Then the
payoff, § 1101.560(c): "A license holder appointed under this section may provide opinions and advice
during negotiations to the party to whom the license holder is appointed." That single subsection is
the reason appointments exist. Without an appointment, the intermediary brokerage is limited to fair
and impartial facilitation. With appointments, each side gets a person who may actually counsel them.

**What an intermediary may never do.** Section 1101.651(d) applies to the broker and to any broker or
sales agent appointed under § 1101.560, and it prohibits five things. Such a person may not:

- "disclose to the buyer or tenant that the seller or landlord will accept a price less than the asking
  price, unless otherwise instructed in a separate writing by the seller or landlord";
- "disclose to the seller or landlord that the buyer or tenant will pay a price greater than the price
  submitted in a written offer to the seller or landlord, unless otherwise instructed in a separate
  writing by the buyer or tenant";
- "disclose any confidential information or any information a party specifically instructs the broker
  or sales agent in writing not to disclose," unless instructed otherwise in a separate writing by that
  party, unless disclosure is required by Chapter 1101 or a court order, or unless "the information
  materially relates to the condition of the property";
- "treat a party to a transaction dishonestly"; or
- "violate this chapter."

Notice the shape of the first three. The permission to disclose price flexibility runs only from the
party whose flexibility it is, and only in a *separate writing* — a general clause buried in the
listing will not do it. And notice the one carve-out that runs the other way: confidentiality never
covers information that "materially relates to the condition of the property." A buyer's confidence
about how high they will go is protected. A seller's confidence about a foundation problem is not.

**The intermediary is not optional once you have both sides.** Section 1101.561(b) is emphatic: "A
broker must agree to act as an intermediary under this subchapter if the broker agrees to represent in
a transaction: (1) a buyer or tenant; and (2) a seller or landlord." And § 1101.561(a) resolves the
conflict-of-laws question that otherwise haunts the arrangement: "The duties of a license holder acting
as an intermediary under this subchapter supersede the duties of a license holder established under any
other law, including common law." When you are the intermediary, Subchapter L is the governing
description of your duties — not the classical fiduciary catalogue you learned for single-agency work.

**Position four: showing property without representing anyone.** Effective January 1, 2026,
§ 1101.562(a) permits a broker to "show real property available for sale or lease to a party without
representing the party if the broker: (1) has not agreed with the party, either orally or in writing,
to represent the party; (2) is not otherwise acting as the party's agent at the time of showing the
real property; (3) does not provide to the party opinions or advice regarding the real property or real
estate transactions in general; and (4) does not perform any other act of real estate brokerage for the
party." Before showing under that section, § 1101.562(b) requires the broker, as applicable, to
disclose representation of the owner as required by § 1101.558(b) if the broker represents the owner,
or to provide the written notice described by § 1101.558(b-1) if the broker does not represent the
owner. Section 1101.562(c) preserves a narrow but practically essential permission: notwithstanding the
bans on opinions, advice and other brokerage acts, "a broker showing real property under this section
may confirm information to a party regarding the size, price, and terms of the real property available
for sale or lease." You may confirm the square footage and the asking price. You may not tell them it
is a good deal.

**The IABS written notice.** Section 1101.558 carries two distinct disclosure obligations and students
routinely merge them. Subsection (b) is the *representation* disclosure: a license holder who
represents a party "shall disclose, orally or in writing, that representation at the time of the
license holder's first contact with: (1) another party to the transaction; or (2) another license
holder who represents another party to the transaction." Oral is enough; the trigger is first contact.

Subsection (b-1) is the *written notice* — the notice the business calls the Information About
Brokerage Services form. "At the time of a license holder's first substantive communication with a
party relating to a proposed transaction regarding specific real property, the license holder shall
provide to the party written notice in at least a 10-point font that: (1) describes the ways in which a
broker can represent a party to a real estate transaction, including as an intermediary; (2) describes
the basic duties and obligations a broker has to a party to a real estate transaction that the broker
represents; (3) describes the basic obligations a broker has to a party to a real estate transaction
that the broker does not represent; and (4) provides the name, license number, and contact information
for the license holder and the license holder's supervisor and broker, if applicable." Subsection (b-2)
provides that the Commission by rule prescribes the text of the notice required under (b-1)(1) and (2)
and establishes the methods by which a license holder must provide it, so the exact wording and
delivery method are matters of Commission rule rather than of the statute. TREC's own explanatory
article on the 2026 changes states that the changes effective January 1, 2026 to the IABS reflect the
new notice requirements.

**The three exceptions in § 1101.558(c).** A license holder is not required to provide the (b-1) notice
if: "(1) the proposed transaction is for a residential lease for less than one year and a sale is not
being considered; (2) the license holder meets with a party who the license holder knows is represented
by another license holder; or (3) the communication occurs at a property that is held open for any
prospective buyer or tenant and the communication concerns that property." The third is the open-house
exception, and it is narrower than its reputation. It excuses the *notice*, at that property, about that
property. It does not excuse anything else the law requires, and after January 1, 2026 something else
is required — as the next paragraphs explain.

**The § 1101.563 written agreement, effective January 1, 2026.** Section 1101.563(a) defines
"residential real property" for the section as a single-family house; a duplex, triplex or quadraplex;
or a unit in a multiunit residential structure in which title to an individual unit is transferred to
the unit owner under a condominium or cooperative system. Subsection (b) then states the rule: "A
license holder who performs any act of real estate brokerage for a prospective buyer of residential real
property must enter into a written agreement with the prospective buyer before: (1) showing any
residential real property to the prospective buyer; or (2) if no residential real property will be shown,
presenting an offer to purchase residential real property on behalf of the prospective buyer."

Subsection (c) prescribes the contents. The agreement must state the services to be provided by the
license holder; the termination date of the agreement; whether the agreement is exclusive or
non-exclusive; as applicable, that the license holder either represents the prospective buyer as the
buyer's agent or does not represent the prospective buyer as the buyer's agent if the only act of
brokerage being performed is showing real property under § 1101.562; and the amount or rate of
compensation the broker will receive and how that amount will be determined. And it must "disclose in
conspicuous language that broker compensation is not set by law and is fully negotiable."

Subsection (d) requires a separate agreement if additional brokerage acts are to be provided after a
showing-only agreement. Subsection (e) caps the showing-only agreement in two ways: it "may not: (1) be
an exclusive agreement with the license holder; or (2) state a termination date more than fourteen days
from the date the agreement is entered into." So a showing-only agreement in Texas is by law
non-exclusive and short — no more than fourteen days.

The consequence of skipping it is not merely a lost deal. Section 1101.652(b)(34), added by the same
2025 legislation, makes it a ground for suspension or revocation that a license holder "fails to enter
into a written agreement with a prospective buyer as required by Section 1101.563."

**The open house, worked through.** TREC's article on the 2026 changes sets out two scenarios, and they
are the cleanest illustration of how § 1101.558(c)(3), § 1101.562 and § 1101.563 interact. Where the
agent hosting the open house is a member of the listing broker's office, the article states that the
agent does not need to provide the IABS or enter into a written agreement with prospective buyers, but
will need to disclose that he or she represents the owner, either orally or in writing, as required by
§ 1101.558(b). Where the hosting agent is not a member of the listing broker's office and does not
represent the seller — a showing agent, for instance — the article states the agent must provide the
IABS and enter into a written agreement as described by § 1101.563(c) with prospective buyers, and that
this is true even when a prospective buyer already has a written representation agreement with another
broker. The article adds that by law the IABS must be provided and the agreement entered into before
the showing, so in practical terms the agreement should ideally be presented and entered into before the
buyer enters the property, or at the very least before the buyer starts viewing it. And it states the
uncomfortable consequence plainly: if a buyer refuses to sign, the agent cannot show the property, and
license holders can face disciplinary action for failing to enter into the agreement required by
§ 1101.563. The article notes that brokers may decide whether to allow their agents to host open houses
for another brokerage and may adopt policies about it — that is brokerage policy, not a statutory rule.

**Subagency, handled accurately.** TREC's article states that two references to subagency were removed
from TRELA, which means the circumstances in which subagency could arise in practice are limited; it
also states that subagency has not been eliminated generally as a legal concept, and advises a license
holder who thinks subagency may be involved to contact the broker or an attorney. Both halves of that
matter. Do not tell a Texas consumer that subagency "no longer exists." Say instead that TRELA no longer
references it, that its practical field has narrowed, and that whether it arises in an unusual
arrangement is a question for the broker and counsel.

**Two disciplinary provisions that police all of this.** Section 1101.652(b)(7) reaches a license holder
who "fails to make clear to all parties to a real estate transaction the party for whom the license
holder is acting." Section 1101.652(b)(8) reaches a license holder who "receives compensation from more
than one party to a real estate transaction without the full knowledge and consent of all parties to the
transaction." Read together with § 1101.559(a)(2)'s requirement that the written consent state the source
of expected compensation, they describe a single Texas instinct: whom you serve and who pays you must
both be on the table, in the open, before the money moves.

## Unit: Brokerage & Nature of the Brokerage Business

A brokerage is a business built on a licence, and almost everything distinctive about how it is
organized traces back to the fact that the licence — not the individual salesperson's reputation, not
the office lease, not the sign — is what the law regulates. This unit describes both the legal
architecture and the way the business actually runs, and it labels which is which.

**The broker is the licensed enterprise.** Under § 1101.002(4), "license holder" means a broker or
sales agent licensed under Chapter 1101. A broker may be an individual or a business entity. Section
1101.351(a-1) provides that unless a business entity holds a licence, the entity may not act as a
broker. Section 1101.355(a) sets the additional eligibility requirements for an entity: it must
"designate one of its managing officers as its broker for purposes of this chapter," and it must
"provide proof that the entity maintains errors and omissions insurance with a minimum annual limit of
$1 million for each occurrence if the designated broker owns less than 10 percent of the business
entity." Subsection (b) adds that an entity may not act as a broker unless its designated broker is a
licensed individual broker in active status and good standing according to Commission records, and
subsection (b-1) lets the Commission consider, in judging good standing, the disciplinary history of
the broker or of entities for which the broker serves or served as designated broker, and the payment
status of amounts owed to the Commission.

There is a narrow registration alternative for the entity through which many individual license holders
take their pay. Section 1101.355(c) requires a business entity that receives compensation on behalf of a
license holder to be licensed as a broker. But § 1101.355(d) provides that an entity is not required to
be licensed if it receives compensation on behalf of a licensed broker or sales agent that the license
holder earned while engaged in real estate brokerage, performs no other acts of a broker, is a limited
liability company as defined by § 101.001 of the Business Organizations Code or an S corporation as
defined by 26 U.S.C. § 1361, is registered with the Commission, and is at least 51 percent owned by the
license holder on whose behalf it receives compensation. Subsection (e) directs the Commission to adopt
rules providing for that registration.

**Becoming a broker.** Section 1101.356(a) requires an applicant for a broker licence to show at least
four years of active experience as a license holder during the 60 months preceding the application, the
hours of qualifying and related coursework required by Commission rule (not to exceed 60 semester hours
or equivalent classroom hours), and attendance at the broker responsibility course approved under
§ 1101.458, which may count toward the related-course hours. Subsection (b) exempts an applicant already
licensed as a broker in another state with comparable requirements, and subsection (b-1) leaves the
definition of "active experience" to Commission rule. The four-year experience requirement is the reason
the Texas brokerage is not a flat organization of equals: the person carrying the licence has, by
statutory design, done the work longer.

**Where the brokerage sits.** Section 1101.552(a) requires a resident broker to maintain a fixed office
in Texas, with the office address designated on the broker's licence. Subsection (b) requires the broker,
not later than the tenth day after moving from the designated address, to apply for a licence designating
the new location. Subsection (d) provides that a nonresident licensed broker is not required to maintain
a place of business in Texas. Subsection (e) requires every license holder to provide the Commission with
a current mailing address, business address, business telephone number and business e-mail address, and,
if the license holder is an associated broker, the name of the broker with whom the license holder is
associated; subsection (f) requires notice of any change in that information. Subsection (g) defines
"associated broker" as "a broker who associates with and is paid through another broker under a
relationship that is intended to be a continuous relationship, including as an employee or an ongoing
independent contractor."

**Sponsorship: the spine of the organization.** Everything the sales agent does is done for the broker.
Section 1101.351(c) forbids a sales agent from engaging or attempting to engage in brokerage unless
sponsored by a licensed broker and acting for that broker. Section 1101.803 makes the broker liable to
the Commission, to the public and to the broker's clients for the conduct of the broker and of any sales
agent associated with or acting for the broker. Section 1101.652(b)(26) makes it a ground for discipline
for a license holder to establish "an association by employment or otherwise with a person other than a
license holder if the person is expected or required to act as a license holder" — the provision that
prevents a brokerage from staffing its production with unlicensed assistants who are quietly doing
licensed work.

As a matter of business practice rather than law, most Texas sales agents are engaged as independent
contractors rather than employees, are compensated by commission split rather than salary, and pay some
combination of desk fees, transaction fees or franchise fees. None of that is prescribed by Chapter 1101,
and this module cites nothing for it. What Chapter 1101 does insist on is that whatever the internal
economics, the *authority* is the broker's.

**Supervision.** Section 1101.458(a) provides that during the term of the current licence, a broker
responsibility course approved by the Commission must be attended by a broker licensed under the chapter
and by a sales agent licensed under the chapter "who supervises another license holder." Subsection (b)
directs the Commission to prescribe the title, content and duration of the course by rule and caps it at
six classroom hours; subsection (c) allows those hours to count toward the hours described by
§ 1101.455(f); subsection (d) exempts a broker who is exempt from continuing education under § 1101.456.
Read that with § 1101.803 and the design becomes obvious: the statute assigns the liability to the broker
and then requires the broker — and any sales agent who supervises — to be trained in carrying it.

**Listing and buyer representation agreements.** In the vocabulary of the business these are often called
**employment contracts**: the client engages the brokerage to perform services and agrees what the
brokerage will be paid if it performs. That characterization is a teaching convention, not a statutory
label, and it does not make the sales agent an employee of the client. What the law adds to these
agreements is specific and worth memorizing.

First, a **definite termination date**. Section 1101.652(b)(12) makes it a ground for discipline to fail
to specify "a definite termination date that is not subject to prior notice" in a contract — other than a
property management contract — in which the license holder agrees to perform services for which a licence
is required.

Second, on the buyer side and for residential property, the **§ 1101.563 written agreement** described in
the previous unit, with its required contents, its separate-agreement rule in subsection (d), and its
non-exclusivity and fourteen-day cap for showing-only agreements in subsection (e).

Third, the **writing requirement for suing over a commission**. Section 1101.806(c) provides that "A
person may not maintain an action in this state to recover a commission for the sale or purchase of real
estate unless the promise or agreement on which the action is based, or a memorandum, is in writing and
signed by the party against whom the action is brought or by a person authorized by that party to sign the
document." Section 1101.806(b) adds that no one may maintain an action to collect compensation for an act
as a broker or sales agent performed in Texas without alleging and proving that the person was a license
holder at the time the act was commenced, or an attorney licensed in any state. Subsection (a) carves out
agreements to share compensation among license holders and preserves causes of action among brokers for
interference with business relationships. And subsection (d) attaches a striking forfeiture: a license
holder who fails to advise a buyer as provided by § 1101.555 "may not receive payment of or recover any
commission agreed to be paid on the sale." Section 1101.555 requires that when an offer to purchase Texas
real estate is signed, the license holder shall advise each buyer in writing that the buyer should either
have the abstract examined by an attorney chosen by the buyer or be provided with or obtain a title
insurance policy. The same failure is independently a ground for discipline under § 1101.652(b)(29).

**Compensation: how it flows, and what you must say about it.** Three rules control the flow. Section
1101.651(a): a licensed broker may not pay a commission to or otherwise compensate a person directly or
indirectly for performing an act of a broker unless the person is a license holder, or a broker licensed
in another state who does not conduct any of the negotiations in Texas. Section 1101.651(b): a sales agent
may not accept compensation for a real estate transaction from anyone other than the broker sponsoring the
sales agent, or the broker who was sponsoring the sales agent when the compensation was earned. Section
1101.651(c): a sales agent may not pay a commission to a person except through the sponsoring broker.
Section 1101.652(b)(11) makes paying or dividing a fee with a person who is not a license holder or a
broker or sales agent licensed in another state a ground for discipline. The practical effect is a closed
loop: money for brokerage work moves broker-to-broker and broker-to-sponsored-agent, and a grateful buyer
cannot hand a cheque directly to the sales agent who found the house.

Now the sentence that must be said out loud. **Broker compensation is not set by law and is fully
negotiable.** That is not a slogan; it is a statutory disclosure. Section 1101.563(c)(2) requires the
written agreement with a prospective residential buyer to "disclose in conspicuous language that broker
compensation is not set by law and is fully negotiable," and § 1101.563(c)(1)(E) requires the agreement to
state "the amount or rate of compensation the broker will receive and how this amount will be determined."
There is no statutory commission rate in Chapter 1101, no customary rate recognized by the chapter, and any
suggestion to a consumer that a rate is standard, required or set by anyone is both false and, if it
misleads, exposed under § 1101.652(b)(23)(A) and under the DTPA taken up in Unit 5.

Related duties on money: § 1101.652(b)(13) makes it a ground for discipline to accept, receive or charge
"an undisclosed commission, rebate, or direct profit on an expenditure made for a principal," and
§ 1101.652(b)(8) reaches compensation from more than one party without the full knowledge and consent of
all parties.

*Illustration, for arithmetic only.* Suppose a Texas seller and a listing broker negotiate a total fee of
5.5% on a $400,000 sale, and the listing broker's written agreement with a cooperating broker allocates
2.5 percentage points of it to the cooperating side. Total fee: $400,000 × 0.055 = $22,000. Cooperating
side: $400,000 × 0.025 = $10,000. Listing side: $22,000 − $10,000 = $12,000. If the listing brokerage's
internal split with its sponsored sales agent is 70/30 in the agent's favour, the agent's gross share is
$12,000 × 0.70 = $8,400 and the brokerage retains $3,600 — and under § 1101.651(b) that $8,400 must reach
the sales agent through the sponsoring broker, not from the title company or the seller directly. Every
number in that paragraph is invented for the arithmetic. No percentage in it is standard, customary or
required, and you must not repeat any of them to a consumer as though it were.

**Advertising, names and teams.** Section 1101.652(b)(23) makes it a ground for discipline to publish or
cause to be published an advertisement that "(A) misleads or is likely to deceive the public; (B) tends to
create a misleading impression; (C) implies that a sales agent is responsible for the operation of the
broker's real estate brokerage business; or (D) fails to include the name of the broker for whom the
license holder acts, which name may be the licensed name, assumed name, or trade name of the broker as
authorized by a law of this state and registered with the commission." Read subparagraphs (C) and (D)
together and you have the statutory basis for how Texas treats **team names**: a team may market under a
name, but the advertising must carry the broker's name and must not imply that the team or the sales agent
runs the brokerage. The detailed requirements for registering assumed names, alternate names and team names
are matters of Commission rule; those rules are not among the sources read for this module, so this module
states only what the statute says and does not describe the rule requirements.

Section 1101.652(b)(18) forbids placing a sign on real property offering it for sale or lease without the
written consent of the owner or the owner's authorized agent; (b)(19) and (b)(20) forbid offering to sell
or lease property without the owner's knowledge and consent or on terms other than those the owner
authorized. Section 1101.804 provides a protection worth knowing for anyone assembling market data: a
license holder or nonprofit real estate board or association that provides information about sales prices
or terms of sale for the purpose of facilitating listing, selling, leasing, financing or appraisal "is not
liable to another person for providing that information unless the disclosure of that information is
specifically prohibited by statute."

**Poaching and interference.** Section 1101.652(b)(22) makes it a ground for discipline to negotiate or
attempt to negotiate a sale, exchange or lease with an owner, landlord, buyer or tenant "with knowledge
that that person is a party to an outstanding written contract that grants exclusive agency to another
broker in connection with the transaction," and (b)(21) reaches inducing a party to break a contract in
order to substitute a new one. Section 1101.557(b)(1) forbids a representing broker from instructing
another broker to violate (b)(22) directly or indirectly. But § 1101.557(c)(3) creates a careful safe
harbour: the sole delivery of an offer to a party does not violate (b)(22) if the party's broker consents
to the delivery, a copy is sent to the party's broker unless a sealed-bid governmental process forbids it,
and the person delivering the offer does not engage in any other activity that violates (b)(22).

**Two structural cautions.** First, § 1101.652(b)(16) makes it a ground for discipline to act "in a dual
capacity as broker and undisclosed principal in a real estate transaction," and § 1101.652(a-1)(1) reaches
misrepresentation, dishonesty or fraud when a license holder buys, sells, trades or leases property in the
license holder's own name, a spouse's name, or the name of a person related within the first degree by
consanguinity. A license holder buying for their own account is not doing anything unlawful — but doing it
without saying so is. Second, § 1101.801 removes any comfort a license holder might take from surviving a
Commission proceeding: "Disciplinary action taken against a person under Section 1101.652 does not relieve
the person from civil or criminal liability." The licence and the lawsuit are separate exposures, and Unit
5 is about the second one.

### Applied exercise: setting up a new Texas brokerage

Dana holds a Texas sales agent licence and has been active for five of the past sixty months. She forms a
Texas limited liability company, "Hill Country Doors LLC," intending it to hold listings and receive
commissions, and asks a broker friend to be listed as its designated broker while owning none of the
company. She plans to have the company advertise under the team name "The Door Team," to hire an unlicensed
assistant to hold open houses and answer buyer questions about the properties, and to pay a $500 referral
bonus to a past client who sends her business.

1. Under § 1101.351(a-1) and § 1101.355(a), what does the LLC need before it may act as a broker, and what
   additional proof does the designated broker's zero ownership trigger?
2. Under § 1101.356(a), is Dana herself presently eligible to apply for a broker licence? Which element is
   the obstacle?
3. Under § 1101.652(b)(26), what is wrong with the plan for the unlicensed assistant?
4. Under § 1101.651(a) and § 1101.652(b)(11), may the $500 referral bonus be paid to the past client?
5. Under § 1101.652(b)(23)(C) and (D), what must "The Door Team" advertising include, and what must it not
   imply?

## Unit: Fiduciary Duties

An agent occupies a position of trust, and the duties that flow from that position are called
**fiduciary duties**. They are duties of the whole person, not of the contract: they require the agent to
subordinate the agent's own interest to the client's, to a degree that ordinary arm's-length dealing never
demands. The classical catalogue has six entries, and although Chapter 1101 does not set them out as a list
under that heading, nearly every one of them has a Texas disciplinary provision standing behind it. This
unit pairs each duty with the provision that gives it teeth, and is explicit where no held source supplies
one.

Before the list, one Texas-specific override. Section 1101.561(a) provides that the duties of a license
holder acting as an intermediary under Subchapter L "supersede the duties of a license holder established
under any other law, including common law." When you are an intermediary, the governing description of your
obligations is the Subchapter L framework taught in Unit 2 — written consent, fair and impartial conduct,
the appointment mechanics, and the five prohibitions of § 1101.651(d) — and not the single-agency fiduciary
catalogue below.

**Loyalty.** The agent must act in the client's interest, not the agent's own, and must not profit secretly
from the agency. Three provisions enforce it. Section 1101.652(b)(13) makes it a ground for discipline to
accept, receive or charge "an undisclosed commission, rebate, or direct profit on an expenditure made for a
principal" — the repair invoice with a quiet markup, the vendor kickback. Section 1101.652(b)(16) reaches a
license holder who acts "in a dual capacity as broker and undisclosed principal in a real estate
transaction" — buying the client's listing, directly or through a controlled entity, without disclosing
that the buyer is you. And § 1101.652(b)(8) reaches receiving "compensation from more than one party to a
real estate transaction without the full knowledge and consent of all parties to the transaction," which is
also why § 1101.559(a)(2) requires the parties' intermediary consent to state the source of expected
compensation. Notice what each provision actually condemns: not the profit, not the second cheque, not the
purchase — the *undisclosed* profit, the *unconsented* second cheque, the *undisclosed* self-dealing.
Disclosure is what converts a breach into a permissible arrangement.

**Obedience to lawful instructions.** The agent must follow the client's instructions within the scope of
the agency — and only lawful ones. Sections 1101.652(b)(19) and (20) supply the enforcement: it is a ground
for discipline to offer to sell or lease property "without the knowledge and consent of the owner of the
real property or the owner's authorized agent," or to offer it "on terms other than those authorized by the
owner." Section 1101.652(b)(18) adds the sign: no sign offering property for sale or lease without the
owner's written consent or that of the owner's authorized agent. The limiting word is *lawful*. A seller's
instruction not to present offers from buyers of a particular race is not an instruction the agent may obey;
§ 1101.652(b)(32) makes discrimination against an owner, potential buyer, landlord or potential tenant on
the basis of race, color, religion, sex, disability, familial status, national origin or ancestry — including
steering a prospect interested in equivalent properties to a different area on those bases — an independent
ground for discipline. Obedience never reaches an unlawful instruction.

**Disclosure.** The agent must tell the client what the client needs to know. Section 1101.557(b)(2)
requires a representing broker to inform the party if the broker "receives material information related to a
transaction to list, buy, sell, or lease the party's real estate, including the receipt of an offer by the
broker," and § 1101.557(b)(3) requires the broker at a minimum to answer the party's questions and present
any offer to or from the party. Toward the other side of the transaction, disclosure is a duty too, and a
sharper one than students expect. Section 1101.652(b)(3) makes it a ground for discipline to make "a material
misrepresentation to a potential buyer concerning a significant defect, including a latent structural defect,
known to the license holder that would be a significant factor to a reasonable and prudent buyer in making a
decision to purchase real property," and (b)(4) reaches failing to disclose such a known defect to a potential
buyer. That duty runs to a *potential buyer* — a customer, not a client. Representing the seller does not
license you to conceal a known significant defect from the person across the table.

Two boundaries on disclosure deserve equal emphasis. Section 1101.556 provides that notwithstanding other
law, a license holder is not required to inquire about, disclose or release information relating to whether a
previous or current occupant of real property had, may have had, has or may have AIDS, an HIV-related illness
or an HIV infection as defined by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, or whether "a death occurred
on a property by natural causes, suicide, or accident unrelated to the condition of the property." Section
1101.802 backs it up by providing that a person is not civilly or criminally liable for failing to inquire
about, disclose or release information relating to the HIV/AIDS matters described. And § 1101.555 imposes an
affirmative written advisory: when an offer to purchase Texas real estate is signed, the license holder shall
advise each buyer in writing that the buyer should have the abstract examined by an attorney of the buyer's
choosing or be provided with or obtain a title insurance policy — with the discipline in § 1101.652(b)(29) and
the loss of commission in § 1101.806(d) if it is skipped.

**Confidentiality.** The agent must not disclose the client's confidences, above all those that would weaken
the client's bargaining position. In the intermediary setting the duty is spelled out in statute:
§ 1101.651(d)(1) and (2) forbid disclosing that the seller will take less or that the buyer will pay more,
absent a separate written instruction from that party, and (d)(3) forbids disclosing "any confidential
information or any information a party specifically instructs the broker or sales agent in writing not to
disclose," subject to three exceptions — a separate written instruction from the party, a requirement to
disclose under Chapter 1101 or a court order, and information that "materially relates to the condition of the
property." Outside the intermediary setting, Chapter 1101 does not contain a general confidentiality section,
and this module does not pretend that it does; the duty in ordinary single agency is a common-law fiduciary
duty. But the property-condition carve-out in (d)(3) tells you how Texas resolves the tension whenever it
arises: negotiating posture is confidential, defects in the house are not.

**Reasonable care and diligence.** The agent must bring competence and effort to the work. Section
1101.652(b)(1) makes it a ground for discipline that a license holder "acts negligently or incompetently."
Section 1101.652(a)(6) reaches a license holder who "fails to consider market conditions for the specific
geographic area in which the license holder is providing a service." Section 1101.652(b)(2) reaches conduct
"that is dishonest or in bad faith or that demonstrates untrustworthiness," and (b)(5) reaches making "a false
promise that is likely to influence a person to enter into an agreement when the license holder is unable or
does not intend to keep the promise." Section 1101.652(b)(28) requires providing, on request, a copy of a
transaction document to a person who signed it, and § 1101.652(a)(5) reaches failing to surrender to the owner,
without just cause, a document or instrument in the license holder's possession that the owner requests. The
duty of care is also where the boundary of competence lives: § 1101.654 draws the line at practising law, and
the diligent answer to a legal question is a referral, not a guess.

**Accounting.** The agent must account for and safeguard money and property that belong to another. Section
1101.652(b)(9) makes it a ground for discipline to fail "within a reasonable time to properly account for or
remit money that is received by the license holder and that belongs to another person." Section
1101.652(b)(10) reaches commingling — mixing another person's money with the license holder's own. Section
1101.652(b)(30) requires depositing money received as escrow or trust funds within a reasonable time, either
in trust with a title company authorized to do business in Texas or in a custodial, trust or escrow account
maintained for that purpose in a banking institution authorized to do business in Texas. Section
1101.652(b)(31) forbids disbursing money from such an account "before the completion or termination of the real
estate transaction." Section 1101.652(b)(24) adds the paperwork side: withholding from or inserting into a
statement of account or invoice a statement the license holder knows makes it materially inaccurate.

**Which duties survive the end of the agency.** The classical teaching is that when the agency terminates,
the forward-looking duties end — loyalty in future dealings, obedience, the duty to keep working — while two
obligations continue: **confidentiality**, because the client's secrets do not become yours when the listing
expires, and **accounting**, because money that belongs to another does not become yours when the relationship
does. Be precise about the authority for that. No source read for this module states a survival rule in so
many words. What the statutory text supports is this: the accounting provisions in § 1101.652(b)(9), (10),
(30) and (31) are written in terms of money "that belongs to another person" and of the transaction's
completion or termination, not in terms of a live agency, so an expired listing does not release the license
holder from them; and the confidentiality prohibition in § 1101.651(d)(3) contains no expiration and no
exception for a concluded transaction. Beyond that textual observation, the survival of the confidentiality
duty in ordinary single agency is a common-law proposition, and this module identifies it as one rather than
citing a statute for it.

Finally, hold on to § 1101.801: "Disciplinary action taken against a person under Section 1101.652 does not
relieve the person from civil or criminal liability." A fiduciary breach in Texas can cost the licence and
cost a judgment, and the two proceedings do not substitute for each other.

### Applied exercise: the repair invoice and the expired listing

A license holder representing a Bryan seller arranges pre-listing repairs. The contractor bills $4,200. The
license holder forwards the seller an invoice for $4,700, keeping the difference as "coordination time," and
does not mention the arrangement. Separately, the seller told the license holder in confidence that a divorce
is forcing a quick sale. The listing expires unsold. Two months later the license holder, now representing a
buyer, shows that same house — relisted with another brokerage — and remarks to the buyer, "They're divorcing;
they'll take a lot less."

1. Which subsection of § 1101.652 does the invoice markup implicate, and what one act by the license holder
   would have changed the analysis?
2. Was the seller's motivation the sort of information § 1101.651(d)(3) protects — and what does this module
   say about the authority for treating confidentiality as surviving the end of the agency?
3. If the license holder is still holding $500 of the seller's unspent repair advance, which two subsections of
   § 1101.652 govern what must happen to it, and does the expiration of the listing change the answer?
4. Suppose the Commission takes no action. Under § 1101.801, is the license holder therefore safe from the
   seller?

## Unit: Texas Deceptive Trade Practices - Consumer Protection Act

Business & Commerce Code Chapter 17, Subchapter E — cited under its own short title, in § 17.41, as the
**Deceptive Trade Practices-Consumer Protection Act** — is the statute that most often converts a careless
sentence about a property into personal liability for the person who said it. It is not a real estate statute.
It is a general consumer statute, and that is precisely why it reaches so far into this business.

**How the statute is to be read.** Section 17.44(a) instructs that the subchapter "shall be liberally construed
and applied to promote its underlying purposes, which are to protect consumers against false, misleading, and
deceptive business practices, unconscionable actions, and breaches of warranty and to provide efficient and
economical procedures to secure such protection." Section 17.44(b) provides that Chapter 27, Property Code,
prevails over the subchapter to the extent of any conflict. Section 17.43 makes the remedies cumulative rather
than exclusive, while cautioning that a violation of another law is not by itself a DTPA violation, and that no
recovery of both damages and penalties under the DTPA and another law is permitted for the same act.

**Waiver is presumptively void.** Section 17.42(a) provides that "Any waiver by a consumer of the provisions of
this subchapter is contrary to public policy and is unenforceable and void," subject to a narrow exception: a
waiver is valid only if it is in writing and signed by the consumer, the consumer is not in a significantly
disparate bargaining position, and the consumer is represented by legal counsel in seeking or acquiring the goods
or services. Section 17.42(b) voids the waiver if the consumer's counsel was directly or indirectly identified,
suggested or selected by a defendant or a defendant's agent. Section 17.42(c) requires the waiver to be
conspicuous, in boldface type of at least 10 points, headed "Waiver of Consumer Rights" or words of similar
meaning, and substantially in the statutory form quoted in the section. A license holder should draw the obvious
inference: you cannot paper your way out of the DTPA with a clause in your own agreement.

**Who is a "consumer."** This is the threshold question in every DTPA analysis and the one students most often
skip. Section 17.45(4) defines "Consumer" as "an individual, partnership, corporation, this state, or a
subdivision or agency of this state who seeks or acquires by purchase or lease, any goods or services, except
that the term does not include a business consumer that has assets of $25 million or more, or that is owned or
controlled by a corporation or entity with assets of $25 million or more." Two elements: the person must *seek or
acquire* — an actual or prospective purchaser or lessee, not a bystander — and what is sought or acquired must be
*goods or services*.

Both of those terms are defined, and both reach real estate. Section 17.45(1): "'Goods' means tangible chattels or
real property purchased or leased for use." Section 17.45(2): "'Services' means work, labor, or service purchased
or leased for use, including services furnished in connection with the sale or repair of goods." Section 17.45(6)
defines "trade" and "commerce" to mean "the advertising, offering for sale, sale, lease, or distribution of any
good or service, of any property, tangible or intangible, real, personal, or mixed, and any other article,
commodity, or thing of value, wherever situated," and to include any trade or commerce directly or indirectly
affecting the people of Texas. So a Texas buyer of a house is a consumer seeking goods; a Texas client engaging a
brokerage is a consumer seeking services. Section 17.45(10) separately defines "business consumer" as an
individual, partnership or corporation seeking or acquiring goods or services for commercial or business use,
excluding the state and its subdivisions and agencies — the category the $25 million asset ceiling in (4) operates
on. Section 17.45(12) defines "residence" for the subchapter as a single-family house, duplex, triplex, quadruplex,
or a unit in a multiunit structure with condominium or cooperative unit title, that is occupied or to be occupied
as the consumer's residence; that definition matters for the exemptions discussed below.

**What is unlawful.** Section 17.46(a) declares that "False, misleading, or deceptive acts or practices in the
conduct of any trade or commerce are hereby declared unlawful," subject to action by the consumer protection
division of the attorney general's office under §§ 17.47, 17.58, 17.60 and 17.61. Section 17.46(b) then supplies
the **laundry list** — a long enumeration introduced by the words "the term 'false, misleading, or deceptive acts
or practices' includes, but is not limited to, the following acts." The "but is not limited to" language governs
enforcement by the attorney general. For a *private consumer suit* the list is a closed set: § 17.46(d) provides
that for purposes of the relief authorized in § 17.50(a)(1), the term "is limited to the acts enumerated in
specific subdivisions of Subsection (b)." A private plaintiff must therefore point at a numbered item.

Here are the enumerated items a real estate license holder is most likely to meet, in the statute's words:

- **§ 17.46(b)(5)** — "representing that goods or services have sponsorship, approval, characteristics,
  ingredients, uses, benefits, or quantities which they do not have or that a person has a sponsorship, approval,
  status, affiliation, or connection which the person does not." This is the workhorse. "The addition was
  permitted." "It's zoned for a duplex." "That's a new roof." "The schools are in the district." Each is a
  representation about a characteristic, use or benefit of the property.
- **§ 17.46(b)(7)** — "representing that goods or services are of a particular standard, quality, or grade, or that
  goods are of a particular style or model, if they are of another."
- **§ 17.46(b)(9)** — "advertising goods or services with intent not to sell them as advertised." The listing that
  exists to generate calls rather than to sell that house on those terms.
- **§ 17.46(b)(12)** — "representing that an agreement confers or involves rights, remedies, or obligations which it
  does not have or involve, or which are prohibited by law." Telling a buyer the contract gives a right it does not
  give; telling a seller the listing obliges something it does not.
- **§ 17.46(b)(14)** — "misrepresenting the authority of a salesman, representative or agent to negotiate the final
  terms of a consumer transaction." Note how directly this bears on the agency material in Unit 1: overstating your
  own authority to bind is itself an enumerated deceptive act.
- **§ 17.46(b)(20)** — "representing that a guaranty or warranty confers or involves rights or remedies which it does
  not have or involve." The residential service contract or builder warranty described more generously than it reads.
- **§ 17.46(b)(24)** — "failing to disclose information concerning goods or services which was known at the time of
  the transaction if such failure to disclose such information was intended to induce the consumer into a transaction
  into which the consumer would not have entered had the information been disclosed." Read every element. The
  information must have been *known*, at the time, and the failure must have been *intended to induce*. Silence alone
  is not (b)(24); silence deployed to get the deal signed is.

Section 17.46(c)(1) adds an interpretive instruction: in construing subsection (a) in suits brought under § 17.47,
courts are to be guided so far as possible by subsection (b) and by the interpretations given by the Federal Trade
Commission and the federal courts to section 5(a)(1) of the Federal Trade Commission Act.

**The consumer's causes of action.** Section 17.50(a) provides that "A consumer may maintain an action where any of
the following constitute a producing cause of economic damages or damages for mental anguish": (1) the use or
employment of a false, misleading or deceptive act or practice that is "specifically enumerated in a subdivision of
Subsection (b) of Section 17.46" *and* "relied on by a consumer to the consumer's detriment"; (2) breach of an express
or implied warranty; (3) "any unconscionable action or course of action by any person"; or (4) an act or practice in
violation of Chapter 541, Insurance Code. Two features of (a)(1) deserve underlining: the standard is **producing
cause**, not the more demanding proximate cause, and the laundry-list route requires **detrimental reliance**.

"Unconscionable action or course of action" is defined in § 17.45(5) as "an act or practice which, to a consumer's
detriment, takes advantage of the lack of knowledge, ability, experience, or capacity of the consumer to a grossly
unfair degree." That is a high bar — "grossly unfair" — but it is a route that does not depend on any numbered item.

**Damages, and what "knowingly" and "intentionally" do to them.** Section 17.50(b)(1) is the provision to know
verbatim, because the escalation is where the exposure becomes serious. A prevailing consumer may obtain "the amount
of economic damages found by the trier of fact. If the trier of fact finds that the conduct of the defendant was
committed knowingly, the consumer may also recover damages for mental anguish, as found by the trier of fact, and the
trier of fact may award not more than three times the amount of economic damages; or if the trier of fact finds the
conduct was committed intentionally, the consumer may recover damages for mental anguish, as found by the trier of
fact, and the trier of fact may award not more than three times the amount of damages for mental anguish and economic
damages."

Parse the two tiers. A finding of **knowingly** unlocks mental anguish damages plus additional damages of up to three
times *economic* damages. A finding of **intentionally** unlocks mental anguish damages plus additional damages of up
to three times the *combined* mental anguish and economic damages. The base measure is defined in § 17.45(11):
"'Economic damages' means compensatory damages for pecuniary loss, including costs of repair and replacement," and it
excludes exemplary damages and damages for physical pain and mental anguish, loss of consortium, disfigurement,
physical impairment, or loss of companionship and society.

The mental states are defined too, and the definitions are the practical heart of DTPA risk management. Section
17.45(9): "'Knowingly' means actual awareness, at the time of the act or practice complained of, of the falsity,
deception, or unfairness of the act or practice giving rise to the consumer's claim... but actual awareness may be
inferred where objective manifestations indicate that a person acted with actual awareness." Section 17.45(13):
"'Intentionally' means actual awareness of the falsity, deception, or unfairness of the act or practice... coupled with
the specific intent that the consumer act in detrimental reliance on the falsity or deception or in detrimental
ignorance of the unfairness," and intention "may be inferred from objective manifestations that indicate that the
person acted intentionally or from facts showing that a defendant acted with flagrant disregard of prudent and fair
business practices to the extent that the defendant should be treated as having acted intentionally." Note both
inference clauses. Neither state requires a confession.

Section 17.50(b) also authorizes injunctive relief, orders restoring money or property acquired in violation of the
subchapter, and other relief the court deems proper. Section 17.50(d) provides that "Each consumer who prevails shall
be awarded court costs and reasonable and necessary attorneys' fees" — mandatory, and often the largest single number
in a small case. Section 17.50(c) balances it: on a finding that the action was groundless in fact or law, brought in
bad faith, or brought for harassment, the court "shall award to the defendant reasonable and necessary attorneys' fees
and court costs." Section 17.50(e) excludes attorneys' fees, costs and prejudgment interest from the computation of
additional damages, and § 17.50(g) provides that Chapter 41, Civil Practice and Remedies Code, does not apply to a DTPA
cause of action.

**Pre-suit notice and the settlement window.** Section 17.505(a) makes notice a prerequisite: "As a prerequisite to
filing a suit seeking damages under Subdivision (1) of Subsection (b) of Section 17.50 of this subchapter against any
person, a consumer shall give written notice to the person at least 60 days before filing the suit advising the person
in reasonable detail of the consumer's specific complaint and the amount of economic damages, damages for mental
anguish, and expenses, including attorneys' fees, if any, reasonably incurred by the consumer in asserting the claim."
During that 60-day period a written request to inspect the goods may be presented to the consumer. Section 17.505(b)
excuses the notice where 60 days' notice is impracticable because suit must be filed to prevent limitations from
running, or where the claim is asserted as a counterclaim. Section 17.505(c) lets a defendant who did not receive notice
file a plea in abatement not later than the 30th day after filing an original answer; under § 17.505(d) the court shall
abate after a hearing, and the suit abates automatically on the 11th day after a verified, uncontroverted plea; under
§ 17.505(e) the abatement continues until the 60th day after notice is properly served.

That notice period is an opportunity, not merely a formality. Section 17.5052(a) permits a person who receives notice
under § 17.505 to tender an offer of settlement at any time during the 60 days after receipt. Section 17.5052(d)
requires the offer to state separately an amount as settlement of the damages claim and an amount to compensate the
consumer for reasonable and necessary attorneys' fees incurred as of the date of the offer. Under § 17.5052(e) the
offer is rejected unless both parts are accepted within 30 days. And § 17.5052(g) and (h) supply the incentive: if the
court finds the damages amount tendered was the same as, substantially the same as, or more than the damages found by
the trier of fact, the consumer may not recover damages exceeding the lesser of the amount tendered or the amount
found, and attorneys' fees may be capped at the amount tendered where the tender met the same test. Section 17.5052(k)
provides that an offer is not an admission of liability. Section 17.5051 separately allows a party to move to compel
mediation within 90 days after service of a pleading seeking DTPA relief.

**Defenses.** Section 17.506(a) gives a defendant a defense to any damages or attorneys' fees on proof that "before
consummation of the transaction he gave reasonable and timely written notice to the plaintiff of the defendant's
reliance on" written information about the particular goods or service obtained from official government records, or
from another source, or written information concerning a test required or prescribed by a government agency — in each
case where the information was false or inaccurate and "the defendant did not know and could not reasonably have known
of the falsity or inaccuracy." Section 17.506(b) requires the defendant to prove the written information was a producing
cause of the alleged damage, and provides that a finding of one producing cause does not bar recovery if other conduct
of the defendant was also a producing cause. Section 17.506(c) permits suit against the third party who supplied the
written information, without privity, where that third party knew or should reasonably have foreseen the information
would be provided to a consumer.

There is a second, complete defense in § 17.506(d): a defendant who proves he received the consumer's notice and,
"within 30 days after the day on which the defendant received the notice," tendered the economic damages and mental
anguish damages claimed plus the consumer's reasonably incurred expenses including attorneys' fees, has a defense to
the cause of action. Section 17.565 supplies the limitations period: all actions under the subchapter must be commenced
within two years after the date the false, misleading or deceptive act or practice occurred, or within two years after
the consumer discovered or in the exercise of reasonable diligence should have discovered it — extendable by 180 days
where the plaintiff proves the delay was caused by the defendant knowingly engaging in conduct solely calculated to
induce the plaintiff to postpone suit.

**The exemptions — including one written for license holders.** Section 17.49 contains several. Subsection (c) is the
**professional services exemption**: "Nothing in this subchapter shall apply to a claim for damages based on the
rendering of a professional service, the essence of which is the providing of advice, judgment, opinion, or similar
professional skill." But it then removes five things from the exemption's protection: an express misrepresentation of a
material fact that cannot be characterized as advice, judgment or opinion; a failure to disclose information in
violation of § 17.46(b)(24); an unconscionable action or course of action that cannot be characterized as advice,
judgment or opinion; breach of an express warranty that cannot be so characterized; and a violation of § 17.46(b)(26).

Subsection (i) is the provision written for this profession, and every Texas license holder should be able to recite its
shape: "Nothing in this subchapter shall apply to a claim against a person licensed as a broker or salesperson under
Chapter 1101, Occupations Code, arising from an act or omission by the person while acting as a broker or salesperson.
This exemption does not apply to: (1) an express misrepresentation of a material fact that cannot be characterized as
advice, judgment, or opinion; (2) a failure to disclose information in violation of Section 17.46(b)(24); or (3) an
unconscionable action or course of action that cannot be characterized as advice, judgment, or opinion."

Understand what that gives you and what it does not. It gives you protection for the *advisory* part of the job — the
market opinion, the pricing judgment, the strategic counsel. It gives you nothing at all for a flat factual assertion.
"I think this will appraise" is advice. "It appraised at $412,000 last month" is an express misrepresentation of
material fact if it did not. The exemption is drawn precisely along the line that separates opinion from fact, and it
is on the fact side of that line that license holders get sued.

Two further exemptions turn on transaction size. Section 17.49(f) exempts a claim arising out of a written contract if
the contract relates to a transaction or set of related transactions involving total consideration by the consumer of
more than $100,000, the consumer was represented in negotiating the contract by legal counsel not identified, suggested
or selected by the defendant or the defendant's agent, and "the contract does not involve the consumer's residence."
Section 17.49(g) exempts a cause of action arising from a transaction or set of related transactions involving total
consideration by the consumer of more than $500,000, "other than a cause of action involving a consumer's residence."
Note the residence carve-out in both: the statute is at its most protective exactly where an individual's home is
concerned. Section 17.49(e) provides that except as specifically provided by § 17.50(b) and (h), nothing in the
subchapter applies to a cause of action for bodily injury or death or for the infliction of mental anguish.

**The practical lesson.** Bring the three doctrines together and you have the whole risk profile. First, your Texas
buyer or seller is almost certainly a "consumer" under § 17.45(4), because a house is "goods" under § 17.45(1) and
brokerage is "services" under § 17.45(2). Second, the sentence that hurts you is the confident factual sentence — it
lands in § 17.46(b)(5) or (7), it is outside the § 17.49(i) exemption because it cannot be characterized as advice or
opinion, and under § 17.50(a)(1) it needs only to be a producing cause of economic damages and to have been relied on.
Third, if the trier of fact concludes you had actual awareness of its falsity — inferable, under § 17.45(9), from
objective manifestations — mental anguish damages and up to treble economic damages come into play under
§ 17.50(b)(1), together with the consumer's mandatory attorneys' fees under § 17.50(d). And § 1101.801 reminds you that
none of this is instead of a Commission proceeding.

The defensive habits follow from the statute rather than from folklore. Attribute every fact to its source — the survey,
the appraisal district record, the inspection report, the seller's disclosure — and say the source out loud, in writing,
which is also the posture § 17.506(a) rewards. Say "I don't know; let's verify" rather than answering from memory.
Disclose what you know about the property, particularly anything that meets the "known at the time" element of
§ 17.46(b)(24), and note that Chapter 1101 independently requires disclosure of known significant defects under
§ 1101.652(b)(3) and (4). And when a demand letter arrives, read § 17.505 and § 17.5052 before responding: the 60-day
window is the cheapest moment the dispute will ever have.

### Applied exercise: the flooded garage in Katy

A married couple buys a house in Katy for $365,000 as their residence. During a showing, the sales agent — who
represents the seller — is asked whether the garage has ever taken water. The agent, who two months earlier read an email
from the seller describing water in the garage after a storm, replies, "No, this place has never had any water
intrusion." The buyers rely on the answer, buy the house, and after the first heavy rain spend $18,000 on drainage and
remediation. They also testify to sleeplessness and distress. The agent was sponsored by a broker at all relevant times.

1. Are the buyers "consumers" under § 17.45(4)? Identify the goods or services and confirm that no part of the
   definition's exclusion applies on these facts.
2. Which subdivision of § 17.46(b) does the statement most directly implicate, and could § 17.46(b)(24) also be in play?
   What element of (b)(24) would have to be proved?
3. Does the § 17.49(i) exemption protect the agent here? Identify the words of the exemption and of its exception that
   decide it.
4. Assume the trier of fact finds $18,000 of economic damages and finds the conduct was committed knowingly. Under
   § 17.50(b)(1), what becomes available that was not available without that finding, and what is the maximum multiple
   of economic damages the trier of fact may award? Now assume the finding is "intentionally" instead — what changes
   about the multiplier's base?
5. Under § 17.505(a), what must the buyers do at least 60 days before filing suit, and under § 17.5052 and § 17.506(d),
   what two responses are open to the agent and the broker during that period?
6. Under § 1101.652(b)(3) and (4) and § 1101.801, what exposure exists alongside the civil claim, and does resolving one
   dispose of the other?

### Module recap

This module traced one relationship from its creation to its consequences. It began with agency itself: the arrangement
in which one person acts for another and binds that other, declared for Texas brokerage by Occupations Code
§ 1101.557(a) and organized so that the agency belongs to the broker while the sales agent works through the broker
under § 1101.351(c) and § 1101.803. It distinguished the ways an agency comes into being and the difference between the
authority the principal actually gave and the authority a third person is reasonably led to perceive, and it marked
where Texas has legislated over the common law — in § 1101.805 on liability for misrepresentation, in § 1101.557(c)(1)
on the power to bind, and in § 1101.654 on the boundary at the practice of law.

It then named the positions a Texas license holder may occupy. Seller representation and buyer representation are
familiar. The two that make this a Texas course are the **intermediary** — the mechanism Chapter 1101 supplies in place
of the dual agency other states use, requiring written consent under § 1101.559, permitting appointments under
§ 1101.560, and constrained by the five prohibitions of § 1101.651(d) — and the **non-representation showing** under
§ 1101.562, effective January 1, 2026, which arrives paired with the written-agreement requirement of § 1101.563 and its
fourteen-day, non-exclusive limits on showing-only agreements. Around both sits the disclosure architecture of
§ 1101.558: an oral-or-written representation disclosure at first contact, a written notice at first substantive
communication about specific property, and three exceptions, including the narrow open-house exception.

The brokerage unit put those positions inside a business — licensed entities and designated brokers under § 1101.355,
sponsorship and supervision under § 1101.351(c), § 1101.458 and § 1101.803, agreements with definite termination dates
under § 1101.652(b)(12), a closed compensation loop under § 1101.651(a)–(c), and the disclosure, required by
§ 1101.563(c)(2), that broker compensation is not set by law and is fully negotiable. The fiduciary unit gave the
relationship its moral content and attached each duty — loyalty, obedience, disclosure, confidentiality, care,
accounting — to the subsection of § 1101.652 that makes its breach a disciplinary matter, while being candid about which
propositions rest on common law rather than on a statute read for this course. The DTPA unit then showed what happens
when the duty of accurate speech fails: a consumer under § 17.45(4), an enumerated act under § 17.46(b), a claim under
§ 17.50 that the § 17.49(i) exemption will not cover because it was fact rather than opinion, and damages that escalate
on findings of knowing or intentional conduct.

You should now be able to name your position in any Texas transaction in one sentence, produce the writing that proves
it, state which duties you owe and what a breach costs under § 1101.652, and recognize before you speak the difference
between an opinion the law protects and a fact the law will hold you to.
