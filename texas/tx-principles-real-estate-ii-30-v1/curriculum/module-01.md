# Texas Principles of Real Estate II — Module 01

## TREC Topic: Real Estate Math

**Prescribed time: 30 minutes.** This module carries the two units the Commission's PRINS2-1 course
approval form places under *Real Estate Math*, whose scope note is *"Key terms/overview."*

**Learning objectives.** After completing this module you will be able to: state the order of
operations a calculation must follow, use a calculator's memory and percent functions without
introducing error, and apply a rounding rule that keeps a commission or proration accurate to the
cent; and convert among fractions, decimals and percentages, compute any one of part, whole or rate
from the other two, and distinguish a percentage increase from a percentage decrease when applying
either to commission splits, list-to-sale price ratios and fractional ownership shares.

**Every legal statement in this module comes from a source that was read for this course.** This
module makes no legal statement at all: it teaches arithmetic as arithmetic. No Texas statute, TREC
rule or federal law prescribes the order of operations, a rounding convention, or a commission rate,
and none is cited here. Every dollar figure and every percentage in this module is an illustration
chosen to make the arithmetic visible. None of them is a required, customary or standard figure, and
you should not carry any of them into a real transaction.

## Unit: Calculators

Real estate arithmetic is not difficult mathematics. Almost everything a Texas license holder
computes in a working week — a commission, a seller's net, a tax proration, a price per square foot,
a loan-to-value figure — is multiplication, division, and the occasional subtraction. What makes real
estate math go wrong is almost never the mathematics. It is the machine, and the habit of reaching
for the machine before the problem has been stated.

So begin with the discipline that prevents most errors: **write the equation before you touch the
keys.** On paper or on screen, put down what you are solving for, what you know, and how they
connect — *commission = sale price × commission rate*, or *daily amount = annual amount ÷ 365* —
before you enter a single digit. A calculator will faithfully compute the wrong thing. Writing the
equation first is what catches the wrong thing while it is still cheap to catch.

**Order of operations.** Arithmetic has a fixed precedence: parentheses first, then exponents, then
multiplication and division from left to right, then addition and subtraction from left to right. The
trap is that calculators disagree about whether to honor it. A **scientific calculator** applies
precedence: enter `412500 − 412500 × 0.06 =` and it multiplies first, returning 387,750. A basic
**four-function calculator** typically evaluates strictly left to right as you press keys: it
subtracts 412,500 from 412,500 to get zero, multiplies zero by 0.06, and returns 0. Both machines are
working correctly. Only one is answering the question you asked. The fix is not to memorize which
machine you own but to break the calculation into single operations you control: compute
412,500 × 0.06 = 24,750 first, then subtract that from 412,500 to reach a seller's figure of 387,750.

**The percent key.** The `%` key is the most misleading key on the keypad because it does different
things in different contexts, and often something other than what the user expects. On many
calculators `200 × 6 %` returns 12 — the key divided by 100 for you. On many of those same
calculators, `200 − 6 %` returns 188, because the key interpreted 6 as *6 percent of 200* and
subtracted 12. On others, the same keystrokes return 194, because the key simply converted 6 to 0.06
and subtracted it. A common error is to assume the key behaves consistently. The safe practice is to
never use it: convert the percentage to a decimal yourself by moving the decimal point two places
left — 6% becomes 0.06, 5.5% becomes 0.055, 0.5% becomes 0.005 — and multiply. You lose nothing, and
you always know what the machine did.

**The memory keys.** `M+` adds the display to memory, `M−` subtracts from it, `MR` (or `MRC`) recalls
it, and `MC` clears it. Their real value is not storage but *precision*: they let you carry a full
unrounded intermediate result into the next step instead of writing down a rounded one and keying it
back in. Clear memory before every new problem. A stale value in memory is a silent error, because
nothing on the display announces it.

**Rounding discipline.** Round once, at the end, to the precision the answer actually requires —
money to the cent, square footage to the whole foot, a rate to the hundredth of a percent. Rounding
an intermediate result and then multiplying it magnifies the error by the multiplier.

*Illustration.* Suppose an annual property tax bill of $4,382 is being prorated over 214 days.
The daily amount is 4,382 ÷ 365 = 12.005479…. Round that to $12.01 first and multiply:
12.01 × 214 = $2,570.14. Keep the full figure and multiply: 12.005479 × 214 = $2,569.17. The two
answers differ by $0.97 on a single line item, and every party at closing is entitled to the second
one. The half-cent you discarded was multiplied 214 times.

**Sanity-check by estimation.** Before you accept any number the calculator gives you, produce an
independent rough answer in your head and compare. The most useful tool for this is the **1% method**:
1% of any figure is that figure with the decimal moved two places left, and every other percentage is
a small multiple or fraction of it. One percent of $412,500 is $4,125; six percent is six of those,
about $24,750 — so a displayed commission of $2,475 or $247,500 is wrong by a factor of ten, and you
know it in two seconds. Ten percent, one percent, and one-half are enough to bracket nearly any real
estate figure. A calculator answer that survives an estimate is usually right; one that contradicts
an estimate is a mis-keyed digit or a misplaced decimal until proven otherwise.

## Unit: Fractions and Percentages

A **fraction**, a **decimal** and a **percent** are three notations for one quantity, and fluent
movement among them is most of what real estate math asks of you. To convert a fraction to a decimal,
divide the top number by the bottom: 3 ÷ 4 = 0.75. To convert a decimal to a percent, move the
decimal point two places right and add the sign: 0.75 becomes 75%. To convert a percent back to a
decimal, move two places left: 5.5% becomes 0.055. Committing a handful of equivalents to memory pays
for itself daily — 1/2 = 0.5 = 50%, 1/4 = 0.25 = 25%, 3/4 = 0.75 = 75%, 1/3 = 0.3333 ≈ 33.33%,
2/3 = 0.6667 ≈ 66.67%, 1/5 = 0.2 = 20%, and 1/8 = 0.125 = 12.5%.

**The percentage relationship has three forms.** Every percentage problem you will meet contains a
**part**, a **whole** (sometimes called the base) and a **rate**, and gives you two of the three:

- part = whole × rate
- rate = part ÷ whole
- whole = part ÷ rate

The arithmetic is trivial; the skill is labeling. *Identify the whole first.* The whole is the figure
the percentage is taken **of** — the sale price a commission is computed on, the list price a
reduction is measured against, the total proceeds a share is carved out of. Once the whole is
correctly named, the rest follows.

*Illustration, finding the part.* A home sells for $385,000 at a total commission rate of 5.5%.
Whole = 385,000, rate = 0.055. Part = 385,000 × 0.055 = **$21,175**.

*Illustration, finding the rate.* A brokerage was paid $21,175 on a $385,000 sale. Rate =
21,175 ÷ 385,000 = 0.055 = **5.5%**.

*Illustration, finding the whole.* A total commission of $24,750 was earned at 6%. Whole =
24,750 ÷ 0.06 = **$412,500**, the sale price.

**Increase and decrease are not symmetric,** and this is the single most reliable source of error in
percentage work. A percentage always refers to a base, and when the quantity moves, the base moves
with it. Take a Texas listing at $425,000 reduced by 10%: 425,000 × 0.10 = 42,500, so the new price
is $382,500. Now restore it. Adding 10% to the *new* base gives 382,500 × 1.10 = $420,750 — $4,250
short of where you started. To get back to $425,000 you need 42,500 ÷ 382,500 = 0.1111, an increase
of **11.11%**. A common error is to assume that a decrease and an equal-sized increase cancel. They
never do, because the decrease is taken on the larger number and the increase on the smaller one.

**List-to-sale price ratio.** This is a plain application of *rate = part ÷ whole*, where the sale
price is the part and the list price is the whole. A property listed at $425,000 that closes at
$382,500 has a ratio of 382,500 ÷ 425,000 = 0.90 = **90%**. Say what you mean: the property sold *at*
90% of list, which is the same as saying it sold *10% below* list. The two figures always sum to 100%,
and confusing "sold at 90%" with "sold 90% below" is the kind of slip that survives all the way into
a listing presentation.

**Shares of ownership.** Fractional interests convert to percentages the same way any fraction does,
and the check is that they must sum to one whole. Suppose three co-owners hold a tract in shares of
5/8, 1/4 and 1/8, and net proceeds of $96,000 are to be distributed. Convert or multiply directly:
96,000 × 5/8 = $60,000; 96,000 × 1/4 = $24,000; 96,000 × 1/8 = $12,000. Sum: 60,000 + 24,000 +
12,000 = $96,000. If the shares do not add to 1 (or 100%), or the distributions do not add to the
total, you have mis-stated a share — stop and find it before you go further.

**Commission splits are chained percentages,** each taken on the output of the step before it. A
$412,500 sale at 6% produces 412,500 × 0.06 = $24,750 in total commission. If the listing and buyer's
brokerages divide it equally, each side receives 24,750 ÷ 2 = $12,375. If the listing sales agent's
agreement with the sponsoring broker is a 70/30 split in the agent's favor, the agent receives
12,375 × 0.70 = **$8,662.50** and the broker retains 12,375 × 0.30 = $3,712.50. Notice that the agent
did not receive 70% of the commission or 70% of anything the seller paid; the agent received 70% of
one brokerage's half. Working the chain one link at a time, and naming the whole at each link, is the
entire technique.

### Applied exercise: a Texas listing that sold under asking

*A San Antonio seller lists a house at $349,900. It closes at $341,000. The listing agreement
provides for a total commission of 5.5% of the sale price, of which 3% is retained by the listing
brokerage and 2.5% is paid to the buyer's brokerage. The listing sales agent's agreement with her
sponsoring broker is a 70/30 split of the listing brokerage's share, in the agent's favor.*
*(Figures are illustrative only — no rate here is standard, customary or required.)*

**Task.** (1) Compute the list-to-sale price ratio to two decimal places, and state the same fact as
a percentage below list. (2) Compute the total commission and each brokerage's share. (3) Compute the
listing agent's share and her broker's. (4) Sanity-check the total commission by estimation before
accepting it.

**Model answer.** (1) Rate = part ÷ whole = 341,000 ÷ 349,900 = 0.97456… = **97.46%** of list. The
discount is 349,900 − 341,000 = $8,900, and 8,900 ÷ 349,900 = 0.02544 = **2.54% below list**; the two
figures sum to 100%. (2) Total = 341,000 × 0.055 = **$18,755**. Listing side = 341,000 × 0.03 =
$10,230; buyer's side = 341,000 × 0.025 = $8,525; check: 10,230 + 8,525 = 18,755. (3) Agent =
10,230 × 0.70 = **$7,161**; broker = 10,230 × 0.30 = $3,069; check: 7,161 + 3,069 = 10,230.
(4) Estimate: 1% of $341,000 is $3,410, so 5% is about $17,050 and another half-percent is about
$1,705 — roughly $18,750. The computed $18,755 sits right on the estimate, so no decimal has slipped.

### Module recap

This module established the two habits that make every later calculation in this course reliable.
The first is procedural. Write the equation before touching the keys; break a calculation into single
operations rather than trusting a machine to honor the order of operations; convert percentages to
decimals by hand instead of relying on a percent key that behaves differently from machine to
machine; use memory keys to carry unrounded intermediates forward; round once, at the end, to the
precision the answer requires; and check every result against a quick estimate built from the 1%
method. The tax-proration illustration showed why the rounding rule is not fussiness — rounding a
daily figure a half-cent too early moved a single closing line item by $0.97, because the error was
multiplied 214 times.

The second habit is conceptual. Fractions, decimals and percentages are one quantity in three
notations, and every percentage problem is the same relationship in one of three arrangements:
part = whole × rate, rate = part ÷ whole, whole = part ÷ rate. The work is not the arithmetic but the
labeling, and the decisive step is naming the whole — the figure the percentage is taken *of*. That
single discipline is what makes a list-to-sale price ratio (sale ÷ list), a fractional ownership
distribution (total × share, with the shares checked to 1), and a chained commission split (sale price
to total commission, to one brokerage's half, to the agent's 70%) all instances of one idea rather
than three formulas to memorize. It is also what exposes the asymmetry of increase and decrease: a
10% reduction followed by a 10% increase does not return you to the starting price, because the two
percentages were taken on different bases.

Nothing in this module is a rule of Texas law. The order of operations, the rounding conventions and
the split structures described here are arithmetic and business practice; every dollar figure was an
illustration. You should now be able to state the order of operations, use a calculator without
letting it introduce error, convert freely among the three notations, solve for part, whole or rate,
and carry a commission or price-ratio problem through to a checked answer.
