# Texas Principles of Real Estate II — Module 03

## TREC Topic: Real Estate Appraisal

**Prescribed time: 220 minutes.** This module carries the six units the Commission's PRINS2-1 course
approval form places under *Real Estate Appraisal*, whose scope note is *"Appraisal."*

**Learning objectives.** After completing this module you will be able to: state, from Chapter 1103 of
the Texas Occupations Code, who regulates appraisers in Texas, what credential categories the statute
authorizes, and how the statute structures the education, experience and examination requirements;
define the core vocabulary of appraisal — market value, price, cost, highest and best use, the four
characteristics of value, the three forms of depreciation, gross living area, comparable, and
adjustment — and distinguish the terms that are commonly confused; list the steps of the appraisal
process in order and describe what the appraiser does at each step; explain the economic principles
that drive value and identify each one at work in a Texas example; compute an indication of value under
the sales comparison, cost and income approaches and explain which approach dominates in which
assignment; and prepare and communicate a price opinion for a seller within the boundary Occupations
Code § 1101.002(1) draws between brokerage and appraisal.

**Every legal statement in this module comes from a source that was read for this course.** Where a
statement is practice rather than law, the module says so and cites nothing for it.

## Unit: Requirements to Become an Appraiser

You are studying for a real estate license, not an appraiser credential. You still need this unit,
for two reasons. The first is that you will spend your career working alongside appraisers and
sometimes in tension with them — an appraisal that comes in below the contract price is one of the most
common ways a Texas transaction goes sideways — and you cannot deal intelligently with a professional
whose qualifications and constraints you do not understand. The second reason is sharper: the same
Texas statute that tells you who may perform an appraisal also tells you what *you* may not do. The
line between what a license holder produces for a seller and what an appraiser produces for a lender
is drawn in statute, and it is drawn in this chapter. Learn the appraiser's world and you will learn
the edge of your own.

**The statute and its purpose.** Appraisers in Texas are regulated under Chapter 1103 of the
Occupations Code, which § 1103.001 tells us "may be cited as the Texas Appraiser Licensing and
Certification Act." Section 1103.002 states the chapter's purpose in two clauses: to "conform state law
relating to the regulation of real estate appraisers to the requirements adopted under Title XI,
Financial Institutions Reform, Recovery, and Enforcement Act of 1989," and to "enforce standards for
the appraisal of real property." That first clause explains almost everything peculiar about appraiser
regulation. Texas did not decide on its own to license appraisers. It did so because Congress, in the
wake of the savings-and-loan collapse of the 1980s, conditioned federal recognition of appraisals on
state licensing systems that meet federal criteria. Chapter 1103 is a state statute built to satisfy a
federal design, and it therefore repeatedly hands the details to national bodies rather than fixing
them in the Texas code. That is not a drafting failure. It is the point.

**The Board.** Section 1103.051 provides that "[t]he Texas Appraiser Licensing and Certification Board
is an independent subdivision of the Texas Real Estate Commission." Read that sentence carefully,
because students routinely get it wrong in both directions. The **Texas Appraiser Licensing and
Certification Board** — TALCB — is not a wholly separate agency floating free of TREC, and it is not
simply a department of TREC that answers to the Commission on appraiser matters. It is an *independent
subdivision*: administratively housed within the Commission, substantively independent in the exercise
of its own authority. Section 1103.003(5) confirms that throughout Chapter 1103 the word "board" means
this body. TREC licenses brokers and sales agents; TALCB licenses and certifies appraisers. If a
consumer complains about an appraisal, the complaint belongs to TALCB.

Section 1103.052(a) sets the Board's composition: nine members, consisting of "the executive secretary
of the Veterans' Land Board or the executive secretary's designee" and "eight members appointed by the
governor with the advice and consent of the senate," of whom four must be "certified or licensed
appraisers actively engaged in the practice of appraising real property" and four must be "public
members who qualify for appointment based on their recognized business ability." Subsection (b) makes
the Veterans' Land Board representative a voting member. Subsection (c) directs the governor, in making
appointments, to ensure that no single appraisal organization is overrepresented, that the Board is
independent of any trade association, profession or industry, and that the Board represents both the
diverse geographic areas of the state and a cross-section of appraisal disciplines. Appointed members
serve staggered six-year terms under § 1103.055(a), must be qualified voters of this state under
§ 1103.053(a), and must take the constitutional oath of office not later than the fifteenth day after
appointment. Section 1103.054 makes a person ineligible to serve as a *public* member if the person or
the person's spouse is a certified or licensed appraiser, is licensed by an occupational regulatory
agency in real estate brokerage or appraisal, owns or controls a business whose primary purpose is real
estate sales, brokerage or appraisal, or is employed by or participates in the management of such a
business. The Legislature's evident concern in these provisions is capture: a regulator staffed
entirely by the regulated is a regulator with a conflict, and the statute answers that concern with a
half-public board and explicit anti-overrepresentation instructions.

**The credential categories.** Here is where the federal architecture shows. Section 1103.152 does not
list the credentials. It says the Board "may … establish certificate and license categories that are
consistent with the categories recognized by the Appraiser Qualifications Board" and "prescribe
qualifications for each category that are consistent with the qualifications established for that
category by the Appraiser Qualifications Board." The **Appraiser Qualifications Board**, defined at
§ 1103.003(4), is the Appraiser Qualifications Board of the Appraisal Foundation — a national private
body, not a Texas agency. Chapter 1103 delegates the *content* of the credentials to it and keeps the
*authority to issue* them in Texas.

The definitions section nonetheless names the tiers the chapter contemplates. Section 1103.003(5-a)
defines a **certified appraiser** as "a person who is certified under this chapter to practice as a
certified general or certified residential appraiser." Section 1103.003(7) defines a **licensed
appraiser** as "a person who is licensed under this chapter to practice as a residential real estate
appraiser." Section 1103.003(4-a) defines an **appraiser trainee** as "an appraiser trainee licensed
under this chapter." So the statutory ladder, in the chapter's own vocabulary, runs: appraiser trainee;
licensed residential appraiser; certified residential appraiser; certified general appraiser. The
certified general credential is the one associated with commercial and complex property work; the
residential credentials with dwellings. Chapter 1103 itself does not state the property-type or
transaction-value limits attached to each tier, because § 1103.152 sends those to Board rule tracking
the Appraiser Qualifications Board's categories.

**Qualification: the three-legged structure.** Section 1103.202 is the section to know. To be eligible
for a certificate or license under the chapter, an applicant must:

> (1) pass the applicable examination required by Subchapter F; (2) successfully complete the number
> and type of classroom hours or other educational qualifications required by the Appraiser
> Qualifications Board; (3) provide evidence satisfactory to the board that the applicant has at least
> the minimum number of hours of experience in performing appraisals over the specified number of
> calendar years as required by the Appraiser Qualifications Board; (4) satisfy the board as to the
> applicant's honesty, trustworthiness, and integrity; and (5) comply with the requirements of Sections
> 1103.203 and 1103.2031.

Notice what subdivisions (2) and (3) do *not* say. They do not say "150 hours" or "1,000 hours" or "two
years." They say "the number and type of classroom hours … required by the Appraiser Qualifications
Board" and "the minimum number of hours of experience … over the specified number of calendar years as
required by the Appraiser Qualifications Board." **Chapter 1103 does not state the hour counts.** Any
course that tells you Texas statute requires a specific number of qualifying education hours or
experience hours for an appraiser credential is telling you something the statute does not contain.
The numbers exist, they change, and they live in the Appraiser Qualifications Board's criteria as
adopted by TALCB rule — which is exactly why the Legislature wrote the statute this way. If you need
the current figures, you go to the Board's rules and the AQB criteria, not to Chapter 1103. This course
holds neither of those documents, and so this course does not state the numbers.

The statute does say useful structural things about each leg. On **education**, § 1103.204(b) provides
that the chapter "does not limit the amount of time in which an applicant … is required to satisfy the
education requirements" — there is no statutory expiration on qualifying coursework. Subsection (c) is
directly relevant to you: the Board "shall give an applicant … credit toward fulfilling the requirements
of Section 1103.202(2) for classroom hours taken in the course of becoming licensed as a real estate
broker or sales agent or for professional development or continuing education courses taken," provided
those hours "satisfy the requirements established by the guidelines recognized by the Appraiser
Qualifications Board." A sales agent who later pursues an appraiser credential does not automatically
start from zero.

On **experience**, § 1103.205(a) likewise imposes no time limit for satisfying the requirement.
Subsection (c) provides that acceptable appraisal experience "includes any one or any combination of
the categories recognized by the Appraiser Qualifications Board" — again the delegation. Subsection (d)
is precise in a way worth noticing: "an hour of experience means 60 minutes spent in one or more of the
acceptable areas of appraisal experience recognized under this subchapter. Calculation of the hours of
experience must be based solely on actual hours of experience." Not billable hours, not estimated
hours; actual clock time. Section 1103.206(b) requires the Board to adopt a reliable verification
method that "must include the review of appraisal experience of all applicants for certification," and
gives an applicant at least sixty days after notice is sent to produce requested records. Section
1103.207 lets the Board demand more detail where a fraud, incompetency or malpractice complaint appears
reasonable or other just cause exists, including a listing of each claimed appraisal stating the
municipality or county, the type and description of the property, the approach to value used, and the
actual hours spent.

On **examination**, § 1103.251 requires the Board to prescribe an appraiser examination that must be
"written" and "approved by the Appraiser Qualifications Board." Section 1103.255 imposes a sequencing
rule students often reverse: "An applicant for the examination must fulfill the applicable experience
requirement for a certificate or license *before* taking the examination." Experience first, then exam.
Section 1103.256 sets the passing standard by reference — "the minimum score required by the Appraiser
Qualifications Board" — so again, no number in the Texas statute. Section 1103.253 requires the exam to
be offered at least once each month in Austin and at such other locations and times as the Board
determines. Section 1103.254(b) makes an examination application active for one year; an applicant who
has not passed by the first anniversary must reapply and pay again. Section 1103.257(a) requires notice
of results not later than the tenth day after the examination, and subsection (d) makes results
confidential. Under § 1103.258(b), an applicant who fails three consecutive times may not reapply
without evidence of additional education prescribed by the Board.

**The trainee tier.** Because experience must precede the examination, there must be a lawful way to
acquire experience before holding a credential, and Subchapter H supplies it. Section 1103.353 sets
trainee eligibility: at least eighteen years of age; a citizen of the United States or a lawfully
admitted alien; satisfying the Board as to honesty, trustworthiness and integrity; compliance with
§§ 1103.203 and 1103.2031; and meeting "all other qualifications established by the board." Section
1103.351 authorizes the Board to permit a certified appraiser meeting Board requirements to act as a
**supervisory appraiser**, and expressly allows a trainee to have more than one. Section 1103.354 is
the operative provision: a trainee "may perform an appraisal under the direction of a supervisory
appraiser," the supervisory appraiser "shall sign each report prepared by the appraiser trainee," and
the supervisory appraiser "is responsible to the public and to the board for the appraiser trainee's
reports and conduct." The trainee may co-sign. Responsibility, in other words, does not dilute as it
travels down; the supervisor owns the work.

**Holding the credential.** Section 1103.201(a) states the prohibition plainly: "A person may not
perform an appraisal of real estate unless the person is licensed or certified as an appraiser under
this chapter, registered as a temporary out-of-state appraiser under this chapter, or acting as an
appraiser trainee under the supervision of a supervisory appraiser." Subsection (b) adds a title
restriction — no using "certified real estate appraiser" or "licensed real estate appraiser," and no
calling one's own work a "certified appraisal" or "licensed appraisal," without the corresponding
credential. Section 1103.401 broadens that to any "title, designation, initials, or other insignia or
identification that would mislead the public" about one's credentials or competency. The teeth are
real: § 1103.5545 makes engaging in activity requiring a certificate or license without holding one a
Class A misdemeanor; § 1103.554 makes a knowing violation of § 1103.401 a Class B misdemeanor; and
§ 1103.5535 exposes an unlicensed person who received consideration to a civil penalty of not less than
the value of that consideration and not more than three times its value.

A credential expires "on the second anniversary of the date of issuance" under § 1103.211(a), and
renewal under subsection (b) requires the fee, evidence of continuing education complying with AQB
guidelines and Board rule, satisfaction of the Board as to honesty, trustworthiness and integrity, and
compliance with §§ 1103.203 and 1103.2031. Section 1103.2111 grades late renewal: expired ninety days
or less, one and one-half times the renewal fee; more than ninety days but less than six months, two
times the fee; six months or longer, no renewal at all — the person "may obtain a new certificate or
license by complying with the requirements and procedures for an original application." Subsection (c)
warns that no one may perform an appraisal in a federally related transaction while not actively
licensed or certified. Section 1103.213 provides for inactive status, during which the holder "may not
perform any activity regulated under this chapter" but must still pay annual renewal fees. Out-of-state
appraisers get Subchapter G: § 1103.301 lets an appraiser certified or licensed by another state
appraise here without a Texas credential if the person registers with the Board, and § 1103.303
conditions eligibility on the other state's program not having been disapproved by the Appraisal
Subcommittee and on the person's Texas appraisal business not exceeding six months. Section 1103.304
allows one ninety-day extension, and only one. Full reciprocity is separate: § 1103.209(a) requires the
Board to issue a reciprocal license or certificate where the other state's program complies with 12
U.S.C. § 3331 et seq., the applicant's home-state requirements meet or exceed Texas's, and the
honesty-and-integrity and application requirements are met.

**Standards of practice.** Section 1103.405(a) requires a person holding a certificate, license or
registration under the chapter to comply with "the most current edition of the Uniform Standards of
Professional Appraisal Practice adopted by the Appraisal Standards Board of the Appraisal Foundation"
or with other Board-rule standards "at least as stringent" as USPAP. Section 1103.154 authorizes the
Board to adopt rules incorporating USPAP and requiring compliance with its competency, conduct and
ethics standards. Two further practice rules matter to you as a license holder dealing with appraisers:
§ 1103.402(a) forbids a credentialed person to "sign or endorse an appraisal unless the appraisal is
substantially produced by that person," with subsection (b) defining substantial production as
contributing "in a material and verifiable manner to the research or analysis that results in the final
opinion of value"; and § 1103.404 requires retention of all business records relating to each appraisal
"until at least the fifth anniversary of the date of the appraisal." Section 1103.403(a) requires a
Texas-resident credentialed appraiser to maintain a fixed office in this state.

## Unit: Key Appraisal Terms

Appraisal has a vocabulary, and the vocabulary is unusually unforgiving. Words that are near-synonyms
in ordinary speech — value, price, cost — name three different quantities in this field, and confusing
them is the single most common error a new license holder makes when talking to a seller.

**Appraisal.** Start with the statutory definition, because in Texas it is broader than students
expect. Section 1103.003(1) provides that "appraisal" means, "regardless of whether prepared for a
federally related transaction," either "(A) an opinion of value; or (B) the act or process of developing
an opinion of value." Both the noun and the verb. Both the product and the activity. Nothing in that
definition requires a lender, a form, a fee, or a federally related transaction; the definition
expressly says otherwise. What keeps a license holder's pricing work outside this definition is not the
absence of an opinion of value — it is a separate statutory carve-out you will meet in the last unit of
this module.

**Market value, price, and cost.** These three are the foundation.

**Price** is what a particular buyer actually paid a particular seller on a particular day. It is a
historical fact. It is knowable with certainty once it happens, and it may be a terrible guide to value
if the transaction was unusual — a sale between relatives, a distressed sale, a sale where the seller
paid an enormous concession, a sale to a buyer with a strategic reason to overpay.

**Cost** is the total expenditure required to create the improvement: materials, labor, contractor's
overhead and profit, permits, soft costs. Cost is a supply-side number. It has no necessary
relationship to what anyone will pay. A homeowner in a Dallas suburb who spends $95,000 on a custom
recording studio in the garage has incurred $95,000 of cost; the market's willingness to pay for a
garage recording studio is a separate question, and in most neighborhoods the answer is a fraction of
that.

**Market value** is neither of those. It is an *opinion* of the most probable price a property should
bring in a competitive and open market, under conditions that make a sale fair, with buyer and seller
each acting prudently and knowledgeably, each typically motivated, neither under duress, both well
informed, payment made in cash or its equivalent, and the price unaffected by special financing or
concessions. Every clause in that formulation is doing work. "Most probable," not highest possible or
lowest acceptable. "Competitive and open market" — the property was exposed for a reasonable time.
"Typically motivated" and "neither under duress" — this excludes the forced sale and the desperate
buyer. "Unaffected by special financing" — a seller who buys down the buyer's interest rate by $14,000
has effectively discounted the price, and the recorded number overstates value by roughly that amount.
A common error is to treat the most recent sale price of a nearly identical house as market value
without asking whether that sale satisfied these conditions. Often it did not.

The relationship among the three is worth memorizing as a sentence: *cost is what you spend, price is
what you get, value is what it is worth.* They can coincide. In a healthy new-construction subdivision
in Katy or Prosper, cost plus a builder's profit and price and value may sit within a few thousand
dollars of one another. In an older neighborhood they diverge routinely, and the divergence is where
appraisal lives.

**Highest and best use.** The **highest and best use** of a property is the reasonably probable use
that is *legally permissible*, *physically possible*, *financially feasible*, and *maximally
productive* — and it must satisfy all four tests, applied in that order, because a use that zoning
forbids is not rescued by being profitable. Appraisers analyze highest and best use twice: for the site
*as though vacant*, and for the *property as improved*. The two answers can differ, and when they
differ dramatically the improvement is a candidate for demolition. A 1,150-square-foot 1948 bungalow on
a corner lot two blocks from a light rail station in a Dallas corridor rezoned for mid-rise mixed use
may have a highest and best use, as though vacant, of a four-story apartment building; the value of the
property is then essentially land value less demolition cost, and the appraiser's opinion will look
nothing like a comparison to other bungalows. This is also why an appraisal of a Hill Country tract
with a modest house on 40 acres may turn less on the house than on whether the tract's highest and best
use is continued agricultural use or subdivision into five-acre homesites.

**The four characteristics of value.** For a thing to have value in the economic sense, four conditions
must be present at once. The classic mnemonic is **DUST**.

- **Demand** — someone must want it, and want it with purchasing power behind the wanting. Desire
  without the ability to pay does not create market demand.
- **Utility** — it must be capable of satisfying some want or need. A parcel with no legal access, no
  possibility of septic approval and no available water has limited utility no matter how attractive
  the acreage.
- **Scarcity** — it must be in limited supply relative to demand. Air has enormous utility and universal
  demand and no market price, because it is not scarce.
- **Transferability** — ownership or the right of use must be capable of being conveyed. A property with
  a clouded title, an unreleased lien, or a boundary dispute that no title company will insure around
  is impaired in value precisely to the extent that transferability is impaired.

Remove any one of the four and market value collapses. This is a useful diagnostic tool: when a listing
will not sell at any price you consider reasonable, ask which of the four is missing.

**Depreciation.** In appraisal, **depreciation** means loss in value from any cause — not the
accounting concept and not the tax concept. It comes in three forms, and the distinction matters
because two of them can be cured and one cannot.

**Physical deterioration** is wear, tear, and the action of the elements on the improvement itself: a
twenty-year-old composition roof, a failing HVAC compressor, rotted fascia, a cracked slab. It is
*curable* when the cost to fix is equal to or less than the value the fix adds, and *incurable* when it
is not. Replacing a $9,000 roof that adds $12,000 to marketability is curable deterioration; the
gradual aging of the structural frame is not.

**Functional obsolescence** is a loss in value caused by something within the property's own design or
layout that the market no longer wants: a four-bedroom house with one bathroom; a bedroom that can only
be reached by walking through another bedroom; a 1970s floor plan with a kitchen walled off from every
living space; an oversized improvement, like a 5,600-square-foot house in a subdivision of
2,000-square-foot houses. Functional obsolescence can be curable (open the kitchen wall) or incurable
(the house is simply too big for its street).

**External obsolescence** is a loss in value caused by something *outside* the property boundary. It is
always incurable by the owner, because the owner does not control the cause. A house whose back fence
now abuts a widened six-lane arterial; a neighborhood downwind of a new rendering plant; a subdivision
whose largest employer closed. The test students should hold onto: physical and functional causes are
inside the fence line, external causes are outside it, and only the owner's own causes are potentially
curable.

**Gross living area.** **Gross living area**, universally abbreviated **GLA**, is the total finished,
above-grade, heated and cooled living space of a dwelling, measured to the exterior walls. Each element
of that definition excludes something. *Finished* excludes an unfinished attic or an unfinished bonus
room. *Above-grade* excludes a basement even if fully finished — rare in most of Texas, but it appears
in parts of the Hill Country and in hillside construction. *Heated and cooled* excludes an unconditioned
sunroom, a screened porch, and an unconditioned garage conversion. *Measured to the exterior walls*
means GLA is larger than the sum of interior room dimensions, because the walls themselves are counted.
A common and expensive error in Texas listings is to add a garage conversion or an enclosed patio to
the advertised square footage. If the space is not conditioned and permitted as living area, an
appraiser will not count it in GLA, the appraised value will not reflect it, and the license holder who
published the inflated figure has a problem.

**Comparable.** A **comparable** — a "comp" — is a property similar enough to the subject in location,
physical characteristics, use and conditions of sale that its transaction supplies reliable evidence of
the subject's value. Three qualifiers do the real work. *Similar enough* is a matter of degree, and the
degree is measured by how much adjustment the comparison requires: a sale needing $40,000 of gross
adjustment on a $380,000 house is telling you it is not very comparable. *Location* in appraisal means
the competing market area, which in Texas practice frequently means the same subdivision and even the
same school attendance zone, since school boundaries can split a market in half across a single street.
*Conditions of sale* means an arm's-length transaction: a foreclosure sale, a sale between family
members, or a relocation-company sale is evidence of something, but not of market value, unless the
market in question is itself a distressed market.

**Adjustment.** An **adjustment** is a dollar amount added to or subtracted from a comparable's sale
price to account for a difference between the comparable and the subject. The single rule that governs
the direction of every adjustment, and the one students most often invert: **adjust the comparable, not
the subject.** If the comparable is *superior* to the subject in some respect, subtract from the
comparable's price. If the comparable is *inferior*, add. You are answering the question "what would
this comparable have sold for if it had been just like the subject?" A comparable with a pool when the
subject has none sold for more than it would have without the pool, so the pool adjustment is negative.
The size of an adjustment is supposed to be derived from market evidence — paired sales, depreciated
cost, rent differentials — not from a rule of thumb, and an appraiser who cannot support an adjustment
should not make it.

## Unit: Appraising & The Appraisal Process

An appraisal is not a lookup. It is a structured problem-solving procedure, performed in a fixed order,
in which each step constrains the next. Skipping a step or performing steps out of order is how bad
appraisals get made. This unit walks the process in order, says what the appraiser is actually doing at
each step, and — because you are studying to be a license holder rather than an appraiser — says at each
step what you should and should not do to assist.

**Step one: define the problem.** Before any data is gathered, the appraiser must establish what
question is being answered. That means identifying the client and the intended users of the report; the
intended use of the appraisal; the type of value being developed; the effective date of the opinion;
the property to be appraised; and the property rights being appraised.

Each of those is capable of changing the answer. *Intended use* changes it because an appraisal
developed for a mortgage lender, an appraisal developed for an estate's date-of-death valuation, and an
appraisal developed for a condemnation proceeding are different assignments even on the same house.
*Type of value* changes it because market value, insurable value, investment value and assessed value
are different quantities; the county appraisal district's assessed value for property tax purposes is
not an opinion of market value produced for a lender, and citing it to a seller as if it were is a
mistake. *Effective date* changes it because value is a snapshot; an appraisal with a retrospective
effective date of March 2022 must ignore everything the appraiser learned afterward. *Property rights
appraised* changes it because the fee simple estate and the leased fee estate are not the same thing: a
small office building leased for eight more years at rents thirty percent below market is worth less as
a leased fee than the same building would be worth vacant and available, and an appraiser who values
the fee simple when the client needs the leased fee has answered the wrong question.

*What you should do:* when you order or coordinate an appraisal, be precise about the property and the
rights. Give the appraiser the correct legal description, the correct GLA basis if you know it, and any
lease, easement, or right-of-way document that affects the estate. *What you must not do:* never
suggest a target value, a "number we need," or a range. You may transmit the contract, and you should —
the contract price is a legitimate item of assignment information — but the request "we need $415,000 to
make this work" is an attempt to influence an opinion of value, and it is exactly the conduct the
independence provisions of appraisal regulation exist to prevent.

**Step two: determine the scope of work.** Scope of work is the extent of the research and analysis the
assignment requires: which approaches to value will be developed, how the property will be inspected
(interior and exterior, exterior only, or not at all), how far the data search will extend in geography
and in time, and what will be verified with whom. Scope is set by the appraiser, not by the client, and
it must be sufficient to produce credible results for the intended use. A client may ask for a cheaper,
faster product; the client may not compel a scope so thin that the result is not credible.

This step is where the appraiser also identifies **extraordinary assumptions** — things assumed true
that, if false, would change the opinion (for example, assuming a permitted addition was in fact
permitted) — and **hypothetical conditions** — things known to be contrary to fact but assumed for the
assignment (valuing a house "as if repaired" when it currently has fire damage). Both must be
disclosed, because both change what the number means.

*Your role:* provide access. An appraiser who cannot get into the house cannot perform an interior
inspection, and a report built on an exterior-only scope in a market that expects interior inspections
may be rejected by the lender. Coordinate keys, alarm codes, and tenant notice promptly. Do not
accompany the appraiser in order to advocate; you may be present to provide access and answer factual
questions.

**Step three: collect and analyze the data.** Appraisers divide data into two classes, and the division
is more than bookkeeping.

**General data** is information about forces outside the property that affect all properties in the
market: regional and local economic conditions, employment and wage trends, population and household
formation, interest rates, construction costs, the supply pipeline, and governmental influences —
zoning, land use regulation, tax rates, and public services. In Texas this analysis is genuinely local.
An appraiser working the Permian Basin is analyzing an economy whose housing demand tracks the rig
count; an appraiser in Austin is analyzing an economy whose demand tracks technology employment and a
constrained supply of developable land; an appraiser in the Rio Grande Valley is analyzing a different
market again. General data explains *why* the market is where it is.

**Specific data** is information about the subject property and about the comparables: the site (size,
shape, frontage, topography, drainage, access, utilities, easements, restrictions); the improvements
(GLA, room count, age, condition, quality of construction, systems, garage, outbuildings, pool); and
the transaction facts for each comparable (sale price, date, financing terms, concessions, conditions
of sale, days on market, and whether the sale was arm's length).

Data collection includes **verification**. A sale is not usable until the appraiser has confirmed the
essential facts with a party to it or an equally reliable source — the seller, the buyer, a broker, or
a closing document. Verification is where seller-paid concessions, undisclosed personal property
included in the price, and non-arm's-length relationships come to light, and each of those can move an
adjusted sale price by five figures. As a matter of practice and not of any statute cited in this
course, appraisers and license holders working in Texas rely heavily on multiple listing service data
and on sales confirmed directly with the parties, because a Texas deed does not ordinarily state what
the buyer paid; that data reality is why verification takes more effort here than in states where sale
prices are recorded.

*Your role:* be a source of accurate specific data and nothing more. Provide the MLS sheet, the survey,
the seller's disclosure, the list of improvements with dates and costs, and the concession detail from
the closing statement. Provide comparables if you like — appraisers routinely accept them — but provide
them because they are genuinely comparable, not because they are high. Supplying a cherry-picked packet
of the four highest sales in the subdivision while omitting the three closest is advocacy dressed as
assistance.

**Step four: apply the approaches to value.** The appraiser develops the approaches that are applicable
and necessary for credible results: the **sales comparison approach**, the **cost approach**, and the
**income approach**. Not every assignment requires all three; the scope-of-work decision governs, and an
approach that is not applicable is omitted with an explanation rather than performed badly. The next
two units of this module take these approaches apart in detail; what matters here is their place in the
sequence. They come *after* problem definition, scope, and data — never before. An appraiser who begins
by pulling three sales and averaging them has skipped the first three steps and does not yet know what
question is being answered.

**Step five: reconcile.** Each approach developed produces an *indication* of value, and the
indications will not agree. Reconciliation is the analytical step in which the appraiser weighs those
indications and arrives at a single opinion. Two rules govern it.

First, **reconciliation is not averaging.** The appraiser does not add three indications and divide by
three. The appraiser asks, for each indication, how reliable the underlying data was, how much
adjustment was required, and how well the approach matches the way buyers in this market actually
behave. An indication resting on four recent sales in the subject's own subdivision requiring small
adjustments deserves more weight than one resting on two sales a mile away requiring twenty percent
gross adjustment. If sales comparison indicates $389,000, cost indicates $383,400, and income indicates
$385,050 for a suburban house, the appraiser does not report $385,817; the appraiser weights sales
comparison heavily, treats the other two as support, and reports a rounded conclusion.

Second, reconciliation happens *within* each approach as well as *among* them. Within the sales
comparison approach, the appraiser reconciles the adjusted sale prices of the individual comparables
into one indication before ever comparing that indication to the cost or income figures.

**Step six: report the opinion.** The final step is communicating the result. The report must state the
opinion and the effective date, identify the client and intended users and the intended use, describe
the scope of work performed, disclose any extraordinary assumptions and hypothetical conditions, and
present sufficient information for the intended users to understand the analysis. Section 1103.405(a)
requires a Texas credentialed appraiser to comply with the current edition of the Uniform Standards of
Professional Appraisal Practice or with Board-rule standards at least as stringent, and § 1103.154
authorizes the Board to adopt rules incorporating USPAP. The report is signed subject to
§ 1103.402(a), which forbids signing or endorsing an appraisal "unless the appraisal is substantially
produced by that person" — meaning, under subsection (b), that the signer contributed "in a material
and verifiable manner to the research or analysis that results in the final opinion of value." Where a
trainee did the work, § 1103.354(b) requires the supervisory appraiser to sign and makes that appraiser
"responsible to the public and to the board for the appraiser trainee's reports and conduct." And under
§ 1103.404, the appraiser must retain the business records relating to the appraisal "until at least the
fifth anniversary of the date of the appraisal."

*Your role after the report:* if the appraisal comes in below the contract price, you are not without
options, but the options are factual. You may submit a **reconsideration of value** through the proper
channel identifying specific factual errors — a comparable that was not arm's length, a GLA measurement
that omitted a permitted addition, a superior sale in the same subdivision that closed after the
appraiser's data pull — and you support each item with documents. You may not argue that the number
should be higher because the deal needs it. The distinction is not cosmetic. Occupations Code
§ 1101.652(b)(2) authorizes TREC to discipline a license holder who, while engaged in real estate
brokerage, "engages in conduct that is dishonest or in bad faith or that demonstrates
untrustworthiness," and pressure on a valuation professional is squarely within reach of that language.

## Unit: Value

Value is not a property of an object. It is a relationship between an object and a market, and markets
behave according to economic principles that appraisers have named. What follows are economic
principles, not legal rules; no Texas statute enacts them and none is cited here. They are nonetheless
the analytical vocabulary in which every appraisal report reasons, and each one shows up in Texas in a
form you can see.

**Supply and demand.** Value rises when demand increases against a fixed or slow-growing supply, and
falls when supply outruns demand. Real estate supply is unusually slow to respond because land is fixed
in location and construction takes time, which is why housing markets overshoot in both directions.
When a large employer announced and then built a plant near Taylor in Williamson County, demand for
housing within commuting distance moved faster than builders could deliver lots, and prices in the
small towns around it rose sharply before supply caught up. The mirror image is a Gulf Coast refinery
town where a shutdown removes several hundred households from the demand side within a year; the houses
do not disappear, and prices fall.

**Substitution.** A prudent buyer will pay no more for a property than the cost of acquiring an equally
desirable substitute. This is the master principle — all three approaches to value are applications of
it. Substitution is why a buyer choosing among four nearly identical 2018 houses on the same street in
Pflugerville will not pay $30,000 more for one of them, and why the seller who prices at that premium
sells the other three. Substitution also sets the ceiling in the cost approach: a buyer will not pay
more for an existing house than the cost of buying a comparable lot and building an equivalent house,
less an allowance for the time and risk of doing so.

**Conformity.** Value is maximized where properties in an area are reasonably similar in style, size,
quality and use. Conformity is why deed-restricted subdivisions exist and why lenders and buyers treat
them as lower-risk. A 6,200-square-foot house on a street of 1,900-square-foot houses in an older
Amarillo neighborhood will not command its cost, because the buyers who want 6,200 square feet are
shopping in neighborhoods where that is normal.

**Contribution.** The value of any component of a property equals what it adds to the value of the
whole — not what it cost. This is the principle every remodeling conversation runs into. A $28,000
kitchen renovation in a Houston bungalow may contribute $22,000, or $34,000, or $9,000, depending
entirely on what the market expects at that price point. A swimming pool that costs $65,000 to install
in Lubbock may contribute $20,000 to value, while the same pool in a Rio Grande Valley neighborhood
where pools are common may contribute more. Contribution also explains the diminishing return: the
first full bathroom added to a three-bedroom, one-bath house contributes enormously; the fourth
contributes little.

**Anticipation.** Value reflects the expectation of future benefits, not past expenditures. Buyers are
purchasing what the property will provide going forward: shelter, income, appreciation, use. This is
why land along a planned toll road extension in Collin County rises in value on the announcement rather
than on the ribbon-cutting, and why a commercial building's value depends on the rent it will collect
over the years ahead rather than the rent it collected last decade.

**Change.** No physical or economic condition is permanent. Neighborhoods, markets and property types
move through phases — growth, stability, decline, and revitalization — and an appraisal is a snapshot
with an effective date precisely because change is constant. The East Austin neighborhoods that were in
long decline in the 1980s, stable in the 1990s and in rapid revitalization after 2005 are the same
physical houses; the value pattern belongs to the phase, not the structures.

**Progression and regression.** These are the two directions of one idea. Under **progression**, a
lesser-valued property is worth more because it is surrounded by higher-valued properties: the modest
1,400-square-foot house that survives on a Highland Park–adjacent block benefits from its neighbors.
Under **regression**, a higher-valued property is worth less because it is surrounded by lower-valued
ones: the over-improved house from the conformity example above. The practical lesson for a license
holder is that the worst house on a good street is usually a better purchase than the best house on a
weak one.

**Plottage.** **Plottage** is the increment of value created when two or more adjoining parcels are
combined under one ownership so that the combined parcel has a higher unit value than the parcels had
separately — the act of combining being called **assemblage**. Three contiguous quarter-acre lots on a
Fort Worth commercial corridor might be worth $110,000 each individually, but $420,000 combined, because
only the assembled 0.75-acre tract is deep enough to support a building with the parking a national
tenant requires. The $90,000 excess is plottage value. Note that plottage is not automatic; combining
two parcels that were already large enough for their highest and best use creates nothing.

**Externalities.** Factors outside the property boundary influence its value, positively or negatively,
and the owner cannot control them. A new elementary school with strong ratings, a completed hike-and-bike
trail connection, or the burial of overhead utility lines are positive externalities. A rezoning that
puts a truck terminal at the end of the street, the expansion of an airport flight path, or the arrival
of a wastewater treatment expansion upwind are negative ones. Externalities are the economic engine
behind external obsolescence from the previous unit: the obsolescence is the measured loss, the
externality is the cause.

Three of these principles interact constantly and are worth watching together. Substitution sets the
buyer's ceiling; contribution determines whether an improvement earns its keep; conformity and its
progression/regression corollaries determine whether the neighborhood rewards or punishes the
improvement. When a seller asks you whether to spend $40,000 finishing out the bonus room before
listing, you are being asked a contribution question, and the answer depends on conformity data from
the seller's own market, not on the cost of the work.

## Unit: Three Approaches to Value

Three roads lead to an opinion of value, and each is an application of substitution. The sales
comparison approach asks what buyers have recently paid for substitutes. The cost approach asks what it
would cost to create a substitute. The income approach asks what an investor would pay for a substitute
income stream. Each is developed independently, and reconciliation, not averaging, brings them
together.

**The sales comparison approach.** The appraiser selects closed sales of comparable properties, verifies
each, and adjusts each comparable's sale price for its differences from the subject. Adjust the
comparable, not the subject: superior comparable, subtract; inferior comparable, add. The adjusted sale
prices are then reconciled to one indication.

Consider an illustrative subject in Round Rock: a 1998 single-family house, 1,900 square feet of GLA,
three bedrooms, two baths, two-car garage, no pool, standard interior lot. The adjustment amounts below
are illustrative figures used to show the arithmetic; in a real assignment each would have to be derived
from market evidence.

| | Subject | Comp 1 | Comp 2 | Comp 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sale price | — | $385,000 | $402,000 | $371,000 |
| Months since sale | — | 3 | 1 | 5 |
| GLA (sq ft) | 1,900 | 1,850 | 2,050 | 1,800 |
| Bedrooms | 3 | 3 | 4 | 3 |
| Garage bays | 2 | 2 | 2 | 1 |
| Pool | No | No | Yes | No |

Adjustment rates used, all illustrative: GLA at $70 per square foot; additional bedroom, $6,000; garage
bay, $5,000; pool, $18,000; market conditions, 0.4 percent of sale price per month in a rising market.

*Comparable 1.* GLA: the subject has 50 more square feet, so the comparable is inferior — add
50 × $70 = **+$3,500**. Bedrooms, garage and pool are identical — **$0** each. Market conditions: 3
months × 0.4% = 1.2%; 0.012 × $385,000 = **+$4,620**. Net adjustment **+$8,120**; adjusted sale price
$385,000 + $8,120 = **$393,120**. Gross adjustment $8,120, or 2.1 percent of the sale price.

*Comparable 2.* GLA: the comparable has 150 more square feet, so it is superior — subtract
150 × $70 = **−$10,500**. Bedrooms: the comparable has one more — **−$6,000**. Garage: equal — **$0**.
Pool: the comparable has one and the subject does not — **−$18,000**. Market conditions: 1 month ×
0.4% = 0.4%; 0.004 × $402,000 = **+$1,608**. Net adjustment −$10,500 − $6,000 − $18,000 + $1,608 =
**−$32,892**; adjusted sale price $402,000 − $32,892 = **$369,108**. Gross adjustment $10,500 + $6,000 +
$18,000 + $1,608 = $36,108, or 9.0 percent.

*Comparable 3.* GLA: the subject has 100 more square feet — add 100 × $70 = **+$7,000**. Garage: the
comparable has one bay to the subject's two — **+$5,000**. Bedrooms and pool equal — **$0**. Market
conditions: 5 months × 0.4% = 2.0%; 0.02 × $371,000 = **+$7,420**. Net adjustment **+$19,420**; adjusted
sale price $371,000 + $19,420 = **$390,420**. Gross adjustment $19,420, or 5.2 percent.

The adjusted range runs from $369,108 to $393,120. Notice that the subject is **bracketed**: the
unadjusted sales run from $371,000 to $402,000, so the conclusion sits inside a range the market
actually established rather than being extrapolated beyond it. Notice also which comparable deserves
the most weight. Comparable 1 required 2.1 percent gross adjustment; Comparable 3, 5.2 percent;
Comparable 2, 9.0 percent — the pool and the extra bedroom made it the least similar property. Weighting
Comparable 1 at 50 percent, Comparable 3 at 35 percent and Comparable 2 at 15 percent:

(0.50 × $393,120) + (0.35 × $390,420) + (0.15 × $369,108) = $196,560 + $136,647 + $55,366 = $388,573,
rounded to an indicated value of **$389,000**.

The sales comparison approach dominates in residential appraisal, because residential buyers actually
shop by substitution among near-identical alternatives and because the data — verified closed sales of
similar houses — is plentiful. It weakens exactly where those conditions fail: unique properties, thin
markets, rural tracts where the nearest true comparable is fifteen miles away, and rapidly moving
markets where sales three months old describe a different world.

**The cost approach.** The logic is that a buyer will not pay more for an existing property than the
cost to acquire the land and build an equivalent improvement, less any loss in value the existing
improvement has suffered. The formula:

> Reproduction or replacement cost new of the improvements − accrued depreciation + land value = indicated value.

Two cost concepts must be distinguished. **Reproduction cost** is the cost to construct an exact
duplicate, using the same materials, design and workmanship, including any obsolete features.
**Replacement cost** is the cost to construct an improvement of equivalent utility using current
materials, design and standards. Reproduction cost is the right measure for a historically significant
1910 Victorian in Galveston where the ornamental millwork is part of what the market is buying;
replacement cost is the right measure for ordinary properties, and is the one used far more often,
because no buyer wants a reproduced 1978 electrical panel.

Working the same Round Rock subject, with illustrative figures: replacement cost new of the dwelling at
$165 per square foot × 1,900 square feet = $313,500, plus site improvements (driveway, fencing,
landscaping) of $22,000, for total replacement cost new of **$335,500**.

Accrued depreciation, all three forms:

- *Physical deterioration*, by the age-life method: effective age 12 years against a total economic life
  of 60 years = 12 ÷ 60 = 20 percent. 0.20 × $335,500 = **$67,100**.
- *Functional obsolescence*: the original closed-off galley kitchen, which paired sales in the
  subdivision indicate the market discounts by **$8,000**.
- *External obsolescence*: the property backs to a six-lane arterial, which paired sales indicate costs
  **$12,000**.

Total accrued depreciation $67,100 + $8,000 + $12,000 = **$87,100**. Depreciated cost of the
improvements: $335,500 − $87,100 = **$248,400**. Land value, developed by sales comparison of vacant
lots in the area: **$135,000**. Indicated value: $248,400 + $135,000 = **$383,400**.

The cost approach dominates for new construction, where there is essentially no depreciation to
estimate and cost data is current and reliable, and for special-purpose properties — a church, a school,
a fire station, a grain elevator — where sales of substitutes barely exist and there is no income stream
to capitalize. It weakens as buildings age, because estimating accrued depreciation on a
fifty-year-old improvement involves judgment that can swing the answer by six figures, and it is not
persuasive in a market where houses routinely sell for materially more or less than depreciated cost.

**The income approach.** Here the property is valued as an income-producing asset. Two techniques
matter at this level.

The **gross rent multiplier** is the simpler one, used for small residential rental property. The
multiplier is derived from sales of comparable rental properties: GRM = sale price ÷ monthly gross rent.
Suppose three rental comparables in the subject's own market:

| Rental comparable | Sale price | Monthly rent | GRM |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | $360,000 | $2,400 | 150.0 |
| B | $378,000 | $2,475 | 152.7 |
| C | $345,000 | $2,300 | 150.0 |

The indicated range is 150.0 to 152.7; the appraiser selects **151**. If the subject's market rent is
$2,550 per month, the indication is $2,550 × 151 = **$385,050**. The GRM is quick and crude: it uses
gross rent, so it ignores differences in vacancy, taxes, insurance and operating expense between the
subject and the comparables. It is evidence, not a substitute for analysis.

**Direct capitalization** is the serious technique, and it runs on the IRV relationship:

> **V = I ÷ R**, and therefore **R = I ÷ V** and **I = R × V**,

where **V** is value, **I** is net operating income, and **R** is the capitalization rate. The income
used is *net operating income* — income after vacancy and operating expenses, but before debt service
and before income taxes. Mortgage payments are a fact about the buyer's financing, not about the
property, and they never appear in NOI.

Take an illustrative six-unit retail strip center in Waco:

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Potential gross income (6 units × $2,100/month × 12) | $151,200 |
| Less vacancy and collection loss at 7% | −$10,584 |
| **Effective gross income** | **$140,616** |
| Property taxes | −$24,000 |
| Insurance | −$8,400 |
| Management at 5% of EGI | −$7,031 |
| Maintenance and reserves | −$12,500 |
| Utilities and common area | −$6,200 |
| **Total operating expenses** | **−$58,131** |
| **Net operating income** | **$82,485** |

Comparable sales of similar centers indicate an overall capitalization rate of 8.25 percent. Applying
IRV: V = $82,485 ÷ 0.0825 = **$999,818**, an indicated value of approximately **$1,000,000**.

The other two forms of the formula are just as useful. If an investor pays $1,100,000 for this same
income stream, the rate that transaction establishes is R = $82,485 ÷ $1,100,000 = 0.0750, or **7.5
percent** — a lower rate, meaning the buyer paid more per dollar of income, which is what happens when
buyers perceive less risk or expect more growth. And if a market rate of 8.25 percent is applied to a
target value of $1,200,000, the income required is I = 0.0825 × $1,200,000 = **$99,000**. Hold onto the
inverse relationship: for a fixed income, value moves *opposite* to the capitalization rate. A rate
rising from 8.25 percent to 9.5 percent on the same $82,485 of NOI produces $82,485 ÷ 0.095 = $868,263 —
a loss of roughly $132,000 in value with no change whatsoever to the building or its tenants.

The income approach dominates wherever the property is bought for its income: apartments, office,
retail, industrial, self-storage. It is the approach commercial investors themselves use, which is the
real argument for it — an appraisal is most credible when it models the behavior of the actual buyers.
It is weak or inapplicable for owner-occupied single-family homes, because the buyers of those
properties are not pricing an income stream.

### Applied exercise: Capitalization and the effect of rate movement

Using the Waco strip center above:

1. One tenant vacates permanently and the owner cannot re-lease the space. Recompute potential gross
   income for five occupied units at the same rent, keep vacancy at 7 percent, keep every expense the
   same except management (still 5 percent of the new effective gross income), and find the new NOI.
2. Apply the same 8.25 percent capitalization rate to your new NOI. How much value did the loss of one
   tenant destroy?
3. A buyer offers $940,000 for the center at its original $82,485 NOI. What capitalization rate does
   that offer imply, and is the buyer treating the property as more or less risky than the market rate
   of 8.25 percent suggests?
4. Explain in one or two sentences why the owner's $6,300 monthly mortgage payment appears nowhere in
   any of these calculations.

## Unit: Pricing a Property

Everything to this point has been about the appraiser. This unit is about you, doing the closely related
thing that you are permitted to do, and about the line between the two — because the line is statutory,
and stepping over it is a criminal offense under § 1103.5545.

**The statutory boundary.** Occupations Code § 1101.002(1) defines a broker by listing the acts that
constitute brokerage. One of them, at § 1101.002(1)(A)(xi), is that the person

> provides a written analysis, opinion, or conclusion relating to the estimated price of real property
> if the analysis, opinion, or conclusion: (a) is not referred to as an appraisal; (b) is provided in
> the ordinary course of the person's business; and (c) is related to the actual or potential
> management, acquisition, disposition, or encumbrance of an interest in real property.

Read that as the grant of authority it is. Producing a written price opinion is not merely tolerated for
a Texas license holder; it is one of the enumerated acts that *is* brokerage. Chapter 1103 confirms the
boundary from the other side. Section 1103.004(a) provides that the appraiser chapter "does not apply
to," among others, "(2) a real estate broker licensed under Chapter 1101 or a sales agent acting under
the authority of a sponsoring broker who provides to another person a written analysis, opinion, or
conclusion relating to the estimated price of real property if the analysis, opinion, or conclusion:
(A) is not referred to as an appraisal; (B) is given in the ordinary course of the broker's business;
and (C) is related to the actual or potential acquisition, disposition, encumbrance, or management of an
interest in real property."

Both provisions turn on the same three conditions, and all three must be satisfied.

*It must not be referred to as an appraisal.* This is a rule about words, and it is absolute. Do not
call your work an appraisal. Do not call yourself an appraiser. Do not title the document "Appraisal,"
"Residential Appraisal," or "Appraised Value." Do not write "I appraised the property at $389,000."
Section 1103.201(b) separately forbids using the titles "certified real estate appraiser" or "licensed
real estate appraiser," or calling your product a "certified appraisal" or "licensed appraisal," without
the credential; § 1103.401 extends that to any "title, designation, initials, or other insignia or
identification that would mislead the public" about your credentials or competency. Section 1103.554
makes a knowing violation of § 1103.401 a Class B misdemeanor. Call your work what it is: a
**comparative market analysis** (CMA) or a **broker price opinion** (BPO).

*It must be given in the ordinary course of the brokerage business.* The exemption covers price
opinions that arise out of your brokerage practice — pricing a listing, advising a buyer on an offer,
advising an owner considering a sale or a refinance. It is not a general license to enter the valuation
business. A license holder who builds a separate line of business producing valuations for lenders and
litigants, unconnected to any brokerage relationship, is not obviously operating "in the ordinary course
of the broker's business," and the further that work drifts from brokerage the closer it comes to
§ 1103.201(a) and its penalties.

*It must relate to an actual or potential acquisition, disposition, encumbrance, or management of an
interest in real property.* There must be a real-world transactional purpose.

One more thing that is *not* in the exemption: an appraisal for a lender in a federally related
transaction. Section 1103.003(6-a) defines a **federally related transaction** as a real estate-related
transaction that "requires the services of an appraiser" and "is engaged in, contracted for, or
regulated by a federal financial institution regulatory agency." Your CMA does not become an appraisal
merely because a lender looks at it, but you cannot supply the appraisal that such a transaction
requires. Note also § 1103.004(a)(6): the chapter does not apply to "the procurement or use of an
automated valuation model," and § 1103.003(4-b) defines an **automated valuation model** as an automated
system deriving value from public property records and analytic methodologies. That is why the
consumer-facing online estimates your sellers arrive with are lawful, and also why they are not
appraisals and not CMAs — they have never been inside the house.

**Building the CMA.** A defensible CMA borrows the appraiser's method, at a scope appropriate to the
assignment.

*Start with the subject.* Measure or verify GLA on the correct basis — finished, above-grade,
conditioned, measured to exterior walls. Confirm the room count, garage, lot size, and improvements with
dates. Walk the property. A CMA built from tax roll data on a property you have never entered is a
guess.

*Select comparables.* Prefer closed sales in the subject's own subdivision, within the last three to six
months, similar in GLA, age, quality, condition and lot, and in the same school attendance zone. Three
to five closed sales is a normal working set. Exclude non-arm's-length transactions unless the market
itself is distressed. Add context: current *active* listings tell you the competition the seller will
face, and *pending* sales tell you what the market is accepting right now — but neither is evidence of
value, because an active listing proves only what one seller hopes for.

*Adjust.* Same rule as before: adjust the comparable, not the subject; superior comparable, subtract;
inferior comparable, add. Support your adjustment amounts with market evidence where you can, and be
honest with the seller where you cannot.

*Bracket the subject.* This is the single most important quality check available to you. Your set of
comparables should include at least one property superior to the subject and at least one inferior, so
that your conclusion falls *inside* a range the market has already demonstrated. Applying the Round Rock
grid from the previous unit: the unadjusted sales ran $371,000 to $402,000 and the conclusion landed at
$389,000 — bracketed on both sides. A CMA whose comparables are all inferior to the subject, so that
every adjustment is positive and the conclusion sits above every sale in the set, is extrapolation. It
is also the classic structure of an overpriced listing.

*Reconcile and state a range.* You are not required to produce a single number, and in most cases you
should not. A recommended range with a suggested list price inside it — say, a supported range of
$383,000 to $393,000 with a recommended list price of $389,900 — communicates the real precision of the
work. Then add the market context the seller needs: **months of supply** (divide the number of active
listings by the average monthly closed sales — 63 actives against 21 closings per month is 3.0 months of
supply, a seller's market by most conventions), the median **days on market** for comparable sold
listings, and the **list-to-sale price ratio** in the neighborhood.

*Disclose what it is.* State on the document that it is a comparative market analysis prepared by a
license holder for the purpose of assisting the owner in pricing the property, that it is not an
appraisal, and that it was not prepared under the Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice.
The statute requires a comparable disclaimer of appraisers themselves in the analogous situation:
§ 1103.405(c) requires an evaluation described by § 1103.004(4) or (5) to carry on its first page the
notice "This is not an appraisal performed in accordance with the Uniform Standards of Professional
Appraisal Practice." That specific notice provision governs those evaluations rather than your CMA, but
the plain-language principle it reflects — say what the document is not — is the right practice for your
CMA, and stating it is practice rather than a statutory requirement imposed on license holders.

**Overpricing and days on market.** Sellers overprice, and the pressure on you to help them do it is
constant. The mechanics of why it fails are worth being able to explain in a listing appointment.

A property draws its most concentrated buyer attention in its first two to three weeks on market, when
every buyer already searching the price band sees it as new inventory. Price it above that band and
those buyers never see it — a house listed at $415,000 is invisible to the buyer whose search caps at
$400,000, even though that buyer would happily have paid $392,000. The seller then discovers the problem
slowly, through showings that do not come, and reduces in stages. By the time the price reaches the
supported range the listing has accumulated days on market, and buyers who do see it read the
accumulated days and the reduction history as evidence of a problem. Properties that arrive at the right
price late routinely sell for less than they would have brought if priced correctly at the start.

Illustratively: a house supported at $389,000 is listed at $415,000. It sits fourteen weeks with two
reductions and closes at $379,500. The seller's carrying costs — an illustrative $2,750 per month of
principal, interest, taxes and insurance — ran roughly $9,600 across the extra three and a half months.
Between the lower closing price and the carrying costs, the attempt to capture $26,000 of extra price
cost the seller nearly $19,100. There is nothing unlawful about a seller choosing to list high; the
price is the seller's decision, not yours. What you owe the seller is the analysis that makes the
decision informed.

**Buying a listing.** The specific ethical failure that grows here has a name in the trade: **buying a
listing** — telling a seller the property is worth more than your own analysis supports, in order to win
the listing agreement over a competitor who told the truth, intending to work the price down later. It
is the pricing sin, and it is a discipline problem, not merely a bad look.

Occupations Code § 1101.652(b)(5) authorizes the Commission to suspend or revoke the license of a holder
who, while engaged in real estate brokerage, "makes a false promise that is likely to influence a person
to enter into an agreement when the license holder is unable or does not intend to keep the promise."
A price assurance made to win a listing agreement the license holder does not believe and does not
intend to honor is squarely within that description. Section 1101.652(b)(1) reaches a license holder who
"acts negligently or incompetently," and (b)(2) one who "engages in conduct that is dishonest or in bad
faith or that demonstrates untrustworthiness." Section 1101.652(b)(6) reaches "a continued and flagrant
course of misrepresentation." And § 1101.652(a)(6) is aimed directly at the sloppy end of the same
problem: the Commission may discipline a license holder who "fails to consider market conditions for the
specific geographic area in which the license holder is providing a service." That subdivision is a
statutory instruction to do the analysis — the specific geographic area, not the metro, not the county.

The professional answer is to bring the data and let it speak. Show the grid. Show the bracketing. Show
the months of supply and the list-to-sale ratio. Say plainly what the analysis supports and what it does
not. If the seller insists on a higher price, you may still take the listing at that price — that is the
seller's prerogative — but you should have documented your recommendation and the market evidence behind
it, and you should have agreed in advance on the review points at which you will revisit the price. As a
matter of practice rather than of any statutory requirement, keeping the CMA and your written pricing
recommendation in the transaction file is the license holder's best protection when a disappointed
seller later complains that the property was mispriced.

### Statute reading exercise: The price opinion boundary

Open Occupations Code § 1101.002(1)(A)(xi) and § 1103.004(a)(2), both quoted in this unit, and read them
side by side.

1. The two provisions state the same three conditions. Write them out and mark which is a rule about
   *what you call the document*, which is a rule about *the context in which you produce it*, and which
   is a rule about *its purpose*.
2. A license holder prepares a written valuation of a warehouse for a bank that is deciding whether to
   make a commercial loan, titles it "Broker Price Opinion," charges a flat fee, and has no listing or
   buyer relationship with anyone in the transaction. Which of the three conditions is most doubtful,
   and which section of Chapter 1103 would you look to next?
3. Section 1103.003(1) defines "appraisal" as an opinion of value or the process of developing one,
   "regardless of whether prepared for a federally related transaction." Given that definition, what
   exactly keeps a properly prepared CMA from being an appraisal under Chapter 1103?
4. Under § 1103.5545 and § 1103.5535, what are the two distinct exposures — one criminal, one civil —
   for a person who performs an appraisal without a credential and is paid $600 for it?

### Applied exercise: Pricing a listing in Round Rock

The owners of the Round Rock subject from the previous unit — 1,900 square feet, three bedrooms, two
baths, two-car garage, no pool, backing to a six-lane arterial — call you for a listing appointment. Your
grid produced an indicated value of $389,000 with a bracketed range of $369,108 to $393,120 in adjusted
sale prices. There are 63 active listings in the competing market area and closings have averaged 21 per
month over the trailing six months. The sellers open the meeting by saying that an online estimate shows
$427,000 and that another agent has told them $419,900.

1. Compute the months of supply and state what it tells the sellers about negotiating leverage.
2. Your grid's three comparables all sit below $402,000 unadjusted. If you wanted to support $419,900,
   what would you have to do to the comparable set — and explain, using the bracketing principle, why
   that would make the analysis unreliable rather than merely optimistic.
3. The online estimate is an automated valuation model. Using § 1103.003(4-b) and § 1103.004(a)(6),
   explain to the sellers in two sentences what that estimate is, why it is lawful, and what your CMA
   has that it lacks. Then identify the one physical fact about this property that an AVM is least
   likely to have captured.
4. The sellers say they want to "start at $419,900 and come down." Draft the two-sentence response you
   would give, and identify which subdivision of § 1101.652 you would be at risk under if instead you
   simply agreed that $419,900 was what the property was worth.

### Module recap

This module moved from the regulator to the number on your listing presentation, and the through-line is
the difference between an appraisal and a price opinion.

It began with Chapter 1103 of the Occupations Code, the Texas Appraiser Licensing and Certification Act,
whose stated purpose under § 1103.002 is to conform Texas law to the federal Title XI framework and to
enforce appraisal standards. That federal origin explains the chapter's most distinctive feature: it
creates the Texas Appraiser Licensing and Certification Board as an independent subdivision of TREC
(§ 1103.051), gives it authority to establish credential categories and issue credentials, and then
sends the substance — the hour counts, the categories, the passing score — to the Appraiser
Qualifications Board. Section 1103.202 states the three-legged qualification structure of education,
experience and examination without stating a single number, and the honest thing to say about the hours
is that Chapter 1103 does not contain them.

From regulation the module moved to vocabulary: cost is what you spend, price is what you get, value is
what it is worth; highest and best use must be legally permissible, physically possible, financially
feasible and maximally productive; value requires demand, utility, scarcity and transferability;
depreciation is physical, functional or external, and only the causes inside the fence line are within
the owner's power to cure. Then to method — the ordered process of defining the problem, setting scope,
collecting and verifying general and specific data, applying the approaches, reconciling without
averaging, and reporting — and to your part in it, which is to supply accurate data and access and never
a target number. Then to the economics that make markets move, and to the three approaches themselves,
each worked with arithmetic you should now be able to reproduce: a sales comparison grid adjusted and
bracketed to $389,000, a cost approach at $383,400, a gross rent multiplier at $385,050, and direct
capitalization solving V = I ÷ R to approximately $1,000,000.

Finally the module returned to you. Section 1101.002(1)(A)(xi) makes producing a written price opinion
one of the enumerated acts of brokerage, and § 1103.004(a)(2) carves that same work out of the appraiser
statute, on three conditions: it is not referred to as an appraisal, it is given in the ordinary course
of the brokerage business, and it relates to an actual or potential transaction in real property. You
should now be able to build a CMA the way an appraiser builds a grid, bracket your conclusion, state a
supported range, explain to a seller why overpricing costs money, and refuse to buy a listing — a refusal
that § 1101.652(a)(6) and (b)(1), (2), (5) and (6) turn from a matter of taste into a matter of your
license.
