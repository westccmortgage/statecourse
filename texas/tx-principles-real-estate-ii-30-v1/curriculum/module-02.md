# Texas Principles of Real Estate II — Module 02

## TREC Topic: Real Estate Financing Principles

**Prescribed time: 275 minutes.** This module carries the nine units the Commission's PRINS2-1 course approval form places under *Real Estate Financing Principles*, whose scope note is *"Finance & regulations"*.

## Unit: Sources of Real Estate Financing

Almost no one buys Texas real estate with cash. The overwhelming majority of residential transactions in this state close because a third party advances money against the property, and the license holder who does not understand where that money comes from cannot answer the two questions clients ask most often: *who decides whether I get this loan*, and *who am I going to be dealing with for the next thirty years*. Those are different questions with different answers, and the answers are different because the mortgage business has separated three functions that a student naturally assumes are one function.

Those three functions are **origination**, **funding**, and **servicing**. Origination is the work of taking an application, collecting documentation, ordering the appraisal and title work, underwriting the file against a set of credit standards, and preparing the loan for closing. Funding is the act of actually putting money on the table at closing — of being the party whose dollars are advanced and whose lien is created. Servicing is everything that happens afterward: collecting the monthly payment, allocating it between principal and interest, holding and disbursing escrowed taxes and insurance, sending statements, handling payoffs, and releasing the lien when the debt is satisfied. One institution may perform all three. Far more often, in current practice, three different institutions perform them, and the borrower's file is handed from one to the next.

Texas law itself recognizes that originators come in distinct legal flavors. Finance Code § 343.105(a) provides that "[a] lender, mortgage banker, or licensed mortgage broker shall provide to each applicant for a home loan a written notice at closing." That single sentence names the three categories of originator the Legislature expected to be present in a Texas home loan, and it names them separately because they are not the same thing.

**Depository institutions.**

A **depository institution** is one that takes deposits from the public and lends a portion of those deposits out. Commercial banks, savings institutions, and credit unions are depositories. The defining feature, for our purposes, is that a depository has its own balance sheet of customer deposits available as a funding source. It can originate a loan and fund it with money that is already sitting inside the institution, and if it chooses, it can keep that loan on its own books — hold it "in portfolio" — collect the payments itself, and never involve anyone else. When a depository holds a loan in portfolio it has originated, funded, and serviced the loan, and the borrower deals with one name from application through payoff.

**Credit unions** are depositories organized as member-owned cooperatives. A member's deposit is a share in the institution rather than a claim against a stockholder-owned company, and lending is made to the membership. That cooperative structure is a matter of how credit unions are chartered and organized rather than anything stated in the Texas or federal sources read for this course; treat the description as an account of how the institutions are built, not as a legal rule you can cite. What matters practically is that a credit union is a depository: it has deposits to lend, it can hold loans in portfolio, and it frequently does, which is why credit union borrowers often find that their loan never leaves the institution.

A depository's portfolio lending gives it a freedom the rest of the market does not have. Because the depository is going to keep the risk, it can write the loan to whatever terms it is willing to live with. It does not have to make the loan fit anyone else's rulebook. That flexibility is why unusual properties, unusual income, and unusual structures — a rural tract with a manufactured home, a self-employed borrower with volatile receipts, a small commercial building — are often financed by a local depository when the standardized market has no box that fits.

**Non-depository lenders: mortgage bankers.**

A **mortgage banker** is a lender that does not take deposits. It has no reservoir of customer money. Instead it funds loans out of its own capital plus a short-term revolving line of credit — commonly called a warehouse line in the trade — and then sells the closed loans, usually within weeks, to replenish the line and fund the next batch. The distinction that matters is this: *the mortgage banker is the lender*. The note is made payable to the mortgage banker, the deed of trust names the mortgage banker, and the mortgage banker's money funds the closing. It then transfers that note to a purchaser.

The consequence for the borrower is immediate and is the single most common source of client confusion in Texas closings. A borrower who has spent six weeks talking to one company's loan officer receives, a month after closing, a letter announcing that a company they have never heard of will be collecting the payment. Nothing has gone wrong. The mortgage banker did exactly what it always intended to do — it sold the loan — and the buyer of the loan, or a servicer the buyer hired, now performs the servicing function. A license holder who explains this at the beginning of the transaction rather than after it prevents a panicked phone call later.

**Non-depository intermediaries: mortgage brokers.**

A **mortgage broker** is not a lender at all. A broker does not fund. A broker takes the borrower's application, assembles the file, and places it with a lender that will fund it, earning compensation for that intermediary service. Regulation X captures the distinction cleanly in the rule governing the settlement-cost booklet: "If a borrower uses a mortgage broker, the mortgage broker shall distribute the special information booklet and the lender need not do so" (12 C.F.R. § 1024.6(a)(1)). The regulation treats broker and lender as two different persons in the same transaction and assigns the disclosure duty to whichever of them is standing in front of the applicant.

Regulation X draws the same line for the good faith estimate: "not later than 3 business days after a lender receives an application, or information sufficient to complete an application, the lender must provide the applicant with a GFE" (12 C.F.R. § 1024.7(a)(1)). And for the homeownership counseling list, the rule expressly allows either to deliver it while placing ultimate responsibility on one of them: "A mortgage broker or dealer may provide the list of homeownership counseling organizations required under this section to any loan applicant from whom it receives or for whom it prepares an application. If the mortgage broker or dealer has provided the required list, the lender is not required to provide an additional list. The lender is responsible for ensuring that the list of homeownership counseling organizations is provided to a loan applicant in accordance with this section" (12 C.F.R. § 1024.20(a)(3)). Read those three provisions together and the architecture is plain: the broker is the applicant's point of contact and may discharge the disclosure duties, but the lender remains the party accountable for the loan and, in the counseling-list rule, for the disclosure itself.

A common error is to assume that "mortgage broker" and "mortgage banker" are two names for the same business because both appear on yard signs and both send loan officers to broker opens. They are not. The banker funds and the broker does not, and Finance Code § 343.105(a) lists them separately for exactly that reason. A second common error is to assume a mortgage broker is a real estate broker who happens to do loans. It is not; it is a distinct occupation with its own licensing scheme, and Finance Code § 343.105(a) refers to a "licensed mortgage broker" as a category separate from lenders and mortgage bankers.

**The servicer.**

RESPA and Regulation X treat the **servicer** as its own actor with its own duties. Regulation X defines an escrow account as "any account that a servicer establishes or controls on behalf of a borrower to pay taxes, insurance premiums (including flood insurance), or other charges with respect to a federally related mortgage loan" (12 C.F.R. § 1024.17(b)), and the whole apparatus of escrow analysis, initial and annual escrow statements, cushions, shortages, and surpluses in § 1024.17 is addressed to the servicer, not to the originator. Texas law also reaches the servicer directly. Finance Code § 343.106 requires the Finance Commission to adopt rules governing payoff statements and provides that a mortgage servicer who has furnished a compliant payoff statement "may not, on or before the proposed closing date, demand that a mortgagor pay an amount in excess of the payoff amount specified in the payoff statement" (Finance Code § 343.106(e)). Finance Code § 343.108(b) requires the servicer or mortgagee, within sixty days after receiving the correct payoff amount on a home loan, to deliver a release of lien to the mortgagor or file it with the county clerk. Finance Code § 343.103(b) requires a servicer, within thirty days after a qualifying request from a surviving spouse, to provide the loan information a monthly statement would have contained.

These are duties the *servicer* owes, and the servicer is often not the company that made the loan. When your Texas seller cannot get a payoff figure, the person to call is the servicer, and Finance Code § 343.106 is the reason the servicer has to produce one on a prescribed form within a time the Finance Commission sets — a period the statute requires to allow the servicer at least seven business days after receiving the request.

**Seller financing and private lenders.**

Not all mortgage money is institutional. A **seller-financed** transaction is one in which the seller, rather than a lender, extends the credit: the buyer pays part of the price at closing and signs a note to the seller for the balance, secured by a lien on the property being sold. Texas regulates one important form of seller financing — the executory contract, in which the seller keeps legal title while the buyer pays — extensively in Property Code chapter 5, subchapter D, and that regulation is the subject of a later unit in this module. Seller financing matters as a *source* because it is the source that exists when no institution will lend: on unimproved rural land, on properties with condition problems no lender will accept, and for buyers whose credit history closes the institutional door.

**Private lenders** — individuals, investor groups, and small lending companies advancing their own funds — occupy the same space. They fund from their own capital, they set their own terms, and they often lend on speed and collateral value rather than on documented borrower income. Two consequences follow, and both are business facts rather than legal rules, so treat them as description and cite nothing for them: private money is normally more expensive than institutional money, and it is normally shorter in term. Texas does define one product in this family. Finance Code § 343.001(1) defines a **bridge loan** as "temporary or short-term financing requiring payment of only interest until the entire unpaid balance is due" — the classic structure of a loan meant to carry a borrower from one permanent financing to the next.

Where private lending touches consumer home loans, Texas law reaches it. Finance Code § 343.001(2) defines a **home loan** as a loan "made to one or more individuals for personal, family, or household purposes" and secured in whole or part by a manufactured home used or to be used as the borrower's principal residence, or by "real property improved by a dwelling designed for occupancy by four or fewer families and used or to be used as the borrower's principal residence." Nothing in that definition asks whether the lender is a bank. If an individual investor makes a loan that meets the definition to a Texas borrower, chapter 343 applies, because Finance Code § 343.002(a) provides that the chapter "applies to a loan under this chapter that is extended to a person who is located in this state at the time the loan is made."

**Why the license holder needs this.**

Your client will ask you to recommend a lender. Before you answer, understand that you are making a referral in a transaction that federal law polices closely — a subject this module returns to under *Financing Legislation* — and that Occupations Code § 1101.652(b)(13) makes it a ground for disciplinary action if a license holder "accepts, receives, or charges an undisclosed commission, rebate, or direct profit on an expenditure made for a principal." Understand also that the recommendation has consequences your client will live with: whether the loan can be written outside a standard box, how fast it can close, and whether the servicing will move.

## Unit: Mortgage Market

The market for mortgage money has two halves, and the division between them explains nearly everything about how residential lending behaves.

The **primary mortgage market** is where loans are made. It is the market in which a borrower and an originator meet, an application is taken, and a note and lien are created. Everything in the preceding unit — the depository, the mortgage banker, the broker, the seller, the private lender — is a primary-market actor. The primary market is retail: one borrower, one property, one loan at a time.

The **secondary mortgage market** is where loans that already exist are bought and sold. No borrower participates in it. Its participants are the institutions that originated loans and want to sell them, and the investors who want to own the income stream those loans produce. A loan that is sold in the secondary market does not change: the borrower still owes the same principal at the same rate on the same schedule, secured by the same lien on the same Texas property. What changes is who owns the right to be paid.

**Why a secondary market exists.**

The reason is **liquidity**. Consider a Texas mortgage banker with a warehouse line of ten million dollars. If it lends that ten million into thirty-year notes and holds them, it is finished; it has no more money to lend for thirty years, and it collects its return in monthly slices over three decades. If instead it sells those notes within a few weeks at a small profit, the ten million comes back, and it lends the same ten million again — and again, many times a year. The same capital supports many times as much lending. Multiply that across the country and the secondary market is the reason a thirty-year fixed-rate loan is available at all to an ordinary buyer in Amarillo or Beaumont. Without a buyer for long-term mortgage paper, long-term mortgage paper would be scarce and expensive.

The second reason is **risk transfer and diversification**. A depository that holds two hundred loans, all secured by houses in one Texas county, is exposed to that county's economy. If the local employer closes, a large share of those loans deteriorate at once. Selling loans into a national market lets that concentrated risk be spread across many holders and many regions.

The third reason is **rate transmission**. Because mortgages are sold to investors who are choosing among many investments, the price investors will pay for mortgage paper — and therefore the rate a borrower is quoted — moves with the broader market for long-term debt. When investors demand a higher yield to hold mortgage paper, primary-market rates rise the following morning. The secondary market is the mechanism by which national capital-market conditions arrive at the closing table in Texas.

**Standardization: the price of admission.**

An investor buying thousands of loans cannot read thousands of files. It buys on the strength of the representation that every loan in the pool was written to the same rules. That requirement — that loans be underwritten, documented, and delivered to uniform standards so they can be pooled and traded — is **standardization**, and it is the most consequential fact about residential lending in the United States. It is why the application, the income documentation, the appraisal form, and the note itself look the same in El Paso and in Texarkana. It is also why an originator will tell a borrower "the guidelines don't allow that" about something the originator would personally be happy to do: the originator is not describing its own risk appetite but the terms on which someone else will buy the loan.

Texas statute takes formal notice of that standardizing function. In defining a high-cost home loan, Finance Code § 343.201(1)(C) requires that the loan "has a principal amount equal to or less than one-half of the maximum conventional loan amount for first mortgages as established and adjusted by the Federal National Mortgage Association." The Legislature wrote a Texas consumer-protection threshold by reference to a loan limit that Fannie Mae — the Federal National Mortgage Association, named there in a Texas statute — establishes and adjusts. That is a legislature acknowledging, in the operative text of a statute, that a secondary-market institution's loan limit is the reference point the residential market organizes itself around.

Federal law takes similar notice. In requiring creditors to give applicants copies of appraisals on dwelling-secured first-lien loans, the Equal Credit Opportunity Act defines "valuation" to include "any estimate of the value of a dwelling developed in connection with a creditor's decision to provide credit, including those values developed pursuant to a policy of a government sponsored enterprise or by an automated valuation model, a broker price opinion, or other methodology or mechanism" (15 U.S.C. § 1691(e)(6)). The phrase **government sponsored enterprise** appears in the statute because Congress understood that the valuation rules actually governing a great many American mortgage files are set by such an enterprise's policy rather than by the lender.

**The agencies and enterprises.**

Three institutional names dominate the secondary market for residential mortgages: the Federal National Mortgage Association (Fannie Mae), the Federal Home Loan Mortgage Corporation (Freddie Mac), and the Government National Mortgage Association (Ginnie Mae). Their functions, described in market terms, are these. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac purchase loans that meet their published eligibility standards from originators, aggregate them, and issue securities backed by those pools; the standards they publish are the "conforming" standards the primary market writes to. Ginnie Mae does not purchase loans; it guarantees securities backed by pools of loans that already carry a federal insurance or guaranty, which is the channel through which government-backed lending reaches the capital markets.

Of these three, only Fannie Mae appears in a source held and read for this course, in Finance Code § 343.201(1)(C), and that provision tells us only that Fannie Mae establishes and adjusts a maximum conventional loan amount for first mortgages. **This course holds no source stating the charters, statutory authority, ownership status, or conservatorship history of any of these entities**, so this module states no citation for any of that, and you should verify any specific claim about their structure or authority against current agency materials rather than against this text. What you should carry away is the functional picture, which is not in dispute: originators write to standards these entities publish, the entities buy or guarantee the resulting paper, and investors buy the securities. That chain is why standardization exists and why your client's loan gets sold.

**Conforming and the language of the market.**

Because the secondary market buys to published standards, the primary market divides loans into those that meet the standards and those that do not. A **conforming** loan is one that satisfies the applicable purchase eligibility standards, including the loan-amount limit; a **non-conforming** loan does not, whether because of size, property type, documentation, or borrower profile. The distinction is discussed further under conventional lending. What belongs here is the point that "conforming" is a secondary-market concept that has migrated into ordinary conversation: it describes not a legal category but a marketability category.

## Unit: Application for Credit

The credit application is the point at which an abstract buyer becomes an underwritten file. It is also the point at which the largest body of consumer-protection law in this module attaches, because it is the moment when a creditor is deciding about a person.

**What the underwriter is deciding.**

Lenders describe the underwriting question in three words that have been standard vocabulary in the business for decades: **capacity**, **credit**, and **collateral**. Capacity asks whether the borrower's income and existing obligations leave enough room to make this payment. Credit asks whether the borrower's history of paying obligations suggests that the borrower will make it. Collateral asks whether the property, if the borrower does not pay, is worth enough to satisfy the debt. Some versions of the framework add capital — the borrower's assets and down payment — as a fourth. This vocabulary is industry practice, not a statutory test, and no held source states it; treat it as a useful organizing scheme rather than as law.

Texas law does, however, impose a repayment-ability rule for one category of loan. Finance Code § 343.204(b) provides that a lender "may not engage in a pattern or practice of extending credit to consumers under high-cost home loans based on the consumers' collateral without regard to the obligor's repayment ability, including the obligor's current and expected income, current obligations, employment status, and other financial resources, other than the obligor's equity in the dwelling that secures repayment of the loan." Section 343.204(a) defines "obligor" to mean "a person obligated to pay a loan, including a borrower, cosigner, or guarantor," and provides that where more than one person is obligated, the term refers to them collectively. Read that carefully: the prohibited practice is lending *on the collateral alone*, and the statute lists the borrower attributes a lender must not disregard.

**Documentation.**

Applications are supported by documentation of income and assets — pay records, tax returns, business records for the self-employed, and statements showing the funds available for the down payment and closing costs. The specific documents a given lender requires are a function of the standards the loan will be sold to, and this course holds no source prescribing them, so no list here should be read as a legal requirement. What the license holder should tell a client is structural and reliably true: the underwriter is trying to establish that the income is real, that it is likely to continue, and that the money for closing exists and can be traced. Documentation requests that feel intrusive are almost always aimed at one of those three points.

**Credit reports and scores.**

Underwriting relies on a **credit report** — a compiled history of the applicant's credit obligations and payment record — and on a **credit score** derived from that history. In general terms, a score summarizes the report into a number intended to rank the likelihood of repayment. This course holds no source that states the scoring models in use, the ranges they produce, or the thresholds any lender applies, so this module states none, and a license holder should never quote a client a score threshold as though it were a rule.

The Equal Credit Opportunity Act does address scoring systems in one specific respect. It is not discrimination for a creditor "to use any empirically derived credit system which considers age if such system is demonstrably and statistically sound in accordance with regulations of the Bureau, except that in the operation of such system the age of an elderly applicant may not be assigned a negative factor or value" (15 U.S.C. § 1691(b)(3)). A statistically validated system may use age; it may not use age against an elderly applicant.

**ECOA: what a creditor may not do.**

The Equal Credit Opportunity Act states its core prohibition in one sentence. Under 15 U.S.C. § 1691(a), "[i]t shall be unlawful for any creditor to discriminate against any applicant, with respect to any aspect of a credit transaction —

> (1) on the basis of race, color, religion, national origin, sex or marital status, or age (provided the applicant has the capacity to contract);
> (2) because all or part of the applicant's income derives from any public assistance program; or
> (3) because the applicant has in good faith exercised any right under this chapter."

Note three features of that text. First, the prohibition reaches "any aspect of a credit transaction," not merely the yes-or-no decision — pricing, terms, the vigor with which an application is processed, and the encouragement or discouragement of an application are all aspects of a credit transaction. Second, the age protection carries a qualifier: age is protected "provided the applicant has the capacity to contract." Third, § 1691(a)(2) protects the *source* of income: a creditor may not discriminate because income derives from a public assistance program.

Section 1691(b) then lists what does **not** constitute discrimination, and these carve-outs are where students most often go wrong. A creditor may "make an inquiry of marital status if such inquiry is for the purpose of ascertaining the creditor's rights and remedies applicable to the particular extension of credit and not to discriminate in a determination of credit-worthiness" (§ 1691(b)(1)). A creditor may "make an inquiry of the applicant's age or of whether the applicant's income derives from any public assistance program if such inquiry is for the purpose of determining the amount and probable continuance of income levels, credit history, or other pertinent element of credit-worthiness as provided in regulations of the Bureau" (§ 1691(b)(2)). And a creditor may inquire about or consider the age of an elderly applicant "when the age of such applicant is to be used by the creditor in the extension of credit in favor of such applicant" (§ 1691(b)(4)).

A common error is to conclude from § 1691(a) that a lender may never ask about marital status or age. The statute says the opposite. It permits the inquiry for defined purposes — marital status because a state's marital property and remedies law may determine what the creditor's lien reaches, which in a community property state like Texas is a live question, and age or public assistance because the amount and probable continuance of income is a legitimate underwriting factor. What is forbidden is using the answer to discriminate.

Section 1691(c) adds that it is not a violation for a creditor to refuse credit offered under "any credit assistance program expressly authorized by law for an economically disadvantaged class of persons," a similar program administered by a nonprofit, or a qualifying "special purpose credit program offered by a profit-making organization to meet special social needs," where the refusal "is required by or made pursuant to such program." Targeted assistance programs may have eligibility rules, and applying those rules is not discrimination.

**Adverse action.**

The Act also regulates the *answer*. Under 15 U.S.C. § 1691(d)(1), "[w]ithin thirty days (or such longer reasonable time as specified in regulations of the Bureau for any class of credit transaction) after receipt of a completed application for credit, a creditor shall notify the applicant of its action on the application." Section 1691(d)(2) entitles each applicant against whom adverse action is taken to a statement of reasons, which the creditor satisfies either by giving written reasons as a matter of course, or by giving written notice of the adverse action disclosing "(i) the applicant's right to a statement of reasons within thirty days after receipt by the creditor of a request made within sixty days after such notification, and (ii) the identity of the person or office from which such statement may be obtained." Section 1691(d)(3) is the provision that gives the rule teeth: "A statement of reasons meets the requirements of this section only if it contains the specific reasons for the adverse action taken." A vague reason is not compliance.

Section 1691(d)(6) defines the term: "the term 'adverse action' means a denial or revocation of credit, a change in the terms of an existing credit arrangement, or a refusal to grant credit in substantially the amount or on substantially the terms requested." The definition then excludes a refusal to extend additional credit under an existing arrangement where the applicant is delinquent or in default, or where the additional credit would exceed a previously established limit. Notice that "adverse action" is broader than denial: approving a borrower for materially less than requested, or on materially different terms, is adverse action.

**The appraisal copy.**

Section 1691(e) gives the applicant a right that arises in nearly every Texas purchase. Each creditor "shall furnish to an applicant a copy of any and all written appraisals and valuations developed in connection with the applicant's application for a loan that is secured or would have been secured by a first lien on a dwelling promptly upon completion, but in no case later than 3 days prior to the closing of the loan, whether the creditor grants or denies the applicant's request for credit or the application is incomplete or withdrawn" (§ 1691(e)(1)). The applicant may waive the three-day timing but not the right to the copy (§ 1691(e)(2)); the applicant may be charged a reasonable fee to reimburse the cost of the appraisal (§ 1691(e)(3)), but "the creditor shall provide a copy of each written appraisal or valuation at no additional cost to the applicant" (§ 1691(e)(4)); and the creditor must notify the applicant in writing of this right at the time of application (§ 1691(e)(5)).

**What must be delivered at and after application.**

Several delivery duties cluster at application. Under Regulation X, the lender must provide the special information booklet on settlement costs "by delivering it or placing it in the mail to the applicant not later than three business days . . . after the application is received or prepared," unless the lender denies the application within that period; and "[i]f a borrower uses a mortgage broker, the mortgage broker shall distribute the special information booklet and the lender need not do so" (12 C.F.R. § 1024.6(a)(1)). Certain transaction types are excepted from the booklet requirement, including refinancings, closed-end subordinate-lien loans, reverse mortgages, and "[a]ny other federally related mortgage loan whose purpose is not the purchase of a 1- to 4-family residential property" (12 C.F.R. § 1024.6(a)(3)).

Regulation X also requires a written list of homeownership counseling organizations "not later than three business days after a lender, mortgage broker, or dealer receives an application, or information sufficient to complete an application," obtained no earlier than thirty days before it is given, from the Bureau's website or from data made available by the Bureau or HUD (12 C.F.R. § 1024.20(a)(1)). Reverse mortgage transactions subject to 12 C.F.R. § 1026.33(a) are exempt from that list requirement (12 C.F.R. § 1024.20(c)(1)) — and § 1026.33(a) is held for this course, so you can read what it exempts: a non-recourse consumer credit obligation secured by the consumer's principal dwelling on which nothing is due, absent default, until the consumer dies, the dwelling is transferred, or the consumer stops occupying it as a principal dwelling. The *Financing Legislation* unit takes up § 1026.33 in full, and the *Texas Loan Programs* unit takes up the Texas constitutional reverse-mortgage rules that sit alongside it.

Texas adds two of its own. Finance Code § 343.104 prohibits a lender from offering prepaid single-premium credit life, disability, or unemployment insurance with a home loan "unless the following notice is provided to each applicant for the loan by hand delivery or mail to the applicant not later than the third business day after the date the applicant's application for a home loan is received." The prescribed notice tells the applicant in terms that the coverage "is NOT required as a condition of closing the mortgage loan and will be included with the loan only at your request," that a single premium "will be financed at the interest rate provided for in the mortgage loan," and that the applicant has a right to cancel — with the statutory notice stating in capitals, "YOU MUST CANCEL WITHIN 30 DAYS OF THE DATE OF THE LOAN TO RECEIVE A FULL REFUND OR CREDIT."

Finance Code § 343.105 requires a written notice at closing, on a separate document, in at least 14-point type, warning that "[i]ntentionally or knowingly making a materially false or misleading written statement to obtain property or credit, including a mortgage loan, is a violation of Section 32.32, Texas Penal Code," and that the offense is "punishable by imprisonment for a term of 2 years to 99 years and a fine not to exceed $10,000" depending on the amount of the loan or value of the property. The notice includes the applicant's representation "that all statements and representations contained in my/our written home loan application, including statements or representations regarding my/our identity, employment, annual income, and intent to occupy the residential real property secured by the home loan, are true and correct as of the date of loan closing," and § 343.105(c) requires the applicant to verify the information and execute the notice. Section 343.105(d) adds that a lender's failure to give a complying notice "does not affect the validity or enforceability of the home loan by any holder of the loan."

That last statute deserves a moment of the license holder's attention. Texas requires a home loan applicant to sign, at closing, an acknowledgment that lying on the application — including about intent to occupy — is a felony-range offense. A license holder who is ever tempted to suggest that a buyer describe an investment purchase as an owner-occupied one should read § 343.105 again.

## Unit: Payment Plans

A loan's **payment plan** is the schedule by which the debt is retired. Two loans with the same principal and the same rate can produce radically different obligations depending on how the payments are structured, and the license holder who can explain the difference protects clients from surprises that arrive years after closing.

**Fully amortizing loans.**

A loan is **fully amortizing** when the scheduled payments, made on time for the full term, retire the entire principal and all accrued interest, leaving a balance of zero at the end. Each payment is split: the interest portion is the interest accrued on the outstanding balance since the last payment, and whatever is left over is the principal portion, which reduces the balance. Because the balance falls, the next month's interest is smaller, so a larger share of the identical payment goes to principal. This is **amortization**, and its signature is that the payment is level while its composition steadily shifts from interest toward principal.

The monthly payment on a fully amortizing loan is computed from the principal, the periodic interest rate, and the number of payments:

> payment = P × i ÷ [1 − (1 + i)^−n]

where P is the original principal, i is the interest rate for one period (the annual rate divided by twelve, for monthly payments), and n is the total number of payments.

**Worked example — illustrative figures only; these are not required or standard terms.** Suppose a Round Rock buyer borrows $250,000 for thirty years at a 6% annual rate, payable monthly.

- Periodic rate: i = 0.06 ÷ 12 = 0.005
- Number of payments: n = 30 × 12 = 360
- (1.005)^360 ≈ 6.022575, so (1.005)^−360 ≈ 0.166042
- Denominator: 1 − 0.166042 = 0.833958
- Numerator: $250,000 × 0.005 = $1,250.00
- Payment: $1,250.00 ÷ 0.833958 ≈ **$1,498.88**

Now build the first three rows of the amortization schedule.

*Month 1.* Interest = $250,000.00 × 0.005 = $1,250.00. Principal = $1,498.88 − $1,250.00 = $248.88. New balance = $250,000.00 − $248.88 = $249,751.12.

*Month 2.* Interest = $249,751.12 × 0.005 = $1,248.76. Principal = $1,498.88 − $1,248.76 = $250.12. New balance = $249,751.12 − $250.12 = $249,501.00.

*Month 3.* Interest = $249,501.00 × 0.005 = $1,247.51. Principal = $1,498.88 − $1,247.51 = $251.37. New balance = $249,501.00 − $251.37 = $249,249.63.

Three months of payments totaling $4,496.64 have reduced the debt by $750.37. That is the fact clients find hardest to believe and the one you should be ready to explain: early payments on a long-term loan are almost entirely interest, because interest is charged on a balance that has barely moved.

Total of payments over the full term: $1,498.88 × 360 = $539,596.80. Total interest: $539,596.80 − $250,000 = $289,596.80. The borrower pays more in interest than the house cost.

Now change one variable. The same $250,000 at the same 6% over **fifteen** years:

- n = 180; (1.005)^180 ≈ 2.454093, so (1.005)^−180 ≈ 0.407481
- Denominator: 1 − 0.407481 = 0.592519
- Payment: $1,250.00 ÷ 0.592519 ≈ **$2,109.64**
- Total of payments: $2,109.64 × 180 = $379,735.20; total interest = $129,735.20

The payment rises by about 41%, and total interest falls by about 55%. That trade — payment size against total cost — is the central arithmetic of consumer mortgage choice, and you can now show it to a client on paper.

**Interest-only loans.**

An **interest-only** payment plan requires the borrower to pay only the accrued interest for some period, so the principal balance does not decline at all. On the $250,000 at 6% above, the interest-only monthly payment is $250,000 × 0.005 = **$1,250.00** — about $249 less than the amortizing payment — but after five years of such payments the balance is still exactly $250,000. Texas defines a product built on this structure: Finance Code § 343.001(1) defines a bridge loan as "temporary or short-term financing requiring payment of only interest until the entire unpaid balance is due."

Interest-only plans typically convert at some point to a fully amortizing schedule over the remaining term, which compresses the amortization into fewer years and raises the payment sharply. A borrower who does not understand that is a borrower headed for trouble.

**Balloon payments.**

A **balloon payment** is a final payment substantially larger than the periodic payments preceding it, arising because the payment schedule was not designed to retire the debt over the term. A loan amortized on a thirty-year schedule but due in seven years will have twenty-three years of principal left outstanding when the note matures, and that remainder is the balloon. Balloons are not inherently abusive — they are ordinary in commercial lending — but on a home loan they place the borrower in a position of having to refinance or sell on a date fixed years in advance, regardless of what interest rates or property values are doing that month.

Texas restricts them on one class of loan. Finance Code § 343.202 provides that "[a] high-cost home loan may not contain a provision for a scheduled payment that is more than twice as large as the average of earlier scheduled monthly payments, unless the balloon payment becomes due not less than 60 months after the date of the loan." The section then excepts a payment schedule "adjusted to account for the seasonal or otherwise irregular income of the borrower" and a bridge loan "in connection with the acquisition or construction of a dwelling intended to become the borrower's principal dwelling." Note the shape of the rule: it does not forbid balloons on high-cost home loans; it forbids early ones, with the statutory line drawn at sixty months and at a payment more than twice the average of the earlier scheduled payments.

**Adjustable-rate mortgages.**

An **adjustable-rate mortgage**, or ARM, is a loan whose interest rate changes over the term according to a formula rather than remaining fixed. The formula has standard components, which are matters of loan-document drafting and market practice rather than of any statute held for this course: an **index**, a published rate that moves with market conditions; a **margin**, a fixed number of percentage points the lender adds to the index; the resulting **fully indexed rate**; an **adjustment period** stating how often the rate may change; and **caps**, which limit how far the rate may move at one adjustment and over the life of the loan. Many ARMs open with an initial rate lower than the fully indexed rate for a stated period.

**Illustrative computation.** Suppose an ARM's index stands at 3.50% and the margin is 2.75%. The fully indexed rate is 3.50% + 2.75% = 6.25%. If the loan started at 5.00% and the periodic cap limits a single adjustment to 2 percentage points, the rate could rise as high as 7.00% at that adjustment — but the formula produces only 6.25%, so 6.25% is the new rate. The cap did not bind. Now suppose the index instead rose to 5.25%: the fully indexed rate would be 8.00%, the cap would hold the adjustment to 7.00%, and the borrower would carry a rate below the formula's output until a later adjustment. These numbers are illustrations chosen to show the mechanism, not market figures.

Texas statute anticipates that a loan's opening rate may not be its real rate. In the refinancing provision of Finance Code § 343.101(a), the Legislature provided that "if the loan's interest rate is a discounted introductory rate or a rate that automatically steps up over time, the fully indexed rate or the fully stepped-up rate, as appropriate, shall be used instead of the rate at the loan's inception." That drafting choice tells you what the Legislature thought was the honest way to describe an adjustable loan's cost.

**Graduated payment plans.**

A **graduated payment** plan starts with a payment lower than a fully amortizing payment and increases it on a predetermined schedule — typically each year for a set number of years — until it reaches a level that fully amortizes the remaining balance over the remaining term. The design serves a borrower who expects income to rise. The risk is structural: in the early years the scheduled payment may be less than the interest accruing, and when that happens the shortfall is added to the principal.

**Negative amortization.**

**Negative amortization** occurs when a scheduled payment is smaller than the interest accrued for the period, so that the unpaid interest is added to the principal balance and the debt grows even though the borrower paid on time.

**Worked example — illustrative figures only.** Take the same $250,000 at 6% (0.5% monthly), but under a plan whose payment in year one is capped at $1,100.

*Month 1.* Interest accrued = $250,000 × 0.005 = $1,250.00. Payment = $1,100.00. Shortfall = $150.00, added to principal. New balance = $250,150.00.

*Month 2.* Interest accrued = $250,150.00 × 0.005 = $1,250.75. Payment = $1,100.00. Shortfall = $150.75. New balance = $250,300.75.

*Month 3.* Interest accrued = $250,300.75 × 0.005 = $1,251.50. Payment = $1,100.00. Shortfall = $151.50. New balance = $250,452.25.

The borrower has paid $3,300 and owes $452.25 more than at the start. Worse, the shortfall grows each month, because interest is charged on a rising balance. A borrower in this plan is building negative equity from the first payment, and if property values do not rise faster than the balance does, the borrower cannot sell without bringing money to closing.

Texas prohibits this structure on high-cost home loans. Finance Code § 343.203 provides that "[a] high-cost home loan may not provide for a payment schedule with regular periodic payments that cause the principal balance to increase," and then carves out negative amortization that arises "as a consequence of a temporary forbearance, bridge loan, or restructure sought by the borrower." Read the exception closely. What the statute forbids is a loan *designed* to negatively amortize under its regular payment schedule. What it tolerates is negative amortization that arises from an accommodation — and Finance Code § 343.001(3) defines "restructure" as "a change in the payment schedule or other terms of a home loan as a result of the borrower's default." A workout that capitalizes arrears is not the evil the section targets.

A common error is to assume negative amortization means the borrower missed a payment. It does not. The borrower in the example above paid exactly what the note required, on time, every month, and still owed more.

## Unit: Conventional, FHA & VA Loans

Residential mortgage loans divide into two families by a single question: does a government agency stand behind the lender's risk of loss?

**Conventional loans.**

A **conventional loan** is a mortgage loan that is not insured or guaranteed by a government agency. The lender bears the credit risk itself, subject to whatever private credit enhancement it obtains and to whatever representations it makes when it sells the loan.

Conventional loans divide again. A **conforming** conventional loan meets the eligibility standards of the secondary-market purchasers described earlier in this module — including their loan-amount limits — and is therefore readily salable into the standardized market. A **non-conforming** conventional loan does not. A loan may be non-conforming because the amount exceeds the applicable limit (loans in that category are commonly called jumbo loans, a market term rather than a statutory one), or because of property characteristics, documentation, or borrower profile. Non-conforming loans are made — depositories in particular make them in portfolio — but they are priced and underwritten on terms the individual holder sets, because there is no standardized buyer waiting.

Texas statute anchors the loan-limit concept. Finance Code § 343.201(1)(C) defines a high-cost home loan by reference to "the maximum conventional loan amount for first mortgages as established and adjusted by the Federal National Mortgage Association." **The dollar amount of that limit is not stated in any source held for this course, it changes over time, and this module therefore does not state it.** A license holder who needs the current figure must look it up.

**Private mortgage insurance.**

Because a conventional lender bears the loss if the borrower defaults and the property sells for less than the debt, lenders reduce that exposure by requiring **private mortgage insurance** (PMI) on loans where the borrower's down payment is small relative to the value of the property. PMI is a private insurance product, paid for by the borrower, that indemnifies the *lender* — not the borrower — against a portion of the loss on default. Students regularly misunderstand this: PMI does not protect the borrower and does not pay the borrower's mortgage if the borrower cannot. It protects the lender, and the borrower pays for it.

The measure that governs whether PMI is required is the **loan-to-value ratio** (LTV), which is the loan amount divided by the lesser of the property's appraised value or sale price, expressed as a percentage. **Illustrative computation:** a $250,000 loan on a property appraised at and selling for $312,500 has an LTV of $250,000 ÷ $312,500 = 0.80, or 80%. A $296,875 loan on the same property has an LTV of 95%.

**The LTV threshold at which PMI is required, the premium rates charged, and the rules for terminating PMI are not stated in any source held for this course, and this module states none of them.** Teach your clients the structure — small down payment means the lender demands credit enhancement, and the borrower pays for it — and send them to the lender for the numbers.

**FHA-insured loans.**

The Federal Housing Administration is a federal housing agency; the Department of Housing and Urban Development is the department in which federal housing programs sit, and RESPA's own findings refer to a joint report to Congress on "Mortgage Settlement Costs" submitted by "the Secretary of Housing and Urban Development and the Administrator of Veterans' Affairs" (12 U.S.C. § 2601(a)). An **FHA-insured loan** is a loan made by an approved private lender under FHA program rules, on which FHA provides *mortgage insurance*: if the borrower defaults and the lender suffers a loss, the insurance fund makes the lender whole within the program's terms. The borrower pays for that insurance, and the insurance protects the lender.

The structural point is the one to fix in memory. **FHA does not lend money.** A borrower does not get a loan from FHA; the borrower gets a loan from a lender, and FHA insures it. Because the lender's downside risk is insured, the lender can accept a borrower profile and a down payment it would not accept on a conventional loan. That is the entire economic logic of the program: government-borne risk in exchange for expanded access.

**The specific down payment percentages, maximum LTVs, mortgage insurance premium rates and durations, loan limits, and qualifying ratios of the FHA program are not stated in any source held for this course, and this module states none of them.** They are set by program rules that change, and a license holder who quotes them from memory will eventually quote them wrong.

**VA-guaranteed loans.**

The Department of Veterans Affairs administers home loan benefits for eligible veterans, service members, and certain surviving spouses. Congress's redesignation of the office is noted in RESPA's statutory notes: a reference to the Administrator of Veterans' Affairs is "deemed to refer to Secretary of Veterans Affairs" (note to 12 U.S.C. § 2601), and 12 U.S.C. § 2607(c)(5) lists "the Secretary of Veterans Affairs" among the officials the Bureau consults. Texas also has a Veterans' Land Board, named in Property Code § 5.062(b)(2)(A), which excludes from the executory-contract subchapter "a sale of land by . . . the Veterans' Land Board."

A **VA-guaranteed loan** is a loan made by a private lender to an eligible borrower under VA program rules, on which VA provides a *guaranty*: VA agrees to indemnify the lender for a portion of the loss on default. As with FHA, **VA does not lend the money**; a private lender does, and the government stands behind part of the lender's risk.

The difference between **insurance** and **guaranty** is worth stating precisely, because it is the classic examination distinction. Insurance is a policy under which the insurer pays the lender's covered loss; the borrower pays a premium for it. A guaranty is a promise by a third party to answer for part of the borrower's debt if the borrower does not pay; the guaranteed portion is expressed as an amount or fraction of the loan rather than as an insurance policy. In practical effect both reduce the lender's exposure, which is why both support financing terms a lender would not otherwise offer. In legal form they are different instruments.

**The amount of VA entitlement, the guaranty percentages, the funding fee, occupancy and eligibility criteria, and any maximum loan amount are not stated in any source held for this course, and this module states none of them.** Entitlement figures in particular change and are the single most commonly misquoted numbers in real estate education. If a client asks, the honest answer is that you will help them get the current figure from VA or from a VA-approved lender rather than guess.

**Comparing the three, structurally.**

Strip away the numbers and the three families differ along four axes.

*Who bears the credit risk.* Conventional: the lender and, on low-down-payment loans, a private mortgage insurer. FHA: the federal insurance fund, within program terms. VA: the government, as guarantor, within program terms.

*Who pays for the risk protection.* In all three, the borrower — through PMI premiums, through FHA mortgage insurance premiums, or through the VA fee structure.

*What limits eligibility.* Conventional: the lender's and the secondary market's credit standards. FHA: program rules open to the general public who meet them. VA: service-based eligibility, which no amount of credit strength can substitute for.

*Where the terms come from.* Conventional conforming: secondary-market eligibility standards. Conventional non-conforming: the individual holder. FHA and VA: the agencies' program rules, applied by approved lenders.

Notice what all three have in common. In every case a private lender advances the money and takes an application; in every case the borrower's file is underwritten to standards someone else publishes; and in every case the license holder's job is the same — to know which family the client is likely to be in, to know that the terms come from a rulebook that changes, and to refuse to invent the numbers.

## Unit: Texas Loan Programs

Texas has its own layer of home-lending law sitting on top of the federal layer, and it has state programs that assist particular categories of buyer. The two are very different in how confidently this course can speak about them, and it is important that you understand why.

**What this course can state, and what it cannot.**

The best-known feature of Texas home-lending law is the homestead protection in the Texas Constitution, article XVI, section 50, and the home equity lending framework that section carries. **That constitutional text is held and was read in full for this course**, and the rest of this unit teaches it from the words of the provision itself, quoting the operative language wherever a number, a deadline, or a condition is doing the work. Section 50 is unusual among the sources in this module in one respect worth noticing at the outset: it is a constitutional provision, so it changes only when the Legislature proposes an amendment and the voters approve it. The section's own credit line records that history — adopted February 15, 1876 and amended repeatedly since, most recently by the amendments approved November 7, 2017 that rewrote subsections (a), (f), (g), and (t) and added (f-1). A rule that changes by statewide election changes slowly and visibly, which is why it is worth learning properly.

What this course still cannot state is the operating detail of Texas's assistance programs, and the contrast with section 50 is instructive: constitutional text is fixed until the voters move it, while program terms are administrative and are revised continually. Texas has a Veterans' Land Board, and this course can say so because Property Code § 5.062(b)(2)(A) names it in excluding "a sale of land by . . . the Veterans' Land Board" from the executory-contract subchapter. Texas also has state housing finance programs that make down-payment assistance and below-market financing available to qualifying buyers, principally first-time buyers and buyers in targeted categories. **No source held for this course states the eligibility criteria, income limits, purchase-price limits, assistance amounts, interest rates, or administering rules of any Texas assistance program**, and this module therefore states none of them. Describe the categories to your clients — veteran land and housing programs, state and local first-time buyer and down-payment assistance programs, mortgage credit certificate programs, and locally funded programs offered by cities and counties — and then send the client to the program's own current materials. Program terms are revised, funding is periodically exhausted and replenished, and a license holder who quotes last year's terms does real harm.

**Article XVI, section 50(a): the homestead and the debts that reach it.**

Section 50 is captioned "PROTECTION OF HOMESTEAD FROM FORCED OR UNAUTHORIZED SALE; EXCEPTIONS; REQUIREMENTS FOR MORTGAGE LOANS AND OTHER OBLIGATIONS SECURED BY HOMESTEAD," and its opening words state the rule that everything else in the section qualifies: "The homestead of a family, or of a single adult person, shall be, and is hereby protected from forced sale, for the payment of all debts except for" the debts the subsection then enumerates (§ 50(a)).

Read that sentence for what it actually does, because students routinely overread it. It is not a protection against *owing* money. A Texas homeowner can incur any obligation anyone else can incur, and a creditor can sue, take judgment, and collect from other assets. What the Constitution withholds from the ordinary creditor is the *homestead as a source of payment*: the homestead may not be sold out from under the family to satisfy a debt unless the debt is one of those section 50(a) lists. Subsection (c) states the same idea from the lender's side and states it absolutely: "No mortgage, trust deed, or other lien on the homestead shall ever be valid unless it secures a debt described by this section," and "[a]ll pretended sales of the homestead involving any condition of defeasance shall be void." A homestead lien that does not fit one of the enumerated categories is not a weak lien or a defective lien. It is not a lien.

The enumerated debts are these.

*Purchase money* — "the purchase money thereof, or a part of such purchase money" (§ 50(a)(1)). The loan that bought the home. This is why an ordinary Texas purchase-money mortgage is valid without any of the machinery described later in this unit.

*Taxes* — "the taxes due thereon" (§ 50(a)(2)).

*Owelty of partition* — "an owelty of partition imposed against the entirety of the property by a court order or by a written agreement of the parties to the partition, including a debt of one spouse in favor of the other spouse resulting from a division or an award of a family homestead in a divorce proceeding" (§ 50(a)(3)). Owelty is the payment one co-owner makes to another to equalize a division of jointly owned property. The clause matters constantly in Texas divorce practice: the spouse awarded the house owes the other spouse for that spouse's share, and this is the provision that lets the obligation be secured by the homestead itself.

*Refinance of a lien* — "the refinance of a lien against a homestead, including a federal tax lien resulting from the tax debt of both spouses, if the homestead is a family homestead, or from the tax debt of the owner" (§ 50(a)(4)). A lien that could validly attach may be refinanced, and the refinancing lien takes the place of the lien it retires. Subsection (e) polices the boundary: a refinance of debt described by subsections (a)(1) through (a)(5) "that includes the advance of additional funds may not be secured by a valid lien against the homestead" unless the refinance is itself a section 50(a)(6) extension of credit, or "the advance of all the additional funds is for reasonable costs necessary to refinance such debt or for a purpose described by Subsection (a)(2), (a)(3), or (a)(5)." In plain terms: you may refinance what was validly owed, and you may add closing costs and taxes and owelty and construction money — but the moment cash goes into the borrower's pocket, the loan has become a home equity loan and must satisfy every condition of section 50(a)(6).

*Work and material — the mechanic's lien* — "work and material used in constructing new improvements thereon, if contracted for in writing, or work and material used to repair or renovate existing improvements thereon" on stated conditions (§ 50(a)(5)). Notice the asymmetry. For *new* improvements the Constitution requires only that the work and material be "contracted for in writing." For *repair or renovation of existing* improvements, four conditions attach, and they are the reason a Texas home-improvement lien is a formal transaction rather than a handshake: (A) the work and material must be "contracted for in writing, with the consent of both spouses, in the case of a family homestead, given in the same manner as is required in making a sale and conveyance of the homestead"; (B) the contract must not be "executed by the owner or the owner's spouse before the fifth day after the owner makes written application for any extension of credit for the work and material"; (C) the contract must "expressly provide[] that the owner may rescind the contract without penalty or charge within three days after the execution of the contract by all parties"; and (D) the contract must be "executed by the owner and the owner's spouse only at the office of a third-party lender making an extension of credit for the work and material, an attorney at law, or a title company." Conditions (B) and (C) both yield where "the work and material are necessary to complete immediate repairs to conditions on the homestead property that materially affect the health or safety of the owner or person residing in the homestead and the owner of the homestead acknowledges such in writing." That emergency carve-out is the Constitution recognizing that a family with a failed roof cannot wait five days.

*An extension of credit meeting the home equity conditions* (§ 50(a)(6)) — treated at length below.

*A reverse mortgage* (§ 50(a)(7)), defined and conditioned by subsection (k), treated below.

*The manufactured-home conversion* — "the conversion and refinance of a personal property lien secured by a manufactured home to a lien on real property, including the refinance of the purchase price of the manufactured home, the cost of installing the manufactured home on the real property, and the refinance of the purchase price of the real property" (§ 50(a)(8)). A manufactured home financed as personal property can be converted to real-property collateral, and the resulting lien is good against the homestead.

Two more general rules belong with the list. Subsection (b) provides that an owner or claimant "may not sell or abandon the homestead without the consent of each owner and the spouse of each owner, given in such manner as may be prescribed by law" — the source of the Texas rule that a non-owner spouse signs the deed. And subsection (d) protects the market: "A purchaser or lender for value without actual knowledge may conclusively rely on an affidavit that designates other property as the homestead of the affiant and that states that the property to be conveyed or encumbered is not the homestead of the affiant." That is the constitutional basis for the non-homestead affidavit your title company will ask for.

**Section 50(a)(6): the home equity conditions.**

Until 1997 a Texas homestead could not be encumbered to take equity out of it at all. Section 50(a)(6), added by the amendment approved November 4, 1997 and amended several times since, created that possibility and hedged it with more conditions than any other credit transaction in Texas law. A loan that satisfies all of them is a valid lien; a loan that misses one is subject to the forfeiture provision discussed further below. The conditions, in the Constitution's own order, are these.

*Voluntary lien, written agreement, consent of each owner and each owner's spouse.* The extension of credit must be "secured by a voluntary lien on the homestead created under a written agreement with the consent of each owner and each owner's spouse" (§ 50(a)(6)(A)). Every word carries weight. The lien must be voluntary, so no judgment or involuntary lien qualifies. It must rest on a written agreement. And consent is required not only from every owner but from every owner's spouse, whether or not that spouse is on title and whether or not that spouse will be on the note.

*Eighty percent of fair market value.* The extension of credit must be "of a principal amount that when added to the aggregate total of the outstanding principal balances of all other indebtedness secured by valid encumbrances of record against the homestead does not exceed 80 percent of the fair market value of the homestead on the date the extension of credit is made" (§ 50(a)(6)(B)). Read the words. The test is not eighty percent of the new loan against value; it is the new principal *plus every other recorded encumbrance* against eighty percent of fair market value, measured on the date the credit is extended. A Texas owner with a first lien at seventy percent of value has room for ten percent, not eighty.

*Non-recourse.* The extension of credit must be "without recourse for personal liability against each owner and the spouse of each owner, unless the owner or spouse obtained the extension of credit by actual fraud" (§ 50(a)(6)(C)). This is the condition license holders most often state incorrectly, and it is worth stating precisely: on a compliant Texas home equity loan the lender's remedy is the house. If the foreclosure sale does not cover the debt, the lender may not pursue the borrower personally for the difference — the sole exception being actual fraud in obtaining the credit.

*Judicial foreclosure only.* The credit must be "secured by a lien that may be foreclosed upon only by a court order" (§ 50(a)(6)(D)). Texas is otherwise a non-judicial foreclosure state, and the ordinary deed of trust is enforced by a trustee's sale on the courthouse steps. A section 50(a)(6) lien is not. Subsection (r) supplies the machinery: "The supreme court shall promulgate rules of civil procedure for expedited foreclosure proceedings related to the foreclosure of liens under Subsection (a)(6) of this section and to foreclosure of a reverse mortgage lien that requires a court order."

*The two percent fee cap.* The credit must not "require the owner or the owner's spouse to pay, in addition to any interest or any bona fide discount points used to buy down the interest rate, any fees to any person that are necessary to originate, evaluate, maintain, record, insure, or service the extension of credit that exceed, in the aggregate, two percent of the original principal amount of the extension of credit" (§ 50(a)(6)(E)). Four categories are excluded from that two percent: "(i) an appraisal performed by a third party appraiser; (ii) a property survey performed by a state registered or licensed surveyor; (iii) a state base premium for a mortgagee policy of title insurance with endorsements established in accordance with state law; or (iv) a title examination report if its cost is less than the state base premium for a mortgagee policy of title insurance without endorsements established in accordance with state law." Note what the cap does not touch — interest, and bona fide discount points used to buy the rate down — and note that the two percent figure is the figure the Constitution currently states, having been amended to it by the 2017 amendment.

*Not an open-end account, unless a HELOC.* The credit must not be "a form of open-end account that may be debited from time to time or under which credit may be extended from time to time unless the open-end account is a home equity line of credit" (§ 50(a)(6)(F)).

*Prepayable without penalty.* The credit must be "payable in advance without penalty or other charge" (§ 50(a)(6)(G)). A prepayment penalty on a Texas home equity loan is not merely a bad term; it is a constitutional defect.

*No additional collateral.* The credit must not be "secured by any additional real or personal property other than the homestead" (§ 50(a)(6)(H)). A lender may not take the borrower's other lot, or the borrower's vehicle, as extra security for a home equity loan. (Paragraph (I) has been repealed and the Constitution now prints it as "(repealed)"; that is why the lettering skips.)

*No acceleration for falling value or unrelated default.* The credit "may not be accelerated because of a decrease in the market value of the homestead or because of the owner's default under other indebtedness not secured by a prior valid encumbrance against the homestead" (§ 50(a)(6)(J)).

*One at a time.* The credit must be "the only debt secured by the homestead at the time the extension of credit is made unless the other debt was made for a purpose described by Subsections (a)(1)-(a)(5) or Subsection (a)(8) of this section" (§ 50(a)(6)(K)). A homestead may carry a purchase-money first lien, a tax obligation, an owelty lien, a refinance lien, a mechanic's lien, or a manufactured-home conversion lien alongside a home equity loan. It may not carry two home equity loans.

*Amortizing payments.* The credit must be "scheduled to be repaid: (i) in substantially equal successive periodic installments, not more often than every 14 days and not less often than monthly, beginning no later than two months from the date the extension of credit is made, each of which equals or exceeds the amount of accrued interest as of the date of the scheduled installment; or (ii) if the extension of credit is a home equity line of credit, in periodic payments described under Subsection (t)(8)" (§ 50(a)(6)(L)). Compare that to the negative-amortization discussion in the Payment Plans unit. A payment that does not cover accrued interest is not merely poor practice on a Texas home equity loan; it fails a constitutional condition.

*The closing-timing rules.* The credit must be "closed not before" three separate moments, all of which must have passed (§ 50(a)(6)(M)):

> "(i) the 12th day after the later of the date that the owner of the homestead submits a loan application to the lender for the extension of credit or the date that the lender provides the owner a copy of the notice prescribed by Subsection (g) of this section;
>
> (ii) one business day after the date that the owner of the homestead receives a copy of the loan application if not previously provided and a final itemized disclosure of the actual fees, points, interest, costs, and charges that will be charged at closing. If a bona fide emergency or another good cause exists and the lender obtains the written consent of the owner, the lender may provide the documentation to the owner or the lender may modify previously provided documentation on the date of closing; and
>
> (iii) the first anniversary of the closing date of any other extension of credit described by Subsection (a)(6) of this section secured by the same homestead property, except a refinance described by Paragraph (Q)(x)(f) of this subdivision, unless the owner on oath requests an earlier closing due to a state of emergency" declared by the president or the governor and applicable to the area where the homestead is located.

Three clocks, then: twelve days from the later of application or the subsection (g) notice; one business day from the loan application copy and the final itemized disclosure of actual fees, points, interest, costs, and charges; and one year from the closing of any prior home equity loan on the same homestead. The one-year clock is the reason a Texas owner cannot take a second equity loan in the same year, and it is the rule most often forgotten by a client who has already borrowed once.

*The place of closing.* The credit must be "closed only at the office of the lender, an attorney at law, or a title company" (§ 50(a)(6)(N)). A Texas home equity loan cannot be signed at the borrower's kitchen table, in a coffee shop, or at your brokerage office. It is closed at one of three places, and nowhere else.

*Interest rate.* The credit "permits a lender to contract for and receive any fixed or variable rate of interest authorized under statute" (§ 50(a)(6)(O)). The Constitution caps fees, not the rate.

*Authorized lenders.* The credit must be made by one of six categories of lender, none of which may have "been found by a federal regulatory agency to have engaged in the practice of refusing to make loans because the applicants for the loans reside or the property proposed to secure the loans is located in a certain area" — a redlining disqualification written into the state constitution (§ 50(a)(6)(P)). The six are: a bank, savings and loan association, savings bank, or credit union doing business under Texas or federal law, including a subsidiary of one; a federally chartered lending instrumentality or a person approved as a mortgagee by the United States government to make federally insured loans; a person licensed to make regulated loans under Texas statute; "a person who sold the homestead property to the current owner and who provided all or part of the financing for the purchase"; "a person who is related to the homestead property owner within the second degree of affinity or consanguinity"; and "a person regulated by this state as a mortgage banker or mortgage company."

*The conditions in paragraph (Q).* Finally, the credit must be made on the condition that the owner is not required to apply the proceeds to repay another debt except debt secured by the homestead or debt to another lender; that the owner not assign wages as security; that the owner "not sign any instrument in which blanks relating to substantive terms of agreement are left to be filled in"; that the owner not sign a confession of judgment or a power of attorney to confess judgment or appear for the owner in a judicial proceeding; that at the time the credit is made the owner receive "a copy of the final loan application and all executed documents signed by the owner at closing related to the extension of credit"; that "the security instruments securing the extension of credit contain a disclosure that the extension of credit is the type of credit defined by Subsection (a)(6)"; that within a reasonable time after termination and full payment the lender cancel and return the note and give the owner a recordable release or a copy of an endorsement and assignment; that "the owner of the homestead and any spouse of the owner may, within three days after the extension of credit is made, rescind the extension of credit without penalty or charge"; and that "the owner of the homestead and the lender sign a written acknowledgment as to the fair market value of the homestead property on the date the extension of credit is made" (§ 50(a)(6)(Q)(i)–(ix)). Subsection (h) then lets a lender or assignee for value "conclusively rely" on that fair market value acknowledgment where the acknowledged value is an appraisal or evaluation prepared under an applicable state or federal requirement and the lender has no actual knowledge that the stated value was incorrect.

**Section 50(g): the notice the borrower must receive before closing.**

Subsection (g) supplies both a timing rule and the text of a notice. "An extension of credit described by Subsection (a)(6) of this section may be secured by a valid lien against homestead property if the extension of credit is not closed before the 12th day after the lender provides the owner with the following written notice on a separate instrument." What follows in the constitutional text is the notice itself, printed in full, headed "NOTICE CONCERNING EXTENSIONS OF CREDIT DEFINED BY SECTION 50(a)(6), ARTICLE XVI, TEXAS CONSTITUTION," and organized as lettered paragraphs (A) through (R) that track the conditions of section 50(a)(6) and, in paragraph (R), the home equity line of credit rules of subsection (t).

A few features of the notice deserve attention. It opens by telling the borrower the consequence in plain words: "IF YOU DO NOT REPAY THE LOAN OR IF YOU FAIL TO MEET THE TERMS OF THE LOAN, THE LENDER MAY FORECLOSE AND SELL YOUR HOME." It then restates the substantive protections in borrower-facing language — that the loan "MUST BE VOLUNTARILY CREATED WITH THE CONSENT OF EACH OWNER OF YOUR HOME AND EACH OWNER'S SPOUSE"; that the principal plus all other liens "IS MORE THAN 80 PERCENT OF THE FAIR MARKET VALUE OF YOUR HOME" is prohibited; that the loan "MUST BE WITHOUT RECOURSE FOR PERSONAL LIABILITY AGAINST YOU AND YOUR SPOUSE UNLESS YOU OR YOUR SPOUSE OBTAINED THIS EXTENSION OF CREDIT BY ACTUAL FRAUD"; that the lien "MAY BE FORECLOSED UPON ONLY WITH A COURT ORDER"; that "FEES AND CHARGES TO MAKE THE LOAN MAY NOT EXCEED 2 PERCENT OF THE LOAN AMOUNT" with the four stated exceptions; that "YOU MAY PREPAY THE LOAN WITHOUT PENALTY OR CHARGE"; that "ONLY ONE LOAN DESCRIBED BY SECTION 50(a)(6) . . . MAY BE SECURED WITH YOUR HOME AT ANY GIVEN TIME"; that "THE LOAN MAY NOT CLOSE BEFORE 12 DAYS AFTER YOU SUBMIT A LOAN APPLICATION TO THE LENDER OR BEFORE 12 DAYS AFTER YOU RECEIVE THIS NOTICE, WHICHEVER DATE IS LATER"; that "THE LOAN MAY CLOSE ONLY AT THE OFFICE OF THE LENDER, TITLE COMPANY, OR AN ATTORNEY AT LAW"; and that the borrower may "WITHIN 3 DAYS AFTER CLOSING, RESCIND THE LOAN WITHOUT PENALTY OR CHARGE."

The notice ends by disclaiming its own authority, in a sentence every license holder should adopt as a personal rule: "THIS NOTICE IS ONLY A SUMMARY OF YOUR RIGHTS UNDER THE TEXAS CONSTITUTION. YOUR RIGHTS ARE GOVERNED BY SECTION 50, ARTICLE XVI, OF THE TEXAS CONSTITUTION, AND NOT BY THIS NOTICE." Subsection (g) closes with a language rule that parallels the executory-contract rule you will meet later in this module: "If the discussions with the borrower are conducted primarily in a language other than English, the lender shall, before closing, provide an additional copy of the notice translated into the written language in which the discussions were conducted."

Two cautions about the notice. First, it is a *twelve-day* notice, not a twelve-point notice — the number in subsection (g) counts days between delivery and closing, and section 50 prescribes no type size for it. Second, the twelve-day period in subsection (g) and the twelve-day period in section 50(a)(6)(M)(i) are the same waiting period seen from two directions, and (M)(i) is the more complete statement, because it runs the clock from "the later of" the application date or the notice date.

**Refinancing a home equity loan: sections 50(f) and (f-1).**

Once a homestead carries a section 50(a)(6) loan, the loan does not lose its character by being refinanced. Subsection (f) provides that a refinance of homestead debt "any portion of which is an extension of credit described by Subsection (a)(6)" may not be secured by a valid lien unless either the refinance is itself an (a)(6) or (a)(7) extension of credit, or all four of these conditions are met: "(A) the refinance is not closed before the first anniversary of the date the extension of credit was closed"; (B) the refinanced credit advances no additional funds other than funds to refinance debt described by subsections (a)(1) through (a)(7) and "actual costs and reserves required by the lender to refinance the debt"; (C) the refinanced principal, added to all other recorded encumbrances, "does not exceed 80 percent of the fair market value of the homestead on the date the refinance of the extension of credit is made"; and (D) the lender provides a prescribed written notice "on a separate document not later than the third business day after the date the owner submits the loan application to the lender and at least 12 days before the date the refinance of the extension of credit is closed."

That subsection (f)(2)(D) notice is worth reading, because it is the Constitution warning a borrower about a trade the borrower may not realize is being made. It tells the owner that the existing loan "IS A HOME EQUITY LOAN," that home equity loans "HAVE IMPORTANT CONSUMER PROTECTIONS," and that a non-home-equity refinance "(1) WILL PERMIT THE LENDER TO FORECLOSE WITHOUT A COURT ORDER; (2) WILL BE WITH RECOURSE FOR PERSONAL LIABILITY AGAINST YOU AND YOUR SPOUSE; AND (3) MAY ALSO CONTAIN OTHER TERMS OR CONDITIONS THAT MAY NOT BE PERMITTED IN A TRADITIONAL HOME EQUITY LOAN." Subsection (f-1) completes the mechanism: a lien securing a refinance under subsection (f)(2) "is deemed to be a lien described by Subsection (a)(4)," and an owner's or spouse's affidavit that the (f)(2) requirements were met "conclusively establishes" that the (a)(4) requirements were met.

Say that plainly to a client who asks. A Texas owner with a home equity loan may, after a year, refinance it into an ordinary non-home-equity loan — but in doing so the owner gives up judicial foreclosure and gives up non-recourse. The Constitution requires the owner be told that in capital letters twelve days ahead of time.

**When the lender gets it wrong: forfeiture and cure.**

Section 50 does not merely list conditions; it states the penalty for missing one and the route back. Paragraph (Q)(x) provides that "the lender or any holder of the note for the extension of credit shall forfeit all principal and interest of the extension of credit if the lender or holder fails to comply with the lender's or holder's obligations under the extension of credit and fails to correct the failure to comply not later than the 60th day after the date the lender or holder is notified by the borrower of the lender's failure to comply." Forfeiture of all principal and interest is an extraordinary remedy — the borrower keeps the house and owes nothing — and it explains why Texas home equity closings are conducted with the care they are.

The same paragraph then supplies the cures, and each is matched to a kind of defect. The lender may pay the owner "an amount equal to any overcharge paid by the owner" where the owner paid more than paragraphs (E), (G), or (O) allow. It may send the owner a written acknowledgment that the lien is valid only up to the eighty percent limit of paragraph (B), or that the lien is not secured by the extra property paragraph (H) forbids. It may send a written notice "modifying any other amount, percentage, term, or other provision prohibited by this section to a permitted amount . . . and adjusting the account of the borrower." It may deliver the documents paragraph (Q)(v) required, or obtain the fair-market-value signatures paragraph (Q)(ix) required. It may acknowledge in writing, where the defect is the extra lien paragraph (K) forbids, "that the accrual of interest and all of the owner's obligations under the extension of credit are abated while any prior lien prohibited under Paragraph (K) remains secured by the homestead." And where none of those fit, there is a catch-all: "if the failure to comply cannot be cured under Subparagraphs (x)(a)-(e) of this paragraph, curing the failure to comply by a refund or credit to the owner of $1,000 and offering the owner the right to refinance the extension of credit with the lender or holder for the remaining term of the loan at no cost to the owner on the same terms, including interest, as the original extension of credit."

Two defects are not curable at all. Under paragraph (Q)(xi) the lender or holder "shall forfeit all principal and interest . . . if the extension of credit is made by a person other than a person described under Paragraph (P) of this subdivision or if the lien was not created under a written agreement with the consent of each owner and each owner's spouse, unless each owner and each owner's spouse who did not initially consent subsequently consents." An unauthorized lender and a missing spousal consent are the two failures the Constitution treats as fatal — subject only, in the consent case, to the missing spouse later consenting.

**Reverse mortgages: sections 50(a)(7) and 50(k).**

Section 50(a)(7) makes "a reverse mortgage" an excepted debt, and subsection (k) defines the term and conditions it. A reverse mortgage under the Texas Constitution is an extension of credit "secured by a voluntary lien on homestead property created by a written agreement with the consent of each owner and each owner's spouse"; "made to a person who is or whose spouse is 62 years or older"; "made without recourse for personal liability against each owner and the spouse of each owner"; and under which advances are provided "based on the equity in a borrower's homestead" or "for the purchase of homestead property that the borrower will occupy as a principal residence" (§ 50(k)(1)–(4)).

The defining structural feature is in subdivision (6): the loan "requires no payment of principal or interest until" all borrowers have died, the property is sold or otherwise transferred, all borrowers cease occupying it "for a period of longer than 12 consecutive months without prior written approval from the lender," a purchase-money reverse mortgage borrower fails to timely occupy the property, or the borrower defaults on an obligation to repair and maintain, pay taxes and assessments on, or insure the property, commits actual fraud in connection with the loan, or fails to maintain the priority of the lender's lien after notice.

Three protective conditions matter to a license holder. Counseling is mandatory and timed: the loan may not be made "unless the prospective borrower and the spouse of the prospective borrower attest in writing that the prospective borrower and the prospective borrower's spouse received counseling regarding the advisability and availability of reverse mortgages and other financial alternatives that was completed not earlier than the 180th day nor later than the 5th day before the date the extension of credit is closed" (§ 50(k)(8)). A separate twelve-day notice is required, which "the lender or originator and the borrower must sign for the notice to take effect," warning in capitals that the borrower may lose the home for failing to pay taxes or insurance, failing to maintain the property, ceasing to occupy it for more than twelve consecutive months, selling or transferring it, dying without the loan being repaid, committing actual fraud, or failing to maintain the lender's lien priority (§ 50(k)(9)). And foreclosure is constrained: the lender may not commence foreclosure until it gives notice that a ground exists and gives the borrower "at least 30 days, or at least 20 days in the event of a default under Subdivision (6)(D)(iii)," to remedy the condition, pay the debt from sale proceeds or other sources, or "convey the homestead property to the lender by a deed in lieu of foreclosure" (§ 50(k)(10)); and the lien "may be foreclosed upon only by a court order, if the foreclosure is for a ground other than" the death of all borrowers or the sale or transfer of the property (§ 50(k)(11)).

Subsection (o) adds a point that matters to older Texas clients weighing the product: for determining eligibility for means-tested state benefits, "reverse mortgage loan advances made to a borrower are considered proceeds from a loan and not income," and "undisbursed funds under a reverse mortgage loan are considered equity in a borrower's home and not proceeds from a loan."

Connect this to Finance Code chapter 343, discussed below. Section 343.002(b) excludes a reverse mortgage from that chapter entirely. Reverse mortgages on a Texas homestead are governed by section 50(a)(7) and (k) of the Constitution, not by chapter 343 — and, on the federal side, by 12 C.F.R. § 1026.33, which the *Financing Legislation* unit takes up.

**Home equity lines of credit: section 50(t).**

Subsection (t) defines the one open-end product section 50(a)(6)(F) permits. A home equity line of credit is an open-end account under which "the owner requests advances, repays money, and reborrows money"; "any single debit or advance is not less than $4,000"; "the owner does not use a credit card, debit card, or similar device, or preprinted check unsolicited by the borrower, to obtain an advance"; the section 50(a)(6)(E) fees "are charged and collected only at the time the extension of credit is established and no fee is charged or collected in connection with any debit or advance"; the maximum principal that may be extended, added to all other homestead indebtedness on the date the credit is established, does not exceed the eighty percent limit of section 50(a)(6)(B); "the lender or holder may not unilaterally amend the extension of credit"; and repayment is made "in regular periodic installments, not more often than every 14 days and not less often than monthly, beginning not later than two months from the date the extension of credit is established," with each installment during the draw period equaling or exceeding accrued interest and installments after the draw period being substantially equal (§ 50(t)(1)–(8)). Paragraph (R) of the subsection (g) notice restates these for the borrower, adding that if the balance ever exceeds eighty percent of the fair market value as determined when the line was established, the owner "MAY NOT CONTINUE TO REQUEST ADVANCES . . . UNTIL THE BALANCE IS LESS THAN 80 PERCENT OF THE FAIR MARKET VALUE."

**What section 50 means for you.**

You are not going to close these loans, and you should not advise on their terms. What you need is the ability to recognize when a transaction has walked into section 50 and to say so out loud before anyone signs anything. Four practical points follow directly from the text.

First, spousal consent. Section 50(b) requires the consent of each owner and the spouse of each owner to sell or abandon a homestead, and section 50(a)(6)(A) and (Q)(xi) require it for a home equity lien — with a failure of consent among the two defects the Constitution makes non-curable. When a Texas seller tells you the spouse "isn't on the deed and doesn't need to sign," that is the moment to route the question to the title company.

Second, the equity math. Because section 50(a)(6)(B) measures the new principal *plus all other recorded encumbrances* against eighty percent of fair market value, a client with an existing first lien has far less borrowing room than the client expects. You can do that arithmetic in front of a client without giving legal advice.

Third, the calendar. Twelve days from the later of application or the section 50(g) notice, one business day from the final itemized disclosure, three days to rescind after closing, and one year between successive home equity loans on the same homestead. Every one of those is in the constitutional text, and none of them can be waived away because a closing is inconvenient.

Fourth, the character of the loan. A compliant Texas home equity loan is non-recourse and can be foreclosed only by court order. Those are real protections with real value, and a client refinancing out of one into an ordinary loan is giving them up — which is exactly why section 50(f)(2)(D) makes the lender say so in capital letters twelve days ahead.

**Finance Code chapter 343.**

Alongside the Constitution, Texas has a statute that governs home loans generally: Finance Code chapter 343, "Home Loans." That chapter was read in full for this module, and it is the operative Texas law that will most often touch your transactions. Where section 50 asks whether a lien on a homestead is valid at all, chapter 343 asks what a lender must disclose, may not charge, and must do after the loan closes. The two operate on different questions and both can apply to the same loan.

**Chapter 343: scope.**

Finance Code § 343.001(2) defines a **home loan** as a loan that is "(A) made to one or more individuals for personal, family, or household purposes; and (B) secured in whole or part by: (i) a manufactured home, as defined by Section 347.002, used or to be used as the borrower's principal residence; or (ii) real property improved by a dwelling designed for occupancy by four or fewer families and used or to be used as the borrower's principal residence."

Take that definition apart. The borrower must be one or more *individuals* — not an entity. The purpose must be *personal, family, or household* — not business. The security must be a manufactured home or a dwelling designed for four or fewer families. And in either case the property must be the borrower's *principal residence*, used or to be used as such. A loan on a fourplex the borrower will live in is within the definition; a loan on the same fourplex bought purely as an investment is not.

Section 343.001 supplies two more definitions used throughout: a **bridge loan** is "temporary or short-term financing requiring payment of only interest until the entire unpaid balance is due," and to **restructure** is "a change in the payment schedule or other terms of a home loan as a result of the borrower's default."

Applicability is territorial and borrower-focused. Section 343.002(a): "This chapter applies to a loan under this chapter that is extended to a person who is located in this state at the time the loan is made." Section 343.002(b) excludes two things entirely: "(1) a reverse mortgage; or (2) an open-end account, as defined by Section 301.002." Both exclusions matter now that you have read section 50. A reverse mortgage on a Texas homestead is left to the Constitution's own regime in section 50(a)(7) and (k) and to 12 C.F.R. § 1026.33, not to chapter 343; and a home equity line of credit, which section 50(a)(6)(F) and (t) permit as the one open-end homestead product, is an open-end account outside chapter 343 as well. And § 343.003 resolves conflicts in the chapter's favor: "If this chapter conflicts with another provision of this title, this chapter controls."

One section in subchapter B is no longer operative, and it is worth knowing this so you do not repeat an obsolete rule you may find in older material. Section 343.102, which required counseling-related disclosures on home loans bearing interest of 12 percent or more a year, states in subsection (b): "This section expires September 1, 2003." The published chapter carries an accompanying note immediately before that section reading: "[n]otwithstanding the approval by the voters on Sept. 13, 2003, of the constitutional amendment authorizing the continuation of this section, this section expired as provided by Subsection (b) on Sept. 1, 2003." Read the note against the text it annotates: the expiry it describes is one "provided by Subsection (b)," and § 343.102(b) is the subsection that provides for expiry. Section 343.101, which restricts the refinancing of certain low-rate home loans made by government or nonprofit lenders before the loan's seventh anniversary, carries no expiry of its own — its subsection (b) is the substantive refinancing restriction, not a sunset — and nothing read for this course states that it has lapsed.

**Chapter 343: what a Texas home loan lender must do.**

**Single-premium credit insurance (§ 343.104).** A lender may not offer prepaid single-premium credit life, disability, or unemployment insurance with a home loan unless the statutory notice is hand delivered or mailed to the applicant "not later than the third business day after the date the applicant's application for a home loan is received." The notice text is prescribed and tells the applicant that the coverage is optional, that a single premium will be financed at the loan's interest rate, and that cancellation within thirty days of the loan date produces a full refund or credit while later cancellation produces a refund or credit of unearned premium.

**Notice of penalties for false statements (§ 343.105).** "A lender, mortgage banker, or licensed mortgage broker shall provide to each applicant for a home loan a written notice at closing." The notice must be a separate document, in at least 14-point type, and must warn that intentionally or knowingly making a materially false or misleading written statement to obtain property or credit, including a mortgage loan, violates Penal Code § 32.32 and is punishable by two to ninety-nine years' imprisonment and a fine not to exceed $10,000, depending on loan amount or property value. The applicant must verify and execute it (§ 343.105(c)). Failure to provide a complying notice "does not affect the validity or enforceability of the home loan by any holder of the loan" (§ 343.105(d)).

**Payoff statements (§ 343.106).** The Finance Commission must adopt rules governing title companies' requests for payoff information from mortgage servicers on home loans, "including rules prescribing a standard payoff statement form that must be used by mortgage servicers." The rules must allow the servicer at least seven business days after receiving the request to deliver the statement (§ 343.106(c)). The prescribed form must state the proposed closing date and "provide a payoff amount that is valid through that date" (§ 343.106(d)). Then the protective rule: where a compliant payoff statement has been furnished, the servicer or mortgagee "may not, on or before the proposed closing date, demand that a mortgagor pay an amount in excess of the payoff amount specified in the payoff statement" (§ 343.106(e)). A servicer that discovers an error may correct and deliver a corrected statement "on or before the second business day before the specified proposed closing date," by certified mail with return receipt requested and, if the requestor has provided the means, electronically (§ 343.106(f)). If a servicer understates the payoff, does not timely correct it, and is paid the stated amount, the difference "remains a liability of the former mortgagor owed to the mortgagee," the lien is released or subordinated as § 343.106(g) provides depending on whether the transaction was a sale or a refinance, and proceeds disbursed at closing to or for the mortgagor's benefit, excluding closing costs, "are subject to a constructive trust for the benefit of the mortgagee to the extent of the underpayment."

That section deserves emphasis because it is one of the few places where a Texas statute directly protects the closing you are working on. The payoff figure your title company obtains is good through the stated closing date, and the servicer cannot ambush the seller with a larger demand at the table.

**Release of lien after payoff (§ 343.108).** Not later than the sixtieth day after receiving the correct payoff amount on a home loan, the mortgage servicer or mortgagee must either deliver a release of lien to the mortgagor or file it with the county clerk for recording (§ 343.108(b)). If, on or before the twentieth day after payoff, the mortgagor delivers a written request for delivery or filing, the deadline shortens: the release must be delivered or filed "not later than the 30th day after the date the mortgagee or mortgage servicer receives the written request" (§ 343.108(c)). Section 343.108(e) limits the servicer's duty to cases where it has authority to deliver or file the release.

**Disclosure to a surviving spouse (§ 343.103).** Within thirty days after receiving a request from the surviving spouse of a mortgagor of a home loan, accompanied by the proof § 343.103(c) requires, the servicer must provide the information a standard monthly statement would contain, including "the current balance information, including the due dates and the amount of any installments," whether the loan is current and any delinquent amounts, the loan number, and "the amount of any escrow deposit for taxes and insurance purposes." The proof required is a death certificate, an affidavit of disinterested witnesses in the form referenced in Estates Code § 203.002 including language that the surviving spouse was married to the mortgagor at the time of death, and an affidavit by the surviving spouse that he or she currently resides in the mortgaged property as the primary residence. The request must include a notice in bold-faced, capital, or underlined letters stating: "THIS REQUEST IS MADE PURSUANT TO TEXAS FINANCE CODE SECTION 343.103. SUBSEQUENT DISCLOSURE OF INFORMATION IS NOT IN CONFLICT WITH THE GRAMM-LEACH-BLILEY ACT UNDER 15 U.S.C. SECTION 6802(e)(8)." A servicer that complies "is not liable to the estate of the mortgagor or any heir or beneficiary of the mortgagor as a result of providing this information to the surviving spouse" (§ 343.103(e)).

This is a provision Texas license holders encounter more often than they expect. A surviving spouse who is not on the note frequently cannot get anyone at the servicer to speak to her. Section 343.103 gives her a statutory route, and knowing it exists — and what proof it requires — is genuinely useful.

**Chapter 343, subchapter C: high-cost home loans.**

Subchapter C creates a category of loan subject to additional restrictions. Finance Code § 343.201(1) defines a **high-cost home loan** as a loan that is made to one or more individuals for personal, family, or household purposes; is secured by a manufactured home or by real property improved by a dwelling for four or fewer families, in either case used or to be used as the borrower's principal residence; "has a principal amount equal to or less than one-half of the maximum conventional loan amount for first mortgages as established and adjusted by the Federal National Mortgage Association"; is not a reverse mortgage or an open-end account as defined by § 301.002; and "is a credit transaction described by 12 C.F.R. Section 1026.32, as amended," with an extension for residential mortgage transactions as defined by 12 C.F.R. § 1026.2 where the total loan amount is $20,000 or more and either "the annual percentage rate exceeds the rate indicated in 12 C.F.R. Section 1026.32(a)(1)(i), as amended" or "the total points and fees payable by the consumer at or before loan closing will exceed the amount indicated in 12 C.F.R. Section 1026.32(a)(1)(ii), as amended." Section 343.201(2) adopts the federal definition of points and fees from 12 C.F.R. § 1026.32(b).

Read the structure before reaching for a number. Texas does not set its own rate or fee trigger; it *incorporates* the federal thresholds by reference, "as amended." When the federal thresholds move, the Texas category moves with them, and there is no independent Texas percentage to learn.

The federal thresholds the Texas definition points to are held and read for this course, and the *Financing Legislation* unit sets them out from 12 C.F.R. § 1026.32(a)(1) in full. In short form: a transaction secured by the consumer's principal dwelling is a high-cost mortgage if its annual percentage rate exceeds the average prime offer rate for a comparable transaction by more than 6.5 percentage points on most first liens, or 8.5 percentage points on a subordinate lien or on a first lien where the dwelling is personal property and the loan amount is under $50,000; or if total points and fees exceed 5 percent of the total loan amount on a loan of $20,000 or more (the $20,000 adjusts annually by CPI); or if the creditor can charge a prepayment penalty more than 36 months after consummation or penalties totaling more than 2 percent of the amount prepaid. Texas then narrows the field further with its own principal-amount ceiling in § 343.201(1)(C) and its own $20,000 floor and rate-or-fee alternative in § 343.201(1)(E). A loan can therefore be a federal high-cost mortgage without being a Texas high-cost home loan, because the Texas category is capped at one-half the maximum conventional first-mortgage loan amount and the federal category is not.

Five restrictions follow.

*Balloon payments (§ 343.202).* No scheduled payment more than twice the average of earlier scheduled monthly payments unless it comes due at least sixty months after the loan date, subject to the seasonal-income and bridge-loan exceptions quoted in the Payment Plans unit.

*Negative amortization (§ 343.203).* No payment schedule with regular periodic payments that cause the principal balance to increase, except negative amortization arising from a temporary forbearance, bridge loan, or restructure sought by the borrower.

*Repayment ability (§ 343.204).* No pattern or practice of collateral-based lending "without regard to the obligor's repayment ability, including the obligor's current and expected income, current obligations, employment status, and other financial resources, other than the obligor's equity in the dwelling that secures repayment of the loan."

*Prepayment penalties (§ 343.205).* "A lender may not make a high-cost home loan containing a provision for a prepayment penalty." That is the whole section — a flat prohibition with no exception.

*Charges for products not received (§ 343.206).* "A lender, in connection with a high-cost home loan, may not charge a borrower an amount for a service or product if the borrower does not receive the service or product."

Notice the pattern across all five. Each targets a specific way a lender can profit from a borrower's weak position: a payment structure the borrower cannot survive, a debt that grows while being paid, a loan underwritten on the house rather than the person, a penalty for escaping, and a charge for nothing. Taken together they are Texas's statement about what high-cost home lending may not be.

## Unit: Govt. Influence in Mortgage Lending

Government influences mortgage lending through three broad channels: monetary policy, which moves the cost of money; the secondary-market institutions, which move the availability of money; and regulation, which moves the terms on which money may be lent. This unit addresses the first two conceptually. The third — the disclosure and anti-discrimination statutes — is the subject of the module's final unit.

Before going further, a caution about the level of confidence appropriate here. **No source held for this course states the structure, powers, or operations of the Federal Reserve System, the Federal Housing Finance Agency, or the government-sponsored enterprises.** What follows is therefore economic description, offered as an account of how the market works rather than as a statement of legal rules, and this module cites nothing for it. Where a held source does touch the subject, the citation appears.

**Monetary policy and the cost of money.**

The central bank of the United States is the Federal Reserve System, and its policy actions influence interest rates and the quantity of money and credit in the economy. Its tools operate on short-term rates and on the supply of reserves in the banking system, and although mortgage rates are long-term rates that the central bank does not set directly, they respond to central bank policy through expectations and through the general level of interest rates in the economy.

The transmission works roughly like this. When policy tightens — when short-term rates are pushed up and the growth of money and credit is restrained — borrowing becomes more expensive throughout the economy. Investors demanding higher yields on other assets will also demand higher yields on mortgage paper, and mortgage rates rise. Higher mortgage rates raise the monthly payment on any given loan amount, which reduces the loan amount a given borrower can carry, which reduces what that borrower can pay for a house. Demand cools. Conversely, when policy eases, rates fall, payments on a given loan amount fall, purchasing power rises, and demand strengthens.

You can put numbers on that transmission with the amortization arithmetic from earlier in this module. **Illustrative computation.** A borrower who can afford $1,498.88 per month can carry a $250,000 thirty-year loan at 6%, as computed earlier. At 5% (i = 0.00416667, n = 360), the payment factor computed in that unit gives a payment of $1,342.06 on $250,000 — so a borrower with the same $1,498.88 budget could carry roughly $279,200 at 5%, since $1,498.88 ÷ $1,342.06 × $250,000 ≈ $279,200. A single percentage point of rate moved that borrower's purchasing power by roughly $29,000 without changing the borrower's income by a dollar. That is the mechanism by which national monetary policy reaches a Texas listing appointment.

The federal statutes read for this course reflect an awareness that credit costs are a matter of national economic concern. The Truth in Lending Act opens by finding that "economic stabilization would be enhanced and the competition among the various financial institutions and other firms engaged in the extension of consumer credit would be strengthened by the informed use of credit" (15 U.S.C. § 1601(a)). Congress framed credit disclosure as an instrument of economic stabilization and competition, not merely of consumer fairness.

**Government-sponsored enterprises and the availability of money.**

The second channel is institutional. As the Mortgage Market unit explained, entities that purchase or guarantee residential mortgage paper make long-term mortgage lending possible by supplying liquidity and standardization. Because these entities decide what they will buy, their eligibility standards function as a national underwriting rulebook, and changes to those standards change who can get a loan. That is influence over *availability* rather than over *price*, and it is at least as consequential for a first-time buyer in Texas.

Congress has legislated with that influence in view. The Equal Credit Opportunity Act's definition of "valuation" reaches "those values developed pursuant to a policy of a government sponsored enterprise or by an automated valuation model" (15 U.S.C. § 1691(e)(6)) — an acknowledgment in the operative text of a federal statute that GSE policy determines how dwellings get valued in the credit process. And the Texas Legislature, as noted, wrote its high-cost home loan threshold around "the maximum conventional loan amount for first mortgages as established and adjusted by the Federal National Mortgage Association" (Finance Code § 343.201(1)(C)).

The enterprises' relationship to the federal government, including the oversight arrangements that have governed them since the financial crisis of 2008, is a matter this course holds no source for. **Do not represent to a client that any particular entity is or is not government-backed, government-owned, or in any particular legal status.** State the function — these entities buy or guarantee mortgage paper and publish the standards loans are written to — and refer questions of legal status to current agency sources.

**Government as insurer and guarantor.**

A third form of influence was covered in the preceding unit: by insuring or guaranteeing loans, federal housing and veterans programs change what private lenders are willing to do. A lender that would not accept a small down payment from a borrower with a thin credit file will accept it when the government bears the loss. That is government influence exercised not by regulating the lender but by absorbing the lender's risk.

**Government as regulator of process.**

Finally, government shapes lending by prescribing the process. RESPA's stated purposes include achieving "more effective advance disclosure to home buyers and sellers of settlement costs," "the elimination of kickbacks or referral fees that tend to increase unnecessarily the costs of certain settlement services," "a reduction in the amounts home buyers are required to place in escrow accounts established to insure the payment of real estate taxes and insurance," and "significant reform and modernization of local recordkeeping of land title information" (12 U.S.C. § 2601(b)). Each of those is a deliberate intervention in how the private settlement market operates.

Regulation X carries the escrow purpose into arithmetic. A servicer may charge, at account creation, an amount sufficient to pay the property charges attributable to the period since they were last paid until the initial payment date, computed so that the lowest month-end target balance projected for the escrow account computation year is zero, "[i]n addition, the servicer may charge the borrower a cushion that shall be no greater than one-sixth (1/6) of the estimated total annual payments from the escrow account" (12 C.F.R. § 1024.17(c)(1)(i)). Throughout the life of the account, the servicer may charge monthly "a monthly sum equal to one-twelfth (1/12) of the total annual escrow payments which the servicer reasonably anticipates paying from the account," plus an amount to maintain the same one-sixth cushion (12 C.F.R. § 1024.17(c)(1)(ii)). All servicers "must use the aggregate accounting method in conducting escrow account analyses" (12 C.F.R. § 1024.17(c)(4)). And Regulation X prescribes how a servicer estimates: if the charge for the next computation year is known, the servicer must use it; if not, it may use the preceding year's charge, or that charge modified by an amount not exceeding the most recent year's change in the national Consumer Price Index for all urban consumers, and in cases of unassessed new construction may estimate from the assessment of comparable residential property in the market area (12 C.F.R. § 1024.17(c)(7)).

That last rule explains something Texas license holders see constantly. A buyer of new construction in a growing Texas suburb closes with an escrow estimate based on comparable assessed property, and a year later — when the improved home is assessed for the first time — the escrow analysis produces a shortage and the payment jumps. Nothing improper has happened; the servicer estimated exactly as § 1024.17(c)(7) permits. But a buyer who was not warned experiences it as a betrayal, and warning the buyer is free.

## Unit: Other Financing Techniques

When institutional financing is unavailable, too slow, or too expensive, the parties themselves become the financing mechanism. Texas regulates some of these techniques heavily and leaves others largely to contract. Knowing which is which is the point of this unit.

**Assumption.**

An **assumption** occurs when a buyer takes over the seller's existing loan, becoming obligated on the note and taking the property subject to the existing lien, instead of obtaining new financing. The attraction is obvious when the existing loan carries an interest rate below the current market: the buyer inherits the seller's rate and avoids the cost of originating a new loan.

Whether a particular loan may be assumed is a question of that loan's own documents and of the program rules under which it was made. **No source held for this course states a general Texas or federal rule on assumability**, so this module states none. Treat assumability as a document question to be answered by reading the note and deed of trust and by asking the servicer, never as something you assume from the loan type.

The arithmetic point students miss is that assumption transfers the loan, not the price. If a Texas seller's house is worth $300,000 and the assumable loan balance is $190,000, the buyer must produce $110,000 — in cash, in a second lien, or in seller financing — to close. A low-rate assumable loan on a property with substantial equity is often less useful than it sounds, because the equity gap has to be financed somehow, frequently at a higher rate than the loan being assumed.

Texas does impose a disclosure duty when residential property is conveyed while a recorded lien remains. Property Code § 5.016(a) provides that "[a] person may not convey an interest in or enter into a contract to convey an interest in residential real property that will be encumbered by a recorded lien at the time the interest is conveyed" unless, on or before the seventh day before the earlier of the effective date of the conveyance or the execution of an executory contract, option contract, or other contract binding the purchaser, the person gives the purchaser and each lienholder a separate written disclosure in at least 12-point type. That disclosure must identify the property and each lienholder's name, address, and phone number; state the amount of debt secured by each lien; specify the terms under which the debt was incurred, including as applicable the rate of interest, the periodic installments required, and the account number; indicate "whether the lienholder has consented to the transfer of the property to the purchaser"; specify insurance details; state property taxes due; and carry at the top a warning in substantially this form:

> "WARNING: ONE OR MORE RECORDED LIENS HAVE BEEN FILED THAT MAKE A CLAIM AGAINST THIS PROPERTY AS LISTED BELOW. IF A LIEN IS NOT RELEASED AND THE PROPERTY IS CONVEYED WITHOUT THE CONSENT OF THE LIENHOLDER, IT IS POSSIBLE THE LIENHOLDER COULD DEMAND FULL PAYMENT OF THE OUTSTANDING BALANCE OF THE LIEN IMMEDIATELY. YOU MAY WISH TO CONTACT EACH LIENHOLDER FOR FURTHER INFORMATION AND DISCUSS THIS MATTER WITH AN ATTORNEY."

A violation "does not invalidate a conveyance," but where the notice was not given the purchaser "may terminate the contract for any reason on or before the seventh day after the date the purchaser receives the notice, in addition to other remedies" (§ 5.016(b)). Section 5.016(c) lists eleven exempt transfers, including transfers under a court order or foreclosure sale, transfers by a trustee in bankruptcy, transfers between co-owners or spouses, transfers to or from a governmental entity, transfers "where the purchaser obtains a title insurance policy insuring the transfer of title to the real property," and transfers "to a person who has purchased, conveyed, or entered into contracts to purchase or convey an interest in real property four or more times in the preceding 12 months." Section 5.016(d) provides a safe harbor where the person required to give notice "reasonably believes and takes any necessary action to ensure that each lien for which notice was not provided will be released on or before the 30th day after the date on which title to the property is transferred."

Read the statutory warning again. The Legislature chose to tell purchasers, in the statute's own words, that conveying encumbered property without lienholder consent may lead the lienholder to demand immediate full payment. That is the risk at the heart of every unapproved assumption and every wraparound.

**Wraparound financing.**

A **wraparound** is a seller-financed transaction in which the seller conveys the property and takes back a note for an amount that includes — wraps around — the balance of an existing loan that remains in place and in the seller's name. The buyer pays the seller on the wrap note; the seller continues paying the underlying lender.

**Illustrative example.** A Texas seller owns a home worth $250,000 with an existing first lien balance of $180,000 at 4.5%. The buyer pays $25,000 down and signs a wraparound note to the seller for $225,000 at 6.5%. The seller continues to pay the underlying note.

- Annual interest the seller collects on the wrap: $225,000 × 6.5% = $14,625
- Annual interest the seller pays on the underlying note: $180,000 × 4.5% = $8,100
- Annual interest spread to the seller: $14,625 − $8,100 = $6,525
- The seller's actual capital in the deal is the wrap balance minus the underlying balance: $225,000 − $180,000 = $45,000
- Effective annual return on that $45,000: $6,525 ÷ $45,000 = 14.5%

That 14.5% is the arithmetic that makes wraps attractive to sellers, and it explains why the technique persists. But look at what the buyer is exposed to. The buyer pays the seller. If the seller does not pay the underlying lender, the underlying lender can pursue its lien against a house the buyer is living in and paying for. And the transaction sits squarely inside § 5.016's warning: the property was conveyed while encumbered by a recorded lien, and the lienholder may not have consented.

Texas also confronts this risk directly in the executory-contract context. Property Code § 5.085(a) provides that "[a] potential seller may not execute an executory contract with a potential purchaser if the seller does not own the property in fee simple free from any liens or other encumbrances," and § 5.085(b) requires the seller to maintain fee simple title free of liens for the contract's duration — with a narrow exception for a purchase-money lien placed before the contract, available only if the seller gives a separate written disclosure at least three days before execution naming the lienholder or servicer, the loan number and outstanding balance, and the monthly payments and due dates, and stating in 14-point type "that, if the seller fails to make timely payments to the lienholder, the lienholder may attempt to collect the debt by foreclosing on the lien and selling the property at a foreclosure sale" (§ 5.085(b)(3)(A)). The exception further requires that the lien attach only to the contracted property and secure no more than the purchaser's outstanding balance (§ 5.085(b)(3)(B)); that the lienholder not prohibit an executory-contract encumbrance and consent to verify loan status on the purchaser's request and to accept payments directly from the purchaser if the seller defaults (§ 5.085(b)(3)(C)); and that the contract contain covenants obligating the seller to make timely payments and give monthly statements, to notify the purchaser within three days of receiving a notice of default, acceleration, or foreclosure or being sued, and warranting that if the seller fails to pay, "the purchaser may, without notice, cure any deficiency with a lienholder directly and deduct from the total outstanding balance owed by the purchaser under the executory contract, without the necessity of judicial action, 150 percent of any amount paid to the lienholder" (§ 5.085(b)(3)(D)).

A violation of § 5.085 "is a false, misleading, or deceptive act or practice within the meaning of Section 17.46, Business & Commerce Code," actionable under the DTPA, and entitles the purchaser to cancel and rescind and to receive back all payments of any kind made to the seller plus reimbursement for taxes paid and the value of improvements made (§ 5.085(c)).

**Contract for deed and other executory contracts.**

A **contract for deed** — in Texas statutory language, an **executory contract for conveyance** — is a seller-financed sale in which the buyer takes possession and pays the price in installments while the seller retains legal title until the price is paid. Texas regulates these contracts more heavily than almost any other financing device, because historically they were used to sell property to buyers who lost everything on a single missed payment.

**Scope.** Subchapter D "applies only to a transaction involving an executory contract for conveyance of real property used or to be used as the purchaser's residence or as the residence of a person related to the purchaser within the second degree by consanguinity or affinity" (Property Code § 5.062(a)). For subchapter purposes, "a lot measuring one acre or less is presumed to be residential property," and "an option to purchase real property that includes or is combined or executed concurrently with a residential lease agreement, together with the lease, is considered an executory contract for conveyance of real property" (§ 5.062(a)(1)–(2)). That second clause is the reason **lease-purchase** and lease-option arrangements belong in this unit: in Texas, a residential lease combined or concurrently executed with a purchase option *is* an executory contract, with the subchapter's duties attached. Section 5.0621(a) adds that both subchapter D and Property Code chapter 92 apply to the lease portion, and § 5.0621(b) provides that after the tenant exercises the option, chapter 92 no longer applies to the lease.

The subchapter does not apply to sales of state land or to sales by the Veterans' Land Board, the state or a political subdivision, or entities created to act on their behalf (§ 5.062(b)), nor to "an executory contract that provides for the delivery of a deed from the seller to the purchaser within 180 days of the date of the final execution of the executory contract" (§ 5.062(c)). That 180-day rule matters: an ordinary earnest-money contract closing in six weeks is not what subchapter D is aimed at.

**Form and disclosure before signing.** Before the purchaser signs, the seller must provide a survey completed within the past year or a plat of a current survey, a legible copy of any document describing an encumbrance or other claim affecting title, and an attached written condition notice, executed by both parties, headed "WARNING — IF ANY OF THE ITEMS BELOW HAVE NOT BEEN CHECKED, YOU MAY NOT BE ABLE TO LIVE ON THE PROPERTY," with checkboxes for whether the property is in a recorded subdivision, has potable water service, has sewer service or septic approval, has electric service, and is not in a floodplain (§ 5.069(a)). The seller must also provide a tax certificate for each taxing unit and a legible copy of any insurance policy or binder showing insurer, insured, property, and amount (§ 5.070(a)); failure to do so "is a false, misleading, or deceptive act or practice within the meaning of Section 17.46, Business & Commerce Code" and "entitles the purchaser to cancel and rescind the executory contract and receive a full refund of all payments made to the seller" (§ 5.070(b)).

The seller must also disclose the financing itself. Section 5.071 requires a written statement specifying the purchase price; the interest rate charged; the dollar amount, or an estimate if the rate is variable, of the interest charged for the term; the total of principal and interest to be paid; the late charge, if any; and "the fact that the seller may not charge a prepayment penalty or any similar fee if the purchaser elects to pay the entire amount due under the contract before the scheduled payment date under the contract."

**Writing required.** "An executory contract is not enforceable unless the contract is in writing and signed by the party to be bound or by that party's authorized representative" (§ 5.072(a)); rights and obligations are determined solely from the written contract (§ 5.072(b)); and the contract "may not be varied by any oral agreements or discussions that occur before or contemporaneously with the execution" (§ 5.072(c)). Section 5.072(d) requires a separate signed statement in 14-point boldface or uppercase type reading substantially: "THIS EXECUTORY CONTRACT REPRESENTS THE FINAL AGREEMENT BETWEEN THE SELLER AND PURCHASER AND MAY NOT BE CONTRADICTED BY EVIDENCE OF PRIOR, CONTEMPORANEOUS, OR SUBSEQUENT ORAL AGREEMENTS OF THE PARTIES. THERE ARE NO UNWRITTEN ORAL AGREEMENTS BETWEEN THE PARTIES." Failure is again a DTPA violation and entitles the purchaser to rescind with a full refund (§ 5.072(e)).

**Forbidden terms.** Section 5.073(a) bars a seller from including a provision that imposes a late fee exceeding the lesser of eight percent of the monthly payment or the actual administrative cost of processing the late payment; that prohibits the purchaser from pledging the purchaser's interest to obtain a loan for improvements, including utility or fire protection improvements; that imposes a prepayment penalty or similar fee; that forfeits an option fee or other option payment for a late payment; or that increases the price, imposes any fee or charge, or otherwise penalizes a purchaser leasing with an option to buy "for requesting repairs or exercising any other right under Chapter 92." And § 5.073(b): "A provision of the executory contract that purports to waive a right or exempt a party from a liability or duty under this subchapter is void."

**A fourteen-day right to cancel.** "[T]he purchaser may cancel and rescind an executory contract for any reason" by sending signed written notice by telegram or certified or registered mail, return receipt requested, or delivering it in person, "not later than the 14th day after the date of the contract" (§ 5.074(a)). Within ten days of receiving that notice the seller must return the executed contract and any property exchanged or payments made, and cancel any security interest arising out of the contract (§ 5.074(b)). The contract must carry, immediately next to the purchaser's signature space, a 14-point statement reading substantially: "YOU, THE PURCHASER, MAY CANCEL THIS CONTRACT AT ANY TIME DURING THE NEXT TWO WEEKS. THE DEADLINE FOR CANCELING THE CONTRACT IS (date). THE ATTACHED NOTICE OF CANCELLATION EXPLAINS THIS RIGHT." (§ 5.074(c)). A separate cancellation form must be given at signing (§ 5.074(d)), and "[t]he seller may not request the purchaser to sign a waiver of receipt of the notice of cancellation form" (§ 5.074(e)).

**Language.** "If the negotiations that precede the execution of an executory contract are conducted primarily in a language other than English, the seller shall provide a copy in that language of all written documents relating to the transaction, including the contract, disclosure notices, annual accounting statements, and a notice of default required by this subchapter" (§ 5.068).

**Recording and annual accounting.** The seller must record the contract, including the § 5.069 disclosure statement, "on or before the 30th day after the date the contract is executed," and must record the terminating instrument if the contract ends (§ 5.076(a), (c)); damages for violation may not exceed $500 for each calendar year of noncompliance (§ 5.076(e)). Each January the seller must provide an annual statement — postmarked no later than January 31 if mailed — stating the amount paid, the remaining amount owed, the number of payments remaining, amounts paid to taxing authorities and insurers on the purchaser's behalf if collected by the seller, an accounting of insurance proceeds applied to the property, and a copy of any changed policy (§ 5.077(a), (b)). A seller conducting fewer than two such transactions in twelve months who fails to comply owes $100 in liquidated damages per missed statement plus reasonable attorney's fees; a seller conducting two or more owes "$250 a day for each day after January 31 that the seller fails to provide the purchaser with the statement, but not to exceed the fair market value of the property," plus fees (§ 5.077(c), (d)).

**Default: notice, cure, and equity protection.** This is the heart of the subchapter. "[D]efault" means the failure to make a timely payment or to comply with a term of the contract (§ 5.061). Before enforcing rescission or forfeiture and acceleration, the seller must give written notice by registered or certified mail, return receipt requested, conspicuous and in 14-point boldface or uppercase type, including on a separate page: "NOTICE — YOU ARE NOT COMPLYING WITH THE TERMS OF THE CONTRACT TO BUY YOUR PROPERTY. UNLESS YOU TAKE THE ACTION SPECIFIED IN THIS NOTICE BY (date) THE SELLER HAS THE RIGHT TO TAKE POSSESSION OF YOUR PROPERTY." (§ 5.063(a)). The notice must identify and explain the remedy, itemize the delinquency into principal and interest with any additional charges and the period they relate to, and, for a non-payment default, identify the term violated and the action required to cure (§ 5.063(b)).

The seller may enforce rescission or forfeiture and acceleration "only if" the seller gave notice of the intent and of the purchaser's right to cure within thirty days, the purchaser failed to cure, § 5.066 does not apply, "and the contract has not been recorded in the county in which the property is located" (§ 5.064). Section 5.065 gives the purchaser the cure right: "Notwithstanding an agreement to the contrary, a purchaser in default . . . may avoid the enforcement of a remedy described by Section 5.064 by complying with the terms of the contract on or before the 30th day after the date notice is given under that section."

Section 5.066 is the equity-protection provision, and it changes the remedy entirely once the purchaser has built up a stake. "If a purchaser defaults after the purchaser has paid 40 percent or more of the amount due or the equivalent of 48 monthly payments under the executory contract or, regardless of the amount the purchaser has paid, the executory contract has been recorded, the seller is granted the power to sell, through a trustee designated by the seller, the purchaser's interest in the property as provided by this section. The seller may not enforce the remedy of rescission or of forfeiture and acceleration after the contract has been recorded" (§ 5.066(a)). In that circumstance the seller must give notice and allow at least sixty days to cure, using a substituted statement warning that "A TRUSTEE DESIGNATED BY THE SELLER HAS THE RIGHT TO SELL YOUR PROPERTY AT A PUBLIC AUCTION" (§ 5.066(b)); the trustee must post, file, serve, and conduct the sale as prescribed by Property Code § 51.002, and a notice of sale "is not valid unless it is given after the period to cure has expired" (§ 5.066(c), (d)). The seller must convey fee simple title to the sale purchaser and warrant the property free of encumbrance (§ 5.066(d)). Critically: "If the proceeds of the sale exceed the debt amount, the seller shall disburse the excess funds to the purchaser under the executory contract" (§ 5.066(e)). Where the purchaser defaults before reaching 40 percent or 48 payments, the seller may still use rescission or forfeiture and acceleration if the § 5.063 and § 5.064 notice requirements are met (§ 5.066(g)).

Understand what §§ 5.064 and 5.066 do together. Recording the contract, or reaching 40 percent paid or 48 payments, converts the seller's remedy from *taking the property back and keeping everything paid* into *selling it at auction and handing the surplus to the buyer*. That is the Legislature protecting accumulated equity, and it is the single most important thing to know about Texas contracts for deed.

**Converting to ordinary title.** Section 5.081(a) gives the purchaser a right "at any time and without paying penalties or charges of any kind" to convert the interest into recorded, legal title, "regardless of whether the seller has recorded the executory contract." If the purchaser tenders the full balance, the seller must transfer recorded legal title (§ 5.081(b)). If the purchaser delivers a promissory note equal to the balance "and that contains the same interest rate, due dates, and late fees as the contract," the seller must execute a deed with any warranties the contract requires, and the purchaser simultaneously executes a deed of trust with the same terms, securing the note and conferring a power of sale on the trustee (§ 5.081(c)). Within ten days of receiving a substantially complying note, the seller must either deliver a written explanation legally justifying refusal or contact the purchaser to schedule execution (§ 5.081(d)). Section 5.081(g) adds that "[t]he appropriate use of forms published by the Texas Real Estate Commission for transactions described by this section constitutes compliance with this section." On execution of the conveyances, the executory contract "is considered completed" and "has no further effect" (§ 5.081(f)).

**Title transfer at payoff and the price of delay.** A recorded executory contract "shall be the same as a deed with a vendor's lien," the vendor's lien being for the unpaid contract price less lawful deductions, enforceable by sale under § 5.066 or by judicial foreclosure, with a general warranty implied unless the recorded contract limits it (§ 5.079(a)). If the contract was neither recorded nor converted, the seller must transfer recorded legal title "not later than the 30th day after the date the seller receives the purchaser's final payment." A seller who violates that owes liquidated damages of "$250 a day for each day the seller fails to transfer the title . . . during the period that begins the 31st day and ends the 90th day after" final payment, "$500 a day" thereafter, plus reasonable attorney's fees (§ 5.079(b)).

**Agents are the seller.** Property Code § 5.080 provides: "For purposes of this subchapter, a disclosure required by this subchapter that is made by a seller's agent is a disclosure made by the seller." A Texas license holder assisting on an executory contract is making the seller's disclosures, with the seller's consequences.

**Improper platting.** A purchaser may cancel and rescind at any time on learning the seller has not properly subdivided or platted the property in accordance with state and local law, by signed written notice delivered in person or sent by telegram or certified or registered mail, return receipt requested (§ 5.083(a)). The seller then has ten days either to give written notice of intent to plat properly or to return all payments and reimburse taxes the purchaser paid and the value of improvements the purchaser made (§ 5.083(b)); the purchaser loses the cancellation right if the seller properly plats and gives notice within ninety days (§ 5.083(c)); and the seller "may not terminate the purchaser's possession . . . before the seller pays the purchaser any money to which the purchaser is entitled" (§ 5.083(d)).

**Self-help deduction.** "If a seller is liable to a purchaser under this subchapter, the purchaser, without taking judicial action, may deduct the amount owed to the purchaser by the seller from any amounts owed to the seller by the purchaser under the terms of an executory contract" (§ 5.084).

**Equity sharing.**

**Equity sharing** is an arrangement in which one party supplies capital — typically the down payment — and another occupies the property, with the parties dividing appreciation on sale or refinance according to their agreement. It is a matter of private contract and of how title and the occupancy arrangement are documented. **No source held for this course addresses equity sharing**, and this module therefore states no rule about it. Two cautions belong here nonetheless, both derived from provisions already read. First, if the arrangement is structured so that the occupant holds a purchase right combined with a residential lease, Property Code § 5.062(a)(2) may make it an executory contract with all of subchapter D's duties. Second, a license holder participating as an investor must remember Occupations Code § 1101.652(b)(16), which makes it a ground for discipline to act "in a dual capacity as broker and undisclosed principal in a real estate transaction."

**Assignments and option sales.**

Texas requires disclosure when someone sells a position rather than a property. Property Code § 5.0205 provides that "[b]efore entering into a contract to sell an option or assign an interest in a contract to purchase real property, a person must disclose in writing to: (1) any potential buyer that the person is selling only an option or assigning an interest in a contract and that the person does not have legal title to the real property; and (2) the owner of the real property that the person intends to sell an option or assign an interest in a contract." Both directions of disclosure are required — to the buyer of the position and to the owner of the property.

**Buydowns.**

A **buydown** is an arrangement in which a party — most often the seller, sometimes a builder or the borrower — pays money at closing to reduce the borrower's interest rate, either for the loan's full term (a permanent buydown) or for an initial period (a temporary buydown). The funds are typically placed with the lender and applied to make up the difference between what the borrower pays and what the note requires.

**Worked example — illustrative figures only.** Take the $250,000 thirty-year loan at a note rate of 6% used earlier, with a monthly payment of $1,498.88. A temporary buydown reduces the borrower's effective rate to 4% in year one and 5% in year two.

Payment at 4% (i = 0.00333333, n = 360): (1.00333333)^360 ≈ 3.313483, so the denominator is 1 − 0.301796 = 0.698204, and the payment is $833.33 ÷ 0.698204 ≈ **$1,193.54**.

Payment at 5% (i = 0.00416667, n = 360): (1.00416667)^360 ≈ 4.467708, so the denominator is 1 − 0.223828 = 0.776172, and the payment is $1,041.67 ÷ 0.776172 ≈ **$1,342.06**.

Year one subsidy: ($1,498.88 − $1,193.54) × 12 = $305.34 × 12 = **$3,664.08**.
Year two subsidy: ($1,498.88 − $1,342.06) × 12 = $156.82 × 12 = **$1,881.84**.
Total buydown funds required: $3,664.08 + $1,881.84 = **$5,545.92**.

Three teaching points follow. First, the note rate never changed: the borrower's loan is a 6% loan, and in year three the payment becomes $1,498.88. Second, the buydown is not free — someone deposited $5,545.92 — and if a seller pays it, that money came out of the seller's proceeds exactly as a price reduction would have. Third, a buyer who can only afford the first-year payment is a buyer who cannot afford this loan; the buydown moves a problem, it does not solve one. Whether and how the underwriter may qualify a borrower on a bought-down payment is a matter of the applicable underwriting standards, which this course holds no source for.

## Unit: Financing Legislation

Four bodies of federal law dominate residential mortgage practice, and a Texas license holder needs to know what each one is *for*, because the purposes explain the rules.

**The Truth in Lending Act.**

TILA is title I of the Consumer Credit Protection Act, Pub. L. 90–321 (May 29, 1968), and Congress stated its purpose in 15 U.S.C. § 1601(a):

> "The Congress finds that economic stabilization would be enhanced and the competition among the various financial institutions and other firms engaged in the extension of consumer credit would be strengthened by the informed use of credit. The informed use of credit results from an awareness of the cost thereof by consumers. It is the purpose of this subchapter to assure a meaningful disclosure of credit terms so that the consumer will be able to compare more readily the various credit terms available to him and avoid the uninformed use of credit, and to protect the consumer against inaccurate and unfair credit billing and credit card practices."

Every element of that sentence is doing work. TILA's mechanism is **disclosure**, not price control: Congress did not cap what credit may cost, it required that the cost be disclosed meaningfully. The stated end is **comparison** — "compare more readily the various credit terms available" — which tells you the disclosures are designed to be read side by side against a competitor's. And the justification is competitive and macroeconomic: informed borrowers make markets work, and working markets stabilize the economy.

Section 1601(b) extends the same logic to consumer leases of personal property, added by the Consumer Leasing Act of 1976, with the purpose of assuring meaningful disclosure of lease terms "so as to enable the lessee to compare more readily the various lease terms available to him, limit balloon payments in consumer leasing, enable comparison of lease terms with credit terms where appropriate, and to assure meaningful and accurate disclosures of lease terms in advertisements."

Congress has amended TILA repeatedly, and the statutory notes to § 1601 name the amendments in ways that map the history of consumer credit regulation: the Fair Credit Billing Act (Pub. L. 93–495, title III), the Truth in Lending Simplification and Reform Act (Pub. L. 96–221, title VI), the Home Equity Loan Consumer Protection Act of 1988 (Pub. L. 100–709), the Home Ownership and Equity Protection Act of 1994 (Pub. L. 103–325, title I, subtitle B), the Mortgage Disclosure Improvement Act of 2008 (Pub. L. 110–289, div. B, title V), and the Mortgage Reform and Anti-Predatory Lending Act (Pub. L. 111–203, title XIV, § 1400(a)).

**Regulation Z: the vocabulary TILA runs on.**

TILA is implemented by Regulation Z, 12 C.F.R. part 1026, and three sections of it are held and were read for this module. Start with the definitions in 12 C.F.R. § 1026.2(a), because they decide the reach of everything else and because Texas incorporates two of them by reference.

*Consumer credit* means "credit offered or extended to a consumer primarily for personal, family, or household purposes" (§ 1026.2(a)(12)) — the same personal/family/household line Finance Code § 343.001(2) draws for a Texas home loan. *Dwelling* means "a residential structure that contains one to four units, whether or not that structure is attached to real property," and "includes an individual condominium unit, cooperative unit, mobile home, and trailer, if it is used as a residence" (§ 1026.2(a)(19)); note that a manufactured home used as a residence is a dwelling for Regulation Z purposes whether or not it has been attached to land, which is why the manufactured-home lending in Finance Code chapter 343 and in Texas Constitution art. XVI, § 50(a)(8) is within the federal scheme too. *Residential mortgage transaction* — the term Finance Code § 343.201(1)(E) borrows — means "a transaction in which a mortgage, deed of trust, purchase money security interest arising under an installment sales contract, or equivalent consensual security interest is created or retained in the consumer's principal dwelling to finance the acquisition or initial construction of that dwelling" (§ 1026.2(a)(24)). That is a purchase-money or initial-construction transaction, not a refinance and not an equity loan.

Two more definitions are worth carrying. *Consummation* means "the time that a consumer becomes contractually obligated on a credit transaction" (§ 1026.2(a)(13)) — not funding, not recording, not the first payment. And *business day* means "a day on which the creditor's offices are open to the public for carrying on substantially all of its business functions," except that for rescission under §§ 1026.15 and 1026.23 and for the enumerated disclosure-timing rules it means "all calendar days except Sundays and the legal public holidays specified in 5 U.S.C. 6103(a)" (§ 1026.2(a)(6)). Regulation Z uses two different business days depending on which rule is being counted, and a license holder who assumes one definition governs everywhere will count a deadline wrong.

Section 1026.2(a)(17)(v) also draws the line for who is a *creditor* at all: a person "regularly extends consumer credit only if it extended credit (other than credit subject to the requirements of § 1026.32) more than 25 times (or more than 5 times for transactions secured by a dwelling) in the preceding calendar year," but "[a] person regularly extends consumer credit if, in any 12-month period, the person originates more than one credit extension that is subject to the requirements of § 1026.32 or one or more such credit extensions through a mortgage broker." Read that second sentence carefully. For high-cost mortgages the threshold collapses almost to zero: two such loans in a year, or one made through a mortgage broker, makes the maker a creditor with all of TILA's obligations. That rule is aimed squarely at the private lender described in the *Sources of Real Estate Financing* unit who assumes that occasional lending is unregulated lending.

**Regulation Z § 1026.32: high-cost mortgages.**

Section 1026.32 is the successor to the Home Ownership and Equity Protection Act of 1994 named in TILA's own amendment notes, and it does two things: it defines coverage, and it forbids certain terms in a covered loan.

*Coverage.* The section applies to "any consumer credit transaction that is secured by the consumer's principal dwelling," subject to stated exemptions, "in which" any one of three tests is met (§ 1026.32(a)(1)). The first is a rate test: the annual percentage rate "will exceed the average prime offer rate, as defined in § 1026.35(a)(2), for a comparable transaction by more than: (A) 6.5 percentage points for a first-lien transaction," other than as described in (B); "(B) 8.5 percentage points for a first-lien transaction if the dwelling is personal property and the loan amount is less than $50,000; or (C) 8.5 percentage points for a subordinate-lien transaction" (§ 1026.32(a)(1)(i)). The second is a points-and-fees test: total points and fees "will exceed: (A) 5 percent of the total loan amount for a transaction with a loan amount of $20,000 or more; the $20,000 figure shall be adjusted annually on January 1 by the annual percentage change in the Consumer Price Index that was reported on the preceding June 1; or (B) The lesser of 8 percent of the total loan amount or $1,000 for a transaction with a loan amount of less than $20,000," with those two figures likewise CPI-adjusted annually (§ 1026.32(a)(1)(ii)). The third is a prepayment-penalty test: coverage attaches if, "[u]nder the terms of the loan contract or open-end credit agreement, the creditor can charge a prepayment penalty . . . more than 36 months after consummation or account opening, or prepayment penalties that can exceed, in total, more than 2 percent of the amount prepaid" (§ 1026.32(a)(1)(iii)).

Three points about those tests. The rate test is *relative*, not absolute: it compares the loan's APR to the average prime offer rate for a comparable transaction, so the trigger rate moves as the market moves and there is no fixed percentage to memorize. The dollar figures in the points-and-fees test are indexed and change every January 1, which is why this module tells you where the rule is rather than what this year's number is. And the third test means a loan can become a high-cost mortgage on the strength of a prepayment term alone, regardless of its rate or its fees.

Four transactions are exempted outright: "(i) A reverse mortgage transaction subject to § 1026.33; (ii) A transaction to finance the initial construction of a dwelling; (iii) A transaction originated by a Housing Finance Agency, where the Housing Finance Agency is the creditor for the transaction; or (iv) A transaction originated pursuant to the United States Department of Agriculture's Rural Development Section 502 Direct Loan Program" (§ 1026.32(a)(2)). Exemption (iii) is the one to notice in a Texas assistance-program context: a loan the housing finance agency itself makes is outside the high-cost regime.

*Disclosure.* In a covered mortgage the creditor must disclose, "in conspicuous type size," a notice in these words: "You are not required to complete this agreement merely because you have received these disclosures or have signed a loan application. If you obtain this loan, the lender will have a mortgage on your home. You could lose your home, and any money you have put into it, if you do not meet your obligations under the loan" (§ 1026.32(c)(1)), together with the annual percentage rate, the regular payment and any balloon payment, a variable-rate warning stating the maximum monthly payment, and the amount borrowed or credit limit (§ 1026.32(c)(2)–(5)).

*Prohibited terms.* Section 1026.32(d) then provides that "[a] high-cost mortgage shall not include the following terms," and lists them: a balloon payment — "a payment schedule with a payment that is more than two times a regular periodic payment" — excepted for a schedule "adjusted to the seasonal or irregular income of the consumer," for "a loan with maturity of 12 months or less, if the purpose of the loan is a 'bridge' loan connected with the acquisition or construction of a dwelling intended to become the consumer's principal dwelling," and for loans meeting stated § 1026.43 criteria (§ 1026.32(d)(1)); negative amortization — "[a] payment schedule with regular periodic payments that cause the principal balance to increase" (§ 1026.32(d)(2)); advance payments consolidating more than two periodic payments and paying them from the proceeds (§ 1026.32(d)(3)); "[a]n increase in the interest rate after default" (§ 1026.32(d)(4)); rebates of interest computed by a method less favorable than the actuarial method (§ 1026.32(d)(5)); "[a] prepayment penalty" (§ 1026.32(d)(6)); and a demand feature permitting acceleration and termination in advance of maturity, except for consumer fraud or material misrepresentation, failure to meet the repayment terms, or "any action or inaction by the consumer that adversely affects the creditor's security for the loan" (§ 1026.32(d)(8)).

**How the Texas and federal high-cost regimes fit together.**

Put § 1026.32(d) beside Finance Code chapter 343, subchapter C, taught in the *Texas Loan Programs* unit, and the relationship becomes visible.

They *overlap* on three restrictions. Both forbid balloon payments on a covered loan, and both carve out seasonal or irregular income and bridge loans — but the lines are drawn differently. The federal rule bars any payment "more than two times a regular periodic payment," full stop; Finance Code § 343.202 bars a payment "more than twice as large as the average of earlier scheduled monthly payments, unless the balloon payment becomes due not less than 60 months after the date of the loan." Texas permits a late balloon; the federal rule does not condition the prohibition on timing at all. Both forbid negative amortization in nearly identical words — compare § 1026.32(d)(2) with Finance Code § 343.203 — though Texas adds its own exception for negative amortization arising from "a temporary forbearance, bridge loan, or restructure sought by the borrower." And both forbid prepayment penalties: § 1026.32(d)(6) as a prohibited term, Finance Code § 343.205 as a flat statutory bar.

They *diverge* elsewhere. Texas adds a repayment-ability rule (§ 343.204) and a prohibition on charging for a product the borrower never receives (§ 343.206) that have no counterpart in the held federal text. The federal rule adds a prohibition on post-default rate increases, on advance-payment consolidation, on unfavorable interest rebates, and on demand features, none of which appears in subchapter C.

And they *nest*. Because Finance Code § 343.201(1)(E) defines a Texas high-cost home loan partly as "a credit transaction described by 12 C.F.R. Section 1026.32, as amended," a loan generally has to clear the federal bar before the Texas restrictions attach — and Texas then narrows further with its own principal-amount ceiling. The practical consequence for a license holder is simple to state and easy to get backwards: a Texas high-cost home loan is a subset, so a loan can be a federal high-cost mortgage and still fall outside chapter 343 subchapter C, but a Texas high-cost home loan will carry the federal restrictions too. When both regimes reach the same loan, the lender must satisfy the stricter of the two on each term, because neither displaces the other.

One caution about the boundary of what is held. The held Regulation Z excerpt is §§ 1026.2, 1026.32, and 1026.33 only. **This course holds no source stating Regulation Z's ability-to-repay and qualified mortgage rules (§ 1026.43), its loan originator compensation rules (§ 1026.36), its higher-priced mortgage loan rules (§ 1026.35), its right-of-rescission rules (§ 1026.23), or the additional restrictions on high-cost mortgages in § 1026.34**, all of which are cross-referenced inside the sections that are held. This module states nothing about any of them.

**Regulation Z § 1026.33: reverse mortgages.**

The federal counterpart to the Texas constitutional reverse-mortgage regime is short and worth reading against it. Regulation Z defines a "reverse mortgage transaction" as "a nonrecourse consumer credit obligation in which: (1) A mortgage, deed of trust, or equivalent consensual security interest securing one or more advances is created in the consumer's principal dwelling; and (2) Any principal, interest, or shared appreciation or equity is due and payable (other than in the case of default) only after: (i) The consumer dies; (ii) The dwelling is transferred; or (iii) The consumer ceases to occupy the dwelling as a principal dwelling" (§ 1026.33(a)).

Lay that beside Texas Constitution art. XVI, § 50(k). The two agree on the essentials — non-recourse, secured by the principal dwelling, no payment due until death, transfer, or the end of occupancy. They differ in what each adds. The federal definition states no borrower age; the Texas provision requires that the borrower or the borrower's spouse be "62 years or older" (§ 50(k)(2)). The federal rule states no counseling requirement; Texas requires written attestation of counseling "completed not earlier than the 180th day nor later than the 5th day before" closing (§ 50(k)(8)). Conversely, the federal rule prescribes a disclosure the Texas provision does not: a statement "that the consumer is not obligated to complete the reverse mortgage transaction merely because the consumer has received the disclosures required by this section or has signed an application for a reverse mortgage loan," a good-faith projection of the total cost of credit "expressed as a table of 'total annual loan cost rates,'" an itemization of loan terms, charges, "the age of the youngest borrower and the appraised property value," and an explanation of the table (§ 1026.33(b)(1)–(4)).

The total annual loan cost table is a genuinely useful idea and worth understanding in outline. The projection must reflect all costs and charges to the consumer, all advances to and for the consumer's benefit, any shared appreciation or equity the creditor is contractually entitled to receive, and any limitation on the consumer's liability such as non-recourse limits (§ 1026.33(c)(1)–(4)). It must then be computed at each of three assumed annual appreciation rates — "(i) 0 percent. (ii) 4 percent. (iii) 8 percent" (§ 1026.33(c)(5)) — and over each of three assumed loan periods: two years; "[t]he actuarial life expectancy of the consumer to become obligated on the reverse mortgage transaction," or of the youngest consumer where there are several; and that life expectancy "multiplied by a factor of 1.4 and rounded to the nearest full year" (§ 1026.33(c)(6)(i)). Because a reverse mortgage's real cost depends on how long the borrower stays and how the property appreciates, a single number would be meaningless; the rule requires a grid instead.

Two cross-references you have already met now make sense. Regulation X exempts reverse mortgage transactions from the special information booklet requirement (12 C.F.R. § 1024.6(a)(3)) and exempts transactions "subject to 12 C.F.R. § 1026.33(a)" from the homeownership counseling list requirement (12 C.F.R. § 1024.20(c)(1)) — and § 1026.32(a)(2)(i) exempts a reverse mortgage subject to § 1026.33 from the high-cost mortgage rules. The federal scheme routes reverse mortgages out of the general disclosure regime and into their own.

**The Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act.**

RESPA is Pub. L. 93–533 (Dec. 22, 1974), and its findings and purposes are in 12 U.S.C. § 2601. Congress found "that significant reforms in the real estate settlement process are needed to insure that consumers throughout the Nation are provided with greater and more timely information on the nature and costs of the settlement process and are protected from unnecessarily high settlement charges caused by certain abusive practices that have developed in some areas of the country" (§ 2601(a)). Section 2601(b) states four purposes: to effect changes in the residential settlement process resulting

> "(1) in more effective advance disclosure to home buyers and sellers of settlement costs;
> (2) in the elimination of kickbacks or referral fees that tend to increase unnecessarily the costs of certain settlement services;
> (3) in a reduction in the amounts home buyers are required to place in escrow accounts established to insure the payment of real estate taxes and insurance; and
> (4) in significant reform and modernization of local recordkeeping of land title information."

Notice how different that is from TILA. TILA is about the *loan*; RESPA is about the *settlement*. TILA's target is uninformed borrowing; RESPA's targets are opaque settlement charges, kickbacks, bloated escrows, and bad land records. Purpose (2) is the one that will govern your own conduct.

**The uniform settlement statement and the integrated disclosures.**

Section 2603(a) directs that "[t]he Bureau shall publish a single, integrated disclosure for mortgage loan transactions (including real estate settlement cost statements) which includes the disclosure requirements of this section and section 2604 of this title, in conjunction with the disclosure requirements of the Truth in Lending Act . . . that, taken together, may apply to a transaction that is subject to both or either provisions of law." The purpose of that model disclosure "shall be to facilitate compliance with the disclosure requirements of this chapter and the Truth in Lending Act, and to aid the borrower or lessee in understanding the transaction by utilizing readily understandable language to simplify the technical nature of the disclosures." The forms "shall conspicuously and clearly itemize all charges imposed upon the borrower and all charges imposed upon the seller in connection with the settlement and shall indicate whether any title insurance premium included in such charges covers or insures the lender's interest in the property, the borrower's interest, or both."

That is the statutory basis for the integration of TILA and RESPA disclosure into a single set of forms. In current practice those forms are known as the **Loan Estimate**, given early in the process, and the **Closing Disclosure**, given before closing. **This course holds no source that prints those forms or states their timing rules.** The integrated-disclosure rules sit in Regulation Z at 12 C.F.R. §§ 1026.19, 1026.37, and 1026.38, and the Regulation Z sections held for this module are §§ 1026.2, 1026.32, and 1026.33 only — none of which prescribes a Loan Estimate or a Closing Disclosure. This module therefore states neither their contents nor their deadlines; verify both against the Bureau's current rule before telling a client anything specific. Note in passing that § 1026.2(a)(6) defines "business day" differently for the § 1026.19 timing rules than for general purposes, which is one more reason not to count those deadlines from memory.

Section 2603(b) governs availability: the forms "shall be completed and made available for inspection by the borrower at or before settlement by the person conducting the settlement," subject to exemptions the Bureau may allow and to the borrower's waiver, and "[u]pon the request of the borrower to inspect the forms . . . during the business day immediately preceding the day of settlement, the person who will conduct the settlement shall permit the borrower to inspect those items which are known to such person during such preceding day." Section 2603(c) permits the standard form to disclose, where an appraisal was coordinated by an appraisal management company, the fee paid directly to the appraiser and the administration fee charged by the company.

Regulation X preserves the older good faith estimate and HUD-1 architecture for transactions where it still governs: the GFE within three business days of application (12 C.F.R. § 1024.7(a)(1)); the HUD-1 or HUD-1A settlement statement, whose use "is exempted for open-end lines of credit (home-equity plans) covered by the Truth in Lending Act and Regulation Z" (12 C.F.R. § 1024.8(a)); the borrower's right to inspect the completed statement "during the business day immediately preceding settlement" (12 C.F.R. § 1024.10(a)); and a five-year retention rule (12 C.F.R. § 1024.10(e)). Regulation X also forbids charging for the paperwork: "No fee shall be imposed or charge made upon any other person, as a part of settlement costs or otherwise, by a lender . . . or by a servicer . . . for or on account of the preparation and distribution of the HUD-1 or HUD-1A settlement statement, escrow account statements required pursuant to section 10 of RESPA (12 U.S.C. 2609), or statements required by the Truth in Lending Act" (12 C.F.R. § 1024.12).

**RESPA § 2607: kickbacks and unearned fees.**

This is the provision most likely to end a Texas license holder's career, and it should be read word for word.

> "(a) Business referrals. No person shall give and no person shall accept any fee, kickback, or thing of value pursuant to any agreement or understanding, oral or otherwise, that business incident to or a part of a real estate settlement service involving a federally related mortgage loan shall be referred to any person.
>
> (b) Splitting charges. No person shall give and no person shall accept any portion, split, or percentage of any charge made or received for the rendering of a real estate settlement service in connection with a transaction involving a federally related mortgage loan other than for services actually performed."
> — 12 U.S.C. § 2607(a), (b)

Four features of subsection (a) matter. It reaches both sides — giving and accepting. It is not limited to money: "any fee, kickback, or thing of value." The agreement need not be written: "oral or otherwise." And Regulation X confirms that no formality is needed at all: "An agreement or understanding for the referral of business . . . need not be written or verbalized but may be established by a practice, pattern or course of conduct. When a thing of value is received repeatedly and is connected in any way with the volume or value of the business referred, the receipt of the thing of value is evidence that it is made pursuant to an agreement or understanding for the referral of business" (12 C.F.R. § 1024.14(e)).

Regulation X's definition of "thing of value" should dispel any idea that only cash is covered. It "includes, without limitation, monies, things, discounts, salaries, commissions, fees, duplicate payments of a charge, stock, dividends, distributions of partnership profits, franchise royalties, credits representing monies that may be paid at a future date, the opportunity to participate in a money-making program, retained or increased earnings, increased equity in a parent or subsidiary entity, special bank deposits or accounts, special or unusual banking terms, services of all types at special or free rates, sales or rentals at special prices or rates, lease or rental payments based in whole or in part on the amount of business referred, trips and payment of another person's expenses, or reduction in credit against an existing obligation," and "payment" is "synonymous with the giving or receiving of any 'thing of value' and does not require transfer of money" (12 C.F.R. § 1024.14(d)).

A "referral" is likewise broad: it "includes any oral or written action directed to a person which has the effect of affirmatively influencing the selection by any person of a provider of a settlement service," and "also occurs whenever a person paying for a settlement service . . . is required to use a particular provider" (12 C.F.R. § 1024.14(f)). And Regulation X states flatly: "Any referral of a settlement service is not a compensable service" (12 C.F.R. § 1024.14(b)).

Subsection (c) then lists what § 2607 does *not* prohibit. Nothing in the section prohibits "(1) the payment of a fee (A) to attorneys at law for services actually rendered or (B) by a title company to its duly appointed agent for services actually performed in the issuance of a policy of title insurance or (C) by a lender to its duly appointed agent for services actually performed in the making of a loan, (2) the payment to any person of a bona fide salary or compensation or other payment for goods or facilities actually furnished or for services actually performed, (3) payments pursuant to cooperative brokerage and referral arrangements or agreements between real estate agents and brokers, (4) affiliated business arrangements" meeting stated conditions, or "(5) such other payments or classes of payments or other transfers as are specified in regulations prescribed by the Bureau." Regulation X adds to that list "[n]ormal promotional and educational activities that are not conditioned on the referral of business and that do not involve the defraying of expenses that otherwise would be incurred by persons in a position to refer settlement services" and "[a]n employer's payment to its own employees for any referral activities" (12 C.F.R. § 1024.14(g)(1)(vi), (vii)).

Paragraph (3) — cooperative brokerage — is the exception Texas license holders live on, and Regulation X narrows it precisely: "The statutory exemption restated in this paragraph refers only to fee divisions within real estate brokerage arrangements when all parties are acting in a real estate brokerage capacity, and has no applicability to any fee arrangements between real estate brokers and mortgage brokers or between mortgage brokers" (12 C.F.R. § 1024.14(g)(1)(v)). A listing broker splitting a commission with a cooperating broker is squarely inside the exception. A real estate broker taking a payment from a mortgage broker is squarely outside it.

The **affiliated business arrangement** exception in § 2607(c)(4) requires three things: a written disclosure to the person being referred of the existence of the arrangement, with a written estimate of the charge or range of charges, delivered at or before the time of the referral (with special timing rules for telephone referrals and lender referrals); that "such person is not required to use any particular provider of settlement services"; and that "the only thing of value that is received from the arrangement, other than the payments permitted under this subsection, is a return on the ownership interest or franchise relationship." Regulation X implements those conditions in 12 C.F.R. § 1024.15(b)(1)–(3) and requires the disclosure to be "provided on a separate piece of paper."

Regulation X also addresses the arrangement where a person in a position to refer takes a second fee for a second service: "When a person in a position to refer settlement service business, such as an attorney, mortgage lender, real estate broker or agent, or developer or builder, receives a payment for providing additional settlement services as part of a real estate transaction, such payment must be for services that are actual, necessary and distinct from the primary services provided by such person" (12 C.F.R. § 1024.14(g)(3)).

The penalties are severe. "Any person or persons who violate the provisions of this section shall be fined not more than $10,000 or imprisoned for not more than one year, or both" (§ 2607(d)(1)). Violators "shall be jointly and severally liable to the person or persons charged for the settlement service involved in the violation in an amount equal to three times the amount of any charge paid for such settlement service" (§ 2607(d)(2)) — treble damages. The Bureau, the Secretary, or a state attorney general or insurance commissioner may sue to enjoin violations, with the Bureau having primary enforcement authority subject to stated limits (§ 2607(d)(4)); a prevailing party in a private action may be awarded costs and reasonable attorney's fees (§ 2607(d)(5)); and, importantly for a state like Texas, "[n]o provision of State law or regulation that imposes more stringent limitations on affiliated business arrangements shall be construed as being inconsistent with this section" (§ 2607(d)(6)). Section 2607(d)(3) supplies one narrow defense, for an unintentional violation of the affiliated-business disclosure requirement resulting from a bona fide error despite reasonably adapted procedures — and Regulation X adds that "[a]n error of legal judgment with respect to a person's obligations under RESPA is not a bona fide error" (12 C.F.R. § 1024.15(b)(1)(iii)).

Texas law layers its own duties on top. Occupations Code § 1101.652(b)(13) makes it a ground for suspension or revocation if a license holder "accepts, receives, or charges an undisclosed commission, rebate, or direct profit on an expenditure made for a principal"; § 1101.652(b)(8) reaches a license holder who "receives compensation from more than one party to a real estate transaction without the full knowledge and consent of all parties to the transaction"; and § 1101.652(b)(11) reaches one who "pays a commission or a fee to or divides a commission or a fee with a person other than a license holder or a real estate broker or sales agent licensed in another state for compensation for services as a real estate agent." A Texas license holder facing a proposed payment arrangement therefore has two questions to answer, not one: is it lawful under § 2607, and is it consistent with chapter 1101?

**The Equal Credit Opportunity Act.**

ECOA is title VII of the Consumer Credit Protection Act, added by Pub. L. 94–239, § 1(b) (Mar. 23, 1976), and its prohibitions, permitted inquiries, adverse-action rules, and appraisal-copy right in 15 U.S.C. § 1691 were set out in full in the *Application for Credit* unit. Read alongside TILA and RESPA, its place in the scheme is clear: TILA governs what the borrower must be told about the cost of credit, RESPA governs the settlement process and the integrity of settlement-service payments, and ECOA governs whether the borrower may be considered at all and on what grounds.

### Applied exercise: A referral-fee proposal

A mortgage company in Frisco proposes the following to your Texas brokerage. For every buyer client your agents send who closes a loan with the company, the company will pay the brokerage $400, described in the proposed written agreement as a "marketing services fee." The brokerage would place the company's logo on its website. No agent would perform any work for the company beyond making the introduction. The buyers would be told nothing about the payment.

1. Apply 12 U.S.C. § 2607(a) to the proposal. Identify the "thing of value," the referral, and the agreement or understanding, quoting the statutory language for each.
2. The mortgage company argues the payment is for marketing, not referrals. Apply 12 U.S.C. § 2607(b) and 12 C.F.R. § 1024.14(g)(3) and state what would have to be true for a marketing payment to be lawful here.
3. Could the arrangement be restructured as an affiliated business arrangement under § 2607(c)(4)? List the three conditions the statute imposes and state which of them this proposal, as written, fails.
4. Independent of RESPA, identify the Texas Occupations Code provision that the undisclosed $400 would implicate, and quote it.

### Module recap

This module traced a single dollar of mortgage money from the institution that originates it to the Texas closing table and out into the secondary market, and then traced the law that governs every step of that journey.

It began with the three functions the residential mortgage business has separated — origination, funding, and servicing — and with the Texas statute that names the three kinds of originator, Finance Code § 343.105(a): a lender, a mortgage banker, and a licensed mortgage broker. It explained why those functions separate: because a secondary market exists to buy mortgage paper, and because the price of admission to that market is standardization, a fact the Texas Legislature itself acknowledged when it wrote a consumer-protection threshold around a loan limit that a secondary-market enterprise establishes and adjusts (Finance Code § 343.201(1)(C)).

From there the module moved to the point where a person becomes a file. It set out what the Equal Credit Opportunity Act forbids a creditor to consider under 15 U.S.C. § 1691(a), what it expressly permits under § 1691(b), what "adverse action" means and what a statement of reasons must contain under § 1691(d), and the applicant's right to appraisal copies under § 1691(e). It laid alongside those the delivery duties of Regulation X and the Texas notices in Finance Code §§ 343.104 and 343.105.

It then made the arithmetic concrete: a $250,000 loan at 6% over thirty years produces a payment of $1,498.88, an opening month in which $1,250.00 of that payment is interest and $248.88 is principal, and total interest of $289,596.80 over the term — against $129,735.20 if the same loan runs fifteen years. It showed how a payment smaller than accruing interest produces negative amortization, and how Texas answers that structure in Finance Code § 343.203, alongside the balloon, repayment-ability, prepayment-penalty, and unearned-charge rules of §§ 343.202 and 343.204 through 343.206.

It taught the Texas Constitution, article XVI, section 50 from the constitutional text: the protection of the homestead from forced sale for all debts except the eight enumerated categories, subsection (c)'s rule that no lien on a homestead is ever valid unless it secures a debt the section describes, and the home equity conditions of section 50(a)(6) — the voluntary lien created by written agreement with the consent of each owner and each owner's spouse, the eighty percent of fair market value limit measured against all recorded encumbrances, the non-recourse character, foreclosure only by court order, the two percent cap on fees to originate, evaluate, maintain, record, insure, or service the loan and the four charges excluded from it, one home equity loan at a time, prepayment without penalty, no additional collateral, installments that at least cover accrued interest, the twelve-day and one-business-day and one-year closing clocks, closing only at the office of the lender, an attorney at law, or a title company, the authorized-lender list, the three-day rescission, the written fair-market-value acknowledgment, the section 50(g) notice, and the forfeiture of all principal and interest that follows an uncured failure to comply. It set the reverse-mortgage rules of section 50(a)(7) and (k) and the home equity line of credit rules of subsection (t) alongside them.

It distinguished conventional lending, FHA insurance, and the VA guaranty structurally, and it deliberately refused to state program figures the course holds no source for. It described the influence of monetary policy and secondary-market standards in economic rather than legal terms, and flagged that limitation. It taught, in detail and from the statute, the regime Texas imposes on executory contracts in Property Code chapter 5, subchapter D — the disclosures before signing, the fourteen-day cancellation right, the forbidden terms, the recording duty, the annual accounting, the thirty-day cure, and above all the equity protection of § 5.066 that converts forfeiture into a trustee's sale with surplus to the buyer. And it closed on the four federal statutes and on Regulation Z — the definitions in 12 C.F.R. § 1026.2 that Texas borrows, the three coverage tests and the prohibited terms for a high-cost mortgage in § 1026.32, and the reverse mortgage definition and total-annual-loan-cost disclosures in § 1026.33 — showing how the federal high-cost regime and Finance Code chapter 343, subchapter C overlap, diverge, and nest. It ended with the provision that will govern your own conduct most directly: 12 U.S.C. § 2607, which forbids giving or accepting any thing of value for the referral of settlement service business.

You should now be able to identify who originated, funded, and services a client's loan; explain why a loan gets sold and what changes when it does; compute a payment and an amortization schedule and show a client the cost of term and rate; recognize when a homestead transaction has crossed into article XVI, section 50 — and in particular when spousal consent, the eighty percent limit, or one of section 50's waiting periods is about to be overlooked; recognize when a Texas transaction has crossed into subchapter D and what that triggers; and evaluate a proposed compensation arrangement against both § 2607 and Occupations Code § 1101.652. Where this module told you a figure is not in a held source, take that seriously: the professional answer to a client's question about a program number is to find the current number, not to remember one.
