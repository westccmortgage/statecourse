# Texas Principles of Real Estate II — Module 09

## TREC Topic: Property Management

**Prescribed time: 120 minutes.** This module carries the three units the Commission's PRINS2-1 course
approval form places under *Property Management*, whose scope note is *"Licensing as a broker or
salesperson; Ethics of practice as a license holder."*

**Learning objectives.** After completing this module you will be able to: identify the functions a
Texas property manager performs, state from Occupations Code § 1101.002(1) which management acts are
acts of a broker, apply the exemptions in § 1101.005 to decide whether a particular person managing a
particular Texas property needs a license, and state the four trust-money grounds for discipline in
§ 1101.652(b)(9), (10), (30) and (31); identify the terms a management agreement must settle, explain
why § 1101.652(b)(12) carves property management contracts out of the definite-termination-date
requirement, compute effective gross income and net operating income from
gross potential rent, vacancy and collection loss and operating expenses, and connect the Property
Code ch. 92 repair duty to the design of a maintenance program; and distinguish the manager's
fiduciary obligation to the owner from the statutory duties that ch. 92 owes the tenant, describe the
asset types and career paths inside the management profession, and distinguish a voluntary
professional designation from a legal requirement.

**Every legal statement in this module comes from a source that was read for this course.** Where a
statement is practice rather than law, the module says so and cites nothing for it. Every dollar
figure and percentage in the worked examples is an illustration chosen to make the arithmetic
visible; none is a required, customary or standard figure.

## Unit: Property Management

A **property manager** operates real property on behalf of someone else. The owner keeps the title,
the equity and the risk; the manager supplies the time, the systems and the judgment. That sentence
explains almost everything that follows, including why Texas regulates the work at all: because the
manager acts *for another person* and does so *for compensation*, the manager stands in the position
the Real Estate License Act was written to police. Start with the work, because the law only makes
sense once you can see what a manager does in a week. The functions divide into seven streams.

**Leasing.** The manager markets vacant space, shows it, screens applicants against written criteria,
negotiates lease terms within the authority the owner has granted, and executes the lease. Leasing is
where the largest sums are set: a lease signed at forty dollars a month under market costs the owner
four hundred and eighty dollars a year for every year the tenant renews at that base.

**Rent collection.** The manager sets the collection calendar, receives payment, posts it, pursues
delinquency, and applies late charges where the lease permits them. Texas regulates those charges
directly: under Property Code § 92.019(a) a landlord may not collect a late fee unless notice of the
fee is in a written lease, the fee is reasonable, and "any portion of the tenant's rent has remained
unpaid two full days after the date the rent was originally due," and § 92.019(c) makes a violating
landlord liable for one hundred dollars, three times the fee collected, and reasonable attorney's
fees. A legacy lease form that charges a flat fee on day one creates that liability across the whole
portfolio at once.

**Maintenance.** The manager receives service requests, dispatches vendors, inspects the work and pays
the invoice. The legal spine is Property Code § 92.052(a), which requires a landlord to "make a
diligent effort to repair or remedy a condition" if the tenant specifies it in a notice to the person
or place where rent is normally paid, the tenant is not delinquent in rent when notice is given, and
the condition materially affects the physical health or safety of an ordinary tenant or arises from a
failure to keep a hot water device supplying water of at least 120 degrees Fahrenheit in good
operating condition. Note who receives that notice: *the place where rent is normally paid* — in a
managed property, the management office. The manager is the statutory intake point for the repair
duty, which is why intake discipline matters.

**Accounting.** The manager collects money that belongs to the owner, pays bills that belong to the
owner, holds deposits that belong to the tenant, and reconciles all of it. This function produces the
most license discipline, and it gets its own treatment below. **Reporting** renders periodic
statements to the owner — what came in, what went out, what is vacant, delinquent or broken.
**Tenant relations** means answering the phone, enforcing rules evenhandedly, and handling the
complaints that never appear in a budget line. **Compliance** keeps the property and the file inside
the law: fair housing in tenant selection and advertising, the ch. 92 duties in operations, and the
Occupations Code in the manager's own conduct.

**Whose property is it, and who is the landlord?**

Before the licensing question, settle a definitional one that trips people constantly. Property Code
§ 92.001(2) defines "landlord" as "the owner, lessor, or sublessor of a dwelling, but does not include
a manager or agent of the landlord unless the manager or agent purports to be the owner, lessor, or
sublessor in an oral or written lease." The manager is ordinarily *not* the landlord for purposes of
ch. 92, whose duties run to the landlord. But a manager who signs a lease as though the manager were
the lessor, rather than as agent for a disclosed owner, has pulled those duties onto itself. A common
error is to treat the signature block as clerical. It decides who the statute is talking about.

Section 92.201(a) reinforces the point from the other direction. A landlord must disclose to a tenant,
or to a government official acting in an official capacity, the name and a street or post office box
address of the holder of record title according to the county deed records, and, "if an entity located
off-site from the dwelling is primarily responsible for managing the dwelling, the name and street
address of the management company." Section 92.201(b) permits the disclosure in writing within seven
days of request, by continuous conspicuous posting within that period, or in the lease or written
rules given before any request; § 92.006(a) makes the duty non-waivable, and the remedies in
§ 92.205(a) run from a court order compelling disclosure to a judgment for one month's rent plus one
hundred dollars, costs and fees, and unilateral termination of the lease without a court proceeding.
Off-site fee management is the ordinary trigger.

**The license question in Texas**

Now the heart of the unit. **Does managing someone else's property in Texas require a real estate
license?** The answer is built from two sections read together.

Occupations Code § 1101.002(1)(A) defines a **broker** as "a person who, in exchange for a commission
or other valuable consideration or with the expectation of receiving a commission or other valuable
consideration, performs for another person one of the following acts," and then lists them. Four
matter to a manager:

- clause (i) — "sells, exchanges, purchases, or **leases** real estate";
- clause (iii) — "negotiates or attempts to negotiate the listing, sale, exchange, purchase, or
  **lease** of real estate";
- clause (iv) — "lists or offers, attempts, or agrees to list real estate for sale, **lease**, or
  exchange"; and
- clause (x) — "**controls the acceptance or deposit of rent from a resident of a single-family
  residential real property unit**."

Section 1101.002(5) then defines "real estate" as "any interest in real property, **including a
leasehold**, located in or outside this state." Leasing is not a lesser activity that falls outside
the Act. It is inside the definition of a broker, by name, four times over.

Three elements must all be present before an act is broker activity: it must be **for another
person**, it must be one of the **listed acts**, and it must be done **for compensation or with the
expectation of compensation**. Strip out any one and the definition is not met. An owner leasing the
owner's own duplex is not acting for another person; a friend who shows a vacant house expecting
nothing is missing consideration — though "other valuable consideration" is broad, and a reduction in
that friend's own rent is consideration.

Clause (x) deserves separate attention because students routinely miss it. It does not say "manages"
and it does not say "leases." It says *controls the acceptance or deposit of rent* from a resident of
a single-family residential real property unit. A person who never shows the house, never negotiates a
term and never signs anything — but who has the tenant's rent routed into an account that person
controls, for another owner, for compensation — has performed an act of a broker. The clause reaches
exactly the arrangement people assume is safely informal: collecting rent on a rent house for an
absentee owner.

Section 1101.351(a) supplies the prohibition: "Unless a person holds a license issued under this
chapter, the person may not: (1) act as or represent that the person is a broker or sales agent; or
(2) act as a residential rental locator." Section 1101.351(a-1) applies the same rule to business
entities, and § 1101.351(c) provides that a licensed sales agent "may not engage or attempt to engage
in real estate brokerage unless the sales agent is sponsored by a licensed broker and is acting for
that broker." A **residential rental locator** is defined in § 1101.002(6) as "a person who offers for
consideration to locate a unit in an apartment complex for lease to a prospective tenant," excluding
"an owner who offers to locate a unit in the owner's complex." Note finally § 1101.002(4): a "license
holder" is a broker or a sales agent licensed under the chapter. Chapter 1101 as read for this course
creates no separate property manager license — management activity falls under the broker and sales
agent licenses.

There is also a hard commercial consequence. Section 1101.806(b) provides that a person "may not
maintain an action to collect compensation for an act as a broker or sales agent that is performed in
this state" unless the person proves that the person was a license holder when the act was commenced,
or an attorney. An unlicensed manager who has to sue for the management fee cannot maintain the
suit.

**The exemptions in § 1101.005**

Section 1101.005 opens: "This chapter does not apply to:" and lists twelve categories. Two decide most
Texas management questions.

**Subdivision (7) — "an on-site manager of an apartment complex."** The resident manager who lives or
works at the complex, shows units in that complex, signs leases for that complex and takes rent at
that complex is outside the chapter. The words do the limiting: *on-site* and *an apartment complex*.
The exemption describes a person at the property, working that property. It is not a general
management exemption, and it does not by its terms describe a person operating scattered single-family
houses from a central office.

**Subdivision (8) — "an owner or the owner's employee who leases the owner's improved or unimproved
real estate."** An owner may lease the owner's own property without a license, and so may the owner's
**employee**. The load-bearing word is *employee*. The exemption is written for the owner's own staff,
not for an outside firm engaged to manage. A management company retained under a contract, paid a
percentage of collections, is not the owner's employee in any ordinary sense; it is a separate person
performing listed acts for another person for compensation.

The other subdivisions matter less often to a manager but you should know they exist: they cover a
Texas-licensed attorney; an attorney-in-fact conducting not more than three transactions annually; a
public official on official duties; a licensed auctioneer performing no other broker act; a person
acting under a court order, will or written trust instrument; a person employed by an owner selling
structures the owner erected in business; transactions in minerals, timber, other energy sources and
cemetery lots, "the lease or management of a hotel or motel," and sales under a power of sale in a
deed of trust; certain partners, managers and employees acting for a limited partnership or limited
liability company; and a person acting for a commercial signage entity. Note (9)(C) in particular:
hotel and motel management is outside the chapter altogether.

Put the pieces together and the Texas rule is workable. Managing another person's property for
compensation is generally brokerage activity requiring a license. The on-site apartment manager and
the owner's own employee are the two stated exemptions carrying most everyday exceptions; a person who
is neither, performing a listed act for another for compensation, needs a license and must be either a
broker or a sponsored sales agent.

One further provision links management to agency. Section 1101.557(c)(1) provides that "a license
holder who has the authority to bind a party to a lease or sale under a power of attorney or a
**property management agreement** is also a party to the lease or sale." A manager with signing
authority is not a bystander to the lease. Section 1101.803 adds the supervisory dimension: "A
licensed broker is liable to the commission, the public, and the broker's clients for any conduct
engaged in under this chapter by the broker or by a sales agent associated with or acting for the
broker." The broker owns the property management department's mistakes.

**Trust money**

A property manager handles other people's money continuously: rent belonging to the owner from the
moment it is received, security deposits belonging to the tenant subject to the landlord's right to
deduct, application deposits, and owner-funded repair reserves. Four grounds for discipline in
Occupations Code § 1101.652(b) govern that handling. The subsection applies to a license holder "while
engaged in real estate brokerage," and the Commission may suspend or revoke the license or take other
authorized disciplinary action if the license holder:

- **(9)** "fails within a reasonable time to properly account for or remit money that is received by
  the license holder and that belongs to another person";
- **(10)** "commingles money that belongs to another person with the license holder's own money";
- **(30)** "fails to deposit, within a reasonable time, money the license holder receives as escrow or
  trust funds in a real estate transaction: (A) in trust with a title company authorized to do business
  in this state; or (B) in a custodial, trust, or escrow account maintained for that purpose in a
  banking institution authorized to do business in this state"; and
- **(31)** "disburses money deposited in a custodial, trust, or escrow account, as provided in
  Subdivision (30), before the completion or termination of the real estate transaction."

Read them as a sequence describing the life of a dollar. Subdivision (30) says get it into the right
account promptly. Subdivision (10) says keep it apart from your own money while it sits there; the
violation is *commingling*, complete when the funds are mixed, with no need to show that a dollar was
stolen. Subdivision (31) says do not pay it out early. Subdivision (9) says account for it and remit
it to its owner within a reasonable time.

The practical failures are mundane. A manager who deposits owner rent into the firm's operating
account "temporarily" has commingled; so has one who leaves a cushion of the firm's own money in the
trust account to prevent overdrafts. A manager who funds payroll out of security deposits has both
commingled and disbursed early. A manager four months behind in owner statements because the
bookkeeper left has failed to account or remit within a reasonable time. None of these requires bad
intent: only some of these provisions are about dishonesty at all, and the rest are about
*administration*, which is where careless managers lose licenses.

Two neighboring provisions belong here. Section 1101.652(b)(13) reaches a license holder who "accepts,
receives, or charges an undisclosed commission, rebate, or direct profit on an expenditure made for a
principal" — the vendor kickback in statutory language — and § 1101.652(b)(24) reaches one who
"withholds from or inserts into a statement of account or invoice a statement that the license holder
knows makes the statement of account or invoice inaccurate in a material way." The owner statement is
not a courtesy document; falsifying it is independently sanctionable.

## Unit: Management Considerations

Everything in this unit follows from a single fact: the manager's authority is delegated, not
inherent. What the manager may do is what the owner has agreed the manager may do, bounded by what the
law permits either of them to do. The document that performs the delegation is the management
agreement, and it deserves to be read line by line before it is signed.

**The management agreement.** A workable Texas management agreement settles at least seven things.

**Identification and scope of authority.** Which property, and which acts the manager may perform
without further approval: sign leases, at what rent and term and with what concessions; approve
repairs up to what amount; sign checks; serve notices and file eviction suits, or refer them to
counsel. Authority left vague becomes authority litigated later, and § 1101.557(c)(1) treats a license
holder with authority to bind the owner to a lease under a management agreement as a party to that
lease.

**Term and termination.** Here Texas law contains a detail worth noticing. Occupations Code
§ 1101.652(b)(12) makes it a ground for discipline if a license holder "fails to specify a definite
termination date that is not subject to prior notice in a contract, **other than a contract to perform
property management services**, in which the license holder agrees to perform services for which a
license is required under this chapter." A listing agreement must state a definite termination date
not subject to prior notice; a property management contract is expressly carved out. The reason is
structural: management is an open-ended operating relationship, not a finite assignment to produce one
transaction, so the carve-out permits an evergreen term ending on notice. It does not require one, and
it does not relieve the agreement of saying how it ends, who may end it, what notice is required, and
what happens to funds, files, keys and pending leases. A common error is to read the carve-out as
saying management agreements need no termination provision at all. It says only that the *statutory
definite-date requirement* does not apply to them.

**Compensation.** Commonly a percentage of collected income, a fixed monthly amount, or a combination,
plus separate leasing or renewal fees. Whether the fee is earned on rent *billed* or rent *collected*
is not a small distinction; on collected income the manager shares the pain of a delinquency. State
whether the manager may retain late fees, application fees or vendor discounts, because
§ 1101.652(b)(13) makes an undisclosed direct profit on an expenditure made for a principal a ground
for discipline.

**Owner obligations.** Funding an operating reserve, paying taxes and premiums the manager does not,
responding to approval requests within a stated time, and disclosing known conditions. A manager who
cannot get the owner to fund a repair still faces the ch. 92 repair duty running against the owner
through the manager's office.

**Indemnification and insurance.** The manager acting within authority ordinarily expects indemnity
from the owner for claims arising from the property, carved out for the manager's own negligence; the
owner ordinarily expects to be named on the liability policy and to see evidence of the manager's
coverage. This is contract, not statute, and the course states no required allocation.

**Accounting and funds.** Which accounts exist, who signs on them, when statements are rendered and
funds remitted, and how deposits are held — written so that compliance with § 1101.652(b)(9), (10),
(30) and (31) follows automatically from following the contract.

**Fair housing and legal compliance.** A clause obligating both parties to comply with fair housing
law, and the owner not to instruct the manager otherwise — worth having because the instruction it
forbids is the one a manager is most likely to receive.

**Budgeting and the operating statement.** Financial reporting rests on one arithmetic chain.
**Gross potential rent (GPR)** is what the property would produce with every unit leased at market for
the whole period — a deliberately counterfactual ceiling. **Vacancy and collection loss** is what GPR
never becomes: rent lost to empty units, to days between tenants, to concessions, and to amounts
billed but never paid. **Effective gross income (EGI)** is GPR minus vacancy and collection loss.
**Operating expenses** are the recurring costs of running the property: taxes, insurance, common-area
utilities, repairs and maintenance, landscaping, on-site payroll, make-ready, administrative and
marketing costs, and the management fee. **Net operating income (NOI)** is EGI minus operating
expenses.

Two exclusions define NOI and cause most errors. Operating expenses **exclude debt service** —
mortgage principal and interest — because NOI measures what the property earns, not how a particular
owner financed it; two identical buildings, one owned free and clear and one heavily mortgaged, have
the same NOI. They also **exclude capital expenditures**: a new roof, a repaved lot or a full HVAC
replacement are capital items funded from reserves. Depreciation, an accounting allocation rather than
a cash cost, is likewise not an operating expense here.

**A worked example — illustration only.** A 24-unit apartment property in a Texas suburb: market rent
$1,250 per unit per month, vacancy and collection loss estimated at 7 percent, management fee 8
percent of effective gross income. Every figure is invented to make the arithmetic visible.

Gross potential rent: 24 units × $1,250 × 12 months = **$360,000**.

Vacancy and collection loss: $360,000 × 0.07 = **$25,200**.

Effective gross income: $360,000 − $25,200 = **$334,800**.

Operating expenses:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Property taxes | $62,000 |
| Insurance | $18,400 |
| Common-area utilities | $9,600 |
| Repairs and maintenance | $26,800 |
| Landscaping and pest control | $7,200 |
| Management fee (8% × $334,800) | $26,784 |
| On-site payroll | $21,000 |
| Make-ready and turnover | $12,500 |
| Administrative and marketing | $5,900 |
| **Total operating expenses** | **$190,184** |

Net operating income: $334,800 − $190,184 = **$144,616**.

Operating expense ratio: $190,184 ÷ $334,800 = 0.568, or **56.8 percent**.

Notice what the ratio does not tell you: nothing about whether the owner's loan payment can be made
from the remaining $144,616, because debt service was never in the statement. Notice too how the
management fee behaves — computed on effective gross income, it makes vacancy cost the manager as well
as the owner, since a rise from 7 percent to 12 percent would cut EGI by $18,000 and the fee by
$1,440. That is a business arrangement, not a legal requirement, and this course prescribes no rate.

**Setting rent from market data.** Rent is set by comparison, not by the owner's needs. The manager
collects current asking and, where available, effective rents from genuinely comparable properties —
comparable in submarket, age, condition, unit size and mix, amenities and utility structure — and
adjusts for differences. Convert concessions before comparing: one month free on a twelve-month lease
at $1,300 is an effective rent of $1,300 × 11 ÷ 12 = $1,191.67, not $1,300, and comparing an
advertised rate against an effective rate is the most common analytical error in leasing. Vacancy is
the more expensive pricing error: a unit vacant one month at $1,250 costs $1,250 that is never
recovered, while a unit leased $25 below market costs $300 over a twelve-month term.

**The maintenance program.** Property Code §§ 92.052 and 92.056 do not merely impose a duty; they set
the tempo of the maintenance operation. Under § 92.056(b) a landlord becomes liable to a tenant when
the tenant has given notice at the place rent is normally paid, the condition materially affects the
physical health or safety of an ordinary tenant, a subsequent written notice has followed a reasonable
time (or the first notice went by a tracked delivery method), the landlord has had a reasonable time
to repair and has not made a diligent effort, and the tenant was not delinquent in rent. Section
92.056(d) then supplies the number that drives everything: "there is a rebuttable presumption that
seven days is a reasonable time," rebuttable by considering the date of receipt, the severity and
nature of the condition, and the availability of materials, labor and utilities.

Seven days is a presumption, not a deadline, and severity moves it both ways. But a maintenance
program that cannot reliably close a health-and-safety work order inside a week is operating against
the presumption. That is why competent managers timestamp every service request at intake, route
health-and-safety items to a separate queue, and document dispatch and completion. Section 92.0561
shows the cost of failing: where the landlord is liable under § 92.056(b) and § 92.0561(d)'s further
conditions are met, the tenant may have the condition repaired and deduct the cost from a subsequent
rent payment, limited by § 92.0561(b) to "the amount of one month's rent under the lease or $500,
whichever is greater." Section 92.056(e) alternatively lets the tenant terminate the lease or pursue
judicial remedies under § 92.0563, and § 92.056(g) requires the lease to state these remedies "in
underlined or bold print."

Against that backdrop, structure the work in four categories. **Routine** maintenance is scheduled,
recurring work — grounds, common areas, filters, lighting. **Preventive** maintenance stops failures
before they happen: servicing HVAC before summer, flushing water heaters, inspecting roofs. Owners cut
it first and regret it most, because it converts predictable small costs into unpredictable large
ones. **Corrective** maintenance repairs what has already failed — the ordinary work order, where the
§ 92.056 clock runs. **Emergency** maintenance cannot wait; § 92.0561(d)(3) and (e) single out sewage
and flooding inside the dwelling, cessation of potable water, and inadequate heating or cooling, with
the tenant's repair right arising immediately for sewage and flooding and after three days for water,
heat or cooling.

Security devices belong in the same plan. Section 92.153(a) requires, without any tenant request and
subject to stated exceptions, window latches, a doorknob lock or keyed dead bolt on each exterior
door, sliding-door pin locks and handle latches or security bars, and a keyless bolting device and
door viewer on each exterior door — installed "at the landlord's expense" under § 92.153(c) and
operable throughout the tenant's possession under § 92.153(h). Section 92.006(a) makes these duties
non-waivable, and § 92.152(b) reaches single-family houses, duplexes and units in apartment,
condominium and townhome projects. A make-ready checklist that skips them is not finished.

**Tenant selection.** This is where fair housing exposure is created or avoided, and the protection is
procedural: written criteria, applied the same way to every applicant, documented every time.

Texas Property Code § 301.021(a) provides that a person "may not refuse to sell or rent, after the
making of a bona fide offer, refuse to negotiate for the sale or rental of, or in any other manner
make unavailable or deny a dwelling to another because of race, color, religion, sex, familial status,
or national origin," and § 301.021(b) extends that to the terms, conditions and privileges of a rental
and to services and facilities provided with it. Section 301.025(a) and (b) apply the same
prohibitions on the basis of disability — of the applicant, of a person who will reside in the
dwelling, or of anyone associated with the applicant — and § 301.025(c) provides that discrimination
includes refusing a reasonable modification at the disabled person's expense where necessary for full
enjoyment, and refusing "to make a reasonable accommodation in rules, policies, practices, or services
if the accommodation may be necessary to afford the person equal opportunity to use and enjoy a
dwelling." Section 301.003(11) defines "to rent" to include "to lease, sublease, or let, or to grant
in any other manner, for a consideration, the right to occupy premises not owned by the occupant" —
the chapter is aimed at what a property manager does all day.

The federal provisions are parallel. Under 42 U.S.C. § 3604(a) it is unlawful "to refuse to sell or
rent after the making of a bona fide offer, or to refuse to negotiate for the sale or rental of, or
otherwise make unavailable or deny, a dwelling to any person" on the same grounds; § 3604(b) covers
terms, conditions and privileges; § 3604(d) prohibits representing that a dwelling is unavailable when
it is in fact available; and § 3604(f)(1) through (3) address handicap, including the
reasonable-modification and reasonable-accommodation duties. Under § 3604(f)(9) nothing in that
subsection requires a dwelling to be made available to a person whose tenancy "would constitute a
direct threat to the health or safety of other individuals"; Property Code § 301.025(f) is to the same
effect.

Advertising is regulated separately and strictly. Property Code § 301.022 prohibits publishing "a
notice, statement, or advertisement that is about the sale or rental of a dwelling and that indicates
any preference, limitation, or discrimination or the intention to make a preference, limitation, or
discrimination because of race, color, religion, sex, disability, familial status, or national
origin," and 42 U.S.C. § 3604(c) is the federal counterpart; Property Code § 301.024 and 42 U.S.C.
§ 3604(e) prohibit inducing a sale or rental for profit by representations about the entry of persons
of a protected characteristic into a neighborhood. Advertise the *property*, never the desired tenant.
Occupations Code § 1101.652(b)(32) adds a licensing consequence: the Commission may discipline a
license holder who "discriminates against an owner, potential buyer, landlord, or potential tenant on
the basis of race, color, religion, sex, disability, familial status, national origin, or ancestry,
including directing a prospective buyer or tenant interested in equivalent properties to a different
area based on" those characteristics. Note that the TRELA list adds *ancestry*.

Within those constraints a manager may screen on legitimate, consistently applied criteria — income
relative to rent, verified rental history, creditworthiness. Write the standards down before the first
application is taken, and never take an owner's instruction to deviate from them; § 1101.652(b)(32)
makes the license holder responsible for the license holder's own act regardless of who suggested
it.

**Reporting to the owner.** Owner reporting closes the loop and prevents most disputes. A monthly
package showing income received, expenses paid with invoices, the operating account's cash position,
deposits held and for whom, occupancy and delinquency, and open and completed work orders leaves
little to argue about. Section 1101.652(b)(9) requires proper accounting and remittance within a
reasonable time and § 1101.652(b)(24) forbids materially inaccurate statements of account, so accuracy
here is not merely good service. Correct an error in writing in the next statement; concealment is
what converts a bookkeeping mistake into a disciplinary matter.

## Unit: The Management Profession

Property management is a career, not a task, and it differs from brokerage in ways that suit some
people very well. Sales brokerage is transactional: episodic work, lumpy income, a client relationship
that ends at closing. Management is operational: recurring income, relationships that last years, and
a measure of good work that is simply that nothing dramatic happened.

**The range of asset types.** The words "property manager" cover several occupations that share a
name. **Single-family and small residential** management handles detached houses, duplexes and small
multiplexes for individual owners; the properties are scattered and the economics turn on efficiency
per door. This is where the Texas licensing question bites hardest, because Occupations Code
§ 1101.002(1)(A)(x) reaches control of the acceptance or deposit of rent from a resident of a
single-family residential real property unit, and because the on-site apartment manager exemption in
§ 1101.005(7) does not describe this work. **Multifamily** management runs apartment communities from
one address, staffed on site and measured on occupancy, delinquency, turnover cost and expense per
unit; careers run from leasing consultant to community manager to regional manager, and § 1101.005(7)
makes the on-site position available without a license, which is why multifamily is a common entry
point into the industry.

**Commercial** management — office, retail and industrial — deals with business tenants, longer and
individually negotiated leases, and rent built from a base plus a share of operating costs, sometimes
plus a percentage of retail sales. It demands lease abstraction skills residential management does
not, and it operates outside Property Code ch. 92, which governs residential tenancies. Property Code
ch. 91 supplies rules applicable to landlords and tenants generally: § 91.001 on notice terminating a
month-to-month tenancy; § 91.005, under which "during the term of a lease, the tenant may not rent the
leasehold to any other person without the prior consent of the landlord"; § 91.004, making a landlord
who fails to comply with the lease liable to a non-defaulting tenant for resulting damages and giving
that tenant a lien on the landlord's nonexempt property in the tenant's possession and on rent due;
and § 91.006's non-waivable duty to mitigate damages when a tenant abandons the premises in violation
of the lease. **Community association** management handles common property and the governing documents
of a homeowners association or condominium regime, where the client is a volunteer board and the
"customers" are owner-members rather than tenants. Above all of these sits **asset management**,
concerned with the investment rather than operations, and property management is the usual path into
it.

**Designations and trade organizations — practice, not law.** The industry supports national trade
associations and professional institutes serving apartment, commercial, single-family residential and
community association management, and these bodies confer designations earned through coursework,
documented experience and examination. State the legal position exactly, because students misremember
it constantly. **No Texas statute read for this course requires a property manager to hold any
professional designation or to belong to any trade organization.** Occupations Code § 1101.351
requires a *license* — a broker or sales agent license — of a person performing the acts of a broker,
and § 1101.002(4) defines a license holder as a broker or sales agent licensed under the chapter. That
is the legal credential. Designations are private and voluntary: conferred by private organizations
under their own rules and conferring no authority a license holder does not already have. This module
cites nothing for the value of any designation, because that is professional practice and not Texas
law, and it names no particular organization. Structured education does make managers better at a job
whose failures are expensive and often invisible until they are catastrophic. That is a good reason to
pursue a designation. "It is required" is not.

**Ethics: standing between the owner and the tenant.** The defining ethical feature of this work is
that the manager sits between two parties whose interests diverge daily and owes each of them
something different.

To the owner, the manager owes the duties of an agent. Occupations Code § 1101.557(a) provides that a
broker who represents a party in a real estate transaction, or who lists real estate for sale under an
exclusive agreement for a party, is that party's agent, and § 1101.557(b) requires that broker to
inform the party of material information related to a transaction to list, buy, sell or lease the
party's real estate, including the receipt of an offer. Chapter 1101's disciplinary grounds reinforce
the point: § 1101.652(b)(1) reaches a license holder who "acts negligently or incompetently"; (b)(2)
conduct "that is dishonest or in bad faith or that demonstrates untrustworthiness"; (b)(7) a failure
"to make clear to all parties to a real estate transaction the party for whom the license holder is
acting"; (b)(8) receiving compensation from more than one party without the full knowledge and consent
of all; and (b)(9) and (10) the accounting and commingling failures examined in Unit 1. Together they
describe loyalty, disclosure, competence and honest accounting — the ordinary content of a fiduciary
relationship — enforced by license discipline rather than only by contract.

To the tenant, the manager owes what the statute owes. The ch. 92 duties run against the landlord, and
§ 92.001(2) tells us the manager is not the landlord unless the manager purports to be the owner,
lessor or sublessor in a lease. But the manager is the person through whom those duties are performed
or breached: the manager receives the repair notice under § 92.052(a) at the place rent is paid, runs
the clock under § 92.056, holds and refunds the security deposit under § 92.103(a) "on or before the
30th day after the date the tenant surrenders the premises," prepares the "written description and
itemized list of all deductions" required by § 92.104(c), and honors or ignores § 92.104(b)'s rule
that no portion of a deposit may be retained "to cover normal wear and tear." Section 92.109(a) makes
a landlord who in bad faith retains a deposit liable for one hundred dollars, three times the portion
wrongfully withheld, and reasonable attorney's fees, and § 92.109(d) presumes bad faith where the
landlord fails either to return the deposit or to provide the written itemization on or before the
thirtieth day after surrender. The penalties fall on the landlord, but they fall because of what the
manager did.

Three ch. 92 provisions exist precisely because managers under pressure have been tempted to shorten
the process. Section 92.008(a) and (b) prohibit a landlord or a landlord's agent from interrupting
utility service, whether paid directly to the utility by the tenant or furnished by the landlord as an
incident of the tenancy, "unless the interruption results from bona fide repairs, construction, or an
emergency," subject to the narrow exception in § 92.008(h) for submetered or allocated electricity;
§ 92.008(f) gives the tenant possession or termination plus actual damages, one month's rent plus
$1,000, fees and costs. Section 92.0081(b) prohibits intentionally preventing a tenant from entering
the premises except by judicial process, subject to stated exceptions including a lock change for a
delinquent tenant under the notice requirements of § 92.0081(c) and (d); § 92.0081(f) requires that
the tenant be given a key "without regard to whether the tenant pays the delinquent rent," and
§ 92.0081(h) provides a civil penalty of one month's rent plus $1,000. Section 92.331 prohibits
retaliation — eviction, deprivation of the premises, decreased services, a rent increase, lease
termination, or a bad-faith course of conduct materially interfering with the tenant's rights — within
six months after the tenant exercises a right, gives a repair notice, complains in good faith to a
code authority, utility or civic agency, or participates in a tenant organization; § 92.332 preserves
ordinary grounds such as delinquency and material breach, and § 92.333 gives a civil penalty of one
month's rent plus $500 with damages, costs and fees.

So how does a competent manager honor both sets of obligations? By recognizing that they conflict less
often than they appear to. Loyalty to the owner is a duty to advance the owner's *lawful* interests,
and an owner's interests are never served by exposure to those penalties. When an owner asks for
something the law forbids, the loyal answer and the lawful answer are the same: explain the provision,
explain the exposure, and decline. Sections 1101.652(b)(1) and (2) both cut against a manager who
follows an unlawful instruction, and § 1101.803 places the broker on the hook for it. Where genuine
tension remains it is usually about candor rather than legality, and the discipline is to be honest
with everyone and keep no confidence about facts the other party is entitled to know. Section
1101.652(b)(7) is the starting point: a tenant who knows the manager works for the owner is not misled
by the manager advancing the owner's interests.

**Continuing competence.** Texas law speaks here too. Occupations Code § 1101.455(b) requires a
license holder not subject to the § 1101.454 education requirements to attend continuing education
courses approved by the Commission during the term of the current license, in a number of classroom
hours the Commission prescribes by rule, "which must be at least 15 classroom hours," and § 1101.455(e)
requires at least eight of those hours to cover listed legal topics — a list that reads like a
property manager's job description: commission rules; **fair housing laws**; **Property Code issues,
including landlord-tenant law**; agency law; disclosures to buyers, landlords, tenants and sellers;
and "the ethical requirements of engaging in real estate brokerage," among others. Beyond that
minimum, competence decays quickly for a specific reason: the Property Code chapters a manager lives
inside are amended regularly, and the amendments are usually procedural — a notice period, a fee cap,
a required disclosure — exactly the details a manager operates on autopilot. As a matter of practice
and not of legal requirement, managers who stay out of trouble re-read the operative chapters
periodically and keep lease forms and notice templates under review rather than treating them as
settled.

### Module recap

This module built one argument in three steps. It began with the work — leasing, rent collection,
maintenance, accounting, reporting, tenant relations and compliance — and showed that the work itself
triggers regulation. Because a manager performs listed acts for another person for compensation,
Occupations Code § 1101.002(1)(A) makes leasing, negotiating leases, listing property for lease and
controlling the acceptance or deposit of single-family rent acts of a broker, and § 1101.351 forbids
performing them without a license. Section 1101.005 supplies the exemptions that decide most real
cases: the on-site apartment manager under subdivision (7), and the owner or the owner's employee
leasing the owner's own real estate under subdivision (8). Because a manager holds other people's
money, § 1101.652(b)(9), (10), (30) and (31) convert sloppy trust accounting into license discipline:
deposit promptly, never commingle, never disburse early, always account and remit within a reasonable
time.

The second unit took that licensed manager into the operating relationship. The management agreement
sets scope of authority, term — with § 1101.652(b)(12) carving property management contracts out of
the definite-termination-date requirement that governs listing agreements — compensation, owner
obligations, indemnification and accounting mechanics. The operating statement supplied the arithmetic
every owner conversation uses: gross potential rent less vacancy and collection loss yields effective
gross income, less operating expenses yields net operating income, with debt service and capital
expenditures excluded by definition. Maintenance is organized into routine, preventive, corrective and
emergency work because § 92.056(d)'s seven-day presumption sets the tempo, and tenant selection is
written and consistent because Property Code ch. 301, 42 U.S.C. § 3604 and Occupations Code
§ 1101.652(b)(32) all reach the same conduct.

The third unit placed the manager in a profession and an ethical position. Designations and trade
memberships are voluntary practice, required by no statute read for this course; the license is the
legal credential. The manager's agency duties run to the owner under § 1101.557 and the § 1101.652(b)
disciplinary grounds, while the ch. 92 duties protect the tenant and are performed or breached through
the manager's hands. Honoring both usually means recognizing that an owner's lawful interest and a
tenant's statutory right point the same way. You should now be able to decide whether a person needs a
Texas license to manage a given property, state the trust-money rules, build an operating statement
through to net operating income, and separate what the law requires from what the profession expects.
