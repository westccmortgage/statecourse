# Texas Principles of Real Estate II — Module 09

## TREC Topic: Property Management

**Prescribed time: 120 minutes.** This module carries the three units the Commission's PRINS2-1 course
approval form places under *Property Management*, whose scope note is *"Licensing as a broker or
salesperson; Ethics of practice as a license holder."*

**Learning objectives.** After completing this module you will be able to: identify the functions a
Texas property manager performs, state from Occupations Code § 1101.002(1) which management acts are
acts of a broker, apply the exemptions in § 1101.005 to decide whether a particular person managing a
particular Texas property needs a license, and state the four trust-money grounds for discipline in
§ 1101.652(b)(9), (10), (30) and (31); identify the terms a management agreement must
settle, explain why § 1101.652(b)(12) carves property management contracts out of the
definite-termination-date requirement, compute effective gross income and net operating income from
gross potential rent, vacancy and collection loss and operating expenses, and connect the Property
Code ch. 92 repair duty to the design of a maintenance program; and distinguish the manager's
fiduciary obligation to the owner from the statutory duties that ch. 92 owes the tenant, describe the
asset types and career paths inside the management profession, and distinguish a voluntary
professional designation from a legal requirement.

**Every legal statement in this module comes from a source that was read for this course.** Where a
statement is practice rather than law, the module says so and cites nothing for it. Every dollar
figure and percentage in the worked examples is an illustration chosen to make the arithmetic
visible; none is a required, customary or standard figure.

## Unit: Property Management

A **property manager** operates real property on behalf of someone else. The owner keeps the title,
the equity and the risk; the manager supplies the time, the systems and the judgment. That single
sentence explains almost everything that follows, including why Texas regulates the work at all.
Because the manager acts *for another person* and does so *for compensation*, the manager is standing
in the position the Real Estate License Act was written to police.

Start with the work itself, because the law only makes sense once you can see what a manager actually
does in a week. The functions divide into seven recurring streams.

**Leasing.** The manager markets vacant space, shows it, screens applicants against written criteria,
negotiates lease terms within the authority the owner has granted, and executes the lease. Leasing is
where the largest sums are set: a lease signed at forty dollars a month under market costs the owner
four hundred and eighty dollars a year for every year the tenant renews at that base.

**Rent collection.** The manager sets the collection calendar, receives payment, posts it, pursues
delinquency, and applies late charges where the lease permits them. Texas regulates late charges
directly. Under Property Code § 92.019(a) a landlord may not collect a late fee unless notice of the
fee is in a written lease, the fee is reasonable, and "any portion of the tenant's rent has remained
unpaid two full days after the date the rent was originally due." Section 92.019(a-1) then supplies a
safe harbor: a late fee is considered reasonable if it is not more than twelve percent of the rent for
the rental period for a dwelling in a structure containing not more than four dwelling units, or ten
percent for a dwelling in a structure containing more than four units. A larger fee is permitted only
up to the landlord's uncertain damages related to the late payment. A landlord who violates the
section is liable under § 92.019(c) for one hundred dollars, three times the late fee collected, and
the tenant's reasonable attorney's fees. A manager who lets a legacy lease form charge a flat fee on
the first day, with no two-day grace, has created that liability for the owner across the whole
portfolio at once.

**Maintenance.** The manager receives service requests, dispatches vendors, inspects the work and pays
the invoice. The legal spine of this function is Property Code § 92.052(a), which requires a landlord
to "make a diligent effort to repair or remedy a condition" if the tenant specifies the condition in a
notice to the person or place where rent is normally paid, the tenant is not delinquent in rent when
notice is given, and the condition materially affects the physical health or safety of an ordinary
tenant or arises from a failure to keep a hot water device in good operating condition supplying water
of at least 120 degrees Fahrenheit. Note who receives that notice: *the place where rent is normally
paid*. In a managed property that is the management office. The manager is the statutory intake point
for the repair duty, which is exactly why intake discipline matters.

**Accounting.** The manager collects money that belongs to the owner, pays bills that belong to the
owner, holds deposits that belong to the tenant, and reconciles all of it. This is the function that
produces the most license discipline, and it gets its own treatment below.

**Reporting.** The manager renders periodic statements to the owner — what came in, what went out,
what is vacant, what is delinquent, what is broken.

**Tenant relations.** The manager answers the phone, enforces the rules evenhandedly, and handles the
complaints that never appear in any budget line.

**Compliance.** The manager keeps the property and the file inside the law: fair housing in tenant
selection and advertising, the ch. 92 duties in operations, and the Occupations Code in the manager's
own conduct.

**Whose property is it, and who is the landlord?**

Before the licensing question, settle a definitional one that trips people constantly. Property Code
§ 92.001(2) defines "landlord" as "the owner, lessor, or sublessor of a dwelling, but does not include
a manager or agent of the landlord unless the manager or agent purports to be the owner, lessor, or
sublessor in an oral or written lease." The manager is ordinarily *not* the landlord for purposes of
ch. 92. The duties in that chapter run to the landlord. But a manager who signs a lease as though the
manager were the lessor, rather than as agent for a disclosed owner, has pulled those duties onto
itself. A common error is to treat the signature block as clerical. It is not; it decides who the
statute is talking about.

Section 92.201(a) reinforces the point from the other direction. A landlord must disclose to a tenant,
or to a government official acting in an official capacity, the name and a street or post office box
address of the holder of record title according to the county deed records, and, "if an entity located
off-site from the dwelling is primarily responsible for managing the dwelling, the name and street
address of the management company." Section 92.201(b) gives three ways to make the disclosure to a
tenant: in writing within seven days of the tenant's request, by continuously posting the information
conspicuously in the dwelling or at the on-site manager's office within that same period, or by
including it in the lease or in written rules given to the tenant before any request. Section 92.006(a)
makes the disclosure duty one that "may not be waived." The remedies in § 92.205(a) include a court
order compelling disclosure, a judgment for the tenant's actual costs of discovering the information,
a judgment for one month's rent plus one hundred dollars, court costs and attorney's fees, and
unilateral termination of the lease without a court proceeding. Texas has decided that a tenant is
entitled to know who owns the building and who runs it. Off-site fee management is the ordinary
trigger.

**The license question in Texas**

Now the heart of the unit. **Does managing someone else's property in Texas require a real estate
license?** The answer is built from two sections read together.

Occupations Code § 1101.002(1)(A) defines a **broker** as "a person who, in exchange for a commission
or other valuable consideration or with the expectation of receiving a commission or other valuable
consideration, performs for another person one of the following acts," and then lists them. Four
matter to a manager:

- clause (i) — "sells, exchanges, purchases, or **leases** real estate";
- clause (iii) — "negotiates or attempts to negotiate the listing, sale, exchange, purchase, or
  **lease** of real estate";
- clause (iv) — "lists or offers, attempts, or agrees to list real estate for sale, **lease**, or
  exchange"; and
- clause (x) — "**controls the acceptance or deposit of rent from a resident of a single-family
  residential real property unit**."

Section 1101.002(5) then defines "real estate" as "any interest in real property, **including a
leasehold**, located in or outside this state." Leasing is not a lesser activity that falls outside
the Act. It is inside the definition of a broker, by name, four times over.

Three elements must all be present before an act is broker activity: it must be **for another
person**, it must be one of the **listed acts**, and it must be done **for compensation or with the
expectation of compensation**. Strip out any one and the definition is not met. An owner leasing the
owner's own duplex is not acting for another person. A friend who shows a vacant house as a favor,
expecting nothing, is missing the consideration element — though "other valuable consideration" is
broad, and a reduction in that friend's own rent is consideration.

Clause (x) deserves separate attention because students routinely miss it. It does not say "manages"
and it does not say "leases." It says *controls the acceptance or deposit of rent* from a resident of
a single-family residential real property unit. A person who never shows the house, never negotiates a
term, and never signs anything — but who has the tenant's rent checks mailed to them, or has the
tenant's electronic rent payments routed into an account that person controls, and does that for
another owner for compensation — has performed an act of a broker. The Legislature aimed this clause
at exactly the arrangement people assume is safely informal: collecting rent on a rent house for an
absentee owner.

Section 1101.351(a) supplies the prohibition: "Unless a person holds a license issued under this
chapter, the person may not (1) act as or represent that the person is a broker or sales agent; or
(2) act as a residential rental locator." Section 1101.351(a-1) applies the same rule to business
entities acting as brokers, and § 1101.351(c) provides that a licensed sales agent "may not engage or
attempt to engage in real estate brokerage unless the sales agent is sponsored by a licensed broker
and is acting for that broker." A **residential rental locator** is defined in § 1101.002(6) as "a
person who offers for consideration to locate a unit in an apartment complex for lease to a
prospective tenant," excluding "an owner who offers to locate a unit in the owner's complex."
Note also § 1101.002(4): a "license holder" is a broker or a sales agent licensed under the chapter.
Chapter 1101 as read for this course creates no separate property manager license. There is no such
credential to obtain; there is a broker license and a sales agent license, and management activity
falls under them.

There is also a hard commercial consequence for getting this wrong. Section 1101.806(b) provides that
a person "may not maintain an action to collect compensation for an act as a broker or sales agent
that is performed in this state" unless the person alleges and proves that the person was a license
holder when the act was commenced, or an attorney licensed in any state. An unlicensed manager who
does broker acts and then has to sue for the management fee cannot maintain the suit.

**The exemptions in § 1101.005**

Section 1101.005 opens: "This chapter does not apply to:" and lists twelve categories. Two decide most
Texas management questions.

**Subdivision (7) — "an on-site manager of an apartment complex."** The resident manager who lives or
works at the complex, shows units in that complex, signs leases for that complex and takes rent at
that complex is outside the chapter. The words do the limiting: *on-site* and *an apartment complex*.
The exemption describes a person at the property, working that property. It is not a general
management exemption, and it does not by its terms describe a person operating scattered single-family
houses from a central office.

**Subdivision (8) — "an owner or the owner's employee who leases the owner's improved or unimproved
real estate."** An owner may lease the owner's own property without a license, and so may the owner's
**employee**. The load-bearing word is *employee*. The exemption is written for the owner's own staff,
not for an outside firm engaged to manage. A management company retained under a contract, paid a
percentage of collections, is not the owner's employee in any ordinary sense; it is a separate person
performing listed acts for another person for compensation.

The remaining subdivisions matter less often but you should know they exist: (1) a Texas-licensed
attorney; (2) an attorney-in-fact authorized under a power of attorney to conduct not more than three
real estate transactions annually; (3) a public official engaged in official duties; (4) a licensed
auctioneer who performs no other act of a broker; (5) a person acting under a court order or the
authority of a will or written trust instrument; (6) a person employed by an owner in the sale of
structures the owner erected in the course of the owner's business; (9), which excludes minerals,
mining, quarries, standing timber, other energy sources, cemetery lots, "the lease or management of a
hotel or motel," and sales under a power of sale in a deed of trust; (10) and (11), covering general
partners, managers, managing members and certain employees acting for a limited partnership or limited
liability company; and (12), a person acting on behalf of a commercial signage entity. Note (9)(C) in
particular: hotel and motel management is outside the chapter altogether.

Put the pieces together and the Texas rule is workable. Managing another person's property for
compensation is generally brokerage activity requiring a license, because leasing, negotiating leases,
listing property for lease and controlling single-family rent are all acts of a broker under
§ 1101.002(1)(A). The on-site apartment manager and the owner's own employee are the two stated
exemptions that carry most of the everyday exceptions. If a person is neither of those, and is
performing a listed act for another person for compensation, the person needs a license and must be
either a broker or a sponsored sales agent.

One further provision links management to agency. Section 1101.557(a) states that "a broker who
represents a party in a real estate transaction or who lists real estate for sale under an exclusive
agreement for a party is that party's agent," and § 1101.557(c)(1) provides that "a license holder who
has the authority to bind a party to a lease or sale under a power of attorney or a **property
management agreement** is also a party to the lease or sale." A manager with signing authority is not
a bystander to the lease. Section 1101.803 adds the supervisory dimension: "A licensed broker is
liable to the commission, the public, and the broker's clients for any conduct engaged in under this
chapter by the broker or by a sales agent associated with or acting for the broker." The broker owns
the property management department's mistakes.

**Trust money**

A property manager handles other people's money continuously and in volume: rent that belongs to the
owner from the moment it is received, security deposits that belong to the tenant subject to the
landlord's right to deduct, application deposits, and reserves the owner has funded for repairs. Four
grounds for discipline in Occupations Code § 1101.652(b) govern that handling. The subsection applies
to a license holder "while engaged in real estate brokerage," and the Commission may suspend or revoke
the license or take other authorized disciplinary action if the license holder:

- **(9)** "fails within a reasonable time to properly account for or remit money that is received by
  the license holder and that belongs to another person";
- **(10)** "commingles money that belongs to another person with the license holder's own money";
- **(30)** "fails to deposit, within a reasonable time, money the license holder receives as escrow or
  trust funds in a real estate transaction: (A) in trust with a title company authorized to do business
  in this state; or (B) in a custodial, trust, or escrow account maintained for that purpose in a
  banking institution authorized to do business in this state"; and
- **(31)** "disburses money deposited in a custodial, trust, or escrow account, as provided in
  Subdivision (30), before the completion or termination of the real estate transaction."

Read them as a sequence describing the life of a dollar. It arrives. Subdivision (30) says get it into
the right account promptly — a trust, escrow or custodial account maintained for that purpose at a
Texas-authorized banking institution, or in trust with a Texas-authorized title company. Subdivision
(10) says while it sits there, keep it apart from your own money; the violation is *commingling*, and
it is complete when the funds are mixed, without any need to show that a dollar was stolen.
Subdivision (31) says do not pay it out early. Subdivision (9) says account for it and remit it to the
person it belongs to within a reasonable time.

The practical failures are mundane. A manager who deposits owner rent into the firm's operating
account "temporarily" because the trust account signature card has not been updated has commingled. So
has a manager who leaves a cushion of the firm's own money in the trust account to prevent overdrafts.
A manager who funds this month's payroll out of security deposits has both commingled and disbursed
early. A manager four months behind in owner statements because the bookkeeper left has failed to
account or remit within a reasonable time. None of these requires bad intent: only some of these
provisions are about dishonesty at all, and the rest are about *administration*, which is where
careless managers lose licenses.

Two neighboring provisions belong in the same discussion. Section 1101.652(b)(13) reaches a license
holder who "accepts, receives, or charges an undisclosed commission, rebate, or direct profit on an
expenditure made for a principal" — the vendor kickback, in statutory language. Section
1101.652(b)(24) reaches one who "withholds from or inserts into a statement of account or invoice a
statement that the license holder knows makes the statement of account or invoice inaccurate in a
material way." The owner statement is not merely a courtesy document. Falsifying it is independently
sanctionable.

## Unit: Management Considerations

Everything in this unit follows from a single fact: the manager's authority is delegated, not
inherent. What the manager may do is what the owner has agreed the manager may do, bounded by what the
law permits either of them to do. The document that performs the delegation is the management
agreement, and it deserves to be read line by line before it is signed.

**The management agreement**

A workable Texas management agreement settles at least seven things.

**Identification and scope of authority.** Which property, described precisely, and which acts the
manager may perform without further approval. Sign leases? At what rent, for what term, with what
concessions? Approve repairs? Up to what dollar amount per occurrence before the owner must be
consulted? Sign checks on the operating account? Serve notices and file eviction suits, or only refer
them to the owner's counsel? Authority left vague becomes authority litigated later. Remember
§ 1101.557(c)(1): a license holder with authority to bind the owner to a lease under a property
management agreement is treated as a party to that lease.

**Term and termination.** Here Texas law contains a detail worth noticing. Occupations Code
§ 1101.652(b)(12) makes it a ground for discipline if a license holder "fails to specify a definite
termination date that is not subject to prior notice in a contract, **other than a contract to perform
property management services**, in which the license holder agrees to perform services for which a
license is required under this chapter." A listing agreement must state a definite termination date
that is not subject to prior notice; a property management contract is expressly carved out of that
requirement. The reason is structural: management is an open-ended operating relationship, not a
finite assignment to produce one transaction. The carve-out therefore permits an evergreen term ending
on notice. It does not require one, and it does not relieve the agreement of the need to say how it
ends, who may end it, what notice is required, and what happens to funds, files, keys and pending
leases on termination. A common error is to read the carve-out as saying management agreements need no
termination provision at all. It says only that the *statutory definite-date requirement* does not
apply to them.

**Compensation.** How the manager is paid — commonly a percentage of collected income, a fixed monthly
amount, or a combination, plus separate leasing or renewal fees. Whether the fee is earned on rent
*billed* or rent *collected* is not a small distinction; on collected income the manager shares the
pain of a delinquency. State whether the manager may retain late fees, application fees or vendor
discounts, because § 1101.652(b)(13) makes an undisclosed direct profit on an expenditure made for a
principal a ground for discipline.

**Owner obligations.** Funding an operating reserve, paying taxes and insurance premiums the manager
does not, responding to approval requests within a stated time, and disclosing known conditions of the
property. A manager who cannot get the owner to fund a repair still faces the ch. 92 repair duty
running against the owner through the manager's office, which is why funding mechanics belong in the
contract.

**Indemnification and insurance.** The manager acting within authority ordinarily expects to be
indemnified by the owner for claims arising from the property, with a carve-out for the manager's own
negligence or misconduct; the owner ordinarily expects to be named on the property's liability policy
and to see evidence of the manager's coverage. What the parties agree here is contract, not statute,
and this course states no required allocation.

**Accounting and funds.** Which accounts will exist, who signs on them, when statements are rendered,
when funds are remitted, and how security deposits are held. Write these terms so that compliance with
§ 1101.652(b)(9), (10), (30) and (31) is the automatic result of following the contract.

**Fair housing and legal compliance.** A clause obligating both parties to comply with fair housing
law, and obligating the owner not to instruct the manager to do otherwise. This clause is worth
having precisely because the instruction it forbids is the one a manager is most likely to receive.

**Budgeting and the operating statement**

The manager's financial reporting rests on one arithmetic chain, and every serious conversation about
a rental property uses it.

**Gross potential rent (GPR)** is what the property would produce if every unit were leased at market
for the entire period, with nothing vacant and nothing uncollected — a deliberately counterfactual
ceiling. **Vacancy and collection loss** is what GPR never becomes: rent lost to empty units, to the
days between one tenant leaving and the next arriving, to concessions, and to amounts billed but never
paid. **Effective gross income (EGI)** is gross potential rent minus vacancy and collection loss — the
money the property actually brings in. **Operating expenses** are the recurring costs of running the
property: property taxes, insurance, common-area utilities, repairs and maintenance, landscaping and
pest control, on-site payroll, make-ready and turnover costs, administrative and marketing costs, and
the management fee itself. **Net operating income (NOI)** is effective gross income minus operating
expenses.

Two exclusions define NOI and are the most common source of error. Operating expenses **exclude debt
service** — mortgage principal and interest — because NOI measures what the property earns, not how a
particular owner financed it. Two identical buildings, one owned free and clear and one heavily
mortgaged, have the same NOI. Operating expenses also **exclude capital expenditures**: a new roof, a
repaved parking lot, a full HVAC replacement. Those are capital items funded from reserves, not
operating costs of the period. Depreciation, an accounting allocation rather than a cash cost, is
likewise not an operating expense here.

**A worked example — illustration only.** A 24-unit apartment property in a Texas suburb. Market rent
is $1,250 per unit per month. Vacancy and collection loss is estimated at 7 percent. The management
fee is 8 percent of effective gross income. Every figure here is invented to make the arithmetic
visible.

Gross potential rent: 24 units × $1,250 × 12 months = **$360,000**.

Vacancy and collection loss: $360,000 × 0.07 = **$25,200**.

Effective gross income: $360,000 − $25,200 = **$334,800**.

Operating expenses:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Property taxes | $62,000 |
| Insurance | $18,400 |
| Common-area utilities | $9,600 |
| Repairs and maintenance | $26,800 |
| Landscaping and pest control | $7,200 |
| Management fee (8% × $334,800) | $26,784 |
| On-site payroll | $21,000 |
| Make-ready and turnover | $12,500 |
| Administrative and marketing | $5,900 |
| **Total operating expenses** | **$190,184** |

Net operating income: $334,800 − $190,184 = **$144,616**.

Operating expense ratio: $190,184 ÷ $334,800 = 0.568, or **56.8 percent**.

Notice what the ratio tells you and what it does not. It says that fifty-seven cents of every dollar
collected is consumed by operations. It says nothing about whether the owner's loan payment can be
made from the remaining $144,616, because debt service was never in the statement. A manager
presenting this statement to an owner who is thinking about a refinance should say so out loud.

Notice also how the management fee behaves. Because it is computed on effective gross income, a
vacancy costs the manager as well as the owner: a rise in vacancy from 7 percent to 12 percent would
cut EGI by $18,000 and the fee by $1,440. That alignment is one of the reasons percentage-of-collections
compensation is common. It is a business arrangement, not a legal requirement, and this course
prescribes no rate.

**Setting rent from market data**

Rent is set by comparison, not by the owner's needs. The manager collects current asking and, where
available, effective rents from genuinely comparable properties — comparable in submarket, age,
condition, unit size and mix, amenity set, and utility structure — and adjusts for differences. A
concession must be converted before comparison: one month free on a twelve-month lease at $1,300 is an
effective rent of $1,300 × 11 ÷ 12 = $1,191.67 per month, not $1,300. Comparing an advertised rate
against an effective rate is the single most common analytical error in leasing.

The market signal to watch is the relationship between price and time on market. A unit that leases in
three days at the asking rent was probably priced below market; a unit that sits for six weeks is
priced above it. Vacancy is the more expensive error: a unit vacant for one month at $1,250 costs
$1,250 that is never recovered, while a unit leased $25 below market costs $300 over a twelve-month
term. That comparison, not the owner's mortgage payment, is what should drive a pricing decision.

**The maintenance program**

Property Code § 92.052 and § 92.056 do not merely impose a duty; they set the tempo of the maintenance
operation. Under § 92.056(b) a landlord becomes liable to a tenant when the tenant has given notice at
the place rent is normally paid, the condition materially affects the physical health or safety of an
ordinary tenant, the tenant has given a subsequent written notice after a reasonable time (or sent the
first notice by certified mail, registered mail, or another tracked delivery method), the landlord has
had a reasonable time to repair after those notices, the landlord has not made a diligent effort, and
the tenant was not delinquent in rent when the notices were given. Section 92.056(d) then supplies the
number that drives everything: "there is a rebuttable presumption that seven days is a reasonable
time," rebuttable by considering the date of receipt, the severity and nature of the condition, and
the reasonable availability of materials, labor and utilities. Section 92.056(c) provides that the
landlord is considered to have received notice when the landlord or the landlord's agent or employee
actually receives it, or when the postal service has attempted delivery.

Seven days is a presumption, not a deadline, and severity moves it in both directions. But a
maintenance program that cannot reliably close a health-and-safety work order inside a week is a
program operating against the presumption. That is why competent managers timestamp every service
request at intake, route health-and-safety items to a separate queue, and document the dispatch and
completion of each one. The documentation is what rebuts the presumption when materials genuinely were
not available.

Section 92.0561 shows the consequence of failing. If the landlord is liable under § 92.056(b), and the
further conditions of § 92.0561(d) are met, the tenant may have the condition repaired and deduct the
cost from a subsequent rent payment, limited by § 92.0561(b) to "the amount of one month's rent under
the lease or $500, whichever is greater," with the repair performed by an independent company,
contractor or repairman under § 92.0561(f). Section 92.056(e) gives the tenant, in the alternative,
the right to terminate the lease, or to pursue judicial remedies under § 92.0563. Section 92.056(g)
requires the lease to inform the tenant of these remedies "in underlined or bold print."

Against that legal backdrop, structure the work in four categories. **Routine** maintenance is the
scheduled, recurring work that keeps the property presentable and functional — grounds, common areas,
filters, lighting. **Preventive** maintenance is work performed on a schedule to stop failures before
they happen: servicing HVAC before summer, flushing water heaters, sealing and inspecting roofs.
Preventive work is the category owners cut first and regret most, because it converts predictable
small costs into unpredictable large ones. **Corrective** maintenance is repair of something that has
already failed — the ordinary work order, and the category where the § 92.056 clock runs. **Emergency**
maintenance is the subset that cannot wait: sewage backing up inside the dwelling, flooding from
broken pipes, a total loss of potable water, a gas odor, a fire, a security breach. Section
92.0561(d)(3) and (e) treat sewage and flooding, cessation of potable water, and inadequate heating or
cooling as their own accelerated categories, with the tenant's repair right arising immediately for
sewage and flooding and after three days for water or for heating and cooling. A property with no
after-hours emergency number is not running a maintenance program.

Security devices belong in the same plan. Section 92.153(a) requires, without any request from the
tenant and subject to the exceptions in subsections (b) and (e) through (h), that a dwelling be
equipped with a window latch on each exterior window, a doorknob lock or keyed dead bolt on each
exterior door, a sliding door pin lock and a sliding door handle latch or security bar on each
exterior sliding glass door, and a keyless bolting device and door viewer on each exterior door.
Section 92.153(c) requires these to be installed "at the landlord's expense," and § 92.153(h) requires
them to be operable throughout the tenant's possession. Section 92.152(b) reaches single-family
houses, duplexes, triplexes, mobile homes, and units in apartment, condominium, cooperative and
townhome projects, while § 92.152(a) excludes hotel, motel and similar transient housing. Under
§ 92.006(a) these duties may not be waived. A make-ready checklist that does not verify every
§ 92.153 device before a new tenant takes possession is not finished.

**Tenant selection**

Tenant selection is where fair housing exposure is created or avoided, and the protection is
procedural: **written criteria, applied the same way to every applicant, documented every time.**

Texas Property Code § 301.021(a) provides that a person "may not refuse to sell or rent, after the
making of a bona fide offer, refuse to negotiate for the sale or rental of, or in any other manner
make unavailable or deny a dwelling to another because of race, color, religion, sex, familial status,
or national origin," and § 301.021(b) extends the prohibition to the "terms, conditions, or privileges
of sale or rental" and to services and facilities provided in connection with a rental. Section
301.025(a) and (b) apply the same prohibitions on the basis of disability — of the applicant, of a
person who will reside in the dwelling, or of anyone associated with the applicant — and § 301.025(c)
provides that discrimination includes refusing to permit a reasonable modification of the premises at
the disabled person's expense where necessary for full enjoyment, and refusing "to make a reasonable
accommodation in rules, policies, practices, or services if the accommodation may be necessary to
afford the person equal opportunity to use and enjoy a dwelling." Section 301.003(11) defines "to
rent" to include "to lease, sublease, or let, or to grant in any other manner, for a consideration,
the right to occupy premises not owned by the occupant" — which is to say, the chapter is aimed
squarely at what a property manager does all day.

The federal provisions are parallel. Under 42 U.S.C. § 3604(a) it is unlawful "to refuse to sell or
rent after the making of a bona fide offer, or to refuse to negotiate for the sale or rental of, or
otherwise make unavailable or deny, a dwelling to any person because of race, color, religion, sex,
familial status, or national origin"; § 3604(b) covers terms, conditions and privileges; § 3604(d)
prohibits representing that a dwelling is unavailable for inspection or rental when it is in fact
available; and § 3604(f)(1) through (3) address handicap, including the reasonable-modification and
reasonable-accommodation duties. Under § 3604(f)(9), nothing in that subsection requires a dwelling to
be made available to an individual whose tenancy "would constitute a direct threat to the health or
safety of other individuals or whose tenancy would result in substantial physical damage to the
property of others"; Property Code § 301.025(f) is to the same effect.

Advertising is regulated separately and strictly. Property Code § 301.022 prohibits making, printing
or publishing "a notice, statement, or advertisement that is about the sale or rental of a dwelling and
that indicates any preference, limitation, or discrimination or the intention to make a preference,
limitation, or discrimination because of race, color, religion, sex, disability, familial status, or
national origin," and 42 U.S.C. § 3604(c) is the federal counterpart. Property Code § 301.024 and
42 U.S.C. § 3604(e) prohibit inducing a sale or rental for profit by representations about the entry
or prospective entry into a neighborhood of persons of a particular protected characteristic.
Advertise the *property*, never the desired tenant.

Occupations Code § 1101.652(b)(32) adds a licensing consequence on top of the housing statutes: the
Commission may discipline a license holder who "discriminates against an owner, potential buyer,
landlord, or potential tenant on the basis of race, color, religion, sex, disability, familial status,
national origin, or ancestry, including directing a prospective buyer or tenant interested in
equivalent properties to a different area based on" those characteristics. Note that the TRELA list
adds *ancestry*.

Within those constraints, a manager may screen on legitimate, consistently applied criteria — income
relative to rent, verified rental history, and creditworthiness among them. Two disciplines matter.
Write the standards down before the first application is taken, and never take an owner's instruction
to deviate from them; § 1101.652(b)(32) makes the license holder responsible for the license holder's
own act regardless of who suggested it.

**Reporting to the owner**

Owner reporting closes the loop and, done well, prevents most disputes. A monthly package that shows
income received, expenses paid with supporting invoices, the current cash position of the operating
account, security deposits held and for whom, occupancy and delinquency, and completed and open work
orders leaves very little to argue about. Section 1101.652(b)(9) requires proper accounting and
remittance within a reasonable time and § 1101.652(b)(24) forbids materially inaccurate statements of
account, so accuracy and timeliness in this package are not merely good service. A manager who
discovers an error in a prior statement should correct it in writing in the next one; concealment is
what converts a bookkeeping mistake into a disciplinary matter.

### Applied exercise: Build the operating statement

A Texas owner asks you to project the first year of a 12-unit property. Market rent is $1,100 per unit
per month. You estimate vacancy and collection loss at 8 percent. Your management fee is 8 percent of
effective gross income. Projected expenses are: property taxes $31,200; insurance $9,400; common-area
utilities $5,100; repairs and maintenance $14,300; landscaping and pest control $4,800; make-ready and
turnover $7,600; administrative and marketing $3,100. All figures are illustrative.

1. Compute gross potential rent, vacancy and collection loss, and effective gross income, showing each
   step.
2. Compute the management fee, total operating expenses, and net operating income.
3. Compute the operating expense ratio. Would replacing the roof for $46,000 change it? Explain by
   reference to the definition of operating expenses given in this unit.
4. The owner's lender requires a mortgage payment of $9,300 per month. Does your net operating income
   figure tell you whether the property covers it? What would you have to add to the statement to
   answer that question?

## Unit: The Management Profession

Property management is a career, not a task, and it looks different from brokerage in ways that suit
some people very well. Sales brokerage is transactional: the work is episodic, the income is lumpy,
and the relationship with a client typically ends at closing. Management is operational: the income is
recurring, the relationships last years, and the measure of good work is that nothing dramatic
happened. Managers tend to be people who like systems, tolerate interruption, and take satisfaction
from a well-run building rather than from a signed contract.

**The range of asset types**

The word "property manager" covers several distinct occupations that happen to share a name.

**Single-family and small residential.** Managing detached houses, duplexes and small multiplexes for
individual owners, often owners who moved and kept the house. The properties are scattered, the
economics turn on efficiency per door, and the owner is frequently unsophisticated and emotionally
attached to the property. This is also the segment where the Texas licensing question bites hardest,
because Occupations Code § 1101.002(1)(A)(x) reaches control of the acceptance or deposit of rent from
a resident of a single-family residential real property unit, and because the on-site apartment
manager exemption in § 1101.005(7) does not describe this work.

**Multifamily.** Apartment communities, from garden properties to high-rise. The work is concentrated
at one address, staffed on site, and measured relentlessly: occupancy, traffic, closing ratio,
delinquency, turnover cost, expense per unit. Careers here run from leasing consultant to assistant
manager to community manager to portfolio or regional manager. Section 1101.005(7) makes the on-site
manager position available to a person without a license, which is why multifamily is a common entry
point into the industry.

**Commercial — office, retail and industrial.** The tenants are businesses, the leases are longer and
individually negotiated, and the rent structures are more complex: base rent plus a share of operating
costs, sometimes plus a percentage of the tenant's sales in retail. Commercial management demands
lease abstraction and reconciliation skills that residential management does not, and it operates
outside Property Code ch. 92, which governs residential tenancies. Property Code ch. 91 supplies rules
applicable to landlords and tenants generally: § 91.001 on notice terminating a month-to-month
tenancy; § 91.005, under which "during the term of a lease, the tenant may not rent the leasehold to
any other person without the prior consent of the landlord"; § 91.004, making a landlord who fails to
comply with the lease liable to a non-defaulting tenant for resulting damages and giving that tenant a
lien on the landlord's nonexempt property in the tenant's possession and on rent due; and § 91.006's
non-waivable duty to mitigate damages when a tenant abandons the premises in violation of the lease.

**Community associations.** Managing common property and enforcing the governing documents of a
homeowners association or condominium regime. The client is a volunteer board, the "customers" are
owner-members rather than tenants, and the political dimension is unavoidable.

**Institutional and asset management.** Above the property manager sits the asset manager, concerned
less with operations than with the investment: hold or sell, refinance or not, reposition or maintain.
Property management is the usual path into that role.

**Designations and trade organizations — practice, not law**

The industry supports national trade associations and professional institutes serving apartment,
commercial and industrial, single-family residential and community association management, and these
bodies confer designations earned through coursework, documented experience and examination. Employers
sometimes prefer them; clients sometimes ask about them.

State the legal position exactly, because students misremember it constantly. **No Texas statute read
for this course requires a property manager to hold any professional designation or to belong to any
trade organization.** Occupations Code § 1101.351 requires a *license* — a broker or sales agent
license — of a person performing the acts of a broker, and § 1101.002(4) defines a license holder as a
broker or sales agent licensed under the chapter. That is the legal credential. Designations are
private, voluntary and contractual: conferred by private organizations under their own rules,
revocable under those same rules, and conferring no authority a license holder does not already have
and no exemption from anything a license holder must do. This module cites nothing for the existence
or value of any designation, because that is professional practice and not Texas law, and it names no
particular organization. Structured education and peer contact do make managers better at a job whose
failures are expensive and often invisible until they are catastrophic. That is a good reason to
pursue a designation. "It is required" is not.

**Ethics: standing between the owner and the tenant**

The defining ethical feature of property management is that the manager occupies a position between
two parties whose interests diverge daily, and owes each of them something different.

**To the owner, the manager owes the duties of an agent.** Occupations Code § 1101.557(a) provides
that a broker who represents a party in a real estate transaction, or who lists real estate for sale
under an exclusive agreement for a party, is that party's agent, and § 1101.557(b) requires such a
broker to inform the party of material information related to a transaction to list, buy, sell or
lease the party's real estate, including the receipt of an offer, and at a minimum to answer the
party's questions and present any offer to or from the party. Chapter 1101 reinforces the point with
its disciplinary grounds. Section 1101.652(b)(1) reaches a license holder who "acts negligently or
incompetently"; (b)(2) reaches conduct "that is dishonest or in bad faith or that demonstrates
untrustworthiness"; (b)(7) reaches a failure "to make clear to all parties to a real estate
transaction the party for whom the license holder is acting"; (b)(8) reaches receiving "compensation
from more than one party to a real estate transaction without the full knowledge and consent of all
parties"; (b)(13) reaches undisclosed profit on an expenditure made for a principal; and (b)(9) and
(10) reach the failures of accounting and commingling examined in Unit 1. Read together, these
provisions describe loyalty, disclosure, competence and honest accounting — the ordinary content of a
fiduciary relationship — enforced by license discipline rather than only by contract.

**To the tenant, the manager owes what the statute owes.** The duties in Property Code ch. 92 run
against the landlord, and § 92.001(2) tells us the manager is not the landlord unless the manager
purports to be the owner, lessor or sublessor in a lease. But the manager is the person through whom
those duties are performed or breached: the manager receives the repair notice under § 92.052(a) at
the place rent is paid, the manager runs the clock under § 92.056, the manager holds and refunds the
security deposit under § 92.103(a) "on or before the 30th day after the date the tenant surrenders the
premises," the manager prepares the "written description and itemized list of all deductions" required
by § 92.104(c), and the manager is the person who does or does not honor § 92.104(b)'s rule that no
portion of a deposit may be retained "to cover normal wear and tear." The penalties fall on the
landlord — § 92.109(a) makes a landlord who in bad faith retains a deposit liable for one hundred
dollars, three times the portion wrongfully withheld, and the tenant's reasonable attorney's fees, and
§ 92.109(d) presumes bad faith where the landlord fails either to return the deposit or to provide the
written description and itemization on or before the thirtieth day after surrender — but they fall
because of what the manager did.

Three ch. 92 provisions exist precisely because managers under pressure have been tempted to shorten
the process. Section 92.008(a) and (b) prohibit a landlord or a landlord's agent from interrupting
utility service, whether paid directly to the utility by the tenant or furnished by the landlord as an
incident of the tenancy, "unless the interruption results from bona fide repairs, construction, or an
emergency," subject to the narrow and heavily conditioned exception in § 92.008(h) for submetered or
allocated electricity; § 92.008(f) gives the tenant possession or termination plus actual damages, one
month's rent plus $1,000, fees and costs, and § 92.008(g) voids any waiver. Section 92.0081(b)
prohibits intentionally preventing a tenant from entering the premises except by judicial process,
subject to stated exceptions including a lock change for a delinquent tenant, and then only under the
notice requirements of § 92.0081(c) and (d); § 92.0081(f) requires that the tenant be given a key
"without regard to whether the tenant pays the delinquent rent," and § 92.0081(h) provides a civil
penalty of one month's rent plus $1,000 with actual damages, costs and fees. Section 92.331 prohibits
retaliation — eviction, deprivation of the premises, decreased services, a rent increase, lease
termination, or a bad-faith course of conduct materially interfering with the tenant's rights — within
six months after the tenant exercises a right, gives a repair notice, complains in good faith to a
code authority, utility or civic agency, or participates in a tenant organization; § 92.332 preserves
ordinary grounds such as delinquency and material breach; and § 92.333 gives a civil penalty of one
month's rent plus $500 with actual damages, costs and fees. These are the moments when a frustrated
owner tells a manager to "just change the locks" or "shut the water off."

So how does a competent manager honor both sets of obligations? By recognizing that they conflict less
often than they appear to. The manager's loyalty to the owner is a duty to advance the owner's
*lawful* interests, and an owner's interests are never served by exposure to the penalties above. When
an owner asks for something the law forbids, the loyal answer and the lawful answer are the same:
explain the provision, explain the exposure, and decline. Section 1101.652(b)(2)'s reference to
untrustworthiness and (b)(1)'s reference to incompetence both cut against a manager who follows an
unlawful instruction, and § 1101.803 places the broker on the hook for it.

Where genuine tension remains, it is usually about candor rather than legality. The workable
discipline is to be honest with everyone and to keep no confidence about facts the other party is
entitled to know. Section 1101.652(b)(7)'s requirement to make clear the party for whom the license
holder is acting is the starting point: a tenant who knows the manager works for the owner is not
misled by the manager advancing the owner's interests. What misleads is a manager who lets the tenant
believe otherwise.

**Continuing competence**

The last professional obligation is to stay current, and here Texas law does speak. Occupations Code
§ 1101.455(b) requires a license holder not subject to the § 1101.454 education requirements to attend
continuing education courses approved by the Commission during the term of the current license, in a
number of classroom hours the Commission prescribes by rule, "which must be at least 15 classroom
hours." Section 1101.455(e) requires at least eight of those hours to provide current information on
listed legal topics, and the list reads like a property manager's job description: commission rules;
**fair housing laws**; **Property Code issues, including landlord-tenant law**; agency law; antitrust
laws; the DTPA subchapter; disclosures to buyers, landlords, tenants and sellers; unauthorized
practice of law; case studies involving violations; and "the ethical requirements of engaging in real
estate brokerage," among others. Section 1101.455(f) allows the remaining hours to be devoted to other
approved real estate-related topics.

Beyond the statutory minimum, competence here decays quickly for a specific reason: the Property Code
chapters a manager lives inside are amended regularly, and the amendments are usually procedural — a
notice period, a fee cap, a required disclosure. Those are exactly the details a manager operates on
autopilot. As a matter of practice and not of legal requirement, managers who stay out of trouble
re-read the operative chapters periodically, keep lease forms and notice templates under review rather
than treating them as settled, and treat every complaint as information about a system rather than
about a person.

### Applied exercise: The instruction you should refuse

You manage a 60-unit property in Texas for an out-of-state owner. Three things happen in one week.

1. A tenant delivers a written notice at the leasing office, where rent is paid, that raw sewage is
   backing up into her bathtub. She is current on rent. The owner tells you to wait until next month's
   budget cycle. Which provisions of §§ 92.052, 92.056 and 92.0561 do you cite to the owner, and what
   is the tenant entitled to do if you wait?
2. Another tenant is eleven days delinquent. The owner instructs you to shut off the water to that
   unit "until he pays." Identify the provision that answers this and the tenant's remedy.
3. A third tenant complained to the city code office about a stairwell railing three weeks ago. The
   owner now wants that tenant's lease terminated at the end of the term "because he's a
   troublemaker." Which subchapter applies, what is the time window, and what is the penalty?
4. For each instruction, state whether refusing it is disloyal to the owner. Support your answer from

### Module recap

This module built one argument in three steps. It began with the work — leasing, rent collection,
maintenance, accounting, reporting, tenant relations and compliance — and showed that the work itself
is what triggers Texas regulation. Because a manager performs listed acts for another person for
compensation, Occupations Code § 1101.002(1)(A) makes leasing, negotiating leases, listing property
for lease and controlling the acceptance or deposit of single-family rent acts of a broker, and
§ 1101.351 forbids performing them without a license. Section 1101.005 then supplies the exemptions
that decide most real cases: the on-site manager of an apartment complex under subdivision (7), and
the owner or the owner's employee leasing the owner's own real estate under subdivision (8). Because
a manager holds other people's money, § 1101.652(b)(9), (10), (30) and (31) convert sloppy trust
accounting into license discipline: deposit promptly in a proper account, never commingle, never
disburse early, always account and remit within a reasonable time.

The second unit took that licensed manager into the operating relationship. The management agreement
sets the scope of authority, the term — noting that § 1101.652(b)(12) expressly carves property
management contracts out of the definite-termination-date requirement that governs listing
agreements — the compensation, the owner's obligations, indemnification, and the accounting mechanics
that make compliance automatic. The operating statement gave the arithmetic that every owner
conversation uses: gross potential rent less vacancy and collection loss yields effective gross
income, less operating expenses yields net operating income, with debt service and capital
expenditures excluded from operating expenses by definition. Rent is set from market data with
concessions converted to effective rent. The maintenance program is organized into routine,
preventive, corrective and emergency work because Property Code § 92.056(d)'s rebuttable presumption
that seven days is a reasonable time sets the tempo. Tenant selection is written, consistent and
documented because Property Code §§ 301.021, 301.022, 301.024 and 301.025, 42 U.S.C. § 3604, and
Occupations Code § 1101.652(b)(32) all reach the same conduct from different directions.

The third unit placed the manager in a profession and in an ethical position. Designations and trade
memberships are voluntary practice, required by no statute read for this course; the license is the
legal credential. The manager's agency duties run to the owner under § 1101.557 and the § 1101.652(b)
disciplinary grounds, while the ch. 92 duties — repair, deposits, security devices, disclosure of
ownership and management, the utility-interruption and lockout prohibitions, and the retaliation
rules — protect the tenant and are performed or breached through the manager's hands. Honoring both is
usually a matter of recognizing that an owner's lawful interest and a tenant's statutory right point
the same way, and of refusing, clearly and on the record, the instruction that does not.

You should now be able to decide whether a given person needs a Texas license to manage a given
property, state the trust-money rules that govern the money that passes through a manager's hands,
build and read an operating statement through to net operating income, connect the ch. 92 repair and
deposit obligations to the systems that satisfy them, and distinguish what Texas law requires of a
property manager from what the profession merely expects.
