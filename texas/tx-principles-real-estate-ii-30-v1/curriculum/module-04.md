# Texas Principles of Real Estate II — Module 04

## TREC Topic: Leases

**Prescribed time: 130 minutes.** This module carries the five units the Commission's PRINS2-1 course approval form places under *Leases*. The form gives this topic no scope note, so none is quoted here; the units below, and the minutes assigned to each, are the form's own.

**Learning objectives.** After completing this module you will be able to: distinguish the four leasehold estates and state how each is created and how each terminates; identify which acts in a lease transaction require a Texas real estate license and which fall inside a statutory exemption; explain the function of each common lease provision and identify the party each one protects; state the landlord's statutory repair duty, the tenant's notice obligations, the security deposit deadlines, and the penalties for bad faith under Texas Property Code chapter 92; and compute rent under a percentage lease and classify a lease as gross, net, ground, index, or sale-leaseback.

## Unit: Leasehold Estates

A lease does two things at once. It is a **contract** — a set of mutual promises about rent, repairs, conduct, and term — and it is a **conveyance**, because it transfers a possessory interest in land from the owner to the tenant for a period of time. Texas law recognizes the second half of that description explicitly. Occupations Code § 1101.002(5) defines "real estate" for licensing purposes as "any interest in real property, **including a leasehold**, located in or outside this state." A leasehold is real estate. When a landlord signs a lease, the landlord has parted with something, not merely promised something.

What the landlord parts with is exclusive possession. What the landlord keeps is the **reversion** — the right to have possession come back at the end of the term. The tenant's interest is the **leasehold estate**; the landlord's remaining interest is the **reversionary estate**, sometimes just called the landlord's reversion. These two interests together account for the whole of the ownership. A common error is to say the landlord "still owns the property and the tenant just rents it," as though the tenant held nothing. During the term, the tenant holds the present right to possess, and the landlord's right to enter is limited to whatever the lease and the statutes allow. Property Code § 92.001(5) reflects how large that possessory interest can be: "premises" means "a tenant's rental unit, any area or facility the lease authorizes the tenant to use, and the appurtenances, grounds, and facilities held out for the use of tenants generally."

Property law sorts leasehold estates into four categories. Those four labels are general property-law doctrine rather than definitions written into the Texas Property Code; the Code instead supplies the operating rules — how notice is given, when a tenancy ends, what a landlord must do — that make the categories matter in practice. Learn the four names, but hang the Texas rules on them.

**The tenancy for years** (also called an **estate for years**) is a leasehold with a fixed beginning and a fixed ending stated in advance. The name is misleading: a tenancy for years can run for six months, or eighteen months, or ten years. What defines it is that the ending date is known when the lease is made. A twelve-month apartment lease running from March 1 to the last day of February is a tenancy for years. It is created by agreement of the parties, and Property Code § 92.001(3) tells you that for a residential dwelling that agreement can be oral: "'Lease' means any written or oral agreement between a landlord and tenant that establishes or modifies the terms, conditions, rules, or other provisions regarding the use and occupancy of a dwelling." Oral, however, does not mean enforceable at any length. Business & Commerce Code § 26.01 — Texas's statute of frauds — provides that a promise or agreement is "not enforceable unless the promise or agreement, or a memorandum of it, is (1) in writing; and (2) signed by the person to be charged," and § 26.01(b)(5) applies that requirement to "a lease of real estate for a term longer than one year." So a fourteen-month oral lease is exposed in a way that an eleven-month oral lease is not.

A tenancy for years terminates by the simple expiration of its stated term. Because the ending date was fixed at the start, no notice of termination is required to end it; the parties agreed to the end date when they agreed to everything else. That is the single most useful thing to remember about this estate.

**The periodic tenancy** runs from period to period — month to month is the common form in Texas residential leasing, but week to week and year to year are also periodic — and it renews automatically at the end of each period until one party gives notice. There is no ending date fixed at the outset. Chapter 91 of the Property Code, which supplies rules applicable to landlords and tenants generally, governs how a Texas month-to-month tenancy ends. Section 91.001(a): "A monthly tenancy or a tenancy from month to month may be terminated by the tenant or the landlord giving notice of termination to the other." Either side may end it; neither needs cause. Section 91.001(b) then fixes the effective date: "If a notice of termination is given under Subsection (a) and if the rent-paying period is at least one month, the tenancy terminates on whichever of the following days is the later: (1) the day given in the notice for termination; or (2) one month after the day on which the notice is given." Where the rent-paying period is shorter than a month, § 91.001(c) substitutes a period equal in days to the rent-paying period.

Two refinements matter. First, § 91.001(d) provides that if the tenancy terminates on a day that does not line up with the beginning or end of a rent-paying period, "the tenant is liable for rent only up to the date of termination" — the tenant does not owe a whole final month for occupying four days of it. Second, § 91.001(e) makes the whole scheme a default rather than a mandate: subsections (a) through (d) "do not apply if: (1) a landlord and a tenant have agreed in an instrument signed by both parties on a different period of notice to terminate the tenancy or that no notice is required; or (2) there is a breach of contract recognized by law." A lease that requires sixty days' written notice to terminate a month-to-month tenancy is enforceable on its own terms because § 91.001(e)(1) says so. Read the lease first, then fall back on § 91.001.

**The tenancy at will** exists when a person occupies with the owner's consent but with no fixed term and no agreed periodic term — possession continues at the will of both parties and may be ended by either. It is the loosest of the four. Because a tenancy at will lacks the fixed end date of a tenancy for years and the automatic renewal of a periodic tenancy, it is the estate most likely to be created by accident: an owner lets a relative move in with no agreement about rent or duration, or a seller lets a buyer take possession informally before closing. In residential settings the distinction has real consequences, because chapter 92's protections attach to a "tenant," defined in § 92.001(6) as "a person who is authorized by a lease to occupy a dwelling to the exclusion of others," and § 92.001(3) counts an oral understanding as a lease.

**The tenancy at sufferance** is the odd one out, because it is not consensual. It arises when a tenant who entered lawfully stays past the end of the right to possess — the classic **holdover**. The tenant is no longer a rightful possessor and is not yet a trespasser who entered wrongfully; the tenant is simply there, at the sufferance of the owner, until the owner either evicts or accepts the situation and creates a new tenancy. Texas statutes assume this category without naming it. Property Code § 92.332(b) lists circumstances that are "valid grounds for eviction or lease termination in any event," including, at (b)(4), that "the tenant holds over after giving notice of termination or intent to vacate," and at (b)(5), that "the tenant holds over after the landlord gives notice of termination at the end of the rental term."

Chapter 91 adds three rules that cut across all four estates. Section 91.005 provides: "During the term of a lease, the tenant may not rent the leasehold to any other person without the prior consent of the landlord." Subletting is prohibited by statute unless the landlord consents — the opposite of what many students assume. Section 91.004(a) makes the landlord "liable to the tenant for damages resulting from the failure" if the landlord of a non-defaulting tenant "fails to comply in any respect with the lease agreement," and § 91.004(b) gives that tenant "a lien on the landlord's nonexempt property in the tenant's possession and on the rent due to the landlord under the lease." And § 91.006(a) imposes a duty on the landlord: "A landlord has a duty to mitigate damages if a tenant abandons the leased premises in violation of the lease." Section 91.006(b) makes that duty non-waivable — "A provision of a lease that purports to waive a right or to exempt a landlord from a liability or duty under this section is void." A landlord whose tenant walks out in month four of a twelve-month lease may not simply sit on the vacant unit and bill for the remaining eight months.

### Applied exercise: Which estate, and how does it end?

A Fort Worth owner and a tenant sign a written lease for a duplex unit running September 1 through August 31, at $1,450 per month. The lease says nothing about what happens at the end. On August 31 the tenant does not move out; the tenant tenders $1,450 on September 1 and the owner deposits it.

1. What leasehold estate did the September 1–August 31 lease create, and what notice was required to end it on August 31?
2. On the morning of August 31, before any payment is tendered, how would you classify the tenant's status if the tenant stays? Which subdivision of § 92.332(b) treats that status as a valid ground for eviction?
3. If the owner instead agreed in writing to let the tenant continue month to month at the same rent, and in November the owner wanted the tenant out, what does § 91.001(b) require, and on what date could the tenancy terminate if notice were given on November 20?
4. The lease in this problem is silent on subletting. May the tenant sublet the unit? Cite the section that answers it.

## Unit: Leasing Real Estate

Leasing is brokerage. That single sentence resolves most of the confusion license holders have about rental work. Occupations Code § 1101.002(1)(A) defines a "broker" as a person who, "in exchange for a commission or other valuable consideration or with the expectation of receiving a commission or other valuable consideration, performs for another person" any of a list of acts. Leasing appears throughout that list: (i) "sells, exchanges, purchases, or **leases** real estate"; (ii) "offers to sell, exchange, purchase, or lease real estate"; (iii) "negotiates or attempts to negotiate the listing, sale, exchange, purchase, or **lease** of real estate"; (iv) "lists or offers, attempts, or agrees to list real estate for sale, **lease**, or exchange"; (vii) "aids or offers or attempts to aid in locating or obtaining real estate for purchase or **lease**"; (viii) "procures or assists in procuring a prospect to effect the sale, exchange, or **lease** of real estate"; and (ix) "procures or assists in procuring property to effect the sale, exchange, or lease of real estate."

Subdivision (x) reaches further than the others and catches people who never think of themselves as agents: a broker also means a person who "**controls the acceptance or deposit of rent from a resident of a single-family residential real property unit**." That is a rent-collection act, not a marketing act. A person who, for compensation, takes in and banks the rent on someone else's rent house is performing a broker's act under § 1101.002(1)(A)(x) — even if that person never showed the house, never advertised it, and never negotiated a term.

Section 1101.004(a) ties the definition to conduct: "A person is engaged in real estate brokerage if the person, with the expectation of receiving valuable consideration, directly or indirectly performs or offers, attempts, or agrees to perform for another person any act described by Section 1101.002(1), as a part of a transaction or as an entire transaction." Note "as a part of a transaction." Doing one piece of a lease deal for pay is enough. And § 1101.351(a) closes the loop: unless a person holds a license, the person may not "(1) act as or represent that the person is a broker or sales agent; or (2) act as a residential rental locator." A **residential rental locator** is defined in § 1101.002(6) as "a person who offers for consideration to locate a unit in an apartment complex for lease to a prospective tenant"; the definition expressly excludes "an owner who offers to locate a unit in the owner's complex." Locating apartments for a fee is licensed activity in Texas in its own right. Section 1101.351(c) adds the sponsorship rule: "A licensed sales agent may not engage or attempt to engage in real estate brokerage unless the sales agent is sponsored by a licensed broker and is acting for that broker." A sales agent's leasing work is the broker's work.

Against that broad definition, § 1101.005 sets out who the chapter does not apply to. Four of its subdivisions matter constantly in leasing. Section 1101.005(8) exempts "**an owner or the owner's employee who leases the owner's improved or unimproved real estate**." An owner leasing her own duplex needs no license, and neither does the salaried employee who leases it for her. Section 1101.005(7) exempts "an on-site manager of an apartment complex" — the leasing office staff at a conventional apartment community are outside chapter 1101 by name. Section 1101.005(9)(C) exempts a transaction involving "the lease or management of a hotel or motel." And § 1101.005(10) and (11) exempt leases of real estate owned by a limited partnership or a limited liability company when handled by a general partner, manager, managing member, or an employee who negotiates the transaction in the course of employment.

Draw the boundary carefully, because the exemptions are about **whose property it is**, not about how modest the transaction is. The owner exemption is the owner's; it does not travel to an independent contractor the owner hires, and it does not cover leasing someone else's property. A person who manages four houses for four different owners is not "an owner or the owner's employee" as to any of them. A common error is to assume that a small lease, a short lease, or a lease of a single house is too minor to require a license. Nothing in § 1101.002(1) or § 1101.005 turns on size.

What the license holder actually does in a lease transaction tracks the sale side closely. On the landlord's side: analyzing the rental market for the property, advising on asking rent and lease term as a matter of business judgment, marketing and advertising the unit, showing it, receiving and forwarding applications, coordinating the landlord's screening decision, preparing the lease on the form the parties have agreed to use, coordinating move-in condition documentation, and handling the money. On the tenant's side: identifying suitable properties, arranging showings, disclosing the representation relationship, assisting with the application, and negotiating terms. As a matter of practice, most residential leasing compensation in Texas is paid by the landlord out of the first month's rent or as a percentage of the lease term's rent, and commercial leasing commissions are commonly a percentage of the total rent over the term — those are market customs, not legal requirements, and nothing in the held sources sets a rate.

Handling money is where leasing work most often generates discipline. Occupations Code § 1101.652(b) lists grounds on which the Commission may suspend or revoke a license, and four of them are aimed squarely at funds. Under (b)(9), a license holder who "fails within a reasonable time to properly account for or remit money that is received by the license holder and that belongs to another person" is exposed. Under (b)(10), so is one who "commingles money that belongs to another person with the license holder's own money." Under (b)(30), a license holder must not fail "to deposit, within a reasonable time, money the license holder receives as escrow or trust funds in a real estate transaction: (A) in trust with a title company authorized to do business in this state; or (B) in a custodial, trust, or escrow account maintained for that purpose in a banking institution authorized to do business in this state." And under (b)(31), the license holder must not disburse money deposited in such an account "before the completion or termination of the real estate transaction." Application deposits, security deposits, and rent that come into a brokerage's hands belong to someone else. They go into a trust or escrow account, they are accounted for, and they are not spent.

Section 1101.652(b)(32) applies to leasing in terms: a license holder may not discriminate "against an owner, potential buyer, **landlord, or potential tenant** on the basis of race, color, religion, sex, disability, familial status, national origin, or ancestry, including directing a prospective buyer or tenant interested in equivalent properties to a different area based on" any of those characteristics. Steering a rental prospect is a licensing violation as well as a fair housing violation.

## Unit: Common Lease Provisions

A lease is read clause by clause, and each clause is doing a job. Learn to ask, for every provision, what risk it allocates and to whom.

**Parties.** The lease names the landlord and every tenant. Getting this right is not clerical. Property Code § 92.001(2) defines "landlord" as "the owner, lessor, or sublessor of a dwelling, but does not include a manager or agent of the landlord unless the manager or agent purports to be the owner, lessor, or sublessor in an oral or written lease." A property manager who signs as though he were the owner has made himself the landlord for chapter 92 purposes. Every adult occupant named as a tenant is jointly on the hook for the rent; an occupant not named is not a tenant and, under § 92.001(6), may not hold the tenant's statutory remedies. Where a parent or other person guarantees the lease, § 92.021(a) limits that person's exposure: "A person other than a tenant who guarantees a lease is liable only for the original lease term," unless the original lease contains the specific renewal-guaranty language § 92.021(b) requires.

**Premises.** The clause identifies exactly what the tenant gets — the unit, and what else. Section 92.001(5)'s definition of "premises" reaches "any area or facility the lease authorizes the tenant to use, and the appurtenances, grounds, and facilities held out for the use of tenants generally," so a lease that promises a garage bay, a storage closet, or pool access has enlarged the premises and, with it, the area the landlord's duties cover.

**Term.** The term clause states the beginning and ending dates, which is what makes the lease a tenancy for years rather than a periodic tenancy, and it usually states what happens at expiration. Under Business & Commerce Code § 26.01(b)(5), a term longer than one year must be in a signed writing to be enforceable.

**Rent, and when it is due.** The clause fixes the amount, the due date, the place and method of payment, and whether partial payments are accepted. Method matters in Texas: Property Code § 92.011(a) provides that "a landlord shall accept a tenant's timely cash rental payment unless a written lease between the landlord and tenant requires the tenant to make rental payments by check, money order, or other traceable or negotiable instrument." A landlord who wants to refuse cash must say so in the written lease. A landlord who does take cash must, under § 92.011(b), give a written receipt and record the payment date and amount in a record book.

**Late fees.** Section 92.019(a) permits a late fee only if "(1) notice of the fee is included in a written lease; (2) the fee is reasonable; and (3) any portion of the tenant's rent has remained unpaid two full days after the date the rent was originally due." Section 92.019(a-1) supplies a safe harbor: a fee is considered reasonable if it is not more than 12 percent of the rent for the rental period for a dwelling in a structure with not more than four dwelling units, or 10 percent for a structure with more than four; a larger fee is permitted only up to the landlord's uncertain damages related to the late payment. Under § 92.019(b), an initial fee plus a daily fee count as a single late fee. A landlord who violates the section is liable under § 92.019(c) for "$100, three times the amount of the late fee collected in violation of this section, and the tenant's reasonable attorney's fees," and § 92.019(d) voids any lease provision purporting to waive the section.

**Security deposit.** The clause states the amount, what it secures, and the conditions on its return. Its Texas rules are the subject of the next unit; note here only that § 92.103(b) makes a lease requirement that the tenant give advance notice of surrender "effective only if the requirement is underlined or is printed in conspicuous bold print in the lease." Formatting is substantive.

**Use and occupancy limits.** The use clause confines the premises to residential use and typically bars business use, and the occupancy clause names who may live there and for how long a guest may stay. Texas caps occupancy by statute: § 92.010(a) provides that "the maximum number of adults that a landlord may allow to occupy a dwelling is three times the number of bedrooms in the dwelling," with "adult" meaning a person 18 or older and "bedroom" meaning "an area of a dwelling intended as sleeping quarters," excluding kitchens, dining rooms, bathrooms, living rooms, utility rooms, and closets. Section 92.010(b) requires a higher rate where a fair housing law requires one, and permits one for an adult "seeking temporary sanctuary from family violence" for a period not exceeding one month.

**Maintenance and repair allocation.** This clause divides responsibility: landlord for systems and structure, tenant for cleanliness, light bulbs, filters, and damage the tenant causes. The allocation is not fully negotiable in a Texas residential lease. Section 92.006(c) provides that the landlord's duties and tenant's remedies under subchapter B "may not be waived except as provided in Subsections (d), (e), and (f)." Subsection (f) permits an agreement making the tenant pay for repair of damage from wastewater stoppages caused by foreign or improper objects in lines exclusively serving the tenant's dwelling, damage to doors, windows, or screens, and damage from windows or doors left open — but only if the agreement meets the § 92.006(e)(4) formalities: the lease is in writing, the repair agreement is "either underlined or printed in boldface in the lease or in a separate written addendum," the agreement is "specific and clear," and it is "made knowingly, voluntarily, and for consideration."

**Alterations.** The clause bars painting, holes, fixtures, and installations without consent, and states who owns improvements at the end. One Texas rule intersects it: § 92.163 provides that a security device installed, changed, or rekeyed under subchapter D "becomes a fixture of the dwelling," and, except for the repair-and-deduct remedies, a tenant "may not remove, change, rekey, replace, or alter a security device" without the landlord's permission.

**Entry and access.** The clause states when the landlord may enter and on what notice. Texas backstops the tenant's possession with two hard rules discussed in the next unit — § 92.008 on utility interruption and § 92.0081 on removing doors and locks and excluding the tenant.

**Assignment and subletting.** An **assignment** transfers the tenant's entire remaining interest to a third party; a **sublease** transfers something less, leaving the original tenant as the sublessor with a reversion of his own. In Texas, § 91.005 supplies the default and it is restrictive: during the lease term "the tenant may not rent the leasehold to any other person without the prior consent of the landlord."

**Default and remedies.** The clause defines what counts as a breach and what the landlord may do. Chapter 91 limits the landlord's downside management: § 91.006(a)'s non-waivable duty to mitigate means an abandonment clause cannot promise the landlord the whole remaining term without effort to re-let.

**Holdover.** The clause states the consequence of staying past the term — commonly a stated daily or monthly holdover rate. Section 92.332(b)(4) and (5) confirm that holding over after either party's notice is a valid ground for eviction and is not retaliation.

**Renewal.** The clause states whether the lease renews automatically, converts to month to month, or simply ends. Where it converts, § 91.001's notice rules take over. Finally, § 92.024(a) requires that "not later than the third business day after the date the lease is signed by each party to the lease, a landlord shall provide at least one complete copy of the lease to at least one tenant who is a party to the lease."

## Unit: Texas Landlord & Tenant Statutes

Property Code chapter 92 is the core of Texas residential landlord-tenant law, and § 92.002 fixes its reach: "This chapter applies only to the relationship between landlords and tenants of residential rental property." Commercial leases are governed by their own terms and by chapter 91. Do not carry chapter 92's deadlines into a commercial deal.

**Waiver.** Start with § 92.006, which tells you which of the rules below can be bargained away. Section 92.006(a): a landlord's duty or a tenant's remedy "concerning security deposits, security devices, the landlord's disclosure of ownership and management, or utility cutoffs... **may not be waived**." Those duties may be "enlarged only by specific written agreement" (§ 92.006(b)), and § 92.006(h) bars waiver of the tenant's right to a jury trial. A lease clause contradicting these sections is not merely unwise; as to the covered duties it is ineffective.

**The duty to repair.** Section 92.052(a) states it: "A landlord shall make a diligent effort to repair or remedy a condition if: (1) the tenant specifies the condition in a notice to the person to whom or to the place where rent is normally paid; (2) the tenant is not delinquent in the payment of rent at the time notice is given; and (3) the condition: (A) materially affects the physical health or safety of an ordinary tenant; or (B) arises from the landlord's failure to provide and maintain in good operating condition a device to supply hot water of a minimum temperature of 120 degrees Fahrenheit."

Three things follow. The trigger is notice to the rent-payment address, not to whoever the tenant happens to reach — though § 92.060 makes a managing agent, leasing agent, or resident manager the landlord's agent for notice. The tenant must be current in rent when the notice is given; a tenant who withholds rent first and complains second has forfeited the statute. And the standard is not "annoying" but a condition materially affecting the physical health or safety of an ordinary tenant, with the 120-degree hot water device named so no one has to argue about it. Section 92.052(b) removes the duty where the condition was caused by the tenant, an occupant, a family member, or a guest, "unless the condition was caused by normal wear and tear" — defined in § 92.001(4) as "deterioration that results from the intended use of a dwelling," excluding "deterioration that results from negligence, carelessness, accident, or abuse." Under § 92.052(d), the tenant's notice "must be in writing only if the tenant's lease is in writing and requires written notice."

**Notice mechanics and time for repair.** Section 92.056(b) sets six conditions for landlord liability, and rewards the tenant who sends notice trackably. Liability attaches only if the tenant gave notice to the rent-payment address; the condition materially affects health or safety; the tenant gave "a subsequent written notice to repair or remedy the condition after a reasonable time to repair" **or** sent the first notice "by certified mail, return receipt requested, by registered mail, or by another form of mail that allows tracking of delivery"; the landlord has had a reasonable time to repair; the landlord "has not made a diligent effort" to repair; and the tenant "was not delinquent in the payment of rent at the time any notice required by this subsection was given." The tracked-mail option is what lets a single notice do the work of two.

How long is a reasonable time? Section 92.056(d): "there is a rebuttable presumption that seven days is a reasonable time," rebuttable by considering the date of receipt, "the severity and nature of the condition, and the reasonable availability of materials and labor." Seven days is a presumption, not a deadline; a burst sewer line and a slow-closing window are not on the same clock. Section 92.053(b) adds pressure: if the landlord gives no written explanation for delay "on or before the fifth day after receiving from the tenant a written demand for an explanation," the landlord carries the burden of proving diligence.

**The tenant's remedies.** Under § 92.056(e) a tenant to whom the landlord is liable may terminate the lease, have the condition repaired under § 92.0561, deduct the repair cost from rent "without necessity of judicial action," and obtain judicial remedies under § 92.0563. A tenant who terminates gets a pro rata rent refund and the deposit back but gives up repair-and-deduct (§ 92.056(f)). Section 92.056(g) requires the lease to carry "language in underlined or bold print" informing the tenant of these remedies.

Repair and deduct is capped: the deduction "may not exceed the amount of one month's rent under the lease or $500, whichever is greater" (§ 92.0561(b)), and repeated use is allowed so long as the monthly total stays within that cap. It reaches only the conditions listed in § 92.0561(d)(3) — failure to remedy "the backup or overflow of raw sewage inside the tenant's dwelling or the flooding from broken pipes or natural drainage inside the dwelling," a total cessation of potable water the landlord agreed to furnish, inadequate heat or cooled air certified in writing by a local official, or any condition a local official has certified to materially affect health or safety. Waiting periods under § 92.0561(e) then run: immediately for sewage or flooding; three days after notice of intent to repair for a water cessation or inadequate heat or cooling; seven days otherwise. Repairs must be made by "an independent company, contractor, or repairman," not by the tenant or the tenant's family or employees (§ 92.0561(f)), and the bill and receipt go to the landlord with the rent balance (§ 92.0561(j)). A landlord who is genuinely stuck may deliver a sworn **affidavit for delay** under § 92.0562, buying 15 days where parts cannot be obtained without the landlord's fault or 30 days after a natural disaster, capped at six months.

Judicial remedies under § 92.0563(a) include an order to repair, a rent reduction "in proportion to the reduced rental value," "a civil penalty of one month's rent plus $500," actual damages, court costs, and attorney's fees. The traffic runs both ways: under § 92.058(a), a tenant who withholds rent or deducts in bad-faith violation of the subchapter, after written warning, owes the landlord "a civil penalty of one month's rent plus $500."

**Security deposits.** Subchapter C applies "to all residential leases" (§ 92.101). Section 92.102: "A security deposit is any advance of money, other than a rental application deposit or an advance payment of rent, that is intended primarily to secure performance under a lease of a dwelling." The label on the money does not control; its function does.

The deadline is § 92.103(a): "Except as provided by Section 92.107, the landlord shall refund a security deposit to the tenant **on or before the 30th day after the date the tenant surrenders the premises**." Section 92.107(a) is the exception — no obligation to refund or itemize "until the tenant gives the landlord a written statement of the tenant's forwarding address" — but § 92.107(b) provides the tenant "does not forfeit the right to a refund... merely for failing to give a forwarding address." Under § 92.1041 the landlord is presumed to have complied if the refund or accounting is mailed and postmarked on or before the required date.

Section 92.104 governs deductions. Subsection (a) permits deduction of "damages and charges for which the tenant is legally liable under the lease or as a result of breaching the lease." Subsection (b) is flat: "The landlord may not retain any portion of a security deposit to cover normal wear and tear." Subsection (c) requires the landlord who retains any part to give the tenant the balance "together with a written description and itemized list of all deductions," with one exception: no itemization is required if "(1) the tenant owes rent when he surrenders possession of the premises; and (2) there is no controversy concerning the amount of rent owed."

The consequences of getting this wrong are the most heavily tested figures in Texas residential law. Section 92.109(a): "A landlord who **in bad faith** retains a security deposit in violation of this subchapter is liable for an amount equal to the sum of $100, three times the portion of the deposit wrongfully withheld, and the tenant's reasonable attorney's fees in a suit to recover the deposit." Section 92.109(b): a landlord who in bad faith fails to give the written description and itemized list "forfeits the right to withhold any portion of the security deposit or to bring suit against the tenant for damages to the premises" and owes the tenant's attorney's fees. Section 92.109(c) puts on the landlord the burden of proving retention "was reasonable." And § 92.109(d) makes the deadline bite: a landlord who fails either to refund or to itemize "on or before the 30th day after the date the tenant surrenders possession is presumed to have acted in bad faith."

Tenants face a matching prohibition. Section 92.108(a): "The tenant may not withhold payment of any portion of the last month's rent on grounds that the security deposit is security for unpaid rent"; a tenant who does is presumed to act in bad faith and owes "three times the rent wrongfully withheld" plus the landlord's attorney's fees. Section 92.106 requires accurate deposit records, and under § 92.105(a) a new owner taking title by sale, assignment, death, receivership, or bankruptcy "is liable for the return of security deposits... from the date title to the premises is acquired."

**Security devices.** Subchapter D requires hardware without the tenant having to ask. Under § 92.153(a) a dwelling must have a window latch on each exterior window; a doorknob lock or keyed dead bolt on each exterior door; a sliding door pin lock and either a sliding door handle latch or security bar on each exterior sliding glass door; and "a keyless bolting device and a door viewer on each exterior door." These "must be installed at the landlord's expense" (§ 92.153(c)). Section 92.154 requires a keyed dead bolt or keyless bolting device to sit "not lower than 36 inches from the floor" and no higher than 48 inches if installed on or after September 1, 1993, with a bolt throw of at least one inch.

Rekeying runs on a clock: under § 92.156(a) a device operated by key, card, or combination "shall be rekeyed by the landlord at the landlord's expense **not later than the seventh day after each tenant turnover date**" — the date, per § 92.151(15), a tenant moves in "after all previous occupants have moved out." Further rekeying at the tenant's request is at the tenant's expense, with unlimited requests allowed. Section 92.158 requires the landlord to repair or replace an inoperable device on request, and § 92.159 lets that request be oral unless a written lease requires writing in underlined or boldfaced print. Section 92.161(a) presumes a reasonable compliance time of "not later than the seventh day after the date the request is received," shortened by § 92.161(c) to a presumed 72 hours where the tenant reports an unauthorized entry or attempt in the dwelling, one in another unit of the complex within the preceding two months, or a crime of personal violence in the complex in that period. Remedies under § 92.164(a) include install-and-deduct, unilateral termination without court proceedings after the statutory window, and suit for a court order, actual damages, punitive damages, "a civil penalty of one month's rent plus $500," costs, and fees.

**Disclosure of ownership and management.** Section 92.201(a) requires the landlord to disclose the name and street or post office box address of "the holder of record title, according to the deed records in the county clerk's office," and, where an off-site entity primarily manages the dwelling, the management company's name and street address. Disclosure may be made in writing "on or before the seventh day after the day the landlord receives the tenant's request," by continuous conspicuous posting within that same period, or by including it in the lease or written rules (§ 92.201(b)). Remedies under § 92.205(a) include a disclosure order, the tenant's actual costs of discovering the information, "a judgment against the landlord for one month's rent plus $100," costs and fees, and "unilateral termination of the lease without a court proceeding."

**Utility interruption and lockouts.** These are the two self-help temptations Texas forecloses. Under § 92.008(a) a landlord or landlord's agent "may not interrupt or cause the interruption of utility service paid for directly to the utility company by a tenant unless the interruption results from bona fide repairs, construction, or an emergency," and § 92.008(b) applies the same rule to water, wastewater, gas, or electric service the landlord furnishes as an incident of the tenancy. The tenant may then "either recover possession of the premises or terminate the lease" and recover "actual damages, one month's rent plus $1,000, reasonable attorney's fees, and court costs, less any delinquent rents" (§ 92.008(f)); any waiver is void (§ 92.008(g)). A narrow, heavily conditioned exception in § 92.008(h) lets a landlord who submeters or allocates electricity interrupt service for an unpaid electric bill, but only under a written lease and with the specific notices the statute spells out. Separately, § 92.301 gives the tenant termination, reconnection-payment, and deduction remedies when the *utility company* cuts off service the landlord agreed to furnish because the landlord did not pay the bill.

Section 92.0081(a) forbids removing "a door, window, or attic hatchway cover or a lock, latch, hinge, hinge pin, doorknob, or other mechanism connected to" one, or landlord-furnished furniture, fixtures, or appliances, "unless the landlord removes the item for a bona fide repair or replacement." Section 92.0081(b): "A landlord may not intentionally prevent a tenant from entering the leased premises **except by judicial process**" unless the exclusion results from bona fide repairs, construction, or an emergency; removing the contents of abandoned premises; or "changing the door locks on the door to the tenant's individual unit of a tenant who is delinquent in paying at least part of the rent."

That last exception is narrow and conditional. Under § 92.0081(d) the lockout right must be in the lease, the tenant must be delinquent, and the landlord must have mailed notice "not later than the fifth calendar day before" the lockout or hand-delivered or posted it inside the main entry door "not later than the third calendar day before," stating the earliest proposed date, the amount that would prevent the lockout, where rent may be paid, and, "in underlined or bold print, the tenant's right to receive a key to the new lock at any hour, regardless of whether the tenant pays the delinquent rent." Section 92.0081(c) requires a notice on the front door at the time of the lockout giving a 24-hour on-site key location or a 24-hour phone number with a key delivered within two hours, and § 92.0081(f) is unconditional: the landlord "must provide the tenant with a key to the changed lock... without regard to whether the tenant pays the delinquent rent." The landlord may not change locks "when the tenant or any other legal occupant is in the dwelling" or "more than once during a rental payment period" (§ 92.0081(k)), and may not lock the tenant out of common areas (§ 92.0081(e-1)). Violation costs possession or termination at the tenant's election plus "a civil penalty of one month's rent plus $1,000, actual damages, court costs, and reasonable attorney's fees" (§ 92.0081(h)), with an additional month's rent for refusing the key (§ 92.0081(i)). Section 92.009 gives the locked-out tenant a **writ of reentry** from the justice court on a sworn complaint; § 92.0091 gives an equivalent **writ of restoration of utility service**.

**Retaliation.** Section 92.331(a) forbids retaliation because the tenant in good faith exercises a right or remedy "granted to the tenant by lease, municipal ordinance, or federal or state statute"; gives a notice to repair or exercises a chapter 92 remedy; complains in good faith to a code-enforcement body, public utility, or civic or nonprofit agency about a code violation or utility problem; or "establishes, attempts to establish, or participates in a tenant organization." Section 92.331(b) makes the window explicit: a landlord may not, "**within six months after the date of the tenant's action**," retaliate by "(1) filing an eviction proceeding, except for the grounds stated by Section 92.332; (2) depriving the tenant of the use of the premises, except for reasons authorized by law; (3) decreasing services to the tenant; (4) increasing the tenant's rent or terminating the tenant's lease; or (5) engaging, in bad faith, in a course of conduct that materially interferes with the tenant's rights under the tenant's lease."

Section 92.332 preserves the landlord's legitimate grounds: raising rent "under an escalation clause in a written lease for utilities, taxes, or insurance" is not retaliation, nor is raising rent or reducing services "as part of a pattern... for an entire multidwelling project." Section 92.332(b) lists valid grounds "in any event," including rent delinquency when the notice to vacate is given or the eviction filed, intentional property damage or threats to personal safety, material breach other than holding over, and the holdover situations described earlier. The tenant's remedy under § 92.333 is "a civil penalty of one month's rent plus $500, actual damages, court costs, and reasonable attorney's fees," less delinquent rent; and under § 92.335, "in an eviction suit, retaliation by the landlord under Section 92.331 is a defense." A tenant who sues for retaliation in bad faith faces a mirror-image penalty under § 92.334(b).

**Applications.** Subchapter I governs the front of the transaction. An **application deposit** is refundable if the applicant is rejected; an **application fee** is "a nonrefundable sum of money... given to the landlord to offset the costs of screening an applicant" (§ 92.351(1), (1-a)). Section 92.3515(a) requires the landlord to make available, when the application is provided, printed notice of the tenant selection criteria and the grounds for denial; a landlord who rejects an applicant without having done so "shall return the application fee and any application deposit" (§ 92.3515(e)). Under § 92.352(a), an applicant "is deemed rejected by the landlord if the landlord does not give notice of acceptance... on or before the seventh day after" the completed application is submitted on the landlord's form. Bad-faith failure to refund costs "$100, three times the amount wrongfully retained, and the applicant's reasonable attorney's fees" (§ 92.354).

Finally, § 92.061 explains how this fits with older law: subchapter B's duties and remedies "are in lieu of existing common law and other statutory law warranties and duties of landlords for maintenance, repair, security, habitability, and nonretaliation." In Texas residential leasing, the statute is the field.

## Unit: Types of Leases

The four leasehold estates classify a lease by its **duration**. This unit classifies leases by their **rent structure** — who pays which of the property's operating costs, and how the rent is computed. These categories are commercial practice and market vocabulary rather than Texas statutory definitions; no held Texas source defines "triple net" or "percentage lease," and you should present them to a client as customary terms whose meaning comes from the lease document itself, never from a statute. That caution is not academic: two landlords in the same market can use "net lease" to mean two different cost splits. Always read the expense clauses.

**Gross lease.** The tenant pays a single stated rent and the landlord pays the operating expenses out of it — property taxes, hazard insurance, common area maintenance, and often some or all utilities. The tenant's cost is predictable; the landlord absorbs the risk that taxes or insurance rise mid-term. Most Texas residential leasing is effectively gross: the tenant pays rent, the landlord pays the tax bill and the property insurance. A **modified gross** lease, common in office space, is a middle ground in which the tenant reimburses the landlord for increases in specified expenses over a base year while the landlord continues to pay the base amounts.

**Net lease.** The tenant pays a base rent plus some of the operating costs directly. Practice describes the variants by counting the categories the tenant picks up. A **single net (N)** lease commonly adds property taxes to the tenant's obligation. A **double net (NN)** lease commonly adds taxes and insurance. A **triple net (NNN)** lease commonly adds taxes, insurance, and maintenance, leaving the landlord with structural and capital items and a nearly passive income stream. An **absolute net** or bondable lease pushes even structure and casualty risk onto the tenant. The economic logic is straightforward: the further right you move on that scale, the lower the base rent a landlord can command, because the tenant is absorbing costs the landlord would otherwise pay and the tenant is also absorbing the risk that those costs rise.

**Percentage lease.** Used in retail — shopping centers, malls, and pad sites — a percentage lease sets a base rent plus a percentage of the tenant's gross sales above a stated **breakpoint**. It aligns the landlord's return with the tenant's success, which is the point: the landlord's leasing, tenant mix, and center management contribute to the tenant's sales volume, and the percentage clause lets the landlord share in the result. A **natural breakpoint** is the sales volume at which the percentage rent alone would equal the base rent — base rent divided by the percentage rate. Parties may also negotiate an **artificial breakpoint** at any number they choose. Percentage leases customarily also define "gross sales" in detail (excluding, for instance, returns and sales taxes), require the tenant to report sales, give the landlord audit rights, and often include a continuous operation clause requiring the tenant to keep the store open — again, all contract terms, not statutory rules.

Here is a worked computation. **The numbers below are an illustration invented for teaching; no Texas statute sets base rents, percentage rates, or breakpoints.**

> A retail tenant leases 2,400 square feet in a Texas shopping center. Base rent is $4,000 per month, or $48,000 per year. The lease calls for percentage rent of 6 percent of annual gross sales above a stated breakpoint of $800,000. In the lease year just ended, the tenant's gross sales were $1,150,000.
>
> Step 1 — Base rent for the year: $4,000 × 12 = **$48,000**.
> Step 2 — Sales above the breakpoint: $1,150,000 − $800,000 = **$350,000**.
> Step 3 — Percentage rent: $350,000 × 0.06 = **$21,000**.
> Step 4 — Total rent for the year: $48,000 + $21,000 = **$69,000**.
>
> Check the breakpoint against the base rent. The natural breakpoint at a 6 percent rate would be $48,000 ÷ 0.06 = $800,000. Here the stated breakpoint equals the natural breakpoint, so the tenant pays percentage rent only on sales beyond the point at which 6 percent of sales would have covered the base rent by itself. Had the parties instead set an artificial breakpoint of $600,000, percentage rent would have been ($1,150,000 − $600,000) × 0.06 = $33,000, and total rent $81,000 — the same sales, $12,000 more rent, purely because of where the breakpoint was drawn.

A common error is to multiply the percentage rate by *total* sales rather than by sales *above* the breakpoint. In the illustration, 6 percent of $1,150,000 is $69,000, which would nearly double the percentage rent actually owed. Read the clause: overage rent is computed on the excess.

**Ground lease.** The tenant leases land only and builds the improvements at the tenant's expense, typically under a long term — long enough for the tenant to amortize the cost of a building. Ground leases are usually net in structure, with the tenant paying taxes, insurance, and all maintenance. The central negotiating issue is what happens to the improvements at the end of the term: ordinarily they revert to the landowner with the land, which is why ground lease terms are long and why lenders financing a leasehold improvement scrutinize the remaining term. Recall from the first unit that the tenant's interest here is real estate under Occupations Code § 1101.002(5), which is why a leasehold can be mortgaged, assigned, and brokered.

**Index lease and escalation clauses.** An **index lease** adjusts rent periodically by reference to a published index, so the rent moves with a measure of general price levels rather than by a flat negotiated step. An **escalation clause** more broadly is any provision that raises rent on a stated trigger — a fixed annual step-up, an index adjustment, or a pass-through of increases in a defined expense. Texas law notices this last variety in one narrow place: Property Code § 92.332(a)(1) provides that a landlord is not liable for retaliation for "increasing rent under an escalation clause in a written lease for utilities, taxes, or insurance." That provision does not authorize escalation clauses — the lease does — but it confirms that a written escalation clause for those three expenses is a recognized, legitimate reason for a rent increase and not evidence of retaliation.

**Sale-leaseback.** An owner-occupant sells the property to an investor and simultaneously leases it back, becoming the tenant of the property it formerly owned. The seller-tenant converts the equity locked in real estate into cash while keeping possession and operating continuity; the buyer-landlord acquires a property with a creditworthy tenant already in place and a lease already signed. Sale-leasebacks are commonly structured as long-term net leases. As a matter of practice, the seller-tenant trades an owned asset for a stream of rent obligations and gives up future appreciation; whether that trade is advantageous is an accounting, tax, and capital-allocation question for the client's CPA and counsel, not a question a license holder answers.

Two habits will keep you out of trouble across all of these. First, classify a lease by reading its expense and rent clauses, not by the name typed at the top of page one. Second, when a client asks what a lease type "requires," remember that in commercial leasing the answer is almost always "whatever this document says" — the statutory floor you learned in the previous unit is a residential floor, and Property Code § 92.002 confines chapter 92 to "the relationship between landlords and tenants of residential rental property."

### Module recap

This module moved from the shape of the tenant's estate to the substance of Texas regulation of it. The first unit established that a lease is both contract and conveyance: the tenant takes a possessory leasehold, the landlord keeps the reversion, and Occupations Code § 1101.002(5) confirms that a leasehold is real estate. You learned to place a tenancy in one of four categories — tenancy for years, periodic tenancy, tenancy at will, tenancy at sufferance — and, more usefully, to attach the Texas rule that governs each: expiration without notice for the fixed term, Property Code § 91.001's notice mechanics for the month-to-month, and § 92.332(b)'s holdover grounds for the tenant who overstays.

The second unit drew the licensing boundary. Leasing acts are broker acts under Occupations Code § 1101.002(1), down to controlling the acceptance or deposit of rent on a single-family unit; § 1101.005 exempts owners and their employees, on-site apartment managers, hotel and motel leasing, and specified LP and LLC personnel; and § 1101.652(b)(9), (10), (30), and (31) explain why other people's money never touches a license holder's operating account. The third unit walked the lease clause by clause, showing that each provision allocates a risk and that several — late fees under § 92.019, occupancy under § 92.010, cash rent under § 92.011, the copy-of-lease duty under § 92.024 — are constrained by statute regardless of what the form says.

The fourth unit was the module's center of gravity: the landlord's duty to repair conditions materially affecting the physical health or safety of an ordinary tenant and the notice mechanics that unlock it; the presumption that seven days is a reasonable time to repair; repair-and-deduct capped at one month's rent or $500, whichever is greater; the 30-day security deposit deadline, the itemization requirement, and the bad-faith exposure of $100 plus three times the amount wrongfully withheld plus attorney's fees; the security device and rekeying duties; the ownership disclosure rules; the prohibitions on utility interruption and lockouts; and the six-month retaliation window. The fifth unit returned to the market, classifying leases by rent structure and working a percentage-rent computation on a breakpoint.

You should now be able to identify a tenancy and state how it ends, decide whether a proposed leasing activity requires a license, read a lease clause and say what it does and whom it protects, apply chapter 92's deadlines and penalties to a residential dispute, and compute rent under a percentage lease. Where you are unsure which rule governs, ask first whether the property is residential — because that single question decides whether chapter 92 applies at all.
