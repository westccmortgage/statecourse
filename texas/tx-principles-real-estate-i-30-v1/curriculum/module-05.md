# Texas Principles of Real Estate I — Module 05

## TREC Topic: How Home Ownership is Held and Real Estate Business Ownership Issues

**Prescribed time: 180 minutes.** Ten units under the scope note *"Titles to and conveyance of real
estate; finance and regulation."* The module answers two family questions — *in what capacity do
people hold Texas homes?* — and then turns to investment: how real estate is analysed as an
investment and the vehicles through which groups own it.

**Learning objectives.** Identify the ways title is held in Texas — severalty, co-ownership, marital
property, trusts and entities — and state the Texas statutes that make this state's defaults unusual;
explain why survivorship in Texas exists only by written agreement; describe how community property
classifies a married couple's assets and what presumption applies; outline what an express trust is
and how trust ownership appears in a transaction; define the investment vocabulary (income,
appreciation, leverage, liquidity, risk) and apply it to a rental house; and describe, at the
recognition level a licensee needs, the group-investment vehicles — syndicates, REITs and REMICs —
and the securities-law boundary that surrounds them.

## Unit: Forms of Ownership

**Ownership in severalty** is title in one holder — one human or one entity — "severed" from all
others. One name signs, one name conveys. Simplicity is its virtue; exposure (everything rides on
one owner's life, debts and capacity) is its cost. Note at once that in Texas, "one name on the
deed" does not end the inquiry for a married owner: community property (two units ahead) can give a
spouse ownership rights the deed never mentions, which is why Texas practice asks every seller's
marital status and history.

**Co-ownership** is title in two or more persons at the same time. The two working forms:

- **Tenancy in common** — each co-owner holds an undivided fractional interest in the whole
  (undivided: no one owns a particular corner; each owns a share of every square foot). Shares may
  be unequal; each owner may sell, mortgage or devise the owner's share independently; and on death
  the share passes to the owner's estate. Tenancy in common is the Texas default whenever two or
  more take title without more.
- **Joint ownership with right of survivorship** — the decedent co-owner's interest sweeps to the
  survivors, bypassing the estate. Texas treats this form with statutory suspicion, as the next
  paragraphs show: it exists here only by written agreement.

**Ending co-ownership: partition.** Co-ownership is voluntary in a way sole ownership is not — it
lasts only while every co-owner tolerates it. Texas law recognises **partition**: the division of
co-owned property among its owners, either **in kind** (the land itself is divided) or, where land
cannot be fairly divided, by sale and division of proceeds. The doctrine has already appeared in
this course wearing its homestead clothes: Property Code § 41.001(b)(4) speaks of *an owelty of
partition imposed against the entirety of the property by a court order or by a written agreement
of the parties to the partition* — an **owelty** being the equalising payment charged against the
property when a physical division cannot be made exactly even, and the read text shows Texas
treating it as one of the few encumbrances valid even against a homestead, including the
divorce-decree award of a family home to one spouse. For the licence holder, partition is mostly a
listing-intake fact pattern: inherited land held by five siblings, one of whom "wants out," is a
co-ownership on its way to agreement, buyout, or a partition proceeding — and which of those it
becomes is counsel's work. What the agent contributes is the early, accurate map: who owns what
undivided share, and whether every necessary signature can actually be obtained.

**The Texas survivorship rule — the anti-default.** In many states, titling property "as joint
tenants" creates survivorship automatically. Texas law reverses the presumption. Estates Code
§ 101.002 provides that if two or more persons hold an interest jointly and one dies before
severance, the decedent's interest *does not survive* to the remaining joint owners — it *passes by
will or intestacy as if the decedent's interest had been severed*. Survivorship must be added by
agreement: Estates Code § 111.001 authorises joint owners to *agree in writing* that the interest of
a deceased joint owner survives to the surviving owners, and adds, in terms every licence holder
should memorise, that such an agreement *may not be inferred from the mere fact that property is
held in joint ownership*. So: joint names on a Texas deed, without a written survivorship agreement,
means tenancy in common in effect at death — the decedent's share goes to heirs or devisees, not to
the co-owner. Family arrangements built on the opposite assumption discover the truth in probate.

**Practice posture.** Buyers routinely ask agents "how should we take title?" The honest structure
of the answer is: *here are the forms that exist; the choice among them changes your estate,
creditor and tax position; that choice is legal advice, and an attorney should make it with you.* An
agent who explains what tenancy in common *is* teaches; an agent who tells a couple which form to
choose practises law without a licence.

## Unit: Business and Home

Real estate is held not only by people but by entities, and a licence holder needs both directions
of that fact: entities as *owners* of homes and investment property, and the entity rules of the
licensing law itself.

**Why entities hold real estate.** Corporations, limited liability companies and partnerships hold
title in severalty as legal persons. Investors use entities for liability separation (the entity's
debts are the entity's), for continuity (the entity outlives its members), and for pooling capital.
The transaction-desk consequences are mechanical but unforgiving: an entity acts only through
authorised humans, so a contract or deed from "Lone Star Holdings LLC" requires proof of the
entity's existence and of the signer's authority — questions the title company will insist on
resolving, and which the agent should surface at intake, not at closing.

**The licensing law's entity rules — already met, now completed.** The Real Estate License Act
defines a "business entity" by reference to the Business Organizations Code (Occupations Code
§ 1101.002(1-a): a domestic or foreign entity qualified to transact business in Texas), and
§ 1101.351(a-1) requires a business entity that acts as a broker to hold its *own* broker licence —
brokerage through an entity is not covered by the humans' licences alone. And recall from Module
01's exemption catalogue that a limited partnership's or LLC's *internal* transactions — its general
partner, manager or negotiating employees dealing with the entity's own real estate — sit outside
the Act (§ 1101.005): entities selling their own land are owners, not brokers.

**The authority questions, as a checklist.** Entity transactions fail at predictable joints, so the
intake questions are worth listing in the order a title company will ask them. *Existence*: is the
entity validly formed and in good standing — and is the name on the contract the name on file, not
a d/b/a or a near-miss? *Capacity*: is a foreign (out-of-state) entity qualified to transact
business in Texas, as the licensing law's own entity definition presumes (§ 1101.002(1-a) speaks of
an entity *qualified to transact business in this state*)? *Authority*: which humans may bind the
entity for THIS act — selling land is not day-to-day business for most companies, and the governing
documents may reserve it to members, boards or specified officers; the title company will want the
resolution or consent that proves it. *Signature form*: the block signs the entity, by the human,
in the capacity — "Lone Star Holdings LLC, by Maria Ortiz, Manager" — because a bare personal
signature binds the wrong party or nobody. An agent cannot cure a defect in any of the four, but an
agent who asks the four questions at intake turns closing-week emergencies into first-week
paperwork.

**Home and business in one asset.** Texas homestead law, from Module 03, already showed one meeting
point: the urban homestead may serve as *both* a home and a place of business within the 10-acre cap
(Property Code § 41.002). The other meeting point is a caution: moving a personal home *into* an
entity has consequences — homestead character, insurance, lending and taxation all shift — and the
agent's role, once again, is to flag the move as an attorney-and-accountant decision.

## Unit: Co-Ownership

Texas marital property is the co-ownership system most licence holders touch daily, because most
buyers and sellers are married, and Texas is a **community property** state — a civil-law
inheritance shared by only a minority of states.

**The classification, in the statute's words.** Family Code § 3.001 defines **separate property**:
what a spouse owned or claimed *before marriage*; what a spouse acquires during marriage *by gift,
devise, or descent*; and recoveries for personal injuries (except lost earning capacity). Family
Code § 3.002 defines **community property** as the property, *other than separate property*,
acquired by either spouse during marriage. The default is community: earnings, and things bought
with earnings, belong to the marital community regardless of whose name is on the paycheck, the
account or the deed.

**The presumption with teeth.** Family Code § 3.003 supplies the rule of proof: property possessed
by either spouse *during or on dissolution of* marriage is **presumed community**, and the spouse
claiming separate character must prove it by **clear and convincing evidence** — a heightened
standard. Tracing a house to a premarital account or an inheritance is exactly the kind of proof
this demands, and it is lawyer's work.

**Consequences at the transaction desk.** Community property means a home bought during marriage is
generally owned by the community even if deeded to one spouse alone — so Texas practice expects both
spouses' involvement in dealing with it; title companies ask marital history as routinely as they
ask for identification; and a seller's casual "it's only in my name" is the beginning of the
inquiry, not the end. Homestead rules add their own layer for the family home. The licence holder's
job description in this area is one sentence long: *identify marital status early, tell the title
company everything, and promise nothing about who must sign until the title company and counsel have
answered.*

**The classification, drilled.** Run § 3.001, § 3.002 and § 3.003 against the standard fact
patterns until the sorting is reflexive. *A house bought by one spouse two years before the
wedding* — separate: owned before marriage, § 3.001(1); marriage does not convert it. *The same
house's mortgage paid down during marriage from salary* — the house stays separate, but the
community's contributions raise reimbursement questions between the estates: lawyer's territory,
and exactly why the agent reports facts rather than conclusions. *A rental duplex inherited by one
spouse during marriage* — separate: acquired by devise or descent, § 3.001(2). *Rent collected from
that duplex during marriage* — here Texas parts company with several community-property states: the
default classification of income from separate property is a classic tracing battleground, and the
presumption of § 3.003 puts the burden — by clear and convincing evidence — on the spouse claiming
separate character. *A personal-injury settlement to one spouse* — separate under § 3.001(3),
except the portion for lost earning capacity during marriage, which the statute carves back to the
community. *A boat bought during marriage, titled in one spouse's name* — presumed community under
§ 3.003(a): possession during marriage raises the presumption, and the title certificate's single
name does not answer it. The through-line: **title is not classification**. The deed tells you who
holds record title; §§ 3.001–3.003 decide who owns.

### Statute reading exercise: the presumption at work

*A seller tells you: "The house is my separate property — I bought it with money my mother gave
me — so my husband doesn't need to be involved."* From §§ 3.001–3.003 as quoted above: **(1)**
which statute makes the gift-funded character *possible*; **(2)** which statute nonetheless makes
her claim an uphill one as a matter of proof, and to what standard; **(3)** what does the licence
holder do with the statement? *(Answers: (1) § 3.001(2) — property acquired during marriage by
gift is separate, and tracing the purchase to gifted funds is the theory; (2) § 3.003 — possession
during marriage presumes community, and separate character must be proved by clear and convincing
evidence; (3) report the claim and the marital facts to the title company and counsel, and promise
nothing about whose signatures the closing will require — the homestead rules of Module 03 can
require a non-owner spouse's joinder in any event.)*

**Community property with survivorship.** The community estate has its own survivorship statute,
parallel to § 111.001: Estates Code § 112.051 lets spouses agree *between themselves* that all or
part of their community property becomes the survivor's at death, and § 112.052 requires the
agreement to be **in writing and signed by both spouses**, blesses four sufficient phrases — "with
right of survivorship" among them — and repeats the Texas theme: survivorship *may not be inferred*
from a joint account or a "JT TEN" designation. The pattern across both statutes is the state's
settled policy: in Texas, survivorship is spelled out in writing or it does not exist.

**Reading a vesting deed against the regimes.** Practise the sort on granting language. *"To Ana
Reyes, a single woman"* — severalty, with marital history still worth one question (a divorce
decree can leave loose ends). *"To Ben and Carol Chu, husband and wife"* — community-property
questions, and survivorship only if a written § 112.051 agreement exists somewhere; the deed's
phrase alone creates none. *"To Dev Patel and Ellen Wu"* — tenancy in common in effect at death
unless a § 111.001 writing says otherwise; shares presumptively equal absent contrary evidence,
another fact for the title company rather than the agent to settle.

**Severalty, co-ownership, community: the sorting question.** Before leaving the ownership units,
notice that every Texas title question begins with the same sort: *how many owners, and under which
regime?* One owner — severalty — asks only about marital status and homestead. Multiple unmarried
owners — tenancy in common unless a written survivorship agreement says otherwise — ask about
shares, signatures and any § 111.001 agreement. Married owners ask the community-property questions
of the previous unit, plus any § 112.051 survivorship agreement. The regimes can stack: a married
person can hold an undivided one-third as tenant in common with two siblings, that one-third itself
being community or separate property under the Family Code's rules. The licence holder never
resolves the stack — but the intake sheet that records *how many owners, married or not, any
written agreements* is the map every later professional will work from.

## Unit: Trusts

A **trust** splits ownership into management and benefit. Property Code § 111.004(4) defines an
**express trust** as *a fiduciary relationship with respect to property which arises as a
manifestation by the settlor of an intention to create the relationship and which subjects the
person holding title to the property to equitable duties to deal with the property for the benefit
of another person*. Three roles: the **settlor** creates the trust and supplies the property; the
**trustee** holds legal title, bound by fiduciary duties; the **beneficiary** holds the benefit.
Section 111.003 trims the field: for the Texas Trust Code, "trust" means an *express* trust only —
not resulting or constructive trusts (court-imposed remedies), not business trusts, and *not a deed
of trust*, despite the name. That last exclusion untangles a Texas vocabulary knot once and for
all: the **deed of trust** of Modules 03 and 08 is a mortgage instrument, not a trust in this sense;
only the word is shared.

**Why property rides in trusts.** Revocable "living" trusts hold homes for probate avoidance and
incapacity planning; testamentary trusts hold property for minors or dependents; land is bought and
sold by trustees of every stripe. For the licence holder the signals and moves are: title in "Jane
Doe, Trustee of the Doe Family Trust" means the *trustee* is the party who contracts and conveys;
the trustee's authority comes from the trust instrument, which the title company will want to see
(or a certification of it); and recall from Module 01 that a person conducting a transaction *under
the authority of a written trust instrument* is exempt from licensing (§ 1101.005) — the trustee
selling trust land is not thereby a broker. What the agent never does is interpret the trust: whether
this trustee may sell this land on these terms is a question for the instrument, the title company
and counsel.

**The trust vocabulary, completed.** Two more defined terms from the read text of § 111.004 round
out the working set. A **beneficiary** is *a person for whose benefit property is held in trust,
regardless of the nature of the interest* — so contingent, future and partial interests all make
beneficiaries, which is why "who has to approve this sale?" can have a longer answer than a seller
expects. And an **interest** in the Trust Code's sense means *any interest, whether legal or
equitable or both, present or future, vested or contingent, defeasible or indefeasible* — the
statute deliberately sweeping in every gradation this course's estates module taught. Hold the two
titles apart with one sentence: *the trustee holds legal title and signs; the beneficiaries hold
equitable interests and benefit.* A buyer cannot take good title from a beneficiary, however
sincere; a trustee's deed conveys because the trustee is the title holder the trust instrument
empowers.

**The trust transaction, step by step.** When "Trustee" appears in a chain of title or on a listing
intake, the working sequence is: identify the trust exactly as named in the vesting deed; obtain
the trust's authority evidence for the title company — the instrument or the certification
practice the title company specifies; confirm the *current* trustee (settlors die; trustees
resign; successor trustees take over, and the succession must be documented); and write the
contract in the fiduciary capacity — "Jane Doe, Trustee of the Doe Family Trust," never "Jane Doe"
alone, because the capacities are different parties in law. None of this is trust interpretation;
all of it is accurate transmission — and the distinction is the unit's licence-boundary lesson
applied.

## Unit: Investing in Real Estate

*This unit and the two that follow are economics and arithmetic, taught without statutory citation
because no statute supplies their content.*

Real estate investment is the deliberate purchase of property for financial return rather than
occupancy. Its returns arrive through two channels — **income** (rent net of expenses) and
**appreciation** (growth in value realised at sale or refinance) — and its analysis is the
discipline of comparing those returns to the investment's costs and risks.

The investor's vocabulary, which the next unit puts to work:

- **Gross income**: total rents and fees the property produces. **Vacancy and collection loss**:
  the share never collected. **Operating expenses**: taxes, insurance, maintenance, management,
  utilities. **Net operating income (NOI)**: gross income minus vacancy and expenses — the
  property's earning power before financing.
- **Debt service**: loan payments. **Cash flow**: NOI minus debt service — what the owner pockets.
- **Leverage**: using borrowed money to control a larger asset than equity alone could buy.
  Leverage multiplies outcomes in both directions: it raises the return on equity when the
  property's yield exceeds the loan's cost, and it deepens losses — and can produce negative cash
  flow — when the relationship reverses.
- **Liquidity**: how quickly an asset converts to cash near its value. Real estate is
  **illiquid** — selling takes months and heavy transaction costs — and illiquidity is a real cost
  the investor accepts in exchange for the asset's other properties.
- **Risk**: market risk (values and rents move), operating risk (vacancies, repairs, tenants),
  interest-rate risk (financing costs shift), and location risk (the neighbourhood's fortunes).
  Return is the payment for bearing these.

An agent working with investors is a translator of this vocabulary, not a guarantor of outcomes:
projections are estimates, and presenting them as promises is the fastest route from sales talk to
liability.

**Time, the quiet variable.** Every term in the vocabulary is time-shaped. The **holding period**
is the investor's planned tenure, and it disciplines everything else: transaction costs (heavy at
both ends) are amortised over it, so short holds magnify them; appreciation is realised only at its
end; and illiquidity is dangerous in proportion to how badly the investor might need cash before it
ends. The **exit** — sale, refinance, or exchange of one property for another — is decided, in a
well-run analysis, before the purchase: an investor who cannot say how the position ends has not
finished analysing it. And the **market cycle** frames both: rents, vacancies and values move in
long local cycles driven by employment, construction and credit, which is why the same property is
a different investment in different years, and why every projection in this unit carries a date as
surely as it carries a number. None of this is law; all of it is the working honesty the licence
holder owes an investor client — and the vocabulary in which that client's accountant, appraiser
and lender will expect the conversation to be conducted.

## Unit: The Investment

A worked example makes the vocabulary concrete. *An investor considers a rental house priced at
$240,000, buying with $60,000 of her own funds and a $180,000 loan. Market rent is $1,950 a month.
She estimates 5% vacancy and collection loss, and annual operating expenses of $8,400 (taxes,
insurance, maintenance, management). Annual debt service on the loan is $13,200.*

Run the numbers in the standard order:

- Gross scheduled income: $1,950 × 12 = **$23,400**
- Less vacancy at 5%: −$1,170 → effective gross income **$22,230**
- Less operating expenses: −$8,400 → **NOI = $13,830**
- Less debt service: −$13,200 → **cash flow = $630** per year

Now the standard measures. The **capitalization rate** relates NOI to price: $13,830 ÷ $240,000 ≈
**5.8%** — the property's unlevered yield, and the number by which income properties are compared.
The **cash-on-cash return** relates cash flow to the cash invested: $630 ÷ $60,000 ≈ **1.05%** —
thin, and honestly stated as thin. Why might she proceed? Because total return includes principal
repayment on the loan and any appreciation; at, say, 3% annual appreciation the value grows $7,200
in year one — none of it guaranteed. The example also shows leverage's knife edge: a $75/month rent
shortfall or one significant repair pushes cash flow negative, and she funds the difference from her
pocket. The analytical habit to carry: *cap rate for the property, cash-on-cash for the equity, and
a margin for the surprises* — and every figure labelled as an estimate.

**A second pass: comparing two properties.** The measures earn their keep when they rank
alternatives. *The same investor also considers a fourplex priced at $500,000: gross scheduled rents
$54,000 a year, vacancy estimated at 7%, operating expenses $19,800.* NOI = $54,000 − $3,780 −
$19,800 = **$30,420**; cap rate = $30,420 ÷ $500,000 ≈ **6.1%**. Against the rental house's 5.8%,
the fourplex yields more per dollar of price — before financing, and before judgment. Now the
judgment: the fourplex's higher yield prices its higher operating burden (four tenants, four
turnovers), and a single number never settles a purchase. What the comparison discipline forbids is
mixing bases: cap rates compare *properties* (NOI ÷ price, no financing); cash-on-cash compares
*equity positions* (cash flow ÷ cash invested, financing included). An investor who compares one
property's cap rate to another's cash-on-cash is comparing apples to interest rates.

**Sensitivity: the honest final step.** Before any projection is handed to a client, move its
weakest assumption and watch the result. For the rental house: vacancy at 10% instead of 5% cuts
effective gross income by $1,170 and drives cash flow from +$630 to −$540; a $1,200 roof repair in
year one has the same sign. For the fourplex: raising vacancy to 12% removes $2,700 of NOI and drops
the cap rate to 5.5%. The arithmetic is trivial; the practice discipline is not: *every projection
shown to a client should carry its assumptions on its face*, and the sentence "these figures are
estimates based on stated assumptions, not predictions" belongs on the page, not in the agent's
mental reservation.

### Applied exercise: run the numbers yourself

*A client asks you to sanity-check a listing flyer for a rental townhome: price $320,000; rent
$2,400/month; the flyer claims "7.2% cap rate." The client would put $80,000 down and borrow
$240,000 with annual debt service of $18,600. You estimate 6% vacancy and $9,000 annual operating
expenses.* **Task:** compute gross scheduled income, effective gross income, NOI, the true cap
rate, cash flow, and cash-on-cash return; then state what to tell the client about the flyer.
**Model answer:** gross scheduled income $2,400 × 12 = $28,800; less 6% vacancy (−$1,728) →
effective gross income $27,072; less expenses (−$9,000) → **NOI $18,072**; cap rate $18,072 ÷
$320,000 ≈ **5.6%**, not 7.2% — the flyer's figure is reachable only by ignoring vacancy and
understating expenses (gross rent ÷ price = 9%, so the flyer discounted some costs but not all);
cash flow $18,072 − $18,600 = **−$528**; cash-on-cash −$528 ÷ $80,000 ≈ **−0.7%** — negative. Tell
the client: on your stated assumptions the property does not carry its own financing; the flyer's
cap rate is not computed on NOI; and any decision should be made on the client's own verified
figures — delivered without disparaging anyone, and in writing.

## Unit: The Benefits

Why do investors accept illiquidity, tenants and roof repairs? The economic case, stated without
exaggeration:

- **Use and control.** Unlike most investments, real estate can be occupied, improved and actively
  managed; the owner's decisions genuinely move the return.
- **Income.** A sound rental produces recurring cash flow that can rise with rents over time.
- **Appreciation potential.** Values are not guaranteed to rise — markets fall, and Texas has seen
  both directions — but long horizons, population growth and inflation have historically supported
  real asset values.
- **Leverage and amortisation.** Borrowed capital lets modest equity control substantial property,
  and each amortising payment shifts a little debt into equity.
- **Inflation characteristics.** Rents and replacement costs tend to move with the price level,
  which is why real assets are often held as an inflation hedge — a tendency, not a promise.
- **Portfolio diversification.** Real estate's returns do not move in lockstep with financial
  markets, so it can steady a mixed portfolio.

Against these stand the costs already taught: illiquidity, transaction costs, management burden,
concentration of capital in single assets, and every category of risk in the vocabulary unit.

**Leverage and amortisation, illustrated without promises.** The list's financial entries deserve
one worked breath. Take the unit's standing example: $60,000 of equity controlling a $240,000
property. If the property's value rises 3% — $7,200 — the investor's *equity* has grown 12% on her
$60,000, because the gain accrues to the whole asset while her stake is a quarter of it. That is
leverage's arithmetic, and it is symmetric: a 3% *decline* is likewise a 12% equity loss, and a
decline steep enough erases the equity entirely while the debt remains. Amortisation is the
quieter, surer channel: each level payment (Module 08 shows the mechanics) retires a sliver of
principal, converting debt into equity on a schedule that accelerates over the loan's life —
"forced saving," in the vocabulary investors use, funded in a rental by the tenants' rent. Neither
channel is a promise; both are structures — and describing structures accurately, gains and losses
in the same sentence, is what separates client education from sales talk.

**On taxes, this course draws a deliberate line.** Tax consequences — of owning, renting,
improving, exchanging or selling real estate — are governed by the Internal Revenue Code and
change with it. This course does not teach specific federal income-tax rules, and a licence holder
should not either: describing tax outcomes to a client is tax advice. The professional formulation,
which you may use verbatim: *"Ownership may have significant tax consequences; a qualified tax
professional can tell you what they are for your situation."* The only federal tax rules taught in
this program are the two that impose duties directly on transactions — FIRPTA withholding (Module
08) and the REMIC structure (this module's last unit) — each taught from its statute.

## Unit: Real Estate Investment Syndicates

A **syndicate** is any arrangement pooling multiple investors' capital into a real estate venture
under central management — historically general or limited partnerships, today most often limited
liability companies. The economics are straightforward: pooling buys scale (an apartment complex
instead of a condo) and professional management; the investors take fractional interests and rely on
the promoter.

That reliance is precisely what triggers the securities laws. Under the Texas Securities Act, a
**"security"** is defined expansively — Government Code § 4001.068 includes, among a long list, a
*limited partner interest in a limited partnership*, any *note, bond, debenture … or other evidence
of indebtedness*, a *certificate in or under a profit sharing or participation agreement*, and the
catch-all **investment contract** — whether or not evidenced by writing. A passive fractional
interest in a promoter-managed real estate pool fits that frame comfortably, which is why syndicate
interests are ordinarily securities: their offer and sale live under the Securities Act's
registration-or-exemption regime (the Act's exemption provisions occupy their own chapter of the
Government Code), with disclosure duties and anti-fraud rules attached.

**Registration or exemption.** "Living under the registration-or-exemption regime" has a concrete
shape, now read for this course. Government Code § 4005.001 states the scope: except as expressly
provided, the Securities Act *does not apply* to a sale, offer, solicitation, subscription, dealing
or delivery of a security made in a transaction or under a condition the exemptions subchapter
specifies. The exemption real estate syndications most often claim is § 4005.012's **private
limited offering**: sales made *without any public solicitation or advertisement* where either the
issuer has **no more than 35 security holders after the sale**, or the issuer sells to **not more
than 15 persons during the 12 months ending on the sale date** who buy *for their own account and
not for distribution*. Read the conditions as the promoter's fence posts: a syndication marketed on
social media has already left "without any public solicitation"; a pool quietly gathering its
thirty-sixth member has left the holder count; and interests bought for resale defeat the
own-account condition. None of this is the licence holder's compliance to manage — it is the
promoter's and securities counsel's — but knowing the fence exists explains both why legitimate
syndications paper their offerings so carefully and why "just help me find a few more investors"
is a sentence with securities law inside it.

The boundary for a real estate licence holder is bright: a real estate licence authorises brokering
*real estate*; it does not authorise offering or selling *securities*. An agent invited to "help
raise investors" for a syndication — for compensation — is being invited into securities activity,
and the only correct responses are securities licensing and counsel, or refusal. Recognising the
boundary is the competency this unit exists to teach.

### Statute reading exercise: counting to thirty-five

*A promoter forming "Hill Country Apartments LLC" tells investors: "We're exempt from all that
securities registration — we're keeping it small."* From §§ 4001.068, 4005.001 and 4005.012 as set
out above: **(1)** why do the LLC interests need an exemption at all; **(2)** name the two
alternative numeric conditions of the private-limited-offering exemption and the conduct condition
that applies to both; **(3)** the promoter plans to run paid social-media ads "just to gauge
interest" — what does that do? *(Answers: (1) passive fractional interests in a promoter-managed
pool sit within § 4001.068's sweeping definition — investment contract, participation certificate —
so their offer and sale are of securities and need registration or an exemption; (2) either no more
than 35 security holders after the sale, or sales to not more than 15 persons in the trailing 12
months buying for their own account and not for distribution — and in both branches the sales must
be made without any public solicitation or advertisement; (3) paid public advertising is public
solicitation, and the exemption's own words exclude it — which is a matter for the promoter's
securities counsel, and one more reason the licence holder stays on the real estate side of the
line.)*

## Unit: Real Estate Investment Trusts

A **real estate investment trust (REIT)** pools investor capital into a professionally managed
portfolio of income properties or mortgages, with interests (shares) that — in the publicly traded
form — restore the liquidity that direct ownership lacks: the investor holds real estate exposure
in a brokerage account, sellable in a moment.

Texas provides an organisational home for the form: Business Organizations Code § 200.001 defines a
Texas real estate investment trust as an **unincorporated trust** formed by one or more **trust
managers** under Chapter 200 and the code's general provisions — a distinct Texas entity type, with
the statute governing its management. Federal law then supplies the tax architecture that makes
REITs economically distinctive: qualification under the Internal Revenue Code's REIT provisions,
with their asset, income and distribution requirements. Consistent with this course's tax line,
those federal requirements are acknowledged and not detailed here — an investor's REIT tax questions
belong to a tax professional. What the licence holder retains: REIT shares are securities (they sit
squarely within § 4001.068's definitions), buying them is investing, not real estate brokerage, and
a client comparing "buying a rental" with "buying REIT shares" is comparing direct illiquid control
against pooled liquid passivity — the vocabulary of the earlier units does the comparing.

**Direct ownership against the REIT share, side by side.** The comparison a client actually asks
for is best taught as paired sentences. *Control*: the direct owner decides rents, repairs and
sale; the REIT shareholder votes at a distance and controls nothing operational. *Liquidity*: the
direct owner sells in months at material cost; the traded-REIT shareholder sells in seconds at
brokerage cost. *Capital and diversification*: direct ownership concentrates a large sum in one
address; a REIT share spreads a small sum across a portfolio. *Leverage*: the direct owner chooses
and personally bears financing; the REIT's leverage is set by its managers and priced into the
share. *Effort*: tenants and roofs against a brokerage statement. Neither column wins in general —
the columns are the answer, and walking a client down them, without recommending securities, is
exactly the literacy this unit exists to build.

## Unit: Real Estate Mortgage Investment Conduits

A **real estate mortgage investment conduit (REMIC)** is the federal vehicle for mortgage
securitisation: a pool of mortgages placed in a conduit entity that issues classes of interests to
investors, who receive the pooled loans' cash flows. One federal section, read for this course,
supplies the defining feature: under 26 U.S.C. § 860A, a REMIC *is not itself subject to taxation* —
the entity level is transparent — and *the income of the REMIC is taxable to the holders of
interests* in it. That single rule is why the structure exists: it lets mortgage pools pass income
to investors without an entity-level tax, which made the modern secondary mortgage market's
securitisation machinery possible.

**The cast of the secondary market.** Securitisation gives the primary-market participants of
Module 08 their counterparts: the **originator** makes the loan at a Texas closing table; the
**aggregator or sponsor** buys loans in bulk and assembles the pool; the **conduit** (the REMIC)
holds the pool and issues the interests; **investors** hold the interests and receive the cash
flows § 860A taxes to them; and the **servicer** — the borrower's only visible counterparty —
collects payments, manages escrow and administers default for the pool. The homeowner experiences
all of this as a letter announcing that payments go to a new address; the licence holder should be
able to explain, in exactly these terms, that the *servicing* moved, the *loan terms* did not, and
that no borrower consent is required for the debt to be sold — the note the borrower signed is a
negotiable asset, which is the whole premise of the market this unit describes.

For Principles I, the practical chain to remember runs backward from Module 08: a Texas homebuyer
signs a note and deed of trust; the originating lender sells the loan into the secondary market; the
loan is pooled, and interests in the pool — often through a REMIC — are sold to investors
worldwide. Securitisation is why the "mortgagee" of record may be a book-entry system (recall
Property Code § 51.0001's definition), why loan servicing is a distinct industry (the "mortgage
servicer" of the same section), and why local mortgage money is priced by national markets. REMIC
interests, needless to say, are securities in investors' hands; the licence holder's connection to
all this is comprehension, not participation.

### Statute reading exercise: survivorship, spelled out or absent

Answer from Estates Code §§ 101.002, 111.001 and 112.051–.052 as set out above: **(1)** Two sisters
take a Texas deed "as joint tenants." One dies. Who owns her half? **(2)** What single change would
have produced the opposite result? **(3)** Spouses hold a brokerage account styled "JT TEN." Does
that styling create survivorship in their community property? *(Answers: (1) her heirs or devisees —
§ 101.002 passes the interest as if severed, and § 111.001 bars inferring survivorship from joint
ownership alone; (2) a written agreement among the co-owners that the decedent's interest survives,
under § 111.001; (3) no — § 112.052(d) says survivorship may not be inferred from a joint account or
"JT TEN" designation; it takes a written agreement signed by both spouses.)*

### Applied exercise: the intake interview that prevented three disasters

*A listing agent interviews a seller: the house was bought eight years ago, during the seller's
marriage, with title in the seller's name alone; the seller divorced two years ago and has since
remarried; the seller's brother "owns ten percent from when he helped with the down payment,"
nothing in writing; and the seller wants to close in three weeks.* **Task:** identify every
ownership issue this intake surfaces, classify each with this module's tools, and state what the
agent does next. **Model answer:** (1) Purchased during a prior marriage: Family Code § 3.002 makes
it presumptively community property of that marriage (§ 3.003's presumption), so the divorce
decree's property division must be obtained — the ex-spouse may hold an interest. (2) Current
remarriage: the new spouse's homestead and community rights must be evaluated for the conveyance —
title company and counsel territory. (3) The brother's unwritten "ten percent": possibly a claimed
tenancy-in-common interest or a loan; either way it clouds title and must be documented or released.
(4) The three-week timeline is the casualty of honesty: the agent's correct move is to send the
whole file to the title company now, disclose all three issues, and let counsel cure them —
because the alternative is a contract the seller may be unable to perform.

### Module recap

Texas title runs from severalty through tenancy in common (the default), with survivorship existing
only by written agreement — never inferred (Estates Code §§ 101.002, 111.001, 112.051–.052).
Marriage overlays community property: separate is premarital, gifted, devised or inherited (FC
§ 3.001); community is the rest (§ 3.002); possession during marriage is presumed community, rebutted
only by clear and convincing evidence (§ 3.003). Trusts split legal title (trustee) from benefit
(beneficiary) in an express fiduciary relationship (PC §§ 111.003–.004) — and a deed of trust is not
a trust. Entities hold land as legal persons, need their own broker licences to broker (OC
§ 1101.351(a-1)), and sell their own land licence-free. Investment analysis runs income to NOI to
cash flow, prices property by cap rate and equity by cash-on-cash, and treats leverage, illiquidity
and risk as the costs of the asset's benefits — with taxes referred, wholesale, to tax
professionals. Group ownership climbs from syndicates (securities under Government Code § 4001.068's
investment-contract breadth) through Texas-organised REITs (BOC § 200.001) to REMICs, the
entity-transparent conduits of 26 U.S.C. § 860A that carry mortgage cash flows to the investors the
secondary market serves.
