# Texas Principles of Real Estate I — Module 03

## TREC Topic: Interests in Real Estate

**Prescribed time: 205 minutes.** Seven units under the Commission's scope note *"Deeds, encumbrances
and liens."* This is the course's first long module, and it is the conceptual spine of everything
after it: what kinds of interests exist in Texas land, and what claims other people can hold against
them.

**Learning objectives.** Trace how ownership concepts developed and why Texas terminology still
carries that history; identify the freehold and leasehold estates and state the Texas default rule
that makes every conveyance a fee simple unless limited; distinguish money encumbrances (liens) from
non-money encumbrances (easements, restrictions, encroachments); explain how and when the property
tax lien attaches and why it outranks nearly everything; catalogue the voluntary and involuntary
liens a title search reveals, including the constitutional strength of the Texas mechanic's lien
claimant's position; state who owns Texas water and what a buyer of waterfront land actually gets;
and apply the two distinct Texas homestead doctrines — protection from creditors and property-tax
exemption — to a family's principal residence.

## Unit: Historical Background

*This unit is history. It asserts no current legal rule, and no statute is cited for it.*

The vocabulary of this module — estate, fee, tenement, tenure — is medieval English, and knowing the
story keeps the terms from being arbitrary. After the Norman Conquest, England organised land under
**feudal tenure**: all land was ultimately the Crown's, and everyone below the Crown *held of*
someone above in exchange for services — military service, labour, produce. What a person owned was
not the land itself but an **estate**: a bundle of rights in land, measured by duration. The word
**fee** (from *fief*) named an estate that could be inherited; **fee simple** was the fief with the
fewest strings — inheritable by anyone, alienable, enduring indefinitely.

Over centuries the services fell away, statutes made estates freely transferable and devisable, and
tenure hollowed out into a formality. The system that crossed the Atlantic was **allodial** in
substance: individuals own land outright, subject not to a lord's services but to the sovereign's
four residual powers — taxation, eminent domain, police power and escheat — which this course meets
in later units and modules. Texas adds its own layer of history: much of the state was originally
granted under Spanish and Mexican civil law, and the traces persist — surveying in **varas** (the
Spanish unit you will meet in Module 04), community property (Module 05), and generous homestead
protection (this module's final unit), all of them civil-law inheritances that distinguish Texas from
the purely English-derived states.

**The four residual powers, introduced.** The allodial owner's title is complete against every
private claimant but remains subject to four powers every sovereign keeps, and it is worth meeting
them here as a set because the rest of the course takes them up one at a time. **Taxation** — the
government's charge on ownership itself; this module's Tax Liens unit shows Texas securing it with
the strongest ordinary lien in the state. **Eminent domain** — the power to take private property
for public use on payment of compensation, exercised through condemnation; Module 07 places it among
the involuntary transfers. **Police power** — the power to regulate use without taking title:
zoning, building codes, subdivision platting (Module 04 shows the county version), environmental
rules. Regulation under the police power is not compensated; taking under eminent domain is — and
the line between them is one of the most litigated boundaries in American law, which is precisely
why a licence holder describes land-use restrictions and never opines on their validity.
**Escheat** — the state's taking of property left ownerless, the modern echo of land returning to
the lord when a tenant died without heirs; Module 07 meets it at the end of the intestacy ladder.
Four powers, four later landings: the frame built in this unit carries the whole course.

Why keep teaching the feudal frame at all? Because the *duration* idea survives intact. Modern law
still classifies interests by how long they last — forever, for a life, for a term of years — and
still uses the medieval names. When a Texas statute says a conveyed estate is a "fee simple," it is
speaking this language; the next unit shows Texas speaking it plainly.

## Unit: Estates in Land

An **estate** is an interest in real property that is or may become possessory, measured by duration.
Interests that never carry possession — easements, liens — are not estates; they are the subject of
the encumbrance units that follow.

**Freehold estates** last for an indefinite duration.

- **Fee simple absolute** — the greatest estate known to law: indefinite duration, fully inheritable,
  fully transferable, subject only to the sovereign powers and to whatever encumbrances its owner
  created or suffered. Texas states a powerful default in Property Code § 5.001: *an estate in land
  that is conveyed or devised is a fee simple unless the estate is limited by express words or unless
  a lesser estate is conveyed or devised by construction or operation of law* — and the ancient words
  of inheritance ("to A *and his heirs*") are expressly unnecessary. The practical meaning for every
  deed you will ever read: silence means fee simple; a lesser estate must be spelled out.
- **Defeasible fees** — fee estates that can end on a stated event: "to the school district so long
  as the land is used for a school." The holder has a fee that may last forever but is *defeasible* —
  capable of being defeated — with a future interest (a possibility of reverter or right of re-entry)
  waiting in the grantor or the grantor's successors. These appear in Texas practice mostly in old
  grants for schools, churches and railways, and they matter to a licence holder chiefly as a title
  finding that demands a lawyer, not a form.
- **Life estate** — an estate measured by a life: "to my mother for her life." The life tenant
  possesses and uses the property, and owes the future interest holder the duty not to commit
  **waste** — not to consume or degrade what must be handed on. At the measuring life's end the
  property passes to the **remainderman** (if the future interest was granted to a third person) or
  **reverts** to the grantor's successors (a *reversion*). Texas recognises the creation of future
  estates by ordinary deed: Property Code § 5.041 permits an inter vivos conveyance of a freehold
  estate *that commences in the future*, as freely as by will. Life estates arise in Texas both by
  deliberate estate planning and — as Module 07 shows — by operation of the intestacy statute, which
  gives a surviving spouse a life estate in one-third of certain land.

**Leasehold (non-freehold) estates** last for a definite or terminable period — the tenant's
possessory interest under a lease. The classical four:

- **Estate for years** — a fixed term with a stated end date, however short or long; no notice is
  needed to end it, because the calendar ends it.
- **Periodic tenancy** — repeating periods (month to month) that renew until notice.
- **Estate at will** — possession with the owner's consent, no fixed term, terminable by either
  party.
- **Tenancy at sufferance** — the holdover: a tenant who entered rightfully and stays after the
  right ends, possessing without consent but not (yet) a trespasser.

Two Texas anchors for leaseholds now, both already met: a leasehold is "real estate" in a licence
holder's hands (Occupations Code § 1101.002(5) includes leaseholds expressly), and a conveyance of an
estate *for more than one year* must satisfy the writing requirements of Property Code § 5.021 —
which is why the one-year line runs through leasing practice, and why Module 06 returns to it through
the statute of frauds.

**Reading an estate.** Every question about an estate is answered by three smaller questions: How
long does it last? What can end it early? Who takes when it ends? Run those three against any
granting language — "to A for life, then to B"; "to the church while used for worship"; "to T for
five years" — and the estate names fall out mechanically. The licence holder's discipline is humbler
and harder: *notice* when granting language is anything other than a plain fee, and route it to
counsel instead of improvising.

**The estate ladder, worked.** Classification is a skill, not a memory feat, and it is learned by
running the three questions against real granting language. Work each of the following before
reading its resolution.

1. *"To the City of Alpine, so long as the land is used as a public park."* — How long? Potentially
   forever. What ends it early? Non-park use. Who takes? The grantor's successors, holding the
   possibility of reverter. A defeasible fee; a title examiner will flag it, and no buyer should be
   told it is "basically a fee simple."
2. *"To my sister Carmen."* — No duration stated, no condition, no words of inheritance. Under
   Property Code § 5.001 the silence itself answers: fee simple absolute, because a conveyed estate
   is a fee simple *unless limited by express words*, and words of inheritance are unnecessary.
3. *"To Dr. Osei for her life, then to the county hospital district."* — A life estate in Dr. Osei,
   who possesses, pays the carrying costs, and must not commit waste; a remainder in the district,
   which waits. The district's interest is a present, transferable property interest even though
   possession is postponed — exactly the future-estate kind that Property Code § 5.041 lets a deed
   create as freely as a will.
4. *"Lease commencing March 1 and ending the last day of February two years later."* — An estate for
   years: fixed term, ends by the calendar, no notice required. Being longer than one year, it lives
   under the writing requirement the statute of frauds materials in Module 06 develop.
5. *"Tenant may remain month to month after the initial term at the same rent."* — A periodic
   tenancy after the term ends: repeating periods renewing until proper notice.

The pattern to carry out of the drill: the deed's *granting and habendum language* controls, the
statutory default fills silence with fee simple, and every departure from a plain fee is a finding
to disclose and route — never to interpret away.

### Applied exercise: what estate did the seller actually own?

*Reviewing a rural listing's chain of title, you find a 1968 deed: "to the Brewster County School
District so long as the premises are used for school purposes, and if said use ceases, title shall
revert to the grantors, their heirs and assigns." The school closed in 2019; the district has been
renting the building to a feed store since. The district now wants to list the property.* **Task:**
name the estate the district received, name the interest the 1968 grantors kept, and state what the
2019 change of use may have done — then say what a licence holder does with a listing like this.
**Model answer:** the district took a defeasible fee (a fee simple determinable in classical terms:
"so long as" language with express reverter); the grantors kept a possibility of reverter passing to
their heirs and assigns. When school use ceased, the stated event occurred, and the grantors'
successors may now hold title — meaning the district may have nothing to sell. The licence holder
does not resolve this: the listing waits while the district's counsel and a title company determine
ownership. Taking the listing and marketing "fee simple title" would market an estate the seller may
not own.

## Unit: Encumbrances

An **encumbrance** is any right or interest held by someone other than the owner that burdens the
property — diminishing its value or restricting its use — without preventing transfer. The owner can
sell; the buyer takes subject to the encumbrance. Encumbrances divide cleanly in two.

**Money encumbrances — liens.** A **lien** secures a debt: it gives the creditor the right, on
default, to have the property sold and be paid from the proceeds. Property Code § 51.0001 gives the
Texas vocabulary for the consensual kind: a **security instrument** is *a deed of trust, mortgage, or
other contract lien on an interest in real property*; the **mortgagor** is the grantor of the
security instrument (the borrower), the **mortgagee** its holder; a **trustee** or **substitute
trustee** is the person authorised to exercise the power of sale. Liens are classified on two axes
you should apply to every lien you meet:

- **Voluntary or involuntary** — created by the owner's agreement (a deed of trust) or by operation
  of law against the owner's wishes (tax liens, judgment liens, mechanic's liens).
- **General or specific** — reaching all the debtor's non-exempt property (a judgment) or only the
  particular parcel (a property tax lien, a deed of trust, a mechanic's lien on the improved
  property).

The two units after this one walk the involuntary liens in detail; Module 08 gives the deed of trust
its own ninety minutes.

**Non-money encumbrances** burden use rather than secure debt.

- **Easements** — non-possessory rights to use another's land for a defined purpose. An **easement
  appurtenant** serves a neighbouring parcel: the burdened land is the *servient* estate, the
  benefited land the *dominant* estate, and the easement passes automatically with the dominant land.
  An **easement in gross** serves a person or company rather than a parcel — the utility line, the
  pipeline — and in commercial form is transferable. Easements arise by express grant or reservation
  in a recorded instrument; by implication from prior use or by necessity when a conveyance leaves a
  parcel landlocked; and by **prescription** — long, open, adverse use, the easement cousin of the
  adverse possession doctrines you will meet in Module 07. A licence holder's duties are to spot
  easements in the title commitment, walk the land with them in mind (that mowed strip under the
  power line is somebody's right), and never to assure a buyer that an easement is harmless.
- **Restrictive covenants** — private promises recorded against land that bind successive owners:
  subdivision schemes of use, setback, size and architecture. They are enforced by the persons they
  benefit (neighbours, owners' associations), they coexist with zoning, and in practice the stricter
  of the two controls what an owner may actually build.
- **Encroachments** — physical intrusions of a structure across a boundary: the fence two feet over,
  the eave overhanging. An encroachment is discovered by survey, not by the deed records, which is
  one of the two standing reasons every buyer is urged to obtain a new survey (the other is Module
  04's business). Encroachments cloud marketability in both directions — the intruding owner may be
  trespassing; the suffering owner may be losing land toward a prescriptive claim.
- **Licences** — mere permissions to enter, personal and ordinarily revocable; not interests in land
  at all, listed here because buyers routinely mistake a neighbour's long-tolerated permission for an
  easement. The distinction is the difference between a right and a favour.

### Applied exercise: the mowed strip and the gravel drive

*Showing a rural-edge listing, your buyer notices two things: a mowed strip crossing the back of the
property under a power line, and a gravel drive along the side that the neighbour uses daily to
reach his landlocked tract behind. The seller says the neighbour "just uses it — we've never
minded," and no drive appears in the seller's deed.* **Task:** name the interest each observation
suggests, state how each would (or would not) appear in the records, and say what the buyer should
be told. **Model answer:** the mowed strip suggests an easement in gross held by the utility —
commercial, transferable, almost certainly recorded and due to appear on the title commitment; the
buyer takes subject to it. The drive is the dangerous one: if the use is truly permissive it is a
mere licence, revocable — but a landlocked dominant tract suggests an easement by necessity or
implication may already exist, and years of open use point toward prescription. Which it is cannot
be answered by the seller's goodwill or the agent's guess: the buyer is told, in writing, that the
neighbour's use is an unresolved title and survey matter for the title company and counsel, and
that "we've never minded" is exactly how permanent rights get built across other people's land.

### Statute reading exercise: the lien vocabulary

From Property Code § 51.0001 as summarised above, answer: **(1)** In a Texas deed-of-trust loan, who
is the mortgagor, who is the mortgagee, and where does the trustee fit? **(2)** Why does the
definition of "security instrument" matter to the licensing exemption you met in Module 01 for
foreclosure sales? *(Answers: (1) the borrower-grantor is the mortgagor; the lender-holder is the
mortgagee; the trustee is the third party authorised to exercise the power of sale on default;
(2) the Module 01 exemption covers the sale of real property "under a power of sale conferred by a
deed of trust or other contract lien" — the § 51.0001 security-instrument family — so the trustee
conducting that sale needs no broker licence.)*

## Unit: Tax Liens

Property taxation is Texas's principal local revenue engine, and the lien that secures it is the
strongest ordinary lien in the state. Two sections of the Tax Code, both read for this course, give
the doctrine its teeth.

**Attachment: automatic, annual, first.** Under Tax Code § 32.01, *on January 1 of each year a tax
lien attaches to property to secure the payment of all taxes, penalties, and interest ultimately
imposed for the year* — in favour of **each taxing unit** with power to tax the parcel: county, city,
school district, and the special districts. Attachment is automatic; no filing, no recording, no
notice. Section 32.01(d) adds that the lien is *perfected on attachment* and requires no further
action. Every parcel in Texas therefore begins every year encumbered, months before the year's tax
bill is even calculated.

**Priority: over nearly everything.** Tax Code § 32.05 states the rank: the tax lien takes priority
over *a homestead interest in the property*, and over the claims of creditors and of other
lienholders — expressly including owners'-association assessment liens — **regardless of whether the
competing debt or lien existed before the tax lien attached**. First-in-time, the ordering principle
for most liens, is simply suspended. The statute allows only narrow exceptions: certain claims
against a decedent's estate, and recorded restrictive covenants and easements that predate January 1
of the lien year — encumbrances that define the property rather than compete for its value.

**What this means at a closing table.** Because the current year's lien attached on January 1 and the
year's taxes are not yet payable, virtually every Texas closing happens *under* a live tax lien. The
practice consequences run through Module 09: taxes are prorated between the parties, the title
company collects or reserves for them, and a lender escrows for the next bill — all of it machinery
for living with a lien that cannot be beaten, only paid. For the licence holder the operational rules
are: verify the tax status of every listing; never let a client treat delinquent taxes as a minor
title item (penalties and interest compound, and enforcement ends in a tax sale); and remember that
tax prorations at closing are estimates against a lien that already exists.

**The exceptions, precisely.** Because § 32.05's rule is so absolute, its stated exceptions repay
exact reading. The tax lien is *inferior* to only three families of claims in the text as held:
**(1)** a claim against a decedent's estate for any survivor's allowance, funeral expenses, or
expenses of the last illness; **(2)** a recorded restrictive covenant that runs with the land and
was recorded before January 1 of the year the tax lien arose; and **(3)** a valid easement of
record recorded before that same January 1. Notice what the covenant and easement exceptions have
in common: they are not competitors for the property's *money* — they are definitions of what the
property *is*. A tax foreclosure therefore wipes out junior liens but delivers the land still
burdened by its pre-existing recorded covenants and easements. And § 32.05(b-1) closes the last
door: the tax lien's priority prevails *regardless of whether the debt, lien, future interest, or
other encumbrance existed before attachment of the tax lien* — seniority of recording, the rule
that orders nearly every other contest in this course, is expressly overridden.

### Statute reading exercise: ranking against the tax lien

*A parcel carries, in order of creation: subdivision restrictive covenants recorded 1994; a utility
easement recorded 2001; a deed of trust recorded 2015; an owners'-association assessment lien
arising 2023; and the county's tax lien that attached January 1 of this year.* From §§ 32.01 and
32.05 as set out above: **(1)** rank the county's lien against each of the other four; **(2)** state
which of the four would survive a tax foreclosure sale. *(Answers: (1) the tax lien is junior to
the 1994 covenants and the 2001 easement (both recorded before January 1 of the lien year), and
senior to the 2015 deed of trust and the 2023 association lien — § 32.05(b) names association
assessment liens expressly, and (b-1) makes the deed of trust's twenty-year seniority irrelevant;
(2) the covenants and the easement survive; the deed of trust and association lien, being junior,
are subject to elimination by the tax sale.)*

## Unit: Liens other than Taxes

The remaining liens divide by how they arise.

**Voluntary: the deed of trust.** The security instrument of § 51.0001 — the borrower's own grant of
a lien with a power of sale. Named here for completeness; Module 08 is devoted to it.

**Involuntary liens** arise by operation of law:

- **Judgment liens.** A money judgment, once properly abstracted into a county's records, becomes a
  lien on the debtor's non-exempt real property in that county — a *general* lien, reaching whatever
  non-exempt land the debtor owns or acquires there. Two Texas limits tame it: the homestead is not
  "non-exempt" property (this module's final unit), and enforcement is subject to the limitation
  periods of the Civil Practice & Remedies Code you will meet in Modules 06 and 07. For practice: a
  judgment against a seller found in the title search must be released, paid or negotiated before
  closing; a judgment against a *similar name* is resolved by the title company's identity
  procedures, not by the licence holder's guess.
- **Mechanic's and materialman's liens.** Texas gives those who improve land a specific, involuntary
  lien on what they improved, and gives it generously. Property Code § 53.021 extends the lien to a
  person who, under a contract with the owner or the owner's agent, contractor or subcontractor,
  *labors or furnishes labor or materials* for construction or repair of an improvement; to one who
  *specially fabricates* material **even if the material is never delivered**; to licensed
  architects, engineers and surveyors for their plans, plats and surveys; to landscapers of an
  improvement; and to demolition contractors. Section 53.001 defines "improvement" just as broadly —
  buildings and structures of every kind, clearing, fencing, machinery, and design work itself. Two
  practice consequences follow. *First*, recent construction on a listing means potential lien
  claimants the deed records do not yet show — the general contractor may be paid while a
  subcontractor or the lumberyard is not, and their claims can surface after closing. *Second*,
  because the lien can secure work contracted by an "agent, trustee, receiver, contractor, or
  subcontractor," the owner's own diligence — retainage practices, lien waivers, affidavits of bills
  paid at closing — is the real protection, and Texas closing practice is built around collecting
  them. The licence holder's reflex, whenever the phrase "we just remodelled" appears: ask what was
  done, when, by whom, and whether everyone has been paid — and route the paper questions to the
  title company early.
- **Other statutory and equitable liens** — federal tax liens, owners'-association assessment liens
  (which § 32.05 mentions precisely because the property tax lien outranks them), landlord's and
  vendor's liens — populate title searches and are handled, like all title matters, by requiring
  their release or payoff at closing.

**Where the mechanic's lien meets the homestead.** The two strongest ideas in this module collide
on residential remodels. Property Code § 41.001(b)(3) allows a work-and-material encumbrance to be
properly fixed on a homestead only *if contracted for in writing as provided by Sections 53.254(a),
(b), and (c)* — the statute conditions the lien's validity against a homestead on formalities the
parties must satisfy up front, in writing. This course has read § 41.001's cross-reference, not
§ 53.254 itself, so the content of those formalities is left to counsel; the licence holder's
operational rule needs no more: a contractor's claim against a *homestead* stands on different, and
stricter, footing than the same claim against investment property, and a handshake remodel of a
family home is a title problem in the making on both sides of the deal.

**Reading a lien situation.** For any lien, ask the same four questions in order: What debt does it
secure? Is it voluntary or involuntary? Is it general or specific? Where does it rank? Rank —
priority — is usually first-in-time by recording date, except where a statute says otherwise; you
have now met the great exception (the tax lien), and Module 08 completes the picture with
foreclosure, where rank becomes money.

## Unit: Water Rights

Water rights in Texas are a body of law large enough for its own course; the principles a licence
holder must carry are few but firm.

**Surface water in watercourses belongs to the state.** Water Code § 11.021, read in full for this
course, declares that *the water of the ordinary flow, underflow and tides of every flowing river,
natural stream and lake, and of every bay or arm of the Gulf of Mexico, and the storm water,
floodwater and rainwater of every river, natural stream, canyon, ravine, depression and watershed in
the state is the property of the state*. A deed bounded by a river conveys land; the flowing water is
Texas's, used by private persons only under rights granted through the state's permitting system.
Riparian frontage is access and amenity — it is not ownership of the stream.

**Groundwater is different in kind.** Percolating groundwater under a parcel is treated in Texas as
part of the landowner's property, historically governed by the **rule of capture** — an owner may
pump what the owner's wells can produce — with modern management layered on through local groundwater
conservation districts that permit and space wells in much of the state. The licence holder's
takeaways: a working well is not proof of a *right* to pump indefinitely; district rules vary by
county; and groundwater estates, like mineral estates, can be severed and sold apart from the surface.

**Diffuse surface water** — sheet runoff before it reaches a watercourse — follows drainage
doctrines: an owner may not gather and discharge it onto a neighbour to the neighbour's injury.
Grading and drainage disputes are neighbour law, frequent, and firmly in the route-to-counsel
category.

**A vocabulary for water conversations.** Three word-pairs organise nearly every client question.
*Riparian and littoral* describe location: riparian land abuts a flowing watercourse, littoral land
abuts a sea or large lake — and in Texas both words describe frontage, not ownership of the state's
water. *Surface water and groundwater* describe source, and the legal regimes differ in kind: the
state's ownership and permitting under Water Code § 11.021 for the one, the landowner-centred
capture-and-district regime for the other — so "can I use the water?" has no single answer until you
know which water. *Right and permit* describe authority: on working land, what matters is never the
physical presence of water but the documented authority to take it, in whatever amount, for
whatever use, from whatever source. A licence holder who keeps the three pairs straight can frame
any water question accurately — and will notice immediately when a seller's claim ("the place has
water rights") is actually an assumption wearing a deed's clothes.

**For practice:** waterfront and rural listings demand precision. Describe frontage, access and
improvements; never advertise ownership of a flowing stream; treat irrigation rights, well permits
and district rules as documents to obtain, not assumptions to make. Water questions on working land —
who may pump how much, from what, under which permit — decide value, and they are answered from the
permits and the district, not from the seller's memory.

## Unit: Residence as a Homestead

Texas homestead law is the state's signature protection of the family home, and the Commission gives
it fifty minutes because a licence holder meets it constantly: in listing intake, in seller net
sheets, in refinances that cannot lawfully close, and in tax exemption questions every January. The
essential discipline is that **"homestead" names two distinct doctrines** that share a word: an
*exemption from creditors' seizure* in the Property Code, and an *exemption from part of property
taxation* in the Tax Code. Conflating them is the classic error; this unit separates them.

**Doctrine one: protection from forced sale.** Property Code § 41.001 exempts a homestead from
*seizure for the claims of creditors* — with a closed list of exceptions, the encumbrances that
*can* be properly fixed on homestead property. The read text names, among them: **purchase-money**
liens (the loan that bought the home); **taxes on the property**; and **work and material liens**
for improvements, when contracted for in the manner the law requires. The pattern to internalise:
the homestead is armoured against *general* creditors — credit cards, most judgments, business
debts — but not against the debts that created or preserved or improved the property itself. A
judgment creditor of an ordinary kind cannot force the sale of a Texas homestead; the judgment
simply waits, unable to touch the exempt home.

**How much land is protected.** Property Code § 41.002 draws the boundaries: an **urban** homestead
of not more than **10 acres** in one or more contiguous lots, used as a home or as both a home and a
place of business; a **rural** homestead of not more than **200 acres for a family** and **100 acres
for a single adult**, in one or more parcels, with the improvements. The urban/rural
classification follows the statute's own test (services and municipal location), and the acreage
caps are caps on *exempt quantity* — a 400-acre ranch homestead is protected as to 200 acres, and a
creditor's remedies operate on the excess.

**What this means in transactions.** Homestead protection does not make a home unsellable or
unmortgageable — the owner may always sell voluntarily, and may voluntarily grant the liens on the
statutory list. It means, instead, that *non-listed* liens do not attach to the homestead, and that
lending against a Texas homestead is a specialised, rule-bound practice: the categories of valid
homestead liens are limited, home-improvement contracts must be made "in the manner required" for
the work-and-material lien to be good, and a lender's error in this area can void its lien entirely.
The licence holder's role is recognition and routing: know that the homestead rules exist, spot the
transactions they touch — a seller's judgment that may not actually encumber the home; an
improvement contract signed at a kitchen table; a proposed second lien of a kind the list does not
allow — and insist the title company and counsel resolve them before anyone promises a closing.

**The complete list of liens that can bind a homestead.** Because "which debts can reach the home"
is the whole doctrine, the closed list of § 41.001(b) deserves to be read in full. Encumbrances may
be properly fixed on homestead property for: **(1)** purchase money; **(2)** taxes on the property;
**(3)** work and material used in constructing improvements, if contracted for in writing as
provided by §§ 53.254(a)–(c); **(4)** an **owelty of partition** imposed against the entirety of
the property by court order or by the parties' written partition agreement — including a debt of
one spouse to the other resulting from the award of a family homestead in a divorce; **(5)** the
**refinance** of a lien against a homestead, including a federal tax lien resulting from the tax
debt of both spouses (for a family homestead) or of the owner; **(6)** an **extension of credit**
meeting the requirements of Article XVI, § 50(a)(6) of the Texas Constitution — the
constitutionally regulated home-equity loan; and **(7)** a **reverse mortgage** meeting the
requirements of § 50(k)–(p) of that article. The structure teaches the policy: every valid
homestead lien either created the property interest (purchase money), preserves it against the
sovereign (taxes, tax-lien refinance), improved it under written formalities, adjusts ownership
itself (owelty), or is a constitutionally supervised borrowing the owner chose. Everything else —
however large the debt, however solemn the judgment — stays outside.

**The proceeds follow the protection — for six months.** Section 41.001(c) extends the shield past
the sale: *the homestead claimant's proceeds of a sale of a homestead are not subject to seizure
for a creditor's claim for six months after the date of sale.* A seller can therefore sell an
exempt home, hold the money, and reinvest it in a new homestead without the waiting judgment
intercepting the funds — provided the reinvestment happens within the window. For practice, this is
why a seller with known judgments still has a lawful path from one homestead to the next, and why
the timing of that path is a matter for the seller's counsel, not for a licence holder's assurance.

**Urban or rural is a statutory test, not an impression.** Under § 41.002(c), a homestead is
*urban* if, at the time of designation, the property is **(1)** located within the limits of a
municipality or its extraterritorial jurisdiction or a platted subdivision, **and (2)** served by
police protection, paid or volunteer fire protection, and at least three of five listed
municipally provided services: electric, natural gas, sewer, storm sewer, and water. Fail the test
and the homestead is rural, with the larger acreage allowances of § 41.002(b) — 200 acres for a
family, 100 for a single adult, in one or more parcels that need not be contiguous. And
§ 41.002(d) makes the definitions retroactive in reach: they apply *to all homesteads in this
state whenever created*. On the urban side the 10 acres must be contiguous lots; on the rural side
the parcels may be scattered — a farmhouse on forty acres and a hay meadow down the road can both
sit inside one rural homestead.

**Doctrine two: the residence homestead tax exemption.** Entirely separate machinery. Tax Code
§ 11.13, read in full for this course, grants a bundle of exemptions from *ad valorem taxation* for
a **residence homestead** — the owner-occupied principal residence. In the text as held for this
course: § 11.13(a) grants a family or single adult a **$3,000** exemption from the county taxes
levied under Article VIII, Section 1-a of the Texas Constitution (the statute itself names the
constitutional source); § 11.13(b) grants an adult a **$140,000** exemption of appraised value from
**school district** taxation for the residence homestead; § 11.13(c) adds a further **$60,000**
school-tax exemption for an owner who is **disabled or 65 or older**; and § 11.13(d)–(e) authorise
optional additional exemptions that individual taxing units may adopt, of at least $3,000. The
mechanics matter less than the structure: the exemptions subtract from the taxable value of a
qualifying principal residence, unit by taxing unit, on application by the owner — and the dollar
figures are the Legislature's to change, so the current statute, not memory, is always the source
for the numbers.

**Keeping the doctrines straight.** The creditor-protection homestead (Property Code Chapter 41)
asks: *can this debt reach this home?* The tax homestead (Tax Code § 11.13) asks: *how much of this
home's value is taxed?* A property can be a homestead for both purposes, either, or neither; the
qualifications differ; and nothing about claiming the tax exemption creates the creditor protection
or vice versa. When a client uses the word "homestead," the licence holder's first move is to find
out which doctrine the situation actually involves — and the second is to remember that applying
either doctrine to a client's specific facts is legal or tax advice, which the licence holder
recognises, frames and refers.

### Statute reading exercise: the two homesteads

Answer from §§ 41.001–41.002 and 11.13 as set out above: **(1)** A credit-card company wins a
judgment against a homeowner. May it force the sale of the 8-acre urban homestead? **(2)** The same
homeowner owes delinquent property taxes. Different answer? Why? **(3)** A 67-year-old owner asks
what school-tax exemptions the statute gives her principal residence. Name the two figures in the
held text of § 11.13(b) and (c). *(Answers: (1) no — a general judgment is not on § 41.001's list of
encumbrances that may be fixed on a homestead, and 8 urban acres is within § 41.002's 10-acre cap;
(2) yes — taxes on the property are expressly excepted by § 41.001, and Tax Code § 32.05 gives the
tax lien priority even over the homestead interest; (3) $140,000 under § 11.13(b) plus $60,000 under
§ 11.13(c).)*

### Applied exercise: reading a title commitment's Schedule of trouble

*A buyer's title commitment on a 12-year-old suburban home lists: (i) the current year's taxes, not
yet due; (ii) a utility easement along the rear five feet; (iii) subdivision restrictive covenants;
(iv) an abstract of judgment against the seller for $22,000 from a business dispute; (v) a deed of
trust securing the seller's mortgage; and (vi) a mechanic's lien affidavit filed last month by a
pool contractor.* **Task:** classify each item (money/non-money; voluntary/involuntary;
general/specific), state which items will be cleared at closing and which the buyer will live with,
and flag anything requiring special handling. **Model answer:** (i) involuntary, specific, statutory
— attached January 1 under § 32.01; not "cleared" but prorated and provided for; outranks everything
(§ 32.05). (ii) non-money encumbrance appurtenant to the utility — survives closing; buyer takes
subject to it. (iii) non-money covenants running with the land — survive; buyer should receive and
review them. (iv) involuntary, general judgment lien — must be paid or released at closing *unless*
the property is the seller's homestead, in which case it never validly attached (§ 41.001); either
way, resolution is the title company's and counsel's call, not the agent's assurance. (v) voluntary,
specific — paid off and released through closing. (vi) involuntary, specific under § 53.021 — a live
dispute; requires payoff, release, or title-company resolution (indemnity/escrow) before closing;
the agent's job was asking about that new pool at listing intake.

### Module recap

Estates measure ownership by duration, and Texas's default is maximal: a conveyance passes fee simple
unless expressly limited (§ 5.001), future estates may be created by deed (§ 5.041), and leaseholds
complete the ladder. Encumbrances are others' claims — liens for money (the § 51.0001 vocabulary:
security instrument, mortgagor, mortgagee, trustee), easements, covenants and encroachments for use.
The property tax lien attaches to every parcel each January 1 without any filing (§ 32.01) and
outranks homesteads, prior lienholders and association liens alike (§ 32.05). Mechanic's lien
protection reaches labour, materials, special fabrication, design professionals, landscaping and
demolition (§§ 53.001, 53.021), making recent construction a standing closing risk. Flowing water in
natural watercourses is state property (Water Code § 11.021); groundwater follows capture as managed
by districts. And the homestead is two doctrines: Chapter 41's shield against non-listed creditors —
10 urban acres, 200 rural family acres — and § 11.13's tax exemptions ($3,000 county; $140,000
school; $60,000 more at 65 or disabled, in the text as held), separate in purpose, machinery and
consequence.
