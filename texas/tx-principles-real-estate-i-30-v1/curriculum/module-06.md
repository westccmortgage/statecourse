# Texas Principles of Real Estate I — Module 06

## TREC Topic: Real Estate Contracts

**Prescribed time: 215 minutes.** Five units: contract fundamentals, the elements that make an
agreement enforceable, what happens on breach, the real estate sales contract itself, and the TREC
promulgated forms — the unit the Commission weights heaviest in this topic, and the one most
distinctively Texan.

**Learning objectives.** Classify contracts using the standard vocabulary; state the elements of an
enforceable contract and test a fact pattern against them; apply the Texas statute of frauds to real
estate agreements, including the one-year lease line; identify the remedies for breach and the Texas
limitation periods that bound them; walk through the anatomy of a residential sales transaction from
offer to closing; and state — precisely, because licences end over it — what the TREC form rules
require of licence holders, where the forms come from, and where the unauthorized practice of law
begins.

*A scope note: this module teaches contract principles as background for practice. The dedicated
30-hour Law of Contracts and Promulgated Contract Forms courses in this six-course program treat the
subject at full depth; Principles I builds the frame they fill.*

## Unit: Contracts

A **contract** is an agreement the law will enforce: a promise or exchange of promises with legal
consequences for non-performance. Everything a licence holder touches runs on contracts — listing
agreements, buyer representation agreements, sales contracts, leases, options — and the classifying
vocabulary is the profession's grammar.

- **Express or implied.** An express contract is stated in words, written or oral; an implied
  contract arises from conduct.
- **Bilateral or unilateral.** A bilateral contract exchanges a promise for a promise — the sales
  contract: seller promises to convey, buyer promises to pay. A unilateral contract exchanges a
  promise for an *act*: the classic example is an option, where the optionor promises to sell if the
  optionee elects, and the optionee promises nothing.
- **Executory or executed.** An executory contract has performance still owed — every pending sales
  contract between effective date and closing; an executed contract is fully performed. (Texas
  practice also uses "executory contract" in a narrower specialised sense for long-term
  contract-for-deed arrangements, which carry their own regulatory regime treated in later courses;
  in this module the term carries its general meaning.)
- **Valid, void, voidable, unenforceable.** A valid contract binds both parties. A void agreement
  never was a contract (an agreement to do an illegal act). A voidable contract binds unless a
  protected party elects out — the minor's contract, the contract induced by fraud or duress. An
  unenforceable contract is real but the courts will not compel it — the category into which
  otherwise-good oral land contracts fall, as the statute of frauds unit shows.

**The vocabulary, drilled.** Classification questions answer themselves once each pair is asked
separately of the same instrument. Work these:

1. *A listing agreement* — express (written words), bilateral in its usual form (broker promises
   services and diligence, seller promises commission on procuring a buyer), executory until the
   sale closes or the term ends.
2. *An option to purchase held open for 90 days for a $500 fee* — express, **unilateral**: the
   optionor is bound to sell if the optionee elects; the optionee owes nothing but the fee already
   paid, and may simply let the option die. On exercise, a bilateral sales contract springs up on
   the option's stated terms.
3. *A homeowner waves in a neighbour's landscaper who begins mowing; the homeowner watches and
   says nothing* — the frame of an implied contract: assent shown by conduct, with a duty to pay
   the reasonable value.
4. *A signed agreement to sell a liquor licence to a felon barred from holding one* — void: illegal
   object, never a contract, nothing to ratify.
5. *A 17-year-old signs a lease* — voidable at the minor's election, binding on the adult party
   meanwhile: the protection runs one way.
6. *An oral agreement to sell a ranch, admitted by both parties on video* — real agreement,
   **unenforceable** against either party over a statute-of-frauds defence: candour is not a
   writing.

The habit the drill builds: never ask "is this a contract?" as one question. Ask *formed how,
promising what, performed how far, enforceable against whom* — four small questions with four
checkable answers.

## Unit: Elements of a Contract

Enforceability has requirements, and every transaction document can be audited against them.

**1. Competent parties.** Parties must have legal capacity — age of majority and sound mind. Recall
the entity dimension from Module 05: an entity is competent through authorised signers, and
authority is verified, not assumed.

**2. Offer and acceptance (mutual assent).** One party proposes definite terms; the other accepts
them unequivocally. A purported acceptance that changes terms is a **counteroffer** — a rejection
joined to a new offer, which kills the original and reverses the roles. Offers may be revoked before
acceptance; communicated acceptance closes the loop and forms the contract. In practice the licence
holder is the courier of offer and acceptance, and sloppiness here — un-communicated acceptances,
initialed changes never re-signed — creates the disputes that fill contract litigation.

**How offers die.** An offer is a power in the offeree, and it ends in five standard ways, each of
which a licence holder should recognise on sight: **lapse** — the offer's own deadline passes, or a
reasonable time expires where none was stated (which is why every written offer should carry an
express acceptance deadline); **revocation** — the offeror withdraws before acceptance, which the
offeror may generally do even inside a stated period unless an option (separate consideration)
holds it open; **rejection** — the offeree declines, extinguishing the power; **counteroffer** —
rejection plus a new offer, as above, the most common death in residential practice; and **death or
incapacity** of a party before acceptance. The transaction-desk corollaries: a buyer "sleeping on"
a seller's counter holds a power that the seller can revoke at any moment before acceptance is
communicated — so speed is protection; and once a counter is made, the original offer cannot be
"accepted after all" — it is gone, and only a new agreement on its old terms revives it.

**Acceptance and the effective date.** Acceptance must mirror the offer and must be *communicated* —
in Texas form practice, the final contract exists when the last party to sign has signed and that
acceptance is communicated back, and the forms then have the parties fill in an **effective date**
from which every deadline in the document counts. The licence holder's mechanical duties cluster
exactly here: transmit offers and counters promptly, obtain full execution (every change initialed
by all parties — an un-initialed strike-through is an acceptance that varies the offer, i.e. a
counteroffer nobody noticed), communicate final acceptance, and complete the effective date. More
contract disputes grow from this paragraph than from any doctrine in the module.

### Applied exercise: the counteroffer chain

*Monday: Buyer offers $300,000, acceptance deadline Friday noon. Tuesday: Seller signs a counter at
$315,000. Wednesday: before the counter reaches Buyer, a second buyer offers $320,000 and Seller
"accepts" it. Thursday: Buyer, having finally received Tuesday's counter, signs it and her agent
communicates acceptance.* **Task:** who has a contract, and what did each Tuesday-to-Thursday event
do? **Model answer:** Seller's Tuesday counter killed Buyer's $300,000 offer and created a new
offer to Buyer at $315,000. Seller's Wednesday acceptance of the second buyer formed a contract
with that buyer — but it did **not** revoke the outstanding counter to Buyer, because revocation
must be communicated. Buyer's Thursday acceptance of a live counter therefore formed a second
contract at $315,000. Seller now has two enforceable contracts to sell one house — a lawyer's
emergency created entirely by uncommunicated paper. The practice lesson: counters are withdrawn in
writing, communicated, *before* other offers are accepted.

**3. Consideration.** Each side must give something of legal value — a price, a promise, a
forbearance. Adequacy is for the parties (courts do not reprice bargains); existence is for the law.
In the sales contract the promises themselves are the consideration; the earnest money that
accompanies a Texas contract is a performance-assurance deposit, valuable in practice but not the
thing that makes the contract binding.

**Consideration's edge cases, briefly.** Three recurring desk questions. *"The contract says ten
dollars and other good and valuable consideration — is that real?"* Recited nominal consideration
is a drafting convention; what makes the sales contract binding is the exchange of promises
themselves, and courts do not weigh the bargain's adequacy. *"The buyer never deposited the
earnest money — so there's no contract, right?"* Wrong premise: the earnest money is not the
consideration, so its absence does not un-form the contract — but the failure to deliver it is a
breach of a covenant, with whatever consequences the contract attaches. *"Past favours count?"*
No: consideration must be bargained for in the present exchange; gratitude for services already
rendered will not support a new promise. Each answer is a sentence, and each prevents a client
from acting on folk contract law.

**4. Legal purpose.** An agreement to accomplish an illegal object is void.

**5. Reality of consent.** Assent obtained by fraud, misrepresentation, duress, undue influence or
induced mistake makes a contract voidable by the injured party. This element is where a licence
holder's disclosure conduct and contract validity intersect: a seller's or agent's material
misrepresentation can hand the buyer a rescission right.

**Sorting the consent defects.** The reality-of-consent element covers distinct doctrines a
licence holder should keep separate, because the practice responses differ. **Fraud** is a knowing
false representation of material fact intended to induce reliance — the seller who paints over the
crack and denies movement; it supports rescission and, as the previous unit's limitation statute
showed, a four-year clock of its own. **Innocent misrepresentation** is the same false statement
made without knowledge of falsity — less culpable, still capable of unwinding assent.
**Concealment** is fraud by silence where disclosure is due — and Texas structures much of that
duty for home sales through the § 5.008 disclosure notice the next unit places on the calendar.
**Duress and undue influence** substitute another's will for the party's own — rare at closing
tables, live in family and elder transactions, and a reason careful practice slows down when one
signer seems to be steering another. **Mistake** divides in two: a *mutual* mistake about a basic
fact (both parties bargained over a lot that does not exist as described) can unwind the deal; a
*unilateral* mistake (the buyer misjudged renovation costs) generally binds the mistaken party.
For the licence holder the operational rule across all five is identical and blunt: the agent's
own accuracy is part of the contract's validity — a misstatement by the seller's agent can hand
the buyer the rescission right the seller then litigates.

**6. Writing, where a statute requires it** — the subject of the next paragraphs, because for real
estate the writing requirement is the gate through which everything must pass.

**The Texas statute of frauds.** Business & Commerce Code § 26.01, read in full for this course,
provides that certain promises are *not enforceable unless the promise or agreement, or a memorandum
of it, is (1) in writing; and (2) signed by the person to be charged* with it (or that person's
authorised representative). The section's list includes, alongside suretyship and other categories,
the two that define this occupation: **a contract for the sale of real estate**, and **a lease of
real estate for a term longer than one year**. Consequences worth spelling out: an oral contract to
sell land is not void — it simply cannot be enforced against a party who did not sign a writing,
which in litigation amounts to the same thing; a month-to-month oral lease is fine, while an oral
two-year lease is enforceable only as far as the writing requirement's case-law accommodations
allow, which no licence holder should ever rely on; and "signed by the person to be charged" means
enforcement is asymmetric — a writing signed by the seller alone may bind the seller and not the
buyer. Module 03 already showed the conveyance-side twin of this rule: Property Code § 5.021
requires a conveyance of an estate of more than one year to be in writing, subscribed and delivered.
Between them, the two statutes deliver the profession's iron rule: **in Texas real estate, if it is
not in writing and signed, plan as if it does not exist.**

### Statute reading exercise: the writing requirement's edges

From Business & Commerce Code § 26.01 as stated above: **(1)** A landlord and tenant orally agree to
a nine-month lease. Enforceable? **(2)** The same parties orally agree to a two-year lease.
**(3)** A buyer signs a written offer; the seller orally accepts and then refuses to close. May the
buyer enforce? *(Answers: (1) yes — only leases longer than one year are within the statute; (2) not
enforceable over a statute-of-frauds defence; (3) no — the seller is "the person to be charged" and
signed nothing; the buyer's signed offer binds the buyer, not the seller.)*

### Statute reading exercise: § 26.01's list, beyond the sale

*Business & Commerce Code § 26.01 reaches more than sales contracts, and two of its other entries
touch this occupation.* From the section as taught: **(1)** A broker orally promises a lender,
"if the borrower doesn't pay, I will" — enforceable? **(2)** A buyer claims the seller's agent
orally promised that the seller "would pay for a new roof after closing" — what two independent
problems does the claim face? **(3)** State the general rule for what a writing under § 26.01 must
contain and who must sign it. *(Answers: (1) no — a promise to answer for another's debt
(suretyship) is within the statute and unenforceable unsigned; (2) the promise concerns an
agreement about real property performance and was never written, and the person to be charged (the
seller) signed nothing — plus, as the agency materials of later courses develop, the agent could
not bind the seller to it anyway; (3) the promise or agreement, or a memorandum of it, must be in
writing and signed by the person to be charged or that person's lawfully authorized
representative.)*

## Unit: Breach of a Contract

A **breach** is an unexcused failure to perform a contractual duty when due. The law's responses:

- **Damages** — money to put the injured party where performance would have. In a failed sale,
  typically the difference between contract price and market value, plus recoverable consequential
  losses.
- **Specific performance** — a court order compelling actual performance. Land's uniqueness (Module
  01's economics becoming law) makes specific performance a characteristic real estate remedy: money
  is presumed inadequate for a particular parcel, so a buyer can often compel a breaching seller to
  convey.
- **Rescission** — unwinding the contract and restoring the parties to their pre-contract
  positions; the remedy paired with reality-of-consent defects.
- **Liquidated damages** — an amount the parties fixed in advance as the remedy. In Texas
  residential practice the working example is the seller's option, on buyer default, to keep the
  earnest money as liquidated damages — a contract choice, with consequences, made in the form
  itself.

**Rescission's logic, and the election among remedies.** Rescission differs in kind from damages:
damages enforce the bargain's value; rescission declares the bargain undone and pairs with
**restitution** — each side returns what it received, deed against price, possession against
deposits. That is why rescission belongs to the consent-defect cases (fraud, misrepresentation,
mutual mistake): the law unwinds an agreement whose formation was corrupted rather than pricing a
performance that was promised cleanly. And because the remedies aim at incompatible destinations —
keeping the bargain's value against erasing the bargain — an injured party ultimately **elects**:
one cannot both rescind a sale and recover the benefit of it. For the licence holder, the triage
consequence: when a buyer says "they lied about the foundation," the fork in the road (keep the
house and pursue the loss, or seek to unwind entirely) is a strategy election with deadlines
attached, made with counsel — and the agent's contribution is the preserved record: the
disclosure notice, the photographs, the messages, the dates.

**Time limits: the Texas periods.** Rights decay on a statutory clock. Civil Practice & Remedies
Code § 16.004, read for this course, sets a **four-year** limitations period for suits on **specific
performance of a contract for the conveyance of real property**, for **debt**, for **fraud**, and
for **breach of fiduciary duty**; § 16.051 supplies a residual four-year period for actions without
an express one (excepting recovery of real property, which has its own scheme — Module 07's adverse
possession clocks). For the licence holder these periods are professional context, not tools: their
lesson is that disputes have deadlines, that records outlive closings, and that "it's been years"
is a fact for a lawyer to evaluate, not a reassurance to offer.

**Damages, worked once.** *A seller under a $400,000 contract refuses to close in a falling
market; comparable sales now support $385,000. The buyer had paid $600 for an inspection and $450
for an appraisal.* The buyer's expectation measure is the benefit of the bargain: contract price
minus market value at breach — here the market fell *below* the contract price, so the bargain
itself has no positive value to the buyer, and the recoverable core is the reliance outlay (the
inspection and appraisal spent on a performance never delivered). Reverse the market — value risen
to $430,000 — and the same formula yields $30,000 of expectation damages, which is precisely why
sellers breach in rising markets and why the specific-performance remedy matters most exactly
then: the buyer of a unique parcel need not settle for the money difference but can compel the
conveyance itself. Two disciplines ride along in every damages conversation: the injured party is
expected to **mitigate** — act reasonably to limit the loss, as by re-marketing a property after a
buyer's default — and remedies are *elected*, not stacked: the seller who takes the earnest money
as liquidated damages has chosen that remedy in place of proving actual loss.

### Statute reading exercise: the clock on the collapsed deal

From CPRC §§ 16.004 and 16.051 as stated above: **(1)** A seller repudiated a sales contract in
March 2022; the buyer wants specific performance today. Within time? **(2)** A partnership dispute
over a broker's fiduciary duty surfaces five years after the conduct. **(3)** A claim arises under
an agreement with no express limitations statute of its own — what period applies, and what real
property claim does § 16.051 expressly leave to its own scheme? *(Answers: (1) suit for specific
performance of a contract for the conveyance of real property must be brought not later than four
years after the cause of action accrues — March 2022 to today (2026) sits at the boundary the
buyer's lawyer must compute exactly; the licence holder's only correct contribution is "see that
lawyer now"; (2) breach of fiduciary duty carries the same four-year period, so the claim is in
peril; (3) the residual period is four years — and § 16.051 excepts recovery of real property,
which lives under the adverse-possession limitations of Module 07.)*

**Performance short of breach.** Contracts also end without anyone breaching: by full performance;
by mutual agreement to release; by novation (substituting a new contract or party); or by
impossibility of the kinds the law recognises. And a party's duties may be excused by failure of a
**condition** — the financing and inspection contingencies of the next unit — which is exit by the
contract's own terms, not breach at all. Distinguishing "the contract died by its conditions" from
"someone defaulted" is the first triage question in every collapsed deal.

## Unit: Real Estate Contracts for Sale

The residential sales contract is the transaction's constitution — every later event happens
because, when, and how the contract says. Its recurring anatomy:

**Parties and property.** Named parties with capacity; the legal description carried exactly
(Module 04's discipline); included fixtures and excluded items itemised (Module 02's discipline
applied in ink).

**Price and financing.** The price, its composition (cash, financed amount), and the financing
terms or contingency: what loan the buyer will seek, and what happens if it cannot be had. A
financing contingency is a *condition* — with deadlines and notice mechanics that decide who keeps
the earnest money when lending fails.

**Earnest money.** The buyer's deposit, held by an escrow agent — in Texas practice, ordinarily the
title company — as evidence of seriousness and the fund from which liquidated damages are paid if
the buyer defaults. Not consideration, not a cap on every remedy unless the contract makes it one,
and never properly held in a broker's operating account.

**Earnest money's whole life cycle.** Because the deposit is where collapsed deals fight, walk its
path once completely: it is *promised* in the contract (amount and delivery deadline); *delivered*
to the named escrow agent within the stated days, with the contract stating what late delivery
does; *held* by the escrow agent as a stakeholder for both parties — never in a broker's account,
never disbursed on one side's demand; *credited* to the buyer at closing against the purchase
price (Module 09 shows it on the statement); and, in a terminated deal, *released* only on the
parties' signed release or through the escrow agent's prescribed procedures. Three practice
truths: the deposit's size is negotiation, not law; "the title company is holding it" is a
neutral fact, not a signal about who will get it; and an agent who promises a client "you'll get
your earnest money back, no problem" has predicted the outcome of a contract-construction question
that belongs to the documents and, if contested, to lawyers.

**Title and survey.** The seller will furnish a title commitment; the buyer may object to
exceptions; the survey (new or existing) feeds the objection process — the machinery by which
Module 03's encumbrances and Module 04's boundaries are surfaced, negotiated or accepted before
closing. The seller's-disclosure obligation of Property Code § 5.008 (Module 07 treats it with
deeds and title) rides alongside: the seller of a single-dwelling residence delivers the prescribed
condition notice.

**The seller's disclosure notice, § 5.008 in detail.** Because the notice governs nearly every
resale file, its statutory mechanics deserve their own passage, all from the read text. *Who and
what*: a seller of residential real property *comprising not more than one dwelling unit* gives
the purchaser the prescribed written notice — or one substantially similar containing at minimum
all its items. *What the notice is*: the statute's own capitalised preamble answers — a disclosure
of the SELLER'S KNOWLEDGE of the property's condition as of the date signed, NOT a substitute for
inspections or warranties, and NOT A WARRANTY OF ANY KIND by seller or seller's agents. *The
standard*: completed to the best of the seller's belief and knowledge as of signing, and where the
seller does not know, § 5.008(d) says marking the item "unknown" *is compliance* — the notice
demands honesty, not omniscience. *Who is excused*: § 5.008(e) lists transfers outside the duty,
and the list is a map of situations where the transferor lacks occupant's knowledge or the
transfer is not a market sale: court orders and foreclosure sales; trustees in bankruptcy;
transfers to or by mortgagees and foreclosure purchasers; fiduciaries administering estates,
guardianships and trusts; co-owner to co-owner; transfers to a spouse or within the lineal line;
and divorce-decree transfers between spouses, among others. *Timing and the remedy*: under
§ 5.008(f) the notice is delivered **on or before the effective date** of a binding contract — and
if the contract is entered *without* it, the purchaser may terminate **for any reason within seven
days after receiving** the late notice. The agent's operational translation: get the notice into
the buyer's hands before signing, because a late notice hands the buyer a seven-day walk-away the
seller never priced.

**Property condition and inspections.** An inspection window with negotiated repair or termination
rights — in the promulgated forms, structured as a purchased option period giving the buyer a
unilateral right to terminate within the agreed days.

**Closing and possession.** The closing date; possession's transfer at closing or by temporary
lease; prorations of taxes and rents (Module 09's arithmetic).

**Default and remedies.** The paired remedies of the previous unit, allocated by the form:
buyer default → seller's choice of earnest money as liquidated damages or other remedies; seller
default → buyer's choice including specific performance.

**The transaction as a calendar.** Read once as anatomy, the contract should be read a second time
as a schedule, because that is how a licence holder actually lives inside it. From the effective
date: the earnest money and any option fee are delivered within their stated days; the option
period (if bought) runs — the buyer inspects, negotiates repairs, or terminates unilaterally while
it lasts; the seller's disclosure notice under Property Code § 5.008 is delivered if it was not
before signing, with the statute's own remedies when it arrives late; the title commitment issues
and the buyer's objection window opens and closes; the survey arrives on its own deadline and
feeds the same objection machinery; the financing contingency's deadlines run — application,
approval, and the notice that lending failed, each with stated days and stated consequences for
the earnest money; and the closing date arrives with time computed as the contract prescribes. The
professional habit this paragraph exists to install: **calendar every deadline the day the
contract goes effective**, in writing, with reminders ahead of each — because a condition's
deadline missed by a day converts a protected exit into a default, and no apology recovers it.

### Applied exercise: triage of a collapsed deal

*Twenty days into a thirty-day contract, the buyer's loan is denied. The buyer's agent emails the
listing agent: "Loan fell through, we're out, please release the earnest money." The seller
answers: "They defaulted. I'm keeping it."* **Task:** using the covenant/condition distinction and
the calendar discipline above, list the questions that decide who is right, and state the licence
holder's role while they are decided. **Model answer:** the deciding questions are mechanical:
Did the contract include a financing contingency, and on what terms? Did the buyer perform its
covenants under it — timely application, diligence? Was the denial within the contingency's scope
and period, and was notice given in the form and by the deadline the contract requires? If yes
throughout, the condition failed and the buyer exits with the earnest money; if the notice was
late or the buyer never applied, the exit may have converted into default, and the seller's
liquidated-damages election is on the table. The licence holders' role: assemble the dated record
(applications, denials, notices), transmit the release paperwork the escrow agent requires, and
route the dispute — if it stays one — to the parties' attorneys; agents advocate their clients'
positions but do not adjudicate the earnest money, and the escrow agent releases it only on the
parties' agreement or proper process.

**Options and first refusals, worked.** The two satellites earn a worked contrast. *Option:* a
landowner grants an investor a 6-month option to buy at $500,000 for a $5,000 option fee. For six
months the owner cannot sell to anyone else and cannot revoke — the fee purchased that
irrevocability, which is what distinguishes an option from a mere offer. The investor may walk
away silently (losing the fee), or exercise, at which moment a bilateral purchase contract exists
on the option's terms and the ordinary closing machinery begins. Whether the fee credits against
the price is whatever the option says — a drafting question, hence a lawyer's. *Right of first
refusal:* the neighbour holds a ROFR on the same land. Nothing happens — no price is fixed, no
duty to sell exists — until the owner decides to sell and receives a third party's offer; then the
holder may match it within the stated window or lose the right for that sale. The contrast in one
line: an option fixes price and compels nothing of its holder; a ROFR fixes nothing and waits for
the market. Both encumber marketability, both surface in title work, and an owner who grants
either casually has narrowed every future negotiation — which is why the licence holder treats
requests to "just add a first refusal for my brother" as attorney work, not a favour.

**Conditions versus covenants, one more time.** Every clause above is one or the other: a covenant
is a promise whose breach is default; a condition is a gate whose failure excuses performance
without fault. Licence holders who track which is which — and calendar every condition's
deadline — prevent most of the disputes this unit's remedies exist to resolve.

**Options and rights of first refusal**, the sales contract's satellites: an **option** is a
unilateral contract holding an offer open for a fee — binding on the optionor, free on the
optionee until exercise; a **right of first refusal** is a weaker preference, activated only when
the owner elects to sell on terms the holder may match. Both are interests that surface in title
work, and both are drafting-intensive instruments for lawyers.

## Unit: TREC Forms

Texas answers a question most states leave loose — *who writes the contracts non-lawyer agents
use?* — with a public institution, and the licence holder's duties around it are statutory,
specific, and enforced.

**The source of the forms.** Occupations Code § 1101.155, read for this course, authorises the
Commission to *adopt rules in the public's best interest that require licence holders to use
contract forms prepared by the Texas Real Estate Broker-Lawyer Committee and adopted by the
commission*. The Broker-Lawyer Committee — brokers and lawyers together, as the name says — drafts;
the Commission adopts and **promulgates**; and licence holders use the promulgated forms. The same
section builds in the safety valves: the Commission *may not prohibit* a licence holder from using
for a transaction a form **prepared by the property owner** or **prepared by an attorney and
required by the owner**, and government-required and certain institutionally prepared forms have
their own accommodations. The architecture in one sentence: *standard transactions run on
committee-drafted, Commission-promulgated forms; custom drafting belongs to owners and their
lawyers; and the licence holder fills blanks rather than writes instruments.*

**The working library.** The promulgated family covers the recurring residential transactions —
resale contracts (the One to Four Family Residential Contract (Resale) is the workhorse), new
construction, condominiums, unimproved property, farm and ranch — together with promulgated
addenda for the recurring contingencies: financing, sale of other property, HOA matters, and their
companions. Two features of practice under the forms:

- **Fill, don't draft.** The licence holder's authority extends to completing the forms — filling
  blanks with the parties' business terms, checking the boxes the parties chose, attaching the
  promulgated addenda that fit. Striking promulgated language wholesale, writing bespoke special
  provisions that create or modify legal rights, or assembling home-made contracts is the
  **unauthorized practice of law** — the line § 1101.155's structure exists to hold, and a
  discipline case waiting to happen. Factual special provisions ("Seller to leave the pool
  equipment") differ from legal drafting ("Seller warrants and indemnifies…"); when the needed
  language is legal, the answer is the parties' attorney.
- **The special-provisions discipline, restated as tests.** Before writing anything in a
  special-provisions blank, run three tests. *Source test*: is this the parties' factual business
  term (conveys, stays, by when) or a legal device (warrants, indemnifies, releases, is contingent
  on)? *Duplication test*: does a promulgated addendum already handle it — because if one exists,
  it is the required route, and a hand-written paraphrase of it is both UPL-adjacent and worse
  drafting? *Consequence test*: would the sentence create, modify or waive a legal right if a court
  read it literally? Two "factual" words fail it constantly: "as-is" language and anything
  beginning "notwithstanding." A sentence failing any test goes to the parties' attorney, and the
  file notes that it did.

- **Advise the reading, not the law.** Agents explain what the form's provisions do procedurally —
  deadlines, options, notices — and urge parties to read and, where warranted, take legal advice;
  interpreting contested meaning is counsel's work.

**The § 1101.155 exceptions, walked slowly.** The two protected categories in the read text repay
precision, because each answers a recurring desk question. *A form prepared by the property
owner*: the Commission may not prohibit its use for a transaction — so when a builder-seller hands
its own purchase agreement across the table, the licence holder may proceed on the owner's paper;
what changes is posture, not permission: the promulgated protections are absent, and the buyer
should hear, in writing, that the owner's form is the owner's and an attorney can review it. *A
form prepared by an attorney and required by the owner*: same structure — the developer whose
lawyer drafted the subdivision's contract may require it, and the agent may transact on it. What
neither exception covers is the *agent's* home-made form: the protected drafters are owners and
their counsel, never the licence holder, which is the statute's architecture repeating its one
rule. And when a government agency's or lender's required documents enter the file, they ride
under their own accommodations — the licence holder's job is recognising whose paper is whose.

**Matching the addendum to the fact.** Fluency with the promulgated library is a matching skill,
learned by drills like these: the buyer must sell her current house before she can buy — the
sale-of-other-property addendum, not a special-provisions sentence; the house sits in a mandatory
owners' association — the HOA addendum, which carries the resale-certificate machinery; the buyer
will assume or take subject to existing financing, or the seller will carry part of the price —
the financing addenda built for those structures; the parties want the buyer's exit if financing
fails — the financing addendum's contingency terms, completed, not paraphrased. The negative
drill matters as much: a fact with no matching addendum and legal consequences — an indemnity, an
unusual holdback, a bespoke contingency — is the attorney trigger, every time. The promulgated
library is large precisely so that the special-provisions blank can stay small.

**Why Texas built it this way.** The promulgated system standardises the everyday transaction (both
sides' agents work from the same text), embeds consumer protections chosen deliberately by a
public body, and keeps non-lawyers out of drafting while still letting transactions move at market
speed. Its cost is rigidity at the edges — which is exactly where the statute sends the parties to
their own lawyers. A licence holder fluent in this unit knows, for any given piece of paper: where
it came from, what may be written on it, and whose job the rest is.

### Applied exercise: triage at the special-provisions blank

*A buyer's agent is asked by her clients to add three things to a promulgated resale contract:
(a) "Refrigerator, washer and dryer convey."; (b) "Seller shall indemnify Buyer against any future
foundation movement."; (c) "This contract is contingent on Buyer's father approving the house by
Friday."* **Task:** for each, decide whether the agent may write it, and if not, what the correct
route is. **Model answer:** (a) yes — a factual statement of items conveying, squarely within
filling the form's non-realty/items machinery. (b) no — an indemnity is a drafted legal obligation;
writing it is unauthorized practice of law; the buyers need an attorney, or the protection should be
pursued through the form's inspection/option machinery instead. (c) structurally this is a
condition; the honest answers are to use the option period the form already provides (buy days and
a unilateral termination right) rather than draft a bespoke approval contingency — and if the
clients insist on the custom condition, it is attorney-drafting, not agent-drafting.

### Applied exercise: whose paper is this?

*In one week, a Texas sales agent meets four documents: (a) a promulgated One to Four Family
resale contract for an ordinary listing; (b) a production builder's own purchase agreement, which
the builder requires on all its sales; (c) a contract drafted by the seller's attorney that the
seller insists on using for a ranch sale; (d) a "standard net-sale agreement" the agent's team
leader wrote himself and asks the team to use "to save time."* **Task:** for each, may the agent
transact on it, on what authority, and with what accompanying advice? **Model answer:** (a) yes —
the promulgated form is the required instrument for the standard transaction, completed by
filling, not drafting. (b) yes — a form prepared by the property owner is expressly protected
from Commission prohibition under § 1101.155; the buyer should be advised in writing that the
builder's form lacks the promulgated protections and that attorney review is available. (c) yes —
a form prepared by an attorney and required by the owner is the second protected category; same
advisory posture. (d) no — a licence holder's home-made contract sits in neither protected
category, and using it is the unauthorized practice of law the promulgated system exists to
prevent; the correct responses are the promulgated form or the parties' attorneys, and declining
the team leader's paper is not optional.

**One closing thought for the topic.** Contract law rewards the boring virtues: complete
signatures, communicated acceptances, calendared deadlines, factual special provisions, preserved
records. Every doctrine in this module — offer and acceptance, the statute of frauds, conditions,
remedies, the promulgated-forms architecture — converges on the same professional profile: the
licence holder as a precise operator of other people's agreements, never the author of them.

### Module recap

Contracts classify as express/implied, bilateral/unilateral, executory/executed, and
valid/void/voidable/unenforceable; enforceability requires competent parties, mutual assent,
consideration, legal purpose, real consent — and for this industry, writing: B&C Code § 26.01 makes
unwritten real estate sales contracts and leases beyond one year unenforceable against the unsigned,
with Property Code § 5.021 requiring the conveyance side in writing. Breach draws damages, specific
performance (four years to sue, CPRC § 16.004), rescission or liquidated damages — in practice, the
earnest-money election — while conditions end contracts without fault. The sales contract organises
parties, property, price, earnest money, title, survey, disclosure, inspections, closing and
remedies into one calendar of covenants and conditions. And Texas's signature institution: the
Broker-Lawyer Committee drafts, the Commission promulgates (OC § 1101.155), owners' and attorneys'
forms stay protected, and the licence holder fills blanks, attaches promulgated addenda, keeps
special provisions factual — and hands everything else to a lawyer.
