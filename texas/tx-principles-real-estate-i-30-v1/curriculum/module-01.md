# Texas Principles of Real Estate I — Module 01

## TREC Topic: Introduction to Modern Real Estate Practice

**Prescribed time: 100 minutes.** This module carries the four units the Commission's PRINS1-1 course
approval form places under *Introduction to Modern Real Estate Practice*, whose scope note is
*"Licensing as a broker and salesperson."*

**Learning objectives.** After completing this module you will be able to: identify the fields of work
that make up the Texas real estate business and the licence, if any, each requires; explain what the
Texas Real Estate Commission is, who sits on it, and where its authority to regulate education comes
from; describe the economic characteristics that make real estate markets local, slow-moving and
imperfect; state which activities require a Texas licence, in the statute's own terms; and name the
persons and transactions The Real Estate License Act does not reach.

**Every legal statement in this module comes from a section of the Texas Occupations Code that was
read for this course.** Where a statement is practice rather than law — how brokerages are commonly
organised, what a typical day looks like — the module says so, and no section is cited for it.

## Unit: Real Estate - A Business of Many Opportunities

The phrase "real estate business" suggests one occupation: the agent who lists and sells houses. The
business is in fact a cluster of occupations organised around a single asset class, and a student
entering it should be able to see the whole cluster before choosing a place in it — partly because the
choice matters, and partly because the licence you are studying for reaches some of these occupations
and not others.

**Brokerage** is the core licensed activity: bringing parties to a real property transaction together
for compensation. Texas defines it not by the job title but by a list of acts. Occupations Code
§ 1101.002(1) defines a **broker** as a person who, *for a commission or other valuable consideration
or with the expectation of receiving one*, performs *for another person* any of twelve acts — among
them selling, exchanging, purchasing or leasing real estate; offering to do any of those; negotiating
or attempting to negotiate a listing, sale, exchange, purchase or lease; listing real estate for sale;
auctioning it; dealing in options on it; aiding in locating or obtaining real estate for purchase or
lease; procuring a prospect or procuring property to effect a sale, exchange or lease; and controlling
the acceptance or deposit of rent from a resident of a single-family residential unit.

Three features of that definition organise everything else in this course.

**First, the definition runs on compensation.** Every act on the list becomes brokerage only when done
for consideration or the expectation of it. A neighbour who helps you find a house as a favour is not
acting as a broker; the same help for a fee is squarely inside § 1101.002(1).

**Second, the definition runs on acting *for another person*.** An owner selling the owner's own
property is not performing an act "for another," which is why owners can sell their own real estate
without a licence and why several of the statutory exemptions you will meet later in this module are
structural rather than special favours.

**Third, the list is longer than the popular image of the job.** Notice item (x): *controlling the
acceptance or deposit of rent from a resident of a single-family residential real property unit*.
Residential property management — collecting rent on a house for an owner, for a fee — is brokerage in
Texas. Notice also that § 1101.002(1)(B) sweeps in a person *employed by or for an owner to sell any
portion of the owner's real estate*, and a person in the business of charging an **advance fee** to
promote real estate through listing publications or referrals. The Legislature wrote the definition to
follow the money, not the business card.

Around that licensed core, the business offers occupations the licence does not govern, each touching
the transaction at a different point:

- **Lending and mortgage origination** — financing the purchase. Loan origination is regulated under
  finance law rather than under The Real Estate License Act, and it is a separate career path with its
  own licensing.
- **Title insurance and escrow** — searching, examining and insuring title, and closing the
  transaction. In Texas this industry is regulated under the Insurance Code; Module 09 introduces it.
- **Appraisal** — developing opinions of value under a separate licensing scheme. Note the careful
  carve-out in the broker definition itself: § 1101.002(1)(A)(xi) treats a *written analysis, opinion
  or conclusion relating to the estimated price of real property* as a broker act only when it is
  **not referred to as an appraisal**, is given in the ordinary course of business, and relates to the
  management, acquisition, disposition or encumbrance of real property. A licence holder may prepare a
  price opinion; calling it an appraisal is a different, separately licensed act.
- **Inspection** — examining improvements for condition. Texas inspectors are licensed under a
  companion chapter administered by the same Commission (Chapter 1102, named in § 1101.151).
- **Construction, development and land use** — creating the inventory the market trades.
- **Investment and asset management** — holding real estate for income and appreciation, the subject
  of two units in Module 05.

A sales agent's practice touches every one of these. The agent does not originate the loan, close the
sale, appraise the property or inspect the roof — but the agent coordinates with the people who do,
and the client experiences the agent as the guide to the whole process. That is why a principles
course spends its hours on property, ownership, title, finance and closing rather than only on selling.

**Where the licence itself fits.** Occupations Code § 1101.002(7) defines a **sales agent** as *a
person who is sponsored by a licensed broker for the purpose of performing an act described by
Subdivision (1)* — that is, the same acts, performable only through a broker. Section 1101.351
completes the structure: a person may not act as, or represent being, a broker or sales agent without
a licence; a business entity may not act as a broker without its own licence; and § 1101.351(c) states
the sponsorship rule in one sentence — *a licensed sales agent may not engage or attempt to engage in
real estate brokerage unless the sales agent is sponsored by a licensed broker and is acting for that
broker*. The two-tier structure — responsible broker, sponsored agent — is the organising fact of
Texas brokerage practice, and you will meet it again wherever supervision, advertising and
compensation appear in this program.

### Statute reading exercise: the twelve acts

Open the quoted definition of "broker" in Occupations Code § 1101.002(1) as set out above and answer
in your notes: **(1)** Which listed act makes residential property management licensed activity?
**(2)** A person gives a written opinion of a home's likely sale price, calls it a "market analysis,"
and is paid for it as part of taking a listing. Which subparagraph makes this a broker act rather than
an appraisal, and what three conditions does it attach? **(3)** Why does an owner selling the owner's
own home fall outside the definition without needing any exemption? *(Answers: the rent-acceptance
item, § 1101.002(1)(A)(x); the price-opinion item, § 1101.002(1)(A)(xi), with the conditions that it
not be called an appraisal, be given in the ordinary course of business, and relate to actual or
potential management, acquisition, disposition or encumbrance; because the definition requires acting
"for another person," and an owner acts for the owner.)*

## Unit: TREC Education Standards Advisory Committee

The course you are taking exists in the form it does because Texas law places real estate education
under the Commission's control and gives the Commission a mechanism for keeping the standards current.

**The Texas Real Estate Commission.** Occupations Code § 1101.051 establishes a nine-member
commission appointed by the governor with the advice and consent of the senate: six members who have
been engaged in the brokerage business as licensed brokers as their major occupation for the five
years preceding appointment, and three members who represent the public. The mixture is deliberate —
industry knowledge on one side, public accountability on the other. Under § 1101.151 the Commission
administers this chapter and Chapter 1102 (the inspector chapter), may adopt and enforce rules
necessary to administer them, and may establish standards of conduct and ethics for licence holders.

**Where course standards come from.** Section 1101.301 authorises the Commission, by rule, to
establish standards for the approval of qualifying educational programs and courses of study in real
estate conducted in this state — excluding programs offered by public high schools and accredited
colleges and universities — and to develop minimum education and experience requirements for
instructors. Section 1101.003 names the qualifying course subjects, *principles of real estate* among
them, and lets the Commission prescribe their content by rule. The course approval form this module is
built on — PRINS1-1, with its topics, units and suggested minutes — is that prescription at work.

**The advisory committee mechanism.** Section 1101.158 permits the Commission to appoint advisory
committees to perform the advisory functions the Commission assigns; members serve at the will of the
Commission and without compensation, and committee meetings are subject to the open-meetings law. The
Commission maintains an **Education Standards Advisory Committee** under this authority — a standing
committee through which education standards, course content requirements and course approval forms are
reviewed and recommendations are made to the Commission. For a student, the practical meaning is
this: the required topics you are studying are not a school's choices. They are the Commission's,
arrived at through a public advisory process, and a school's course is approved against them.

**Two statutory course-integrity rules worth knowing now**, because they govern your own study:
under § 1101.003(d), *a daily course segment for a qualifying course may not exceed 12 hours* — no
provider may credit you with more than twelve hours of qualifying coursework in a day; and under
§ 1101.003(e), the completion of a correspondence or alternative-delivery qualifying course may not
be reported to the Commission until the elapsed time from your registration exceeds **twice** the
number of credit hours claimed — for this 30-hour course, sixty hours must elapse between
registration and reported completion. Both rules exist to keep a qualifying course from being
consumed faster than anyone could learn from it.

## Unit: The Real Estate Market - Characteristics of Real Estate

Real estate markets behave unlike markets for shares, cars or groceries, and the differences follow
from physical and economic characteristics of the asset itself. This unit is economics rather than
law: no statute is cited because none governs it. What follows is the standard analytical vocabulary,
applied to Texas conditions.

**Physical characteristics.**

- **Immobility.** Land cannot be moved. Every parcel's value is therefore captured by its location,
  and markets are irreducibly local: a surplus of housing in one metropolitan area cannot ship to
  relieve a shortage in another. Immobility is also why real estate is governed and taxed where it
  sits, and why a licence to broker it is issued state by state.
- **Indestructibility.** Land endures. Improvements wear out; the land under them does not. This
  permanence is what makes land a durable store of value and makes long instruments — thirty-year
  loans, ninety-nine-year leases — sensible.
- **Uniqueness (heterogeneity).** No two parcels are identical, because no two occupy the same
  location. The law takes this seriously — you will see in Module 06 that Texas allows four years to
  sue for **specific performance** of a contract to convey real property, a remedy premised on the
  idea that money cannot fully substitute for a particular parcel — and valuation takes it seriously
  too, which is why appraisal compares and adjusts rather than looking up a price.

**Economic characteristics.**

- **Scarcity.** Usable land in a given location is finite even where land in general is abundant —
  a truth vivid in Texas, where vast rural acreage coexists with intense competition for land in the
  growth corridors of the major metropolitan areas.
- **Improvements and modification.** Investment in a parcel changes its character and value, and the
  character of surrounding parcels; a subdivision, a highway or a reservoir re-prices its neighbours.
- **Permanence of investment.** Capital placed in land and improvements is long-lived and largely
  irreversible — the return arrives over decades, which ties real estate to credit markets and makes
  interest rates a dominant force in demand.
- **Location preference (situs).** People's preference for one location over another — commute,
  schools, flood risk, prestige — is the economic engine of value differences between physically
  similar properties.

**What these characteristics do to the market.** Because the asset is immobile and unique, buyers and
sellers cannot meet in a central exchange; they find each other through search, which is costly and
slow. Because transactions are infrequent, prices are discovered deal by deal rather than posted
continuously. Because supply responds slowly — construction takes years, and land in a location
cannot be increased at all — demand shifts move prices sharply before supply can answer. Economists
summarise all this by calling real estate markets **imperfect**: information is incomplete, products
are non-standard, entry is slow, and local conditions dominate. The brokerage business exists
precisely because the market is imperfect — the licence holder's stock in trade is local information,
search and negotiation in a market that lacks a ticker.

## Unit: Real Estate Law

The remaining forty minutes of this topic introduce the legal frame that the rest of the course fills
in: what The Real Estate License Act is, whom it licenses, whom it exempts, and where it sits among
the other bodies of law a licence holder works inside.

**The Act.** Occupations Code § 1101.001 gives Chapter 1101 its citation name: *The Real Estate
License Act*. The chapter defines the licensed occupations (§ 1101.002), creates the Commission
(§ 1101.051) and arms it with rulemaking power (§ 1101.151), controls entry through education and
licensing (§§ 1101.003, 1101.301, 1101.351), and — in subchapters this course meets later in the
program — regulates conduct and provides sanctions.

**The licence requirement, in the statute's words.** Under § 1101.351(a), unless a person holds a
licence issued under this chapter, the person *may not act as or represent that the person is a broker
or sales agent*. Representation alone is enough: holding yourself out — a sign, a website, a business
card — is prohibited conduct for the unlicensed, whether or not a transaction ever happens. Under
§ 1101.351(a-1) a business entity may not act as a broker without an entity licence, and under
§ 1101.351(b) even a successful applicant may not act until the licence has actually issued. "Real
estate" for all these purposes is defined broadly by § 1101.002(5): *any interest in real property,
including a leasehold*, located in or outside this state — though not an interest held merely as
security for an obligation.

**Whom the Act does not reach.** Section 1101.005 lists the exemptions, and they reward careful
reading because each has edges:

- **An attorney licensed in Texas** — exempt as such; but an attorney-in-fact under a power of
  attorney is exempt only for **not more than three** real estate transactions annually, a numbered
  limit that stops the power-of-attorney device from becoming an unlicensed brokerage.
- **A public official** engaged in official duties.
- **A licensed auctioneer** calling the auction of real estate — provided the auctioneer performs no
  other act of a broker; the exemption covers the gavel, not the marketing campaign.
- **A person acting under a court order or under the authority of a will or written trust
  instrument** — executors, administrators, guardians, trustees.
- **A builder's own sales employees**, for structures the owner erected in the course of the owner's
  business — the new-home sales office.
- **An on-site manager of an apartment complex** — on-site being the operative word; § 1101.002(1)
  still makes off-site single-family rent collection for others a broker act.
- **An owner, or the owner's employee, leasing the owner's own improved or unimproved real estate.**
- **Certain transaction types**: interests in minerals, mining and standing timber; cemetery lots;
  hotel and motel leasing; and — significant for Module 08 — *the sale of real property under a power
  of sale conferred by a deed of trust or other contract lien*: the foreclosure trustee conducting the
  sale is not thereby acting as a broker.
- **Internal transactions of limited partnerships and limited liability companies**, conducted by
  their general partners, managers or negotiating employees.

The common thread is the one you met in the broker definition: the Act licenses those who broker
*for others, for compensation*, and leaves alone those who deal with their own property, act under
another licence or a court's supervision, or operate inside listed special markets.

**The wider legal frame.** The Act is one layer. A Texas transaction also moves through the
**Property Code** (conveyances, recording, liens, homestead — Modules 03, 07 and 08), the **Business
& Commerce Code** (the statute of frauds — Module 06), the **Tax Code** (property taxation and tax
liens — Module 03), the **Family Code** (community property — Module 05), the **Estates Code** (wills
and descent — Module 07), the **Insurance Code** (title insurance — Modules 07 and 09), and federal
law (settlement procedures and foreign-seller withholding — Modules 08 and 09). Principles I is the
map of that terrain; the later courses in the six-course program walk each region in depth.

### Statute reading exercise: exemptions with edges

Re-read the § 1101.005 summary above. For each of the following, decide whether a licence is
required, and identify the exemption or definitional reason: **(1)** A ranch owner's full-time
employee negotiates leases of the owner's grazing land. **(2)** A friend holding a power of attorney
sells five houses this year for various acquaintances. **(3)** A trustee named in a recorded deed of
trust conducts the foreclosure auction of the secured property. **(4)** An off-site property manager
collects rent on twelve single-family homes for their various owners, for a monthly fee. *(Answers:
(1) exempt — owner's employee leasing the owner's real estate; (2) licence required — the
attorney-in-fact exemption stops at three transactions annually; (3) exempt — sale under a power of
sale conferred by a deed of trust; (4) licence required — § 1101.002(1)(A)(x) makes controlling the
acceptance or deposit of rent from single-family residents a broker act, and no § 1101.005 exemption
reaches an off-site manager acting for others.)*

### Applied exercise: which door into the business?

*A newly licensed sales agent is weighing three offers: (a) a residential brokerage team promising
listings support; (b) a property management firm managing 300 single-family rentals; (c) a builder's
on-site new-home sales office, which tells her she "won't even need her licence there."* **Task:**
for each offer, state which licensing rule or exemption governs the work, and identify what the agent
must verify before starting at each. **Model answer:** (a) ordinary brokerage under § 1101.002(1);
she may act only through a sponsoring broker per § 1101.351(c), so the team's broker must become her
sponsor. (b) single-family rent collection for others is a broker act under § 1101.002(1)(A)(x); the
firm must be operating under a broker licence and she must be sponsored by that broker — her licence
is fully engaged, not optional. (c) the builder's own sales employees are exempt under § 1101.005 for
the builder's structures; she could work there unlicensed as the builder's employee, but while doing
so she is outside brokerage — and if she wants to keep her licence active through a sponsor, or sell
anything other than the builder's own inventory, the exemption does not stretch to cover it.

### Module recap

The Texas real estate business is a cluster of occupations around one asset; the licence reaches the
brokerage core, defined by twelve compensated acts done for others (§ 1101.002(1)). Sales agents act
only through sponsoring brokers (§§ 1101.002(7), 1101.351(c)); entities need their own licences; and
representation without a licence is itself unlawful (§ 1101.351). The nine-member Commission
(§ 1101.051) administers the Act with rulemaking power (§ 1101.151), prescribes qualifying education
(§§ 1101.003, 1101.301) with advisory committees under § 1101.158, and enforces two study-integrity
rules — the 12-hour daily cap and the elapsed-time rule — that govern this very course
(§ 1101.003(d), (e)). Real estate markets are local, slow and imperfect because the asset is immobile,
indestructible and unique; and the Act's exemptions (§ 1101.005) trace the same boundary as its
definitions — own property, other licences, listed special markets — leaving compensated dealing for
others as the licensed heartland.
