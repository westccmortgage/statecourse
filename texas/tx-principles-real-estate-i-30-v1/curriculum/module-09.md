# Texas Principles of Real Estate I — Module 09

## TREC Topic: Closing Procedures/Closing the Real Estate Transaction

**Prescribed time: 200 minutes.** Four units under the scope note *"Closing Procedures"*: preparing
for closing, conducting it, the federal settlement-statement regime, and the preparation of closing
statements — the module where every earlier module's machinery converges on one table and one
ledger.

**Learning objectives.** Manage the pre-closing critical path — title cure, survey, inspections,
financing, insurance and the walk-through — as a calendar of contract deadlines; describe how a
Texas closing is conducted and by whom, and what happens at funding and recording; place RESPA and
its regulation in the transaction and state what they require and prohibit; and prepare the
arithmetic of a closing statement: debits, credits and the proration methods, computed to the penny.

## Unit: Pre-Closing Procedures/Closing the Transaction

**Closing is a deadline system.** From effective date to closing date, the contract (Module 06)
schedules everything; the licence holder's professional contribution is running that calendar so
that no right lapses unnoticed. The standing critical path:

- **Earnest money and option fees delivered** on the contract's clock, receipted by the escrow
  agent.
- **Title work.** The commitment issues; buyer and lender review; objections go out within the
  contract's window; cures are negotiated. Everything Module 03 taught about liens and Module 07
  taught about chains of title happens *here*, as tasks with dates: releases ordered, payoffs
  quoted, heirship documented, that pool contractor's lien resolved.
- **Survey** obtained or updated (Module 04's five-point pass), feeding both title objections and
  the lender's requirements.
- **Inspections and repairs** inside the option period; repair amendments negotiated in writing.
- **Financing.** Application, appraisal, underwriting, and the approach of the financing
  contingency's deadline — with the agent tracking conditions ("clear to close") rather than
  assuming them.
- **Seller's disclosure** delivered (Property Code § 5.008 — Module 07); **insurance** bound
  (the lender will not fund without it); utilities, HOA documents and resale certificates
  ordered where applicable.
- **The walk-through**: the buyer's final verification that the property's condition and the
  agreed repairs match the contract — the last, cheap moment to catch what would be expensive
  the day after funding.

**"Clear to close," tracked rather than assumed.** Financing is the critical path's longest pole,
and its milestones deserve the same calendar discipline as the contract's: application submitted
(with the § 1024.6 booklet obligations of this module's federal unit starting the clock on the
lender's side); appraisal ordered and received — with the contract's own machinery, not
improvisation, governing what happens if value comes in low; conditional approval, with the
conditions *listed and owned* (whose document, due when); and final underwriting clearance —
"clear to close" — before which no closing date is real, however confidently scheduled. The
licence holder's technique is unglamorous: a standing weekly status call pattern among agent,
lender and escrow officer, and a refusal to let "we're fine" substitute for a conditions list
with names on it.

**The association file, ordered early.** Where a property sits in a mandatory owners'
association, the contract's HOA machinery adds its own pre-closing thread: the association's
documents and account statement are ordered (associations bill for them, and turnaround times
are real), the buyer's review rights under the contract's terms run on their own clock, and
the association's transfer and statement fees take their place on the settlement statement as
the contract allocates them. The dues proration of the statements unit and Module 03's
assessment-lien discussion both land in this thread — one more place where the file's earliest
phone call prevents its latest crisis.

**Funds at closing, and the fraud that hunts them.** The escrow officer will require the buyer's
cash to close in **collected funds** — a wire or the equivalent the instructions specify, not a
personal cheque that clears next week — and the amount comes from the settlement statement, not
from memory. Around that wire has grown the industry's most damaging crime: intercepted and
spoofed emails delivering altered wiring instructions days before closing. The professional
protocols are simple and non-negotiable, and a licence holder repeats them to every buyer:
wiring instructions come from the title company through a verified channel; any change to
wiring instructions is presumed fraudulent; and every wire is confirmed by a phone call to a
number *independently known*, never one taken from the email that asked for the money. Stating
these protocols is not legal advice; it is the standard of care in a market where a
misdirected down payment is usually unrecoverable.

### Applied exercise: the three-week countdown

*A contract goes effective June 2, closing June 30. Option period: 7 days, fee paid. Financing
contingency deadline: June 23. The title commitment arrives June 9 showing an unreleased 1998
deed of trust and the current year's taxes; the survey arrives June 12 showing the neighbour's
fence 18 inches inside the line; the lender's June 16 conditional approval lists an insurance
binder and a repair receipt as open conditions.* **Task:** build the working calendar — what
must happen, by whom, by when — and flag the two items that most threaten June 30. **Model
answer:** June 2–9: earnest money and option fee delivered on the contract's clock; inspections
booked immediately (option days are calendar days spent, not saved); repair negotiation
concluded in writing inside the option period. June 9 forward: title objections to the 1998
deed of trust within the contract's objection window — the release is the title company's cure
to order now, not at month's end (Module 07's ladder); the tax item is standard (prorate, not
cure). June 12: the fence is Module 03's encroachment doctrine on paper — disclosed, discussed,
and either objected to on the contract's terms or accepted in writing; nobody at the table
declares who owns the strip. June 16–23: the buyer's insurance binder ordered and the repair
receipt delivered to underwriting well before the financing deadline; if clearance is in doubt
approaching June 23, the parties face a written extension or the contingency's exit — a
decision made on the deadline's eve, in writing, never assumed. The two June-30 threats: the
old deed of trust (a third party's paperwork on nobody's deadline) and the open lender
conditions (the only items the parties fully control — which is why they get chased daily).

**Who closes in Texas.** Texas closings are conducted overwhelmingly by **title companies**
through their **escrow officers** — the Insurance Code role you met in Module 07: § 2501.003
defines the escrow officer by function — countersigning title forms, supervising the preparation
and delivery of forms, signing escrow checks, and *closing the transaction*. The escrow officer
is a neutral stakeholder executing the contract's instructions and the lender's, not either
party's advocate and not a source of legal advice. Attorneys close some transactions
(particularly commercial), and lenders' instructions govern the funding side everywhere. The
licence holder attends, explains procedure, and — a boundary now familiar — answers no legal
questions the parties should put to counsel.

**Escrow logic.** A Texas closing is an escrow: signed documents and funds accumulate with the
neutral until every condition of the contract and the lender's instructions is satisfied; only
then does the escrow **break** — funds disburse, documents record. If conditions cannot be met,
the escrow unwinds by its terms. This structure is why "we signed" is not "we closed": between
signature and **funding**, nothing has happened yet.

## Unit: Conducting the Closing

**The event itself.** In practice Texas "closing" is often two signing appointments — seller's
package (deed, affidavits, payoff authorisations) and buyer's package (note, deed of trust,
lender disclosures, settlement statement) — coordinated by the escrow officer. The sequence that
turns signatures into a conveyance:

1. **Documents executed and acknowledged** — the notarial acts that qualify instruments for
   recording (Property Code § 12.001; Module 07).
2. **Funding** — the lender wires; the buyer delivers the cash to close (in collected funds, as
   instructed); the escrow officer confirms receipt against the settlement statement.
3. **Disbursement** — payoffs to existing lienholders (Module 08's payoff quotes, good through a
   date, with per-diem interest), taxing units if delinquencies exist, commissions,
   service providers, seller's proceeds.
4. **Recording** — the deed and deed of trust go to the county clerk. Module 07's law makes the
   order of operations obvious: record *immediately*, because § 13.001 protects the world against
   the unrecorded, and the gap between funding and recording is the danger zone the industry
   compresses to hours.
5. **Policies issue** — the owner's and loan title policies (Module 07), converting the
   commitment's promises into insurance.

**Variations on the appointment.** The two-package pattern flexes constantly, and the agent
should be able to narrate each variation without drama. *Mail-out and remote sellers*: a seller
who cannot attend signs a mailed or electronically delivered package before a notary where the
seller is, returned to escrow ahead of funding — the documents, not the people, must be at the
closing. *Powers of attorney*: a party may sign through an attorney-in-fact under a power of
attorney the title company approved **in advance** — surprise powers presented at the table
stop closings, because the instrument's sufficiency and recording needs are title questions,
not courtesies. *Split and sequential closings*: the seller may sign Monday, the buyer
Wednesday, with funding when every condition meets — "closing day" is really "funding day"
wearing a calendar name. *The double contract warning*: what never flexes is the ledger's
honesty — every term of the parties' real agreement appears on the statement the lender sees,
and any proposal to paper one price for the lender and another between the parties is fraud
territory the licence holder refuses in the room, out loud.

**Neutrality under pressure.** The escrow officer's neutrality is easy to state and regularly
tested, and the licence holder should know the standard collisions. *A party demands the earnest
money*: the stakeholder disburses on the parties' signed agreement or prescribed process, never
on one side's insistence, however righteous (Module 06's collapsed-deal triage arriving at the
closing desk). *A party asks the closer "should I sign this?"*: the neutral explains what a
document is and does procedurally and stops at the legal-advice line — the same line the agent
walks. *Last-minute changes appear on the statement*: the closer implements only what the
contract, amendments and lender instructions authorise, which is why changes are papered as
amendments rather than negotiated orally at the table. Understanding the neutral's constraints
keeps the agent from asking for what cannot be given — and positions the agent as the person
who gets the *paper* right so the neutral can act.

**After funding: the file's afterlife.** Closing ends with a package, and clients call about it
for years, so know what lands where: the buyer receives the recorded deed back from the county
(the original returns to the grantee after recording), the owner's title policy issued after
recording, and the settlement statement — the document their tax preparer will ask for. The
lender holds the note; the deed of trust returns recorded to the lender's file; the seller
keeps the statement and the payoff's release evidence (Module 07: an unreleased lien is a
future title requirement with the seller's name on it). The brokerage retains its transaction
file under the records practices the later courses detail. The agent's last professional act
is a closing letter telling the parties exactly this — what they will receive, from whom,
roughly when — because the cheapest client service in the industry is preventing the
"where's my deed?" panic call six weeks out.

**The closing documents, mapped to their modules.** The deed (07); the note and deed of trust
(08); the FIRPTA affidavit (08); the settlement statement (this module's last two units); the
seller's affidavits (debts, liens, parties in possession — the title company's defence-in-depth
against Module 03's off-record claims); tax certificates and prorations (03 and below). A licence
holder who can say what each paper *does* — in one sentence, without giving legal advice — is
worth the commission at the table.

**The neutral's licence and plant, from the statute.** Module 07 introduced § 2501.003's defined
roles; the closing table is where they work. The **escrow officer's** defined functions —
countersigning title forms, supervising preparation and delivery of forms, signing escrow
cheques, closing the transaction — describe exactly what the parties watch happen at signing;
and behind the closer stands § 2501.004's **abstract plant**, the geographically indexed record
set (complete from not later than January 1, 1979, kept current) from which the commitment's
promises were built. The two sections together explain the Texas industry's shape: the same
regulated company examines title, insures it, holds the escrow and conducts the closing — one
accountable neutral, with promulgated premiums (§ 2501.003(8)) removing price from the
competition among them.

### Applied exercise: the five-minute closing orientation

*Your first-time buyers ask you to explain, before their appointment, "what actually happens at
closing."* **Task:** deliver the orientation in plain language — who they will meet, what they
will sign, when the house becomes theirs — without giving legal advice. **Model answer:** "We
will meet the escrow officer — a neutral closer from the title company, not anyone's advocate.
You'll sign the lender's package: the note, which is your promise to repay, and the deed of
trust, which lets the lender foreclose if the loan isn't paid — and the settlement statement,
which itemises every dollar; we will have reviewed it together the day before, line by line.
The seller signs the deed. Signing is not owning: the escrow officer holds everything until the
lender funds and your cash to close arrives by verified wire; only then does money move, and
the deed and deed of trust go straight to the county clerk for recording. You get keys when
the contract says — at funding, unless we agreed otherwise. If any document raises a legal
question, the escrow officer and I will both tell you the same thing: that one is for your
attorney, and we can pause to ask." Every sentence is procedure; not one is legal advice —
which is the demonstration the exercise exists to make.

**Possession and keys** pass per the contract: at funding, or under a temporary lease if
occupancy and funding diverge. The agent's last procedural duty is confirming the contract's
possession terms were honoured; the file then closes into retention (the brokerage's records
obligations live in the licensing law's practice rules treated later in this program).

## Unit: RESPA/Uniform Settlement Statement

**The federal frame.** The Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act governs settlements involving
**federally related mortgage loans** — which, in practice, means most institutionally financed
one-to-four family residential transactions. Its declared purpose, from the text read for this
course (12 U.S.C. § 2601): reforms so that consumers are *"provided with greater and more timely
information on the nature and costs of the settlement process"* and are *"protected from
unnecessarily high settlement charges caused by certain abusive practices."* Two operative pillars
carry that purpose.

**Reading § 2601 as history.** The findings' vocabulary — "abusive practices," charges that
"unnecessarily inflate the costs" — records the pre-1974 settlement market Congress examined:
referral fees moving invisibly between settlement providers, borrowers meeting their costs for
the first time at the table with no ability to compare or refuse. Each pillar answers one
finding: itemised uniform disclosure answers the information failure; § 2607's prohibitions
answer the kickback economy. Knowing the history keeps the compliance from feeling arbitrary —
every disclosure this module walks exists because its absence was once somebody's business
model, and the licence holder's referral relationships are regulated precisely because
referrals are where the old abuses lived.

**Coverage, read from the purpose.** Section 2601's findings name the market the Act polices —
consumers entering **federally related mortgage loans** — and the practical perimeter follows:
the machinery of this unit attaches to the financed one-to-four family residential transaction,
the same territory Texas's own consumer perimeter (Finance Code § 343.001, Module 08) and the
promulgated forms serve. Transactions outside it — the all-cash purchase, the true commercial
deal — close under the same Texas escrow customs but without RESPA's federal disclosures
attached. A licence holder does not adjudicate coverage; a licence holder notices the pattern:
where a consumer, a dwelling and an institutional loan meet, assume the federal regime is in
the room.

### Statute reading exercise: the disclosure pillar's exact words

From §§ 2601 and 2603 as set out above: **(1)** name the two consumer harms Congress's findings
target; **(2)** what must the integrated disclosure itemize, for whom, and what must it say
about title insurance; **(3)** a buyer asks why her closing paperwork looks the same as her
cousin's from another lender — what is the statutory answer? *(Answers: (1) lack of timely
information on the nature and costs of settlement, and unnecessarily high settlement charges
from abusive practices; (2) all charges imposed upon the borrower and all charges imposed upon
the seller in connection with the settlement, conspicuously and clearly — and the statement
must indicate whose interest each title insurance premium covers; (3) the statute directs a
single, standard integrated disclosure precisely so settlement costs are stated uniformly and
can be compared across lenders and transactions.)*

**Pillar one: disclosure — the uniform settlement statement.** 12 U.S.C. § 2603 directs the
federal consumer bureau to publish a **single, integrated disclosure** for mortgage loan
transactions — combining RESPA's settlement-cost statement with the Truth in Lending Act's
disclosures — that **conspicuously and clearly itemizes all charges imposed upon the borrower and
all charges imposed upon the seller** in connection with the settlement, and indicates whose
interest each title insurance premium covers. That statutory mandate is why every financed
closing produces an itemised, standardised accounting of the transaction's money, and why the
classic "uniform settlement statement" the PRINS1-1 unit is named for lives on today as the
integrated disclosure regime (with the traditional statement formats persisting in transactions
outside it). Regulation X — 12 C.F.R. part 1024, held for this course — implements RESPA;
§ 1024.6, in the text as held, adds the early-education piece: the lender delivers the
**special information booklet** to a person from whom it receives, or for whom it prepares, a
written application for a federally related mortgage loan. The through-line for the licence
holder: federal law guarantees the borrower an itemised, comparable account of settlement
costs — and the closing statement unit next door is that account's arithmetic.

**The booklet rule, with the regulation's own edges.** Section 1024.6's mechanics, from the text
as held, are a small study in how Regulation X operates. The lender delivers the special
information booklet, or mails it, **not later than three business days** after receiving or
preparing the written application — but if the application is *denied* within those three days,
the duty lapses; when **two or more persons apply together**, one booklet to one applicant
complies; when a **mortgage broker** is in the chain, the broker distributes the booklet and
the lender need not; and for open-ended home-equity credit plans, the designated home-equity
brochure substitutes. The regulation even states its own intent — *that the applicant receive
the special information booklet at the earliest possible date*. For the licence holder the
detail is orientation, not administration: the borrower-education duty is the lender's and
broker's, it begins at application (long before closing), and an agent who knows the timeline
can tell a confused buyer exactly which professional owes them which paper.

**Pillar two: prohibition — kickbacks and unearned fees.** 12 U.S.C. § 2607, read in full for
this course, is the section every licensed professional must respect in daily referral practice:
**no person shall give and no person shall accept any fee, kickback, or thing of value** pursuant
to any agreement or understanding that settlement-service business incident to a federally
related mortgage loan **shall be referred** to any person (§ 2607(a)); and no one may split any
charge for settlement services **other than for services actually performed** (§ 2607(b)). The
statute's own safe harbours (§ 2607(c)) draw the practice map: payment *for services actually
performed* and *goods actually furnished* is fine; attorneys' fees, title agents' commissions and
lenders' agents' compensation for actual work are fine; **cooperative brokerage and referral
arrangements between real estate agents and brokers** are expressly permitted — the industry's
agent-to-agent referral fee survives; and **affiliated business arrangements** are lawful only on
the conditions stated — disclosure of the affiliation, a written estimate of charges, no
*requirement* to use the affiliate, and no payment beyond a return on ownership interest.
Translate into the licence holder's day: a title company's "marketing fee" for steering closings
is a federal violation on both sides of the envelope; a lender's gift certificates for buyer
referrals, the same; the brokerage's ownership interest in an affiliated title agency is lawful
exactly as far as the affiliated-business conditions are honoured. When compensation appears
anywhere near a referral of settlement business, the two questions are *what service was actually
performed?* and *was the § 2607(c) condition met?* — and if the answers require thought, they
require counsel.

**The affiliated business, worked to its edges.** Because brokerage ownership of title and
mortgage affiliates is now common, walk one arrangement against § 2607(c)'s four conditions. *A
brokerage owns 40% of a title agency and wants its agents to send closings there.* Condition
one: the affiliation is **disclosed** to the client, with the prescribed written disclosure, at
or before referral. Condition two: the client receives a **written estimate** of the
affiliate's charges. Condition three: no one **requires** the client to use the affiliate —
"we close all our deals there" as policy crosses the line the moment a client cannot say no.
Condition four: nothing of value moves on the referrals themselves — the brokerage's lawful
compensation is **a return on its ownership interest** (its 40% share of the agency's profits),
never per-referral bonuses to the company or its agents. Run any proposed incentive through
that fourth condition and most "marketing agreements" answer themselves: value keyed to
referral volume is the thing the statute names, whatever the invoice says. And the mirror
duty: agents *receiving* things of value — fee-sharing from an inspector, a closing gift
program from a lender — face the same two questions this module keeps asking: what service was
actually performed, and which § 2607(c) shelter, exactly, is this standing on?

## Unit: Preparation of Closing Statements

**The ledger's logic.** A closing statement is double-entry bookkeeping for the transaction:
every dollar appears as a **debit** (a charge) to one party or a **credit** (a receipt) to a
party, and the columns must reconcile to the penny before the escrow officer may disburse. The
recurring geography:

- **Buyer debits**: purchase price; lender's charges; title premiums per the contract's
  allocation; recording fees for the buyer's instruments; escrow fee share; prepaid insurance;
  reserves.
- **Buyer credits**: earnest money already deposited; the new loan's proceeds; the seller-paid
  prorations below.
- **Seller debits**: existing loan payoff with per-diem interest; releases and their recording;
  commissions; seller's escrow fee share; accrued-but-unpaid items prorated to the buyer.
- **Seller credits**: the purchase price; items the seller prepaid beyond closing, prorated back.

**And a last vocabulary pair.** *Settlement* and *closing* are the same event wearing federal
and Texas names respectively — the statutes of this module say "settlement," the Texas table
says "closing," and the licence holder translates freely between them, as this course has,
without letting either word obscure the two-stage truth underneath: signing, then funding.

**Rounding, stated once.** Statements compute to the penny and state their rounding: per diems
are carried to at least four decimal places before the final multiplication (the 365-day worked
example's $17.7534 shows why — rounding the per diem first shifts the result by whole cents),
and each final line item rounds to the cent. Small rules; real disputes prevented.

**Debit and credit, de-mystified once.** Students trip on the words because banking reversed
them in everyday speech, so fix the statement's usage plainly: on a closing statement a
**debit is a charge against a party** — it increases what that party must bring or reduces
what that party takes away — and a **credit is money in a party's favour**. The buyer's loan
is a buyer *credit* (it arrives on the buyer's behalf); the price is a buyer *debit* and a
seller *credit* (charged to one, owed to the other); the seller's payoff is a seller *debit*
(paid out of the seller's money at the table). Test any entry with one question — *whose
money, which direction?* — and the labels assign themselves.

**Net sheets: the statement's forecast.** The closing statement has a forward-looking cousin the
licence holder personally prepares: the **estimated net sheet**. For a seller at listing — price
scenarios minus payoff, commission, title and escrow charges per local custom, prorations
estimated — it answers "what will I walk away with?"; for a buyer writing an offer — cash to
close built from price, loan, deposits and estimated charges — it answers "what must I bring?"
Three professional rules govern every net sheet: *label it an estimate*, on its face, with its
assumptions stated (payoff unverified, taxes estimated at last year's figure); *source every
number you can* (order the payoff figure early — Module 08 taught why the recorded lien tells
you nothing about the balance); and *estimate conservatively*, because a seller told $85,000
who receives $89,398 forgives the variance in the pleasant direction only. The net sheet is
also where proration literacy pays daily: the agent who can run the June 15 arithmetic of this
unit fills the sheet's proration lines with computation, not guesswork.

**Prorations: the arithmetic of fairness.** Continuous charges — property taxes above all, plus
rents on tenant-occupied property, HOA dues, sometimes insurance — are divided between the
parties as of the closing date. The standard Texas pattern for taxes follows Module 03's law:
the year's taxes are a lien from January 1 (§ 32.01) but are billed late in the year, so at a
mid-year closing the *seller's* share — January 1 through the day before closing (or through
closing, as the contract allocates the day) — is **credited to the buyer** and debited to the
seller, and the buyer later pays the whole bill. Two computational conventions coexist, and the
statement states which it used: the **statutory/banker's year** (360 days, twelve 30-day months)
and the **actual/calendar year** (365 or 366 days, actual month lengths).

*Worked proration:* closing June 15; annual taxes estimated at $6,480; seller responsible
through June 14. **360-day method:** per diem = $6,480 ÷ 360 = $18.00; seller's share = 5 months
(Jan–May, 150 days) + 14 days = 164 days × $18.00 = **$2,952.00** — buyer credit, seller debit.
**365-day method:** per diem = $6,480 ÷ 365 = $17.7534…; seller's days = 31+28+31+30+31+14 =
165; 165 × $17.7534 = **$2,929.32**. The methods differ by $22.68 on the same facts — the reason
the method is a stated term, not a rounding choice. *Worked rent proration:* a tenant-occupied
property closes June 15 with June rent of $1,800 collected by the seller on June 1; the buyer
owns June 15–30 (16 days of a 30-day month): buyer credit = 16/30 × $1,800 = **$960.00**. And
the tenant's **security deposit** transfers to the buyer as a credit in full — it is the
tenant's money changing custodians, not income to prorate.

**HOA dues and the day itself.** Two conventions complete the proration toolkit. *Association
dues* prorate exactly like taxes but usually run monthly and prepaid: a $360 monthly assessment
paid by the seller on June 1, with closing June 15 and the buyer owning the 15th onward under
the contract's allocation, returns 16/30 × $360 = **$192.00** to the seller — a seller credit
and buyer debit, the mirror of the tax proration's direction, because here the seller *prepaid*
what the buyer will enjoy. *The day of closing* belongs to whichever party the contract says —
proration clauses state it expressly — and the statement's dates must match that allocation;
"through the day before closing" and "through closing" differ by one per diem on every prorated
line, which on the worked tax figures is a visible $18.00. Neither convention is law; both are
contract terms, which is why the agent's review starts from the contract every time.

**A complete miniature statement, reconciled.** Assemble a whole (simplified) closing to see the
columns prove themselves. *Facts: price $300,000; buyer's new loan $240,000; earnest money
$3,000 held by the title company; seller's payoff $187,400; commission 6% split evenly;
seller's escrow fee share $450, buyer's $450; owner's title premium $1,800 paid by seller per
contract; buyer's lender charges $4,200; recording, buyer's instruments, $180; closing June 15,
annual taxes $6,480 prorated on the 360-day method, seller through June 14 (share $2,952.00).*

*Buyer's column* — debits: price $300,000; lender charges $4,200; escrow fee $450; recording
$180 → **$304,830**. Credits: loan $240,000; earnest money $3,000; tax proration $2,952 →
**$245,952**. **Cash due from buyer = $58,878.00.**

*Seller's column* — credits: price **$300,000**. Debits: payoff $187,400; commission $18,000;
owner's premium $1,800; escrow fee $450; tax proration $2,952 → **$210,602**. **Cash due to
seller = $89,398.00.**

*The escrow officer's proof*: receipts (buyer's cash $58,878 + loan $240,000 + earnest money
$3,000 = $301,878) equal disbursements (seller's proceeds $89,398 + payoff $187,400 +
commission $18,000 + owner's premium $1,800 + both escrow fees $900 + lender charges $4,200 +
recording $180 = $301,878). To the penny — and notice *why* it balances: the price and the
proration each appear once in each column, opposite signs, and every third-party charge is
collected from exactly one party. When a real statement refuses to reconcile, the error is
almost always one of those symmetries broken — a proration entered on one side only, a fee
collected twice — and the reviewer who understands the symmetry finds it in minutes.

### Applied exercise: prepare the statement yourself

*Price $220,000; buyer's loan $198,000; earnest money $2,500; seller's payoff $141,300;
commission 5% paid by seller; escrow fees $400 each side; buyer's lender charges $3,100;
recording (buyer) $160; closing September 10, annual taxes $4,320, 360-day method, seller
responsible through September 9.* **Task:** compute the tax proration, each party's totals,
cash due from buyer and cash due to seller, and prove the escrow reconciliation. **Model
answer:** per diem $4,320 ÷ 360 = $12.00; seller's days = 8 × 30 + 9 = 249; proration =
**$2,988.00** (buyer credit, seller debit). Buyer debits: $220,000 + $3,100 + $400 + $160 =
$223,660; credits: $198,000 + $2,500 + $2,988 = $203,488; **cash from buyer $20,172.00.**
Seller credits: $220,000; debits: $141,300 + $11,000 + $400 + $2,988 = $155,688; **cash to
seller $64,312.00.** Proof: receipts $20,172 + $198,000 + $2,500 = $220,672; disbursements
$64,312 + $141,300 + $11,000 + $800 + $3,100 + $160 = $220,672. Balanced — and the exercise's
quiet lesson is that every number came from a document: contract, payoff statement, lender's
figures, tax estimate. A statement is transcription plus arithmetic; the professional's value
is knowing which document each number must match.

**Proration conventions, completed.** Four residual conventions close the toolkit. *Arrears
versus advance*: the proration's direction always follows how the charge is billed — taxes
billed at year's end mean the seller owes the buyer for time already enjoyed (buyer credit);
dues and rents collected in advance mean the seller holds money for time the buyer will own
(again buyer credit for rents, seller credit for the seller's own prepaid dues) — reason from
*who holds money for whose period* and the direction is never in doubt. *The calendar's
wrinkles*: on the 365-day method a leap year makes the divisor 366, and actual month lengths
control the day counts; on the 360-day method every month is 30 days by convention — including
February — which is why the two methods diverge and why the statement names its method.
*Estimates true up*: mid-year tax prorations rest on estimates (often last year's bill); the
contract's proration clause states whether the parties re-prorate when the actual bill issues,
and the agent's net sheets carry that caveat. *Deposits never prorate*: a security deposit
transfers whole — it was never the seller's money — exactly as the worked rent example showed.

**The errors that actually occur.** Statement review is faster with a known defect list, and
five families cover most of it: *identity errors* — names and marital status that do not match
the contract and title work (Module 05's regimes surfacing as typos); *allocation errors* — a
fee the contract assigned to one party charged to the other, the single most common find;
*proration errors* — wrong method, wrong day allocation, last year's taxes without the true-up
caveat; *symmetry errors* — a credit entered without its matching debit, findable because the
reconciliation refuses to balance; and *stale-figure errors* — a payoff past its good-through
date, short by its per diem for every elapsed day. The reviewing agent works from the contract
outward, and flags — never corrects — the closer's document: the statement is the escrow
officer's instrument, amended by the escrow officer on documented instructions.

**Reading the bottom lines.** "Cash due from buyer" = buyer debits − buyer credits; "cash due to
seller" = seller credits − seller debits. The two figures are *not* mirror images — each party's
column carries charges the other never sees — and the escrow officer's reconciliation (total
receipts = total disbursements) is the arithmetic proof the file balances. The licence holder's
review duties before the table: check the sales price, the earnest money, the commission, the
prorations' dates and method, and the contract's allocation of every fee — because the agent is
the person in the room who knows what the contract said, and settlement staff, however skilled,
transcribe at volume.

**Which document proves which number.** The statement-review habit is a matching exercise, so
state the matches once plainly: the *price, earnest money, option money and every allocation of
fees* match the **contract and its amendments**; the *payoff* matches the **lender's payoff
statement** through its good-through date, with per-diem interest for each day beyond; the
*loan amount and lender charges* match the **lender's closing instructions and disclosures**;
the *commissions* match the **listing and compensation agreements**; the *prorations* match
the **tax certificate, association statements and lease documents**, computed by the stated
method from the contract's stated allocation of the closing day; and the *legal description*
matches **Module 04's discipline** — the survey and commitment, exactly. An agent who checks
each number against its source document, rather than checking the arithmetic alone, catches
the errors arithmetic cannot see: the right sum computed from the wrong figure.

### Applied exercise: find the three errors

*The day before a June 15 closing you receive a draft statement to review against a contract
that provides: price $260,000; earnest money $2,600; seller pays the owner's title premium;
taxes prorated on the 360-day method with seller responsible through June 14; commission 6%
paid by seller. The draft shows: price $260,000; earnest money credited to buyer $2,600;
owner's premium $1,650 debited to BUYER; tax proration computed as $6,120 ÷ 365 = $16.77 per
diem × 165 days = $2,767.05; commission debited to seller $15,600.* **Task:** identify every
error, state the correction with the corrected figures, and say what you do with your
findings. **Model answer:** Error one — allocation: the contract assigns the owner's premium
to the seller; $1,650 moves from the buyer's debits to the seller's. Error two — proration
method: the contract says 360-day; the correct computation is $6,120 ÷ 360 = $17.00 per diem ×
164 days (five 30-day months plus 14 days, seller through June 14) = **$2,788.00**, not
$2,767.05 — both the divisor and the day count were wrong, and the errors partially offset,
which is exactly why method-checking beats eyeballing. Error three is a trick of the list: the
commission is correct ($260,000 × 6% = $15,600) — verifying a right number is part of review,
and reporting "the commission is correct as drawn" is a finding. Action: the findings go to
the escrow officer in writing with the contract references, corrections are the closer's to
make, and the corrected statement is re-reviewed before the parties see it — the reviewer
reviews; the instrument's author amends.

### Statute reading exercise: § 2607 at the office door

From 12 U.S.C. § 2607 as set out above: **(1)** A mortgage lender offers a brokerage $200 per
closed referral, papered as a "desk rental" with no desk. Lawful? **(2)** A listing broker pays a
cooperating referral fee to the out-of-state broker who sent the client. Lawful? **(3)** A
brokerage part-owns a title agency and encourages — but does not require — its use, with the
written affiliated-business disclosure and estimate given. Which § 2607(c) conditions is that
practice standing on? *(Answers: (1) no — a thing of value for referrals of settlement business,
and the sham label changes nothing; (2) yes — cooperative brokerage and referral arrangements
between real estate agents and brokers are expressly excepted; (3) the affiliated business
arrangement exception: disclosure, written estimate, no required use, and compensation limited
to ownership returns.)*

### Applied exercise: balance the statement or find the error

*A draft closing statement shows: price $300,000; earnest money $3,000; buyer's new loan
$240,000; taxes $7,200/year prorated on the 360-day method with the seller responsible through
the day before an August 10 closing; seller's payoff $118,400; commission 6% split evenly; each
party pays a $450 escrow fee; owner's title premium $1,900 charged to the seller per the
contract. The preparer shows the tax proration as a $4,240 buyer credit.* **Task:** verify the
proration, then compute the seller's net proceeds. **Model answer:** Per diem = $7,200 ÷ 360 =
$20.00. Seller's period: January 1 – August 9 = 7 months (210 days) + 9 days = 219 days ×
$20.00 = **$4,380.00** — the draft understates the buyer's credit by $140, exactly the kind of
transcription slip the agent's review exists to catch. Seller's side: credits $300,000; debits =
payoff $118,400 + commission $18,000 + escrow $450 + title $1,900 + tax proration $4,380 =
$143,130. **Seller nets $156,870.** (The buyer's cash to close is computed on the buyer's own
column — price and buyer charges less earnest money, loan and the $4,380 credit — and the
reconciled file's receipts equal its disbursements.)

### Module recap

Closing is the contract's calendar executed: title cured, survey passed, inspections resolved,
financing conditioned to "clear to close," disclosure delivered, insurance bound, walk-through
done — then the escrow breaks in order: execute and acknowledge (§ 12.001), fund, disburse,
record without delay (§ 13.001's warning), and issue the policies (§§ 2501.003–.004). Texas
closes through neutral escrow officers, defined by function in the Insurance Code, who advise
no one. Federal law frames the financed closing: RESPA's purpose (12 U.S.C. § 2601), the
integrated, fully itemised settlement disclosure (§ 2603) with Regulation X's machinery
(12 C.F.R. part 1024, § 1024.6's booklet), and § 2607's flat ban on referral kickbacks and
unearned fee-splitting — with safe harbours for work actually performed, agent-to-agent
referrals and conditioned affiliated arrangements. The statement itself is a reconciled ledger
of debits, credits and prorations — 360-day and 365-day methods, stated and checked — and the
licence holder who can re-run every line is the transaction's last, best proofreader.
