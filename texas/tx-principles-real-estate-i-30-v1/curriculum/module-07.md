# Texas Principles of Real Estate I — Module 07

## TREC Topic: Estates, Transfers and Titles

**Prescribed time: 210 minutes.** Nine units under the scope note *"Titles & conveyance of real
estate; deed, encumbrances and liens."* The module's question is the transaction's deepest one:
how does ownership actually move from one holder to the next, and how does anyone prove who holds it
now?

**Learning objectives.** Name the requirements of a valid Texas deed and read the statutory warranty
deed form; distinguish general and special warranty deeds and deeds without warranty by the promises
each carries; explain what recording does and does not do, and state the Texas rule that punishes
the unrecorded; evaluate the forms of title evidence and describe the title insurance machinery
Texas regulates; trace the ways ownership transfers voluntarily and involuntarily, including descent
under the Texas intestacy statutes and the three adverse possession clocks; and identify the
transfer events — death, foreclosure, condemnation, tax sale — a licence holder must recognise in a
chain of title.

## Unit: Deeds

A **deed** is the instrument that conveys an interest in real property from **grantor** to
**grantee**. Its statutory skeleton is short. Property Code § 5.021 — the Texas conveyancing
statute you met in Module 06 — requires that a conveyance of an estate of inheritance, a freehold,
or an estate for more than one year be **in writing**, **subscribed** (signed) by the conveyor or
the conveyor's authorised agent, and **delivered**. Around that core, Texas practice expects: a
competent, identifiable grantor whose name matches the record chain; an identifiable grantee; words
of grant (Texas needs no magic formula — recall § 5.001's rule that a conveyance passes fee simple
unless expressly limited, with no "and his heirs" required); an adequate legal description (Module
04); recital of consideration; and the grantor's acknowledgment before a notary — which, as the
recording unit shows, is what qualifies the deed for the public records rather than what makes it
valid between the parties.

**Delivery and acceptance** deserve their own sentence, because they are where deeds fail
invisibly: a deed conveys nothing until delivered with intent to pass title presently, and a signed
deed in the grantor's desk drawer conveys nothing at all. Delivery is ordinarily accomplished
through closing; oddities — deeds handed over "to take effect when I die," deeds recorded without
the grantor's knowledge — are attorney problems the licence holder routes, not solves.

**The deed's anatomy, part by part.** Practice reads deeds in named pieces, and the vocabulary is
worth owning even though Texas law (as § 5.022(c) says expressly) prescribes no mandatory form.
The **premises** open the instrument: date, parties, recital of consideration. The **granting
clause** carries the operative words — "grant, sell and convey" in the statutory short form — and
with them, under § 5.023, the implied covenants unless the deed says otherwise. The **habendum**
("to have and to hold") states the estate granted, which is where a life estate or defeasible
limitation would appear and where silence, under § 5.001, means fee simple. The **warranty
clause**, if any, grades the deed as the previous discussion describes. **Reservations and
exceptions** subtract from the grant — a reserved mineral interest, an excepted easement — and
misreading them is how buyers come to own less than they celebrated. Then the **execution**
block: the grantor's signature (§ 5.021's "subscribed"), the acknowledgment that qualifies the
deed for recording (§ 12.001), and — from the newly met Property Code § 11.008 — the
confidentiality-rights notice that must ride at the top of instruments transferring an
individual's interest, telling natural persons they may strike their social security and
driver's licence numbers before filing. A licence holder never drafts any of these parts; a
licence holder who can *name* them can read a title company's draft against the contract and
catch the reserved minerals nobody negotiated.

**Correcting a deed that went wrong.** Names misspelled, descriptions mistyped, acknowledgments
botched — errors surface constantly in chains, and practice distinguishes the cures: a
**correction instrument** executed under the applicable statutory procedures for the error's
kind, versus a fresh conveyance where correction rules cannot reach the defect. Which cure fits
which error is precisely the kind of question this course keeps routing: the licence holder's
job is noticing the mismatch between contract, commitment and draft deed — counsel's job is
fixing it.

### Statute reading exercise: three sections, one deed

From §§ 5.021, 5.022 and 5.023 as taught: **(1)** name the three things § 5.021 requires of a
conveyance of a freehold; **(2)** a deed uses the § 5.022 short form but strikes the warranty
paragraph — is it still a valid conveyance, and what does § 5.022(b) say about that? **(3)** the
same deed says only "grant, sell and convey" with no covenants at all — what two promises does
§ 5.023 imply anyway, and can the parties negate them? *(Answers: (1) writing, subscription
(signature by the conveyor or authorised agent), delivery; (2) yes — a covenant of warranty is
not required in a conveyance, and the parties may insert or omit covenants as they choose;
(3) that the grantor has not previously conveyed the estate to another, and that it is free
from encumbrances — implied "unless the conveyance provides otherwise," so express contrary
language controls.)*

## Unit: Title

**Title** is the aggregate of rights that constitute ownership — and, in practice, the *provable
history* of those rights. Texas proves title through an unbroken **chain of title**: the sequence
of recorded conveyances and other instruments linking sovereign grant (Module 04's original
surveys) to the present owner. Breaks in the chain — a missing conveyance, a misspelled grantor, an
undischarged deed of trust, an heir who never signed — are **clouds** on title, curable by
corrective instruments, releases, affidavits or, at the limit, suit to quiet title.

**Warranties: what the deed promises about title.** Texas deeds are graded by the promises they
carry.

- **General warranty deed.** The grantor warrants title against defects arising at *any* time,
  under this grantor or any predecessor — the fullest assurance. Texas provides a statutory form:
  Property Code § 5.022 sets out the short-form general warranty deed ("…do grant, sell, and
  convey… and I do hereby bind myself… to warrant and forever defend all and singular the said
  premises…"), while § 5.022(b)–(c) adds two liberating rules: a covenant of warranty is **not
  required** in a conveyance, and parties may use any form not in contravention of law.
- **Special warranty deed.** The grantor warrants only against defects arising **by, through or
  under the grantor** — "I did nothing to impair title; for what came before me, look elsewhere."
  Common in commercial, foreclosure-related and estate transactions.
- **Deed without warranty.** A grant with no title covenants at all — it conveys whatever the
  grantor has, but a grant nonetheless, keeping the grantee inside the recording system's
  protections.
- **Quitclaim.** A release of whatever interest the maker may have, with no assertion of having
  any. Texas title practice treats quitclaims warily — recall from Module 07's adverse-possession
  unit below and Module 03's five-year statute that a quitclaim is expressly *excluded* as a "duly
  registered deed" for the five-year clock (CPRC § 16.025(b)) — and title companies commonly balk
  at chains that depend on them.

**The grades, priced by a hypothetical.** *In 2010, X forged a deed from the true owner and sold
to Y; in 2018 Y conveyed to Z; in 2025 the true owner surfaces and evicts Z.* What Z recovers
depends entirely on which deed Y signed. If Y gave a **general warranty deed**, Y warranted
against all defects whenever arising — the forgery predates Y, and Y must nonetheless make Z
whole. If Y gave a **special warranty deed**, Y promised only against defects arising by,
through or under Y — the forgery arose under X, and Y's warranty is not breached. If Y gave a
**quitclaim**, Y asserted nothing and released only what Y had (nothing), so Z holds the loss —
though Z's title insurance, if Z bought an owner's policy, answers where the warranty does not,
which is much of why Texas practice leans on policies over covenants. One clarification the
vocabulary invites: warranty grade never changes *what* passes — a special warranty deed conveys
the same estate a general warranty deed would; it changes only who pays when the estate proves
defective.

**Implied covenants ride along.** Even without express warranties, Property Code § 5.023 implies —
unless the conveyance provides otherwise — that the use of "grant" or "convey" promises that the
grantor has not previously conveyed the estate to another, and that the estate is free from
encumbrances; the implied covenant is independently actionable.

**Marketable title, the standard behind the vocabulary.** What all the curing serves is
**marketable title**: title a reasonably prudent buyer, properly advised, would accept — free of
reasonable doubt, not free of every recorded burden. The distinction matters daily: a parcel
subject to ordinary utility easements and subdivision covenants is perfectly marketable *with*
those burdens; a parcel whose chain depends on an unprobated will or a missing heir is not
marketable *at any price* until cured. Contracts promise marketable title implicitly or
expressly; commitments operationalise the promise as requirements; and the buyer's objection
window is where "unmarketable" gets said out loud, in writing, while it can still be fixed.

**Curing the cloud: the escalation ladder.** Clouds are cured along an escalating ladder practice
climbs only as far as necessary: a **release** for the paid-but-never-released deed of trust; a
**correction instrument** for the clerical error; a **new or corrective conveyance** where a
necessary party can still sign; **affidavits** (heirship, identity, non-production) where facts,
not signatures, are missing; and, at the top, the **suit to quiet title** or trespass-to-try-title
action — judicial determination, with time and cost to match. Each rung is someone's professional
work (title company, counsel), and the licence holder's contribution repeats this module's
refrain: find the cloud *early* — at intake, from the seller's own papers and one conversation
with the title company — because every rung climbs faster with weeks than with a closing date
three days out.

**After-acquired title, in the practical sense:** warranties are also why the *form* of deed
matters downstream — a general warranty grantor who later acquires the title the deed purported to
convey is bound by the warranty to make it good, while a quitclaim maker walked away promising
nothing.

## Unit: Public Records, Recording & Acknowledgment

Texas maintains its land records county by county, and the system's logic is **notice**.

**Getting in: acknowledgment.** Property Code § 12.001 provides that an instrument concerning real
property *may be recorded if it has been acknowledged, sworn to with a proper jurat, or proved
according to law* — and that a conveyance may not be recorded unless signed and acknowledged or
sworn to by the grantor before qualified witnesses or an authorised officer, with the recently
added requirement (in the text as held, amended effective December 4, 2025) that an individual
presenting a paper conveyance in person show photo identification to the county clerk. Section
12.0011 tightens the paper path: a paper document must bear an **original** signature, acknowledged
or sworn, to be recorded or to serve as notice — with electronic instruments under the
electronic-recording statutes accommodated separately. The acknowledgment, note well, is a
recording prerequisite: an unacknowledged deed can still convey between the parties; it simply
cannot enter the records and radiate notice.

**Where, and by whom.** Two provisions of Property Code Chapter 11, read for this course, supply
the system's geography and its operator. Section 11.001 fixes the *where*: to be effectively
recorded, an instrument relating to real property must be recorded **in the county in which a
part of the property is located** — recording in the wrong county records nothing, and a tract
spanning a county line is recorded where a part lies, with careful practice filing in both. (The
statute even handles the county that did not exist yet: when a new county is created, a proper
prior recording keeps its validity and notice effect, and the new county obtains and indexes a
transcript.) Section 11.004 supplies the *who*: the **county clerk** must correctly record
qualifying instruments within a reasonable time after delivery, give a receipt, record
instruments relating to the same property **in the order filed** — the sentence undergirding
every priority race in this module — and maintain the indexes by which chains of title are
actually searched. The clerk records what qualifies; the clerk does not judge validity — which
is why forged and defective instruments can sit in the records looking official, and why
examination (the next unit) is a profession rather than a lookup.

**What recording does: the notice statutes.** Property Code § 13.002 states the affirmative side —
a properly recorded instrument is *notice to all persons* of its existence and is open to public
inspection. This is **constructive notice**: the world is charged with knowing what the records
show, searched or not. Property Code § 13.001 states the punishing side: an *unrecorded*
conveyance, mortgage or deed of trust is **void as to a creditor or a subsequent purchaser for
valuable consideration without notice** — while remaining fully binding on the parties themselves,
their heirs, and any subsequent taker who paid nothing or who had notice. Texas is thus a
notice-race jurisdiction in effect for the honest purchaser: the buyer who pays value without
notice of a prior unrecorded deed takes free of it. The moral licences are built on: **record
immediately** — the grantee who delays recording is gambling the property on the grantor's honesty
— and **search always**, because the records bind you whether or not you looked.

**Kinds of notice, assembled.** Constructive notice from the records (§ 13.002); **actual notice**
from what a party in fact knows; and inquiry-type notice from facts demanding investigation — the
occupant who is not the record owner, the visible path across the land. A purchaser's protection
under § 13.001 evaporates with notice of any kind, which is why possession and inspection matter
alongside the record search.

**The records are public — and that fact is managed.** Openness is the system's engine
(§ 13.002's "open to public inspection" is what makes constructive notice fair), and Texas
manages its cost in personal terms through Property Code § 11.008: instruments need no social
security number to record, preparers may not include one, and every instrument transferring an
individual's interest carries the boldfaced NOTICE OF CONFIDENTIALITY RIGHTS telling natural
persons they may strike social security and driver's licence numbers before filing. For the
licence holder the point is practical on both faces: everything recorded — price recitals,
liens, family circumstances an heirship affidavit narrates — is permanently public, a fact
sellers deserve to hear before unusual documents are drafted; and the identifying numbers the
system does not need should never be on the paper in the first place.

## Unit: Evidence of Title

How does a buyer *know* the seller owns what the contract promises? Four instruments of assurance,
in ascending order of modern use:

- **Abstract and attorney's opinion.** An **abstract of title** is a compiled history of every
  recorded instrument affecting the parcel; an attorney examines it and issues an opinion of title.
  Venerable, still met in rural Texas, and limited: the opinion is only as good as the abstract and
  the abstractor, and it insures nothing.
- **Attorney's certificate of title** — examination directly from the records, same limits.
- **Torrens-style registration** — title by government certificate; noted for completeness, not a
  feature of Texas practice.
- **Title insurance** — the Texas standard, and a regulated industry. Insurance Code § 2501.003
  defines **title insurance** as insurance that insures, guarantees or indemnifies an owner of real
  property or another interested party *against loss or damage resulting from a lien or encumbrance
  on or defect in the title, or the invalidity or impairment of a lien* — protection, unlike every
  other insurance, against the **past** rather than the future. Two structural facts distinguish
  Texas: the **premium is promulgated** — § 2501.003(8) defines premium as the rates promulgated by
  the state's insurance regulator, and expressly *includes* the charges for title examination and
  closing the transaction — so title policy pricing does not vary company to company; and the
  industry runs on **abstract plants**: § 2501.004 requires a plant that is geographically arranged
  (indexed by land, not just by names), covers a period beginning not later than January 1, 1979,
  and is kept current. In a transaction the machinery appears as the **commitment** (the insurer's
  offer to insure, listing requirements to cure and exceptions it will not cover — the document the
  Module 03 applied exercise taught you to read), then the **owner's policy** protecting the buyer
  up to the price, and the **loan policy** protecting the lender's lien priority up to the debt.
  The escrow side of the same industry — § 2501.003's defined **escrow officer** — closes the
  transaction; Module 09 picks up there.

**What a policy actually does when title fails.** Assurance instruments are best compared at the
moment of loss. Under an abstract-and-opinion regime, the evicted buyer's recourse is a
professional-negligence claim against the examining attorney — only as good as the attorney's
error and solvency, and unavailable when the examination was flawless but the record lied (the
forged deed that looked perfect). A title policy answers differently: it is *indemnity*, not
opinion — the § 2501.003 definition insures against loss from defects, liens and encumbrances,
so the insurer pays covered losses and defends covered claims whether or not anyone erred. That
difference — negligence versus indemnity — is why the forged-deed hypothetical of the Title unit
lands on insurance, and why lenders will not fund without their own loan policy. The limits are
equally instructive: policies speak as of their date (new defects are the owner's problem),
exclude what their exceptions list, and pay up to stated amounts — the owner's policy at the
price, the loan policy at the debt. Insurance replaces neither examination nor curing; it prices
what examination and curing could not eliminate.

### Applied exercise: explaining the two policies

*At a buyer consultation, the buyers ask: "The lender is making us pay for title insurance —
doesn't that protect us?"* **Task:** answer accurately in plain language, covering who each
policy protects, what a policy does and does not do, and where the premium rules come from.
**Model answer:** the policy the lender requires is a **loan policy** — it protects the lender's
lien up to the loan balance, and pays the buyers nothing if title fails. Protection for the
buyers' own equity is the **owner's policy**, issued for the purchase price. Both are indemnity
against *past* defects — forged links, missed heirs, undischarged liens — as of the policy date;
neither guarantees the property's condition, boundaries beyond what is insured, or future
events. And in Texas the premium rates are promulgated by the state's insurance regulator
(§ 2501.003(8)), so the price is the price — choosing a title company is about service, plant
quality and closing execution, not rate shopping. Who pays which premium is a negotiated
contract term. All of that can be said by a licence holder because it describes the products;
whether these buyers need an owner's policy on these facts is their decision, with counsel if
they want it.

**Reading the commitment like a professional.** The commitment's working logic is two lists. The
first list is *requirements*: what must happen before the insurer will issue the policy — the
seller's deed, releases of the liens being paid off, probate documents where an estate intervenes
(this module's later units in commitment form), entity authority papers (Module 05's checklist),
resolution of the judgment against a similar name. The second list is *exceptions*: what the
policy will not cover if issued — the standard exceptions every Texas policy carries plus the
parcel's own recorded burdens: the easements, covenants and mineral reservations the chain
disclosed. The licence holder's pass over the two lists is triage exactly parallel to Module 03's
applied exercise: every requirement is a task with an owner and a deadline; every exception is a
disclosure conversation with the buyer *before* objections close, not after closing. And one
practice truth deserves stating twice: the commitment is an offer to insure, not a statement that
title is good — the insurer may be pricing a risk the buyer should be refusing.

## Unit: Acquisitions & Transfers

Step back and see the whole map. Ownership moves by exactly four families of events, and every deed
chain is a braid of them:

1. **Voluntary alienation during life** — sale and gift, effected by deed: the ordinary commerce of
   the industry (its own unit, below).
2. **Transfer at death** — by will or by the intestacy statutes, administered through probate (two
   units below).
3. **Involuntary transfer** — by operation of law against the owner's will: foreclosure,
   condemnation, tax sale, adverse possession, escheat (final unit).
4. **Natural forces at the boundary** — accretion (gradual addition of soil to riparian land, which
   the owner keeps) versus avulsion (sudden change, which moves no boundary), and erosion — the
   water-edge doctrines that pair with Module 03's water unit.

**The water's edge, decided by speed.** The fourth family turns on one variable: how fast the
water moved. *Accretion* — the river deposits silt against a bank over decades — grows the
riparian parcel, and the owner keeps the gain; gradual loss to *erosion* shrinks it the same way.
*Avulsion* — the flood that cuts a new channel overnight — moves **no** boundary: the line stays
where the old channel ran, and an owner can end up with land on the far side of the water. On
rivers that shift, the deed's acreage and the ground's acreage drift apart, which is one more
reason rural transactions lean on current surveys (Module 04) rather than old descriptions — and
one more question ("has the river moved?") that belongs on a waterfront intake sheet.

The licence holder's use for the map is diagnostic: every unexplained name in a chain of title —
an executor's deed, a trustee's deed, a sheriff's deed, a decree — announces which family of event
occurred, and therefore which documents must exist to support it.

## Unit: Estate Ownership

When an owner dies, the estate must pass through administration, and Texas's system is notably
efficient. The working concepts: **probate** is the judicial process proving a will and supervising
administration; the **executor** (named in a will) or **administrator** (court-appointed absent
one) collects assets, pays debts and distributes what remains; Texas's hallmark **independent
administration** lets most estates proceed with minimal court supervision once opened, which keeps
Texas probate faster and cheaper than the national reputation of "probate" suggests. Title to a
decedent's real property passes to devisees or heirs at death, subject to administration — so
during administration the personal representative may need to join in or make the conveyance, and
a buyer's title company will insist on the probate documents that authorise it.

**Wills: the Texas formalities.** Estates Code § 251.001 grants will-making power to a person of
sound mind who is 18 or older, *or* is or has been married, *or* is a member of the armed forces.
Section 251.051 states the execution requirements: a will must be **in writing**, **signed** by
the testator (or by another at the testator's direction and in the testator's presence), and
**attested by two or more credible witnesses at least 14 years old** signing in the testator's
presence. Section 251.052 preserves the Texas exception every title examiner knows: a
**holographic will** — wholly in the testator's handwriting — needs no witnesses at all. A person
who dies **testate** distributes by these instruments; the vocabulary of gifts (devise of land,
devisee, legacy) attaches here.

**Reading probate paper in a chain.** Each probate document answers a specific chain-of-title
question, and the working set is short. **Letters testamentary** (or letters of administration)
are the court's certificate that this person is the estate's personal representative — the
signature authority a title company checks against every executor's deed. An **executor's or
administrator's deed** conveys estate property during administration. A **probated will**,
admitted to record, functions in the chain like a conveyance: it shows title's path from decedent
to devisee. An **affidavit of heirship** — Texas's workhorse where no administration was
opened — recites the family history on which intestate title rests; it is evidence, not
adjudication, which is why the blended-family affidavit in this module's closing exercise is a
flashing light: the facts it recites determine fractions under §§ 201.001–201.003, and an
omitted heir is an unsigned owner. The agent's discipline with all of it: collect what exists at
intake, send it to the title company on day one, and calendar the estate's timeline as the
transaction's critical path — because it usually is.

**The formalities, tested on facts.** *A 17-year-old married rancher writes and signs a will* —
valid capacity: § 251.001 grants the power to a person who *is or has been married* regardless of
the 18-year floor, and if the instrument is wholly in his handwriting, § 251.052 excuses
witnesses entirely. *A typed will signed by the testator and one witness* — fails § 251.051's
two-witness demand, and being typed, cannot ride the holographic exception; the estate passes as
if unwritten, into the intestacy statutes of the next unit. *A will signed for a paralysed
testator by her nurse, at her direction and in her presence, then attested by two neighbours aged
16 and 19 signing in her presence* — valid: § 251.051 expressly allows signature *by another
person for the testator, in the testator's presence and under the testator's direction*, and the
witness floor is 14, not 18. The pattern for a licence holder is recognising that will validity
is exactly this technical — and that "Dad left a will" begins, never ends, the title inquiry.

**For the licence holder,** estate transactions carry three standing questions: *who* has authority
to sign (executor? all heirs? a court?), *what* documents prove it (letters testamentary, probate
orders, affidavits of heirship where administration was skipped), and *when* the sale can close —
all of them the title company's and probate counsel's to answer, and the agent's to raise on day
one rather than at the closing table.

## Unit: Transfer of Decedent's Property

When there is no valid will, the **intestacy statutes** write one, and the Texas version cannot be
understood without Module 05's community property system — the two bodies of law interlock.

**The community estate.** Estates Code § 201.003 governs the deceased spouse's share of community
property: it passes **entirely to the surviving spouse** if the decedent leaves no descendant, *or*
if all the decedent's surviving descendants are also descendants of the surviving spouse — the
intact-family rule. But if the decedent left a child of another relationship, the decedent's
one-half of the community passes to the decedent's descendants — and the surviving spouse keeps
only the spouse's own half. The blended-family house is the classic casualty: the surviving
stepparent discovers the stepchildren now co-own the home.

**The separate estate.** Estates Code § 201.002 divides a married decedent's separate property:
with children, the spouse takes **one-third of personal property** and a **life estate in one-third
of the land** (Module 03's life estate arising by statute), with the rest to the children; with no
children, the spouse takes all personal property and **one-half of the land** outright, the other
half passing to the decedent's kin — and the spouse takes everything only if the decedent left no
surviving parent, sibling or their descendants.

**No spouse at all.** Estates Code § 201.001 runs the ladder: children and their descendants; then
parents and siblings by the stated splits; then the estate divides into two **moieties** for the
paternal and maternal kindred, ascending without end. Distribution among descendants follows
§ 201.101's **per capita with representation** rule: those in the nearest degree take equal
shares, and a deceased taker's share drops to that taker's own descendants.

**The statutes, computed.** Intestacy is arithmetic once the estate is classified, so compute
three estates. *Estate one*: H dies intestate, survived by W and their two children; the home is
community. Under § 201.003's intact-family rule, H's half of the community passes entirely to W —
W owns the whole home. *Estate two*: same facts, but one of H's two surviving children is from a
prior marriage. Now H's one-half of the community passes to H's descendants — the two children
take H's half in equal shares (§ 201.101's nearest-degree rule), and W keeps only her own half:
the home is owned half by W, a quarter by each child. *Estate three*: W2 dies intestate owning
**separate-property** land, survived by husband H2 and her three children. Under § 201.002, H2
takes a life estate in one-third of the land, the children take the land subject to it — and if
one of the three children had predeceased W2 leaving two children of his own, § 201.101 drops the
deceased child's one-third to his two children, one-sixth each: per capita with representation
doing exactly what its name says. The discipline in all three: classify first (community or
separate — Module 05's Family Code rules), then apply the right section, then check every taker
against the family facts. Change one fact and the fractions change; that is why heirship is
proved, not assumed.

### Applied exercise: the fractions on the intake sheet

*A caller wants to list "my late husband's ranch." Intake facts: the ranch was H's separate
property, inherited from his father during the marriage; H died last year without a will; H is
survived by the caller (his wife) and by two adult children of the marriage; no probate has been
opened.* **Task:** using §§ 201.002 and 201.101, state who owns what interest today, who must
sign a listing and a deed, and what has to happen before any closing. **Model answer:** separate
land, spouse and children surviving: the wife holds a life estate in one-third of the land
(§ 201.002); the two children took the land at H's death in equal halves, subject to that life
estate. Today's owners are all three — so all three sign the listing and any deed, and the
buyer's title company will require heirship to be established (proceeding or affidavit practice)
since no probate exists. Nothing closes until authority and heirship are papered; the agent who
promises otherwise at intake is promising other people's signatures.

**The ladder without a spouse, once through.** *D dies intestate, unmarried, childless, owning a
duplex. D's parents are dead; D had three siblings — two living, one predeceased leaving a
daughter.* § 201.001 sends the estate to the parents' line: with both parents gone, the whole
passes to the siblings and their descendants — the two living siblings take one-third each, and
the predeceased sibling's third drops to his daughter (§ 201.101 representation again). If no
kindred existed on one side, the statute's moiety structure keeps the other side taking; only
when the whole ladder is empty does the state take by escheat — the involuntary-alienation unit's
closing entry arriving from the probate side.

**Practice meaning.** Heirship is arithmetic on family facts — and the facts are often unproved.
Texas practice fills the gap with heirship proceedings and affidavits of heirship; the licence
holder's contribution is early honesty: a listing "inherited from Mom, no probate" is a title
project, its timeline belongs to the title company and counsel, and every heir the statutes name
must ultimately sign or be dealt with. Selling begins with finding out who owns.

## Unit: Voluntary Alienation

**Alienation** — the transfer of title — is *voluntary* when the owner intends it: the sale
closing in a deed; the **gift** deed, valid without valuable consideration between the parties
(though recall § 13.001: a donee, giving no value, takes subject to prior unrecorded interests the
records would not have shown anyway — the recording system protects purchasers for value);
**dedication** of streets and parks to public use, met in Module 04's plats; and the owner's own
**will**, the voluntary transfer that waits. The unit's single doctrine is one already built:
writing, subscription and delivery (§ 5.021), acknowledgment for the records (§ 12.001), and
immediate recording (§ 13.001) — the voluntary transfer done *right*. Everything else in this
module is what happens when transfer is not voluntary, or not done right.

**Delivery, the quiet failure point, on facts.** Because gift deeds skip the closing table's
discipline, delivery problems cluster around them, and three scenarios teach the doctrine. *The
drawer deed*: mother signs a deed to daughter, tells no one, keeps it in her desk; at her death
the deed is found. Nothing was conveyed — no delivery with present intent during life — and the
land passes under her will or the intestacy statutes, not the deed. *The recorded surprise*:
father signs and records a deed to son without telling him. Recording supplies strong evidence of
delivery, and acceptance of a beneficial grant is presumed — the gift likely stands, though the
question is judicial, not conversational. *The conditional handoff*: "here's the deed, but it
only counts when I die." A deed cannot be a will: instruments taking effect at death must satisfy
the will formalities of this module's earlier unit, and a present deed with oral death
conditions is a litigation generator. The licence holder's translation for clients who ask about
"just deeding it over": gifts of land are real conveyances with real consequences — title,
homestead, the donee's creditors, the § 13.001 value problem — and an estate-planning wish
belongs with an estate-planning lawyer.

## Unit: Involuntary Alienation

Ownership also moves against the owner's will, and each mechanism has already earned a piece of
this course:

- **Foreclosure** — the secured creditor's remedy: the § 51.0001 security instrument enforced by
  sale, previewed in Module 03 and given fully in Module 08.
- **Tax sale** — the Module 03 tax lien (§§ 32.01, 32.05) enforced to judgment and sale; the
  ultimate priority claim collecting itself.
- **Condemnation (eminent domain).** The sovereign's power to take private property for public
  use on payment of adequate compensation, exercised in Texas through a statutory procedure:
  Property Code § 21.012 requires the condemning entity, when it cannot agree with the owner on
  damages, to file a petition that describes the property, states *with specificity* the public
  use, names the owner, affirms the failure to agree — and affirms that the entity provided the
  owner the **landowner's bill of rights statement** and made a **bona fide offer** to buy
  voluntarily first. For a licence holder, a pending or threatened condemnation is a material fact
  in any transaction touching the corridor, and valuation in condemnation is specialist work.
  The § 21.012 petition requirements repay one more pass, because each element protects the
  owner: the petition must *describe the property* (Module 04's discipline, enforced against the
  government), state *with specificity* the public use (no vague "municipal purposes"), name the
  owner, and affirm both that the entity and the owner *could not agree on damages* and that the
  entity provided the **landowner's bill of rights statement** and made a **bona fide offer** —
  meaning the statute forces a real negotiation before the courthouse. For practice, three
  consequences: a corridor rumour is a disclosure conversation and a value question long before
  any petition is filed; an owner approached by a condemnor has statutory protections and should
  have counsel *before* responding to the first offer; and a pending petition against a listing
  is a material fact the licence holder does not soften.
- **Adverse possession.** Long, hostile possession that ripens into title — Texas gives the
  doctrine three clocks, all read for this course in the Civil Practice & Remedies Code, all
  framed as limitation periods barring the record owner's suit:
  - **Three years** (§ 16.024) against a possessor claiming under *title or color of title* — a
    facially plausible but defective chain.
  - **Five years** (§ 16.025) against a possessor who *cultivates, uses or enjoys* the land,
    **pays the applicable taxes**, and claims under a **duly registered deed** — with quitclaim
    and forged deeds expressly disqualified.
  - **Ten years** (§ 16.026) against bare possession that cultivates, uses or enjoys — the
    "naked" claim — capped, absent a title instrument, at **160 acres** including improvements
    unless more is actually enclosed, and extended to instrument boundaries where a registered
    memorandum fixes them.
  The doctrine's elements — peaceable, adverse, continuous, hostile possession — reward the
  vigilant and punish the absent, which is the practice lesson: rural owners inspect their land,
  encroachments (Module 03) are addressed rather than tolerated, and "the neighbour has farmed
  that strip for years" is a sentence that should raise the hair on a licence holder's neck.
**Adverse possession, worked against the clocks.** The three statutes separate cleanly on facts.
*Fact set one*: a rancher fences and grazes a neighbouring strip for twelve years, no deed, no tax
payments. The three-year statute cannot run (no title or color of title); the five-year statute
cannot run (no registered deed, no taxes); the **ten-year** statute can — bare cultivation, use or
enjoyment is its whole demand, and twelve years exceeds it, within § 16.026's acreage frame.
*Fact set two*: a buyer takes a defective deed (void for a forged link upstream), records it,
pays taxes annually, and occupies for six years. The **five-year** statute fits — cultivation or
use, tax payment, and a duly registered deed — unless the deed is the *forged* one itself, which
§ 16.025(b) disqualifies along with quitclaims; against a facially regular but defective chain,
even the **three-year** statute may have run. *Fact set three*: a holdover relative lives in a
decedent's house "by everyone's kindness" for twenty years. No statute runs — possession with
permission is not hostile, and hostility is the element permission always defeats. The doctrine's
architecture, restated: the statutes bar the *record owner's suit* to recover the land; the
possessor's title ripens because the owner's remedy died. Which is why the practice advice runs
to owners, not squatters: inspect, post, paper every permission in writing (permission defeats
hostility), and act within the shortest plausible clock when an encroachment appears.

### Statute reading exercise: which clock, if any?

From §§ 16.024, 16.025 and 16.026 as taught: **(1)** possessor under a recorded *quitclaim*, using
and paying taxes for seven years — which statutes are available? **(2)** naked possessor
cultivating 300 unenclosed acres for eleven years with no instrument — how much can the claim
reach? **(3)** why does "the neighbour said I could use it" end every analysis? *(Answers:
(1) not the five-year statute — § 16.025(b) excludes quitclaims as the registered deed; not the
three-year — a quitclaim asserting no title is not title or color of title; only the ten-year
statute's bare-possession route remains, needing ten years; (2) without a title instrument,
§ 16.026 limits the claim to 160 acres including improvements unless more is actually enclosed —
so 160 of the 300; (3) permissive use is not adverse or hostile, so no limitations clock ever
starts.)*

- **Escheat** — the state takes when an owner dies with no will and no heirs the law can find:
  sovereignty's residual claim, closing the circle begun in Module 03's history unit.

**Two vocabulary distinctions worth cementing.** *Title versus deed*: the deed is paper — an
instrument of conveyance; title is the ownership itself, which the deed transfers and the chain
proves. A person can hold a flawless deed to land the grantor never owned (paper without title)
and can own land with no deed in hand at all (an heir under the intestacy statutes: title without
paper). *Void versus voidable instruments*: a forged deed is void — a nullity conveying nothing
to anyone, however many good-faith purchasers follow it — while a deed obtained from the grantor
by fraud is voidable — effective until set aside, so a later bona fide purchaser for value can
cut off the defrauded grantor's remedy. The pair explains both why forgery is the catastrophe
title insurance exists to absorb, and why speed matters to a defrauded seller.

**Assembling the module: one transaction, every unit.** Close the module by watching a single
resale run through everything taught. The contract signs (Module 06); the commitment issues, its
requirements and exceptions mapping the chain's history — a probated will here, an old deed of
trust needing release there (Evidence of Title, Estate Ownership). The title company examines the
chain the county clerk's order-of-filing index preserves (§ 11.004), trusting the constructive
notice the records radiate (§ 13.002) and hunting the unrecorded interests § 13.001 punishes.
Cures climb the ladder — releases, affidavits, a correction instrument. At closing, the deed is
drafted by counsel in the § 5.022 short form, general warranty as negotiated, § 11.008 notice at
its head; it is signed (§ 5.021), acknowledged (§ 12.001), delivered — and recorded the same day
in the county where the land lies (§ 11.001), because every hour before recording is the
§ 13.001 gamble. Six statutes, four units, one afternoon: the module is not a list of doctrines
but a single machine, and the licence holder is one of its operators.

### Statute reading exercise: the recording gamble

From Property Code §§ 13.001–13.002 as set out above: **(1)** Seller deeds Blackacre to A, who
does not record. Seller then deeds Blackacre to B, who pays market price knowing nothing of A, and
records. Who owns Blackacre, and why? **(2)** Same facts, but B is Seller's nephew who paid
nothing. **(3)** Same facts, but B toured the property and met A living there before buying.
*(Answers: (1) B — as to a subsequent purchaser for valuable consideration without notice, A's
unrecorded deed is void (§ 13.001(a)); (2) A — the unrecorded deed binds a subsequent taker who
does not pay valuable consideration (§ 13.001(b)); (3) A — B's knowledge, and even the inquiry
notice from A's visible possession, defeats the without-notice requirement.)*

### Applied exercise: the chain with three surprises

*A title search on a farm shows: (i) a 2003 general warranty deed to H and W; (ii) H died in 2011;
a 2012 affidavit of heirship recites the marriage, two children of the marriage, and one child of
H from a prior marriage; (iii) a 2015 special warranty deed from W alone to her LLC; (iv) a 2019
deed of trust from the LLC to a bank; (v) nothing else.* **Task:** identify the title problems and
the statutes that create them. **Model answer:** The farm was presumptively community property
(FC § 3.003). On H's intestate death with a child from another relationship, § 201.003(c) passed
H's one-half of the community to H's three children — not to W. W's 2015 deed to her LLC could
convey only W's own half (plus whatever interests, if any, she separately acquired); the LLC's
title is half-owned by the children, and the bank's 2019 lien attaches only to the LLC's actual
interest. The special warranty deed compounds caution: W promised only against her own acts. The
cure — deeds or releases from the three children (now adults or through guardians), or a judicial
proceeding — is counsel's work; the licence holder's work was recognising that an affidavit of
heirship reciting a blended family is a flashing light, not a formality.

**One habit to start today.** Pull the recorded deed for any property you know well — your own,
a family member's — read it part by part against this module's anatomy, and find its chain's
previous link. Twenty minutes with a real instrument will fix this module's vocabulary more
firmly than any rereading, and the county records are public precisely so that anyone can do it.

**Where the next courses pick up.** The 30-hour Law of Contracts course returns to conveyances
and the closing documents in drafting-level detail; Real Estate Finance gives foreclosure and the
lender's title requirements their own hours; and Law of Agency explains whose interests the
licence holder serves while all this paper moves. Principles I's assignment was the map — chains,

**Self-check before the quiz.** Without looking back, answer: What three acts does § 5.021
require of a conveyance? What does recording in the wrong county accomplish? Who takes a
decedent's community half when a stepchild survives? Which adverse-possession statute tolerates a
possessor with no instrument at all, and over what period? If any answer hesitates, the unit it
came from deserves a second pass before the topic quiz — the quiz assumes all four.

**A last word on records and honesty.** Every doctrine in this module presumes the records tell
the truth as far as they go — and the module has shown where they stop: delivery happens off the
record; heirship happens off the record; possession, permission and hostility happen off the
record; forgery defeats the record from inside. The professionals who bridge those gaps — title
examiners, probate counsel, surveyors — depend on facts gathered at intake, which is why so many
of this module's practice notes end at the same place: the licence holder's real title work is
asking complete questions early and writing the answers down.

### Module recap

A Texas deed needs writing, signature and delivery (§ 5.021); warranty grades run from the § 5.022
statutory general warranty form (warranty optional, any lawful form allowed) through special
warranty to deeds without warranty and distrusted quitclaims, with "grant" and "convey" implying
freedom from prior conveyance and encumbrance (§ 5.023). Acknowledgment opens the records
(§§ 12.001, 12.0011); recording radiates constructive notice (§ 13.002); and the unrecorded deed
is void against the value-paying, notice-free purchaser while binding everyone else (§ 13.001).
Title is evidenced today by regulated title insurance — promulgated premiums, plant-based
examination, commitment then policy (Ins. Code §§ 2501.003–.004). Ownership moves voluntarily by
deed, gift, dedication and will; at death by will (formalities and the holographic exception,
Estates Code §§ 251.001–.052) or by intestacy's interlock with community property — the
blended-family rule of § 201.003, the life-estate arithmetic of § 201.002, the kindred ladder of
§ 201.001, per capita with representation (§ 201.101); and involuntarily by foreclosure, tax
sale, condemnation with its bona-fide-offer and bill-of-rights prerequisites (§ 21.012), adverse
possession on the three-, five- and ten-year clocks (§§ 16.024–.026), and escheat. The unit of
professional skill throughout: read every strange deed in a chain as the record of an event, and
demand the documents that prove the event was done right.
