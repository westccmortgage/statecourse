# Texas Principles of Real Estate I — Module 04

## TREC Topic: Legal Description

**Prescribed time: 100 minutes.** Two units under the scope note *"Legal descriptions."* A contract or
deed conveys only what its description identifies, so this topic is where every later document either
stands or falls.

**Learning objectives.** Explain why a street address is not a legal description; describe the three
description methods used in Texas — the original survey and abstract system, metes and bounds, and
lot and block — and read a simple example of each; state what Texas law requires of a recorded
subdivision plat; describe what a surveyor's field notes must contain and what the survey products
used in practice are; and recognise description problems that must go to a surveyor or attorney
rather than be patched by a licence holder.

## Unit: Methods of Describing Real Estate in Texas

**What a legal description is for.** A legal description identifies land with enough certainty that a
stranger — a court, a title examiner, a surveyor a century later — can locate its boundaries on the
ground from the words alone. A street address fails that test: addresses are postal conveniences,
reassigned and duplicated, and they describe a building's door rather than a parcel's boundary. Tax
parcel numbers fail it too: they are an appraisal district's filing system. Contracts and deeds
therefore carry a *legal* description, and the licence holder's first discipline in this module is
never to draft one — the job is to *copy* the existing description exactly from the prior deed or the
title commitment, and to route any doubt to the title company or a surveyor.

**Texas's foundation: the original land grant and survey system.** Texas differs from most states in
a way that shapes every description in it. Because Texas kept its public lands when it joined the
Union, the federal rectangular (government) survey system of townships and sections was never
extended over Texas. The state's land fabric is instead a quilt of **original surveys** — grants
made over three sovereign eras (Spanish and Mexican grants; Republic of Texas grants; State of Texas
grants) to individuals, colonists, railroads and school funds. Each original survey is identified by
its **grantee name, survey number and abstract number** — the abstract number being the General Land
Office's index for the original grant within each county. Land in Texas is ultimately described *by
reference to the original survey it lies within*: a deed may convey "10.00 acres out of the John
Smith Survey, Abstract No. 123, Freestone County," with the tract's boundaries then given by metes
and bounds. Two statutory anchors, both read for this course, tie this system to the present day:
the field notes of a survey of public land must state *the authority under which the survey is made*
and connect it by *calls and connections for identification* (Natural Resources Code § 21.041), and
a recorded subdivision plat must *locate the subdivision with respect to an original corner of the
original survey of which it is a part* (Local Government Code § 232.001(b)(2)). Every modern
description, however new, hangs from that original fabric.

**The Spanish measure survives.** Texas surveying carries its civil-law history in its units:
Natural Resources Code § 21.041(3) directs that field notes give the land *"by proper field notes
with the necessary calls and connections for identification, observing the Spanish measurement by
varas."* A **vara** is the old Spanish yard used throughout Texas land records — treated in Texas
practice as **33⅓ inches**, so that 36 varas equal 100 feet — and old descriptions state distances
in varas as a matter of course. A licence holder need not convert units professionally but must
recognise them: a call of "950 varas" in an old deed is roughly 2,639 feet, and mistaking varas for
feet misplaces a boundary by nearly two-thirds of its length.

**Working with varas.** Because the vara appears constantly in older records, a short drill fixes
the conversions. Treating the vara as 33⅓ inches: one vara is 2.778 feet; **36 varas = 100 feet**;
1,900.8 varas = 5,280 feet, one mile. Old grants also speak of *leagues* and *labors* — larger Spanish land
quantities a licence holder should recognise as historical units to be resolved from the grant
documents themselves, not converted from memory. Run the arithmetic both
ways until it is mechanical: a call of "540 varas" is 540 × 2.778 ≈ 1,500 feet; a fence line paced
at 250 feet is 250 × 0.36 = 90 varas. What the drill buys is not surveying competence but error
detection: when a seller's old abstract says the tract runs "950 varas along the creek" and a
modern flyer says "950 feet of creek frontage," the licence holder who knows the units sees a
2,639-foot boundary being advertised at a third of its length — or, worse, the reverse.

**Method one: metes and bounds.** The oldest and most flexible method describes a tract by its
perimeter: beginning at a defined **point of beginning (POB)**, the description runs boundary by
boundary — each a **call** stating a direction (a bearing such as "S 45° 30′ E") and a distance —
around the tract and back to the POB, "containing 10.00 acres of land, more or less." The
description's skeleton is built from **monuments**: physical or record reference points, either
natural (a creek, a marked tree) or artificial (an iron rod set, a concrete monument, an adjoiner's
surveyed line). Reading one is mechanical once the vocabulary is known:

> *"BEING a 2.000 acre tract out of the Maria Vela Survey, Abstract No. 87, Hays County, Texas,
> described as follows: BEGINNING at a ½-inch iron rod found at the southeast corner of the Garza
> 5.00 acre tract; THENCE N 01° 15′ 00″ E, 417.42 feet to a ½-inch iron rod set; THENCE S 88° 45′
> 00″ E, 208.71 feet to a point in the west line of County Road 214; THENCE S 01° 15′ 00″ W along
> said road, 417.42 feet to a ½-inch iron rod found; THENCE N 88° 45′ 00″ W, 208.71 feet to the
> POINT OF BEGINNING, containing 2.000 acres of land, more or less."*

Two doctrines about metes and bounds matter even at the principles level. *First*, when calls
conflict, the construction hierarchy generally prefers monuments over courses and distances, and
courses and distances over stated area — because a physical monument is harder to mistake than a
transcribed number, and "containing X acres" is the weakest call of all, which is why it travels
with "more or less." *Second*, a description must **close**: followed call by call, it must return
to the point of beginning. A description that fails to close, or that begins at an unlocatable
point, may fail entirely — and a conveyance that fails for description is at best salvageable as a
contract to convey (recall Property Code § 5.002), a lawyer's problem and not a form-filler's.

**Method two: lot and block (recorded plat).** Subdivision converts metes and bounds into
convenience. The developer's surveyor draws the whole tract once — streets, blocks, lots, easements —
onto a **plat**, which is recorded; thereafter each parcel is described by reference: *"Lot 7,
Block C, Oak Hollow Section 2, an addition to the City of Waco, McLennan County, Texas, according to
the plat recorded in Volume 412, Page 88, Plat Records of McLennan County."* The plat, incorporated
by that reference, supplies every boundary, dimension and dedicated easement. Texas law makes the
plat mandatory for land division outside municipal limits: Local Government Code § 232.001 requires
the owner of a tract outside a municipality who divides it into **two or more parts** to lay out a
subdivision, lots, or streets and other public parts to have a plat prepared; the section expressly
adds — closing an old evasion — that a division *by metes-and-bounds description in a deed of
conveyance, a contract for a deed, or an executory contract* is still a division. The plat must
describe the subdivision by metes and bounds, tie to the original survey corner, state dimensions,
be acknowledged like a deed, and be filed with the county clerk. (Municipalities impose parallel
platting regimes inside their limits and their extraterritorial jurisdictions under separate law;
the principle a licence holder carries is that *dividing land in Texas is a regulated act*, and
"we'll just deed off five acres to the buyer" is a sentence that should trigger the platting
question every time.)

**Reading the plat once it is found.** A lot-and-block description is only the address of the real
description — the plat itself. When the plat is pulled (title companies attach it; county clerks
index it by volume and page), a licence holder reads five layers off the sheet: the **boundary** of
the whole subdivision with its tie to the original survey corner (the § 232.001(b)(2) requirement
at work); the **blocks and lots** with each lot's dimensions; the **streets and other public
parts** dedicated by the plat; the **platted easements** — utility and drainage strips drawn lot by
lot, which encumber every lot they cross exactly as Module 03 described; and the **building
setback lines** many plats draw, which operate alongside any recorded covenants. The habit to
build: whenever a contract says "Lot 7, Block C," the plat is part of the description, and the
buyer deserves to see it — the easement running under the planned pool addition is on that sheet,
not in the deed.

### Applied exercise: walking the two-acre tract

*Use the Maria Vela Survey description quoted above.* **Task:** (1) sketch the tract from the
calls, labelling each corner monument and the road; (2) verify closure by comparing opposite calls;
(3) compute the acreage from the dimensions and check it against the stated "2.000 acres"; (4)
state which call would control if the 417.42-foot distance conflicted with the found iron rod at
the end of it. **Model answer:** (1) the tract is a rectangle: north 417.42 ft along the Garza
tract's east line, east 208.71 ft to the county road, south 417.42 ft along the road, west 208.71
ft back to the point of beginning; (2) it closes — the two north-south calls are equal and
opposite, as are the two east-west calls; (3) 417.42 × 208.71 = 87,120 sq ft ÷ 43,560 = 2.000
acres, matching the stated area; (4) the monument — under the construction hierarchy, the found
iron rod outranks the transcribed distance, and the stated acreage is the weakest call of all. The
larger lesson: a description this clean is *checkable* by anyone with the vocabulary, and the
licence holder's pass over it is exactly this exercise done quickly.

**Reference to other recorded instruments.** A third form in daily use describes land by pointing
at a prior recorded description: "being the same land described in the deed recorded in Volume —,
Page —." It is only as good as the instrument it points to, and it inherits every defect. Title
companies accept it; careful practice supplements it with the underlying metes and bounds.

**The licence holder's boundaries in this unit** are worth stating as bluntly as the Commission's
form implies: copy descriptions exactly, from source documents; check that the contract's
description matches the title commitment's; treat any mismatch, gap, overlap, failure to close, or
"part of" description without a survey as a stop-and-refer event; and never write, trim or
"improve" a legal description. Drafting descriptions is surveying and law; transmitting them
accurately is brokerage.

## Unit: Surveying

**What surveyors do.** A survey translates words into ground and ground into words: the surveyor
retraces record boundaries, locates monuments, sets new ones where old ones are gone, measures
improvements and encroachments, and reports the result as a drawing with certified field notes. In
a transaction the survey answers the questions the deed records cannot: where the boundaries
actually run, whether the improvements sit inside them, what physical evidence of easements or
encroachments exists, and whether the fences match the calls.

**Field notes: the statutory anatomy.** For surveys of public land, Natural Resources Code § 21.041
prescribes the contents of the surveyor's field notes, and the list doubles as a reader's guide to
any Texas survey: the county; the authority for the survey and a true description; proper field
notes with the calls and connections needed for identification, observing the Spanish measurement by
varas; a diagram of the survey; State Plane Coordinate values for the beginning point under the
Texas Coordinate Systems, with zone, mapping angle, grid distances, acreage and the reference
station; the names of the field personnel; the date; and the surveyor's signature. Section 21.001 of
the same chapter supplies a definition with practical bite on rural land: a **navigable stream** is
a stream *retaining an average width of 30 feet from the mouth up* — and because beds of navigable
streams belong to the state, the 30-foot test decides where private land stops at the water. A
licence holder who lists creek-crossed acreage should hear "navigable" as a surveyor's and lawyer's
question with a statutory measuring stick, not as a canoeing observation.

**Modern positioning.** Contemporary Texas surveys tie boundaries to the **Texas Coordinate
Systems** — the state plane grid the statute itself references — using GPS-derived positions, which
is why new field notes carry coordinate values alongside the traditional calls. Coordinates
supplement, not replace, the monument-and-call fabric: the hierarchy of calls still runs through
monuments first.

**Survey products a licence holder will meet.** The everyday residential product is the boundary
survey with improvements — commonly prepared to title-industry specifications so the title company
can delete or limit its survey exception — showing boundary lines, found and set monuments,
improvements, visible utilities, platted easements and any encroachments. Rural and commercial work
uses fuller boundary retracement surveys; construction adds staking; lenders sometimes accept a
prior survey with an affidavit that nothing has changed, a cost-saving practice whose risk (the
affidavit can be wrong) belongs in the buyer's decision, not buried by the agent's enthusiasm.
Elevation and floodplain questions produce elevation certificates — a distinct product for a
distinct risk.

**Reading a survey against a transaction: a five-point pass.** (1) Does the survey's description
match the contract's and the commitment's? (2) Do improvements sit within boundary lines and inside
building setback lines shown on the plat? (3) Are there fences off the line, drives crossing
boundaries, structures over easements — the encroachment findings of Module 03? (4) Are platted and
record easements plotted, and does anything visible on the ground (that worn path, that overhead
line) lack a record explanation? (5) Does the acreage or lot dimension materially differ from what
the parties assumed — and if the price was per-acre, does the contract say what happens when the
survey says otherwise? Every "no" or "yes, and it's odd" answer routes to surveyor, title company or
counsel — with the licence holder's contribution being that the question got asked before closing
rather than after.

**When the survey and the record disagree.** The most instructive moments in this unit are the
mismatches, and a licence holder should know the standard families. *Fence-line discrepancies* —
the fence stands off the surveyed line — are the everyday case: fences follow convenience and old
agreements, not calls, and a fence long acquiesced in raises exactly the adverse-possession and
boundary-agreement questions Module 07's limitation statutes govern; the agent's job is to disclose
the discrepancy, never to declare who owns the strip. *Overlaps and gaps (gores)* — two record
descriptions claim the same ground, or leave a sliver claimed by neither — are title defects that
only surveyors and title companies can resolve, typically tracing both chains back toward the
original survey fabric this module began with. *Acreage surprises* — the survey finds materially
more or less land than the parties priced — are contract problems: whether the price adjusts is
answered by what the contract says, which is why per-acre deals should say it expressly and in
advance. *Unexplained occupation* — a neighbour's shed, drive or garden inside the surveyed
boundary — is Module 03's encroachment doctrine arriving with a drawing attached, and it runs both
directions: what the client occupies beyond the line is someone else's problem with the client's
name on it. In every family the pattern is identical: the survey does not *create* the problem, it
*reveals* one that closing would otherwise have buried, which is why the survey is ordered early,
read against the commitment, and never waved through as a formality.

### Statute reading exercise: the plat statute's closed loophole

Re-read the summary of Local Government Code § 232.001. **(1)** A landowner outside city limits
proposes to sell the back 20 acres of a 60-acre tract "by metes and bounds, no plat needed." What
does § 232.001(a-1) say about that method? **(2)** What three content requirements from
§ 232.001(b) tie a new plat back to the original Texas land fabric and make it usable by later
surveyors? **(3)** Where is the plat filed? *(Answers: (1) a division by metes-and-bounds
description in a deed, contract for deed, or executory contract is still a division — the plat
requirement applies; (2) description of the subdivision by metes and bounds, location with respect
to an original survey corner, and stated dimensions of the subdivision and its parts; (3) with the
county clerk of the county where the tract lies.)*

### Applied exercise: three descriptions, three verdicts

*A licence holder preparing a listing file finds three candidate descriptions: (a) "1414 Pecan
Street, Tyler, Texas, being a nice corner lot approximately 90 × 140"; (b) "Lot 3, Block B, Rose
Garden Addition, City of Tyler, Smith County, Texas, per plat recorded in Cabinet D, Slide 219,
Plat Records, Smith County"; (c) "Being 4.71 acres out of the William Reyes Survey, A-412, Smith
County, described by metes and bounds in Exhibit A" — but Exhibit A's calls, when traced, end 60
feet from the point of beginning.* **Task:** evaluate each description's sufficiency and state the
proper next step. **Model answer:** (a) insufficient — an address plus estimated dimensions
identifies no boundaries; obtain the description from the prior deed or commitment. (b) sufficient —
lot-and-block incorporating a recorded plat; verify it matches the title commitment verbatim.
(c) defective — the description fails to close; this is a surveyor-and-title-company problem to be
resolved before any contract is signed, and under no circumstances does the licence holder adjust a
call to force closure. The recurring rule: agents transmit descriptions; they never manufacture
them.

### Applied exercise: the five-acre carve-out

*A rural seller outside any city wants to sell "five acres off the back" of a 30-acre tract to a
neighbour, describing it in the deed by metes and bounds a friend will "write up from the fence
lines." No plat, no survey.* **Task:** identify every problem in that sentence and state what a
licence holder does with it. **Model answer:** (1) dividing a tract outside a municipality into two
or more parts triggers Local Government Code § 232.001, and the statute expressly says a division
by metes-and-bounds description in a deed is still a division — the "just deed it off" plan walks
into the platting requirement by name; (2) a description "written up from the fence lines" by a
friend is drafting by a non-surveyor from the least reliable evidence this module names — fences
are not boundaries; (3) without a survey there is no point of beginning tied to the original survey
fabric, no closure check, and no assurance the described five acres is the five acres both parties
imagine; (4) the licence holder neither drafts the description nor blesses the plan — the
transaction routes to a surveyor for a survey and plat analysis and to the county's platting
authority, before any contract promises a conveyance the law may not allow to record.

### Module recap

A legal description must let a stranger find the boundaries; addresses and parcel numbers cannot.
Texas's land fabric is original surveys under grantee names and abstract numbers — the federal
township-and-section system never applied — and the vara (33⅓ inches; 36 varas to 100 feet) still
measures the old calls (NRC § 21.041). Metes and bounds runs monument-anchored calls from a point of
beginning around to closure, with monuments outranking courses and distances, and area the weakest
call; lot and block incorporates a recorded plat, which for divisions outside municipalities is
statutory: two or more parts triggers platting, deed-description divisions included, with the plat
tied to an original survey corner and filed with the county clerk (LGC § 232.001). Field notes
carry a statutory anatomy down to State Plane Coordinates (NRC § 21.041), the 30-foot average width
test defines navigable streams (NRC § 21.001), and surveys are read against contract, commitment,
setbacks, easements and encroachments — with every defect routed to the professionals licensed to
fix it.
