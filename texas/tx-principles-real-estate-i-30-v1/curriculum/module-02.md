# Texas Principles of Real Estate I — Module 02

## TREC Topic: Real Property

**Prescribed time: 60 minutes.** Two units under the Commission's scope note *"Distinctions between
personal & real property"* — the boundary on which contracts, financing, taxation and closings all
depend.

**Learning objectives.** Distinguish land, real estate and real property as three layered concepts;
classify an item as real or personal by applying the fixture tests; explain why the classification
matters at sale, at loan and at death; and recognise the special case of fixtures financed under a
security agreement, where Texas commercial law and real property law meet.

## Unit: Land, Real Estate and Real Property

The three terms are often used interchangeably in conversation. In analysis they are layers, each
adding something to the one below.

**Land** is the physical layer: the surface of the earth within the parcel's boundaries, everything
beneath it to the centre of the earth, and the airspace above, together with what nature attached —
soil, minerals in place, native trees and standing water. Texas treats the severable natural
components as capable of separate ownership; mineral interests in particular are routinely severed
from the surface and traded on their own, which is why The Real Estate License Act's exemptions you
met in Module 01 carve mineral transactions out of ordinary brokerage.

**Real estate** is land plus **improvements**: everything humans have permanently attached —
buildings, fences, pavement, wells, irrigation works. The additions are called improvements whether
or not they improve value; the term marks permanence, not quality.

**Real property** is the legal layer: real estate plus the **bundle of rights** ownership carries.
The classical bundle is possession (occupy the property), control (determine its lawful use),
enjoyment (use it without outside interference), exclusion (keep others out), and disposition (sell,
lease, mortgage, give or devise it). Every later module of this course is about some stick in that
bundle: estates measure how much of the bundle you hold and for how long; encumbrances are other
people's claims on particular sticks; leases transfer possession while ownership keeps the rest;
mortgages pledge the bundle as security.

Texas statute defines the trading unit of the licensed business in exactly these layered terms:
Occupations Code § 1101.002(5) defines **"real estate"** for licensing purposes as *any interest in
real property, including a leasehold, located in or outside this state* — but not an interest held
merely as security. Notice what that does: the licensed commodity is not dirt but *interests* — a
fee, a life estate, a leasehold, an easement are all "real estate" in a broker's hands, and Module 03
is a catalogue of those interests.

**Water deserves a Texas-specific note now**, ahead of its unit in Module 03: much of the water a
buyer sees is not part of the parcel at all. Water Code § 11.021 declares the water of the ordinary
flow, underflow and tides of every flowing river, natural stream and lake — and the storm and flood
water of every natural watercourse — to be **the property of the state**. A parcel bounded by a
creek includes land; the flowing water belongs to Texas.

**Why the layers matter in practice.** A contract to sell "the property at 214 Brazos Lane" passes
the land, the improvements and the seller's bundle of rights — but only the sticks the seller holds.
If the minerals were severed by a prior owner, the buyer takes the surface without them; if a
recorded easement lets a neighbour cross, the buyer takes subject to it. The licence holder's job at
the contract stage is to know that "the property" is shorthand for a specific, possibly incomplete,
bundle — and that the documents, not the fence line, say what is actually being sold.

## Unit: Personal Property

Everything that is not real property is **personal property** (also *personalty* or *chattels*):
movable things — furniture, vehicles, equipment — and intangible rights. The classification sounds
academic until you watch it decide who owns the dishwasher.

**Crossing the line: annexation and severance.** Personal property becomes real property by
**annexation** — permanent attachment to land or improvements. A pile of lumber is personalty; the
deck built from it is part of the real estate. The reverse movement is **severance**: standing timber
is realty, felled logs are personalty; growing minerals in place are realty, produced oil in the tank
is personalty. Cultivated annual crops (**emblements**) are treated as personal property of the one
who planted them even before harvest — the classic accommodation to agriculture.

**The fixture tests.** A **fixture** is an item that began as personal property and has become part
of the real estate. When a sale contract is silent, fixtures pass with the deed and personalty stays
with the seller — so the classification allocates real money. The common-law tests, applied by Texas
courts and taught in every serious course, ask three questions:

1. **Method of annexation.** How is it attached? Built-in, plumbed, wired or cemented items lean
   real; items resting by their own weight or plugged into an outlet lean personal. Damage on removal
   is strong evidence of annexation.
2. **Adaptation to the realty.** Was the item fitted to this property — custom cabinetry, a pool
   pump, a garage-door opener sized to this door? Adaptation leans real even where attachment is
   light.
3. **Intention of the parties.** The dominant test: would a reasonable person, viewing the
   attachment and adaptation, understand the item to have been made a permanent part of the property?
   Intention is judged objectively from the circumstances, not from a party's private plan.

**The practice answer is always the same: do not litigate the tests — draft around them.** A
well-made Texas contract lists what stays and what goes. The refrigerator, the mounted televisions,
the smart thermostat, the propane tank: name them, and the fixture tests never get called. Module 06
returns to this when it treats the promulgated contract forms.

**Trade fixtures.** Items a commercial tenant attaches for the tenant's business — shelving, ovens,
salon chairs — are **trade fixtures**: as between landlord and tenant they remain the tenant's
personal property, removable at or before the end of the term, with the tenant responsible for the
damage removal causes. Left behind after the tenancy ends, they typically become the landlord's by
abandonment. The category exists so that renting space does not mean donating your equipment to the
building.

**Financed fixtures: where two bodies of law meet.** A dishwasher bought on credit is personal
property subject to a **security interest** under Article 9 of the Business & Commerce Code; built
into a kitchen it becomes a fixture in real estate that may itself be mortgaged. Which creditor wins?
Business & Commerce Code § 9.334 draws the lines: no Article 9 security interest exists at all in
*ordinary building materials incorporated into an improvement* — the lumber merges into the realty,
full stop — but for true fixtures, a **purchase-money security interest** perfected by a **fixture
filing** made before the goods become fixtures **or within 20 days after** takes priority even over
an earlier recorded real property encumbrancer, and readily removable office machines and replacement
domestic appliances get their own priority track. A licence holder does not practise secured-
transactions law; what the licence holder must recognise is the pattern — a financed water softener,
solar array or HVAC system may carry a lien that does not appear in the deed records' mortgage
chain — and the cure, which is asking, in every transaction, *is anything attached to this house
still being paid for?*

**Manufactured housing** is the classification question at its most consequential — a dwelling that
may be personal property (titled like a vehicle) or may have become real property depending on its
installation and the election made under the applicable titling law. This course flags the category;
the dedicated treatment belongs to later courses in the program. What Principles I asks of you is
only the reflex: *never assume a manufactured home is real estate; verify how it is held.*

### Statute reading exercise: the state's water and the merchant's lumber

Answer from the two provisions quoted or summarised above: **(1)** A rural listing's photographs
feature the creek that crosses the land. What does Water Code § 11.021 say about who owns the flowing
water, and what should the listing therefore not promise? **(2)** A supplier delivers brick to a
homesite on credit. After the brick is laid, can the supplier claim an Article 9 security interest in
the brick under Business & Commerce Code § 9.334? What different body of law — previewed in Module 03
— protects those who furnish materials that merge into an improvement? *(Answers: (1) the ordinary
flow of a natural stream is the property of the state; the listing may describe access and frontage
but not ownership of the watercourse; (2) no — § 9.334(a) says no security interest exists in
ordinary building materials incorporated into an improvement; the supplier's protection is the
mechanic's and materialman's lien of Property Code Chapter 53.)*

### Applied exercise: the contested farmhouse closing

*At the walk-through of a farm sale, the buyer finds the seller has removed: the antique dining
chandelier (replaced with a builder-grade light), the above-ground pool, the propane tank serving the
house (leased from a gas company), the corral panels (freestanding, pinned together), and the pump
from the irrigation well. The contract was silent on all five.* **Task:** classify each item using
the fixture tests and state who likely owns it. **Model answer:** chandelier — a fixture (wired,
adapted, objectively permanent); the seller's swap was improper unless the contract excluded it.
Above-ground pool — typically personalty (rests by weight, designed for disassembly); the seller may
take it. Leased propane tank — the gas company's personal property regardless of attachment; neither
party owns it, and the buyer contracts with the supplier. Freestanding corral panels — personalty by
method of annexation, though a close call on adaptation; seller likely keeps them. Irrigation pump —
plumbed and wired into the well and adapted to the land: a fixture that should have stayed. The
larger lesson: every one of these five arguments would have cost nothing if the contract had listed
them — which is the licence holder's actual job.

### Module recap

Land is the physical parcel with what nature attached; real estate adds human improvements; real
property adds the bundle of rights — and the licensed commodity is *interests* in that bundle
(§ 1101.002(5)), down to leaseholds, while flowing water in natural watercourses belongs to the state
(Water Code § 11.021). Personal property crosses into realty by annexation and back by severance;
fixtures are judged by annexation, adaptation and, above all, objective intention; trade fixtures
stay with the commercial tenant who attached them; financed fixtures ride on § 9.334's fixture-filing
priority, and ordinary building materials merge into the realty beyond any security interest. The
professional habit the whole module teaches: classify early, and write the classification into the
contract so the tests are never needed.
