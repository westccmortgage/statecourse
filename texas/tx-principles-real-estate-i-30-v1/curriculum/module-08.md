# Texas Principles of Real Estate I — Module 08

## TREC Topic: Mortgages and Promissory Notes

**Prescribed time: 230 minutes.** Three units — the deed of trust (90 minutes, the largest single
unit on the PRINS1-1 form), the real estate lien note (85), and the FIRPTA affidavit (55). This is
the course's finance module at the instrument level: the papers a borrower signs, what each one
does, and what happens when the promise they secure is broken.

**Learning objectives.** Explain the two-instrument structure of Texas real estate finance — note
and deed of trust — and the role of each; define the parties using the statutory vocabulary; walk
the Texas non-judicial foreclosure timeline at the level the statute fixes it (first Tuesday,
courthouse, statutory hours) and state what a foreclosure sale does to junior interests; compute a
deficiency and apply the Texas fair-market-value offset; read a promissory note's business terms —
principal, rate, term, amortisation — and perform the payment-allocation arithmetic; recognise the
Texas consumer-protection frame for home loans; and explain what FIRPTA requires of a buyer when
the seller is a foreign person, and how the affidavit machinery allocates that risk at closing.

## Unit: Deed of Trust

**The two-instrument structure.** A Texas real estate loan is built from two documents doing two
jobs. The **promissory note** is the debt: the borrower's promise to repay. The **deed of trust**
is the security: the borrower's grant of a lien on the property to assure the note's payment.
Keep the jobs separate and the whole module organises itself — the note answers *what is owed*,
the deed of trust answers *what happens to the property if it is not paid*.

**The vocabulary, from the statute.** Property Code § 51.0001 supplies the cast, already met in
Module 03 and now put to work: the **security instrument** — *a deed of trust, mortgage, or other
contract lien on an interest in real property*; the **mortgagor** — the grantor of the security
instrument, i.e., the borrower; the **mortgagee** — the grantee, beneficiary, owner or holder of
the security instrument, expressly including a **book entry system** (the registry nominee
arrangement securitisation uses — Module 05's REMIC unit is why that clause exists); the
**mortgage servicer** — the party the borrower is instructed to pay, who may or may not be the
mortgagee; and the **trustee** and **substitute trustee** — the persons authorised to exercise the
power of sale. The deed of trust is thus a *three-party* security instrument: borrower conveys a
lien interest to a trustee, for the benefit of the lender. Its practical genius, and the reason
Texas practice uses it rather than the two-party mortgage, is the **power of sale**: the trustee's
authority, on default, to sell the property at a non-judicial foreclosure auction — no lawsuit
required to reach a sale.

**What the deed of trust contains.** Beyond granting the lien with a power of sale: the borrower's
covenants that protect the collateral (pay taxes — remember § 32.01's annual lien; insure; keep in
repair; the **due-on-sale** covenant making the debt callable on unauthorised transfer);
**escrow** provisions collecting taxes and insurance monthly; default definitions; and the
mechanics of acceleration, notice and sale. It is recorded immediately (Module 07's § 13.001
lesson applied to lenders: the unrecorded lien is void against the later value-paying,
notice-free taker), fixing its priority by first-in-time against everything except the statutory
super-priorities you already know.

**The covenants, walked as risks.** Each borrower covenant answers a specific way collateral
loses value, and reading them that way makes the instrument coherent. *Pay the taxes*: § 32.01's
lien attaches every January 1 senior to the lender (§ 32.05), so unpaid taxes erode the lender's
position from above — hence the covenant, and hence escrow. *Insure*: fire takes the improvements
and leaves the lien holding dirt; the policy names the lender as an insured payee. *Maintain and
commit no waste*: deferred roofs and stripped fixtures (Module 02's doctrine, from the other
direction) shrink the collateral sale by sale. *The due-on-sale covenant*: an unauthorised
transfer hands the collateral to a stranger the lender never underwrote, so the covenant makes
the debt callable on transfer — which is the clause that turns casual "wraparound" and
"subject-to" schemes into acceleration risk, and why creative-finance proposals belong with
lenders and lawyers before anyone signs. *Escrow*: the servicer collects one-twelfth of taxes
and insurance monthly and pays the bills itself — the lender protecting its seniors' and
insurers' deadlines with the borrower's money, and the reason a Texas payment quote is "PITI":
principal, interest, taxes, insurance.

**The distressed menu, compared on consequences.** The alternatives short of foreclosure differ
on the questions a distressed owner actually asks. *Reinstatement or payoff* cures everything and
keeps or clears title — pure arithmetic if the money exists. *Modification and forbearance*
change the note's terms or pause enforcement — lender consent, borrower stays. *Deed in lieu*:
fastest exit, but the lender takes title **with** the junior liens still attached (no foreclosure
occurred, so nothing was extinguished) — which is why lenders title-search first and refuse
encumbered deeds-in-lieu, and why a borrower with a second lien and a judgment usually cannot
use one. *Short sale*: a market sale at market exposure, requiring every lienholder's consent to
release for less than payoff — slower, but the property sells to a real buyer at a real price,
and the borrower's remaining exposure is whatever the release agreements say. The licence
holder's chair in these conversations is defined by Module 01: negotiating a short sale for
compensation is a licensed broker act (§ 1101.002(1)(A)(xii)), the tax and credit consequences
of every option are advisor questions, and the timeline is § 51.002's — the first Tuesday does
not wait for deliberation.

### Applied exercise: the seller who is four payments behind

*A homeowner calls: four payments behind, servicer's certified letter arrived yesterday, "a guy
offered to take over my payments if I just deed him the house and move out."* **Task:** identify
what the letter starts, what the "guy's" proposal actually is with its risks to the seller, and
the lawful paths a licence holder may present. **Model answer:** the letter is the § 51.002(d)
cure notice — at least 20 days to cure before notice of sale can even be given, so a calendar
now exists and every option is time-boxed. The proposal is a subject-to transfer: the deed
conveys title, but the note stays the seller's — the debt follows the person, not the house —
so the seller would surrender the asset while keeping the liability, with the due-on-sale
covenant available to accelerate on top; it is also the classic shape of equity-skimming schemes
aimed at distressed owners. Lawful paths to present factually: contact the servicer immediately
(reinstatement, modification, forbearance); list the property now if there is equity — four
payments of arrears against a marketable home is a solvable listing problem inside the
timeline; short sale with lender consent if underwater; and counsel, today, in every branch.
The agent presents the menu, states the deadlines, and recommends professionals — not the deal
in the caller's driveway.

**Default and foreclosure: the statutory skeleton.** On default the holder may **accelerate** —
declare the whole balance due — after the notices Texas law and the instrument require, and direct
the trustee to sell. Property Code § 51.002, read for this course, fixes the sale's
unmistakable Texas signature: a sale under a power of sale in a deed of trust must be a **public
auction held between 10 a.m. and 4 p.m. on the first Tuesday of a month**, at the **county
courthouse** of the county where the land is located (with the section's stated accommodations),
after the notice the section prescribes — including written notice by certified mail to each
debtor obligated to pay the debt, with the statute's minimum notice periods for posting, filing
and serving. "First Tuesday at the courthouse" is not folklore; it is § 51.002, and it is why
Texas foreclosure moves in weeks rather than the years of judicial-foreclosure states. (Judicial
foreclosure — suing to judgment and selling under court order — remains available, and certain
lien types require their own processes; the non-judicial power of sale is the residential
workhorse.)

**The timeline, walked with the statute's own numbers.** For a lien on the debtor's *residence*,
§ 51.002 builds a two-stage clock the licence holder should be able to narrate exactly. *Stage
one — cure*: § 51.002(d) requires the mortgage servicer, notwithstanding any agreement to the
contrary, to serve the defaulting debtor written notice by certified mail stating the default and
giving **at least 20 days to cure** before any notice of sale may even be given. *Stage two —
sale notice*: § 51.002(b) then requires **at least 21 days'** notice of sale, given three ways at
once — posted at the courthouse door of each county where the property lies, filed with the
county clerk of each such county, and served by certified mail on each debtor obligated on the
debt according to the servicer's records. The statute is meticulous about its own arithmetic: the
day the cure notice is given counts inside the 20 days, the day of sale is excluded from the 21
(§ 51.002(g)), and service by certified mail is *complete on deposit* in the mail (§ 51.002(e)) —
mailing, not receipt, starts the clock. The sale itself must begin at the stated time or within
**three hours** after it (§ 51.002(c)); if the first Tuesday falls on January 1 or July 4, the
sale moves to the first Wednesday (§ 51.002(a-1)); weather that closes the courthouse extends
posting and filing by up to 48 hours after reopening (§ 51.002(b-1)); the commissioners court may
designate a public sale area other than the courthouse itself, recorded in the real property
records (§ 51.002(h)); and the clerk's filed notices are public — kept available for
examination, posted with date, time and location on the county's website without charge
(§ 51.002(f), (f-1)). Add the stages and Texas's speed is explained precisely: roughly six weeks
of statutory notice stands between a residential default and a courthouse sale, against the
years a judicial-foreclosure state's docket can consume.

**What the sale does.** The purchaser at a valid foreclosure sale takes the title the deed of
trust encumbered, through a **trustee's deed** — and the sale **extinguishes interests junior**
to the foreclosed lien while leaving senior interests in place. The waterfall logic of Module 03
becomes cash: sale proceeds pay the costs of sale, then the foreclosed debt, then junior
lienholders in priority order, any surplus to the borrower — and the buyer at the courthouse
steps owns subject to whatever outranked the foreclosing lien (that tax lien, above all). Recall
too, from Module 01, that the trustee conducting this sale is exempt from real estate licensing
(§ 1101.005) — the auction is a legal proceeding, not a brokerage listing.

**The waterfall, worked in dollars.** *A second-lien holder forecloses on a property that sells
at auction for $150,000. The property carries: unpaid current-year property taxes; a first-lien
deed of trust with a $90,000 balance; the foreclosing second lien, owed $85,000; and a $30,000
abstract-of-judgment lien recorded after the second.* The purchaser at the sale takes title
**subject to** the first lien and the tax lien — they are senior, and the sale does not touch
them (a fact the bidder priced in). From the $150,000 proceeds: costs of sale first; then the
foreclosing second lien's $85,000; then junior claims in order — the judgment lien's $30,000;
any surplus to the borrower. The junior judgment lien is *extinguished* against the land the
moment the hammer falls — its holder's recourse is the proceeds line it just occupied, which is
the answer to this module's statute exercise question about why juniors watch seniors' sales.
Run the same sale from the *first* lien's side and every consequence flips: the second lien and
the judgment are both wiped, the sale price satisfies the first, and surpluses flow down the
same ladder. Priority, in foreclosure, is simply the order of the queue at the pay window — and
everything this course taught about establishing priority (recording order, § 32.05's tax
super-priority) was really about where each creditor stands in this queue.

**The trustee's seat.** The third party of the three-party instrument deserves its own paragraph.
The trustee is named in the deed of trust at origination and does nothing — often for decades —
until the beneficiary directs a sale; § 51.0001 expressly contemplates the **substitute
trustee**, *appointed by the current mortgagee or mortgage servicer under the security
instrument to exercise the power of sale*, which is how sales actually run: the lender's
foreclosure counsel is routinely substituted in when default ripens. The office is ministerial
and procedural — conduct the § 51.002 sale as the statute and instrument prescribe, execute the
trustee's deed, distribute proceeds down the waterfall — and its holder, recall from Module 01,
needs no broker licence for the auction (§ 1101.005). For the licence holder the vocabulary
matters at intake: "there's a substitute trustee's deed in the chain" is the recorded footprint
of a completed foreclosure, announcing exactly which documents (notices, appointment,
trustee's deed) the title company will verify.

**The courthouse steps, from the bidder's side.** Foreclosure sales are also a market, and a
licence holder will meet buyers who frequent it. What such a buyer must price: seniors survive
(the tax lien and any senior deed of trust ride through, as the waterfall example showed);
there are no seller disclosures — § 5.008(e) expressly excepts foreclosure-related transfers —
no inspections before sale, no title commitment in advance unless the bidder built one, cash on
the statute's schedule, and occupants whose removal is a legal process, not a moving crew. The
professional posture: an agent may factually explain the process and the risks, and should
route financing-of-purchase, eviction and title questions to the professionals who own them;
"courthouse-steps bargains" carry every risk this module has named, priced in.

**Deficiency, and the Texas offset.** When the sale brings less than the debt, the difference is
the **deficiency**, and Property Code § 51.003 governs the creditor's pursuit of it: the action
must be brought **within two years** of the foreclosure sale, and — the borrower-protective
heart of the section — any person sued may have the court determine the property's **fair market
value as of the sale date**; if that value exceeds the sale price, the deficiency is **offset**
by the excess. The statute even lists the competent evidence (expert opinion, comparable sales,
holding costs, discounts). The design forbids the double squeeze — buying the collateral cheap
at a thin auction *and* collecting a deficiency measured by that same thin price. Money received
from private mortgage guaranty insurance is credited against the borrower's obligation as the
section provides.

**Reading § 51.003 as a borrower's shield.** The section's design details reward one more pass.
The two-year clock runs from the foreclosure sale, and it governs the deficiency *action* — the
lender who waits sleeps on the claim. The offset procedure is elective: *any person against whom
the recovery is sought* may **request** the fair-market-value determination, and the statute
then instructs the factfinder on competent evidence — expert opinion testimony, comparable
sales, anticipated marketing time and holding costs, costs of sale, and the necessity and
amount of any discount for a future-cash-flow purchase — a list that lets the borrower prove
what the courthouse-steps price ignored. If no determination is requested, the sale price
controls. And the guaranty-insurance clause closes a subtler gap: money the lender receives
from private mortgage guaranty insurance is credited against the borrower's obligation —
the borrower is not squeezed for a loss the lender already insured away.

### Statute reading exercise: pricing the deficiency fight

From § 51.003 as taught: **(1)** A lender's foreclosure nets $200,000 against a $260,000 debt;
the borrower proves fair market value at sale was $255,000, and the lender collected $20,000 of
mortgage guaranty insurance. Compute the recoverable deficiency. **(2)** The lender sues 30
months after the sale — result? **(3)** The borrower's lawyer offers testimony on comparable
sales and the cost of holding the property through a normal marketing period — why does the
statute welcome both? *(Answers: (1) start at $260,000 − $200,000 = $60,000; the elected offset
reduces it by the $55,000 excess of value over price to $5,000; the $20,000 insurance credit
extinguishes the remainder — recoverable deficiency $0; (2) barred — the action must be brought
within two years of the sale; (3) both are on § 51.003's own list of competent evidence for
fair market value, which exists precisely so the auction price is not the last word.)*

**Alternatives short of foreclosure** complete the practical picture: reinstatement and payoff;
loan modification and forbearance; the **deed in lieu of foreclosure** (a negotiated conveyance
to the lender — faster and quieter, but it does not by itself wipe junior liens, which is why
lenders title-search before accepting one); and the **short sale**, a market sale for less than
the payoff with the lienholder's consent. Module 01 planted the licensing hook that belongs
here: advising an owner on the negotiation or completion of a **short sale** is itself a broker
act under Occupations Code § 1101.002(1)(A)(xii) — the Legislature reached the distressed-market
consultant by name.

**Mortgage theory, and why Texas's instrument fits its law.** The national vocabulary
distinguishes **title-theory** states (the security instrument passes a form of title to the
lender or trustee until payment) from **lien-theory** states (the borrower keeps title; the
lender holds only a lien). Texas practice runs on the deed of trust precisely because the
instrument pairs naturally with a power of sale: the borrower conveys the lien interest with the
sale power to the trustee at origination, so on default the machinery is already in place — no
court needed to create the remedy, only § 51.002's notices to exercise it. What the theory
question changes day to day is almost nothing — the borrower occupies, enjoys and (subject to
the due-on-sale covenant) transfers the property either way — but the vocabulary appears in
national coursework and exams, and a Texas licence holder should be able to say accurately: the
operative Texas fact is not a theory label but an instrument, the deed of trust, and a statute,
§ 51.002, that together make non-judicial sale the default residential remedy.

### Applied exercise: the timeline against the calendar

*A borrower in default on her residence receives the servicer's § 51.002(d) cure letter on
March 3. She does not cure. The servicer wants the earliest lawful sale date.* **Task:** walk the
statute's arithmetic to the earliest first Tuesday, stating each step's authority. **Model
answer:** the 20-day cure period runs from March 3 (that whole day counts, § 51.002(d)),
expiring end of March 22; notice of sale may be given no earlier than March 23 (the day notice
of sale is given is excluded from the cure computation). From a March 23 posting, filing and
mailing (service complete on deposit, § 51.002(e)), the 21-day sale-notice period counts
March 23 as day one (§ 51.002(g)) and excludes the sale day itself — the 21 days
are complete at the end of April 12, so no sale may be held before April 13. The first Tuesday of April will have passed or fallen inside the notice period
(April's first Tuesday can come as early as April 1), so the earliest lawful sale is the
**first Tuesday of May**, between 10 a.m. and 4 p.m., in the designated courthouse-area
location — beginning at the noticed time or within three hours after (§ 51.002(c)). The
exercise's real lesson: every date in a foreclosure file is computed from the statute, and a
one-day error resets the machine a full month.

### Statute reading exercise: the first-Tuesday machine

From Property Code §§ 51.002–51.003 as set out above: **(1)** Name the day, hours and place the
statute fixes for a Texas power-of-sale foreclosure. **(2)** A house sells at foreclosure for
$180,000 against a $240,000 debt; the borrower proves the fair market value at sale was
$230,000. What deficiency may the lender recover, and by when must it sue? **(3)** Why does a
junior lienholder care about receiving notice of a senior's sale? *(Answers: (1) first Tuesday
of the month, between 10 a.m. and 4 p.m., at the county courthouse of the county where the land
lies; (2) $10,000 — the deficiency is offset by the $50,000 excess of fair market value over
sale price ($240,000 − $230,000 = $10,000), and suit must be brought within two years of the

its position around the sale or look to any surplus proceeds.)*

## Unit: Real Estate Lien Note

**The note is the debt.** A **promissory note** — Texas practice says *real estate lien note*
when it is secured by realty — is the borrower's unconditional written promise to pay a sum
certain, with interest, on stated terms. Its business anatomy is the licence holder's daily
vocabulary: the **principal**; the **interest rate** (fixed for the term, or adjustable by index
plus margin with caps); the **term** and **maturity date**; the payment schedule; **late
charges**; the **acceleration clause** that makes the note's whole balance callable on default
(the trigger the deed of trust's machinery waits for); and **prepayment** terms.

**Amortisation, worked honestly.** A fully **amortising** note retires principal over the term
through level payments, each split between interest accrued and principal reduction. *Worked
example — one month of a loan's life:* a $200,000 note at 6% annual interest, 30-year term,
carries a level monthly payment of about $1,199.10. Month one: interest = $200,000 × 6% ÷ 12 =
$1,000.00; principal = $1,199.10 − $1,000.00 = $199.10; new balance $199,800.90. Month two:
interest = $199,800.90 × 0.5% = $999.00; principal $200.10. The pattern — early payments mostly
interest, the mix reversing over decades — explains equity's slow early growth, and reciting it
accurately is the difference between an agent who informs and one who guesses. The variations:
the **interest-only** note (payments cover interest; principal waits); the **balloon** note
(amortised as if long, due early — a large final payment the borrower must plan to refinance or
pay); and **negative amortisation**, where scheduled payments fall short of accrued interest
and the balance *grows* — a structure Texas polices in the consumer context, as the next
paragraph shows.

**Twelve months of the schedule, to see the drift.** Continue the $200,000/6% example across year
one, and watch the columns move (payment $1,199.10 throughout): month one splits $1,000.00
interest / $199.10 principal; month two $999.00 / $200.10; month three $998.00 / $201.10 — each
month's principal share grows by almost exactly one dollar at this rate and stage, because each
month's interest is half a percent of a balance about $200 smaller. By month twelve the split is
near $988.98 / $210.12, the year's principal reduction totals roughly $2,456, and the balance
stands near $197,544 — against $14,389 paid. Recite those two numbers together and the unit's
central honesty is complete: in year one of a 30-year 6% loan, about 17 cents of each dollar
paid builds equity; the rest is the price of the money. The drift accelerates every year — by
the loan's late years the proportions have fully reversed — and the whole trajectory was fixed
the day the note was signed, which is what "fully amortising, level payment" means.

**The arithmetic, extended three ways.** *Total interest over life*: the $200,000 note's level
payment of $1,199.10 × 360 months returns $431,676 in total — $231,676 of it interest, more than
the principal itself, which is the honest cost of long amortisation and the number that makes
extra-principal payments interesting. *The extra-payment effect, mechanically*: any amount paid
above the level payment reduces principal immediately, and because every later month's interest
is computed on the reduced balance (the month-two calculation above, repeated), a modest
recurring extra payment shortens the term by years — the direction of the effect is arithmetic
certainty, its size depends on rate and timing, and an agent states the mechanism while leaving
projections to lenders' amortisation schedules. *Rate sensitivity*: at 7% instead of 6%, the
same $200,000/30-year note carries a payment near $1,330.60 — about $131 more each month, and
month one's interest line rises from $1,000.00 to $1,166.67 ($200,000 × 7% ÷ 12). A licence
holder who can reproduce these three computations on a legal pad — total cost, extra-payment
mechanism, rate sensitivity — can keep a client conversation factual while every opinion
question ("should I pay points? which loan?") goes where it belongs, to the lender and the
client's own advisors.

### Applied exercise: reading a note's business terms

*A buyer shows you a loan estimate she does not understand: $250,000 principal, 6.0% fixed,
30-year term, level monthly payment $1,498.88, with a clause: "Borrower may prepay in whole or
in part at any time without penalty. Late charge: 5% of any payment more than 15 days late. On
default, Lender may accelerate the maturity of this Note."* **Task:** explain each term in
plain language, compute month one's interest/principal split, and identify which sentence
connects this note to the deed of trust's machinery. **Model answer:** principal is the sum
borrowed; the fixed rate holds for the term; month one's interest is $250,000 × 6% ÷ 12 =
$1,250.00, so principal reduction is $1,498.88 − $1,250.00 = $248.88 and the balance falls to
$249,751.12; the prepayment clause means extra payments carry no penalty; the late charge
prices tardiness; and the acceleration sentence is the bridge — on default the lender may call
the entire balance due, which is the precondition to directing the trustee toward the § 51.002
sale process this module walked. What the agent does not do with this document: recommend
taking or refusing the loan, predict rates, or restate the lender's numbers as her own.

**The Texas home-loan frame.** Finance Code Chapter 343, read for this course, defines the
consumer field: a **home loan** is a loan to one or more individuals for personal, family or
household purposes, secured wholly or partly by a manufactured home or by real property improved
by a dwelling for four or fewer families, used or to be used as the borrower's **principal
residence** (§ 343.001) — the same one-to-four family residential territory the promulgated
contract forms serve. Within that field, the chapter's high-cost provisions include a pointed
ban: a **high-cost home loan may not provide for a payment schedule with regular periodic
payments that cause the principal balance to increase** — scheduled negative amortisation —
except where a borrower seeks a temporary forbearance, bridge loan or restructure (§ 343.203).
The unit's professional boundary follows from the subject: loan *origination* is its own
licensed occupation (Module 01's map), rate quotes belong to lenders, and the agent's competence
is fluency — reading the note's terms accurately, running the arithmetic honestly, and steering
clients to lenders early — not advice about which loan to take.

**What the records show, and do not.** One structural fact keeps buyers and title examiners
oriented: the **deed of trust is recorded; the note is not.** The county records show the lien —
its existence, date, priority — but the debt's live terms and balance are private between the
parties, which is why payoffs at closing run on the lender's **payoff statement**, not on
arithmetic from the recorded instrument, and why a "released" lien means a recorded release,
never an assumption that a stale deed of trust "must be paid off by now." The chain-reading
consequence from Module 07 applies with force: an old deed of trust with no release of record
is a live title requirement, however ancient the loan.

**Why § 343.001's definition is the frame.** Definitions allocate protections, and the home-loan
definition draws the consumer perimeter with three simultaneous tests — individual borrowers
for personal, family or household purposes; security wholly or partly in a manufactured home or
one-to-four family improved realty; the borrower's principal residence. An investor's rental
purchase fails the purpose and residence tests and lives outside the chapter; a family's
homestead purchase sits inside it, where the high-cost provisions — § 343.203's scheduled
negative-amortisation ban among them — patrol the loan structures history showed could strip
equity from the households least able to lose it. A licence holder does not administer any of
this; a licence holder who knows the perimeter exists can hear a lender conversation going
somewhere the statute polices and say the one correct sentence: "that's a question for your
lender and your lawyer, and get it in writing."

**Adjustable rates, mechanically.** The fixed-rate note above has one moving part; the
adjustable-rate note has four, and reading them accurately is the same skill. The **index** is a
published market rate the note names — the moving input. The **margin** is the fixed add-on:
index plus margin equals the new rate at each adjustment. The **adjustment schedule** states
when: an initial fixed period, then periodic resets. The **caps** bound the movement — per
adjustment, and over the loan's life — and the floor bounds it downward. The reading discipline:
every one of these is *printed in the note*; none is folklore; and the honest client sentence is
always conditional — "if the index stands at X at your first reset, your rate becomes X plus
your margin, subject to your caps" — with the lender's written disclosures, not the agent's
memory, supplying the numbers.

### Statute reading exercise: inside or outside the home-loan frame?

From § 343.001 and § 343.203 as taught: **(1)** An investor borrows against a rental fourplex
she has never lived in — is this a Chapter 343 home loan? **(2)** A family finances the
manufactured home they live in — inside or outside? **(3)** A lender proposes a high-cost home
loan whose scheduled payments are less than accruing interest "to keep payments affordable" —
what does § 343.203 say, and what exceptions does the read text allow? *(Answers: (1) no — the
purpose is not personal/family/household and the property is not her principal residence, so
two of the definition's tests fail; (2) inside — the definition expressly includes security in
a manufactured home used as the principal residence; (3) a high-cost home loan may not provide
a payment schedule with regular periodic payments that cause the principal balance to
increase — scheduled negative amortisation is banned — except where the borrower seeks a
temporary forbearance, bridge loan or restructure.)*

**Secondary market connection, closed loop.** The note is what the secondary market buys.
Module 05 traced the pooling machinery (REMICs, 26 U.S.C. § 860A); § 51.0001's book-entry
mortgagee and mortgage-servicer definitions are the recording system's accommodation of it; the
borrower's experience of all this is one line — *"your loan has been sold; make payments to the
new servicer"* — and the licence holder's use of it is to explain, correctly and calmly, that a
sold loan changes the payee, not the terms.

**One transaction, three markets — the module in a single loan.** Follow one $250,000 purchase
through everything taught. In the **primary market**, the buyer signs the note (the debt, on the
terms the applied exercise read) and the deed of trust (the lien, with its covenants and power
of sale) at a Module 09 closing; the deed of trust records the same day, fixing priority. In the
**servicing layer**, a § 51.0001 mortgage servicer collects PITI, runs the escrow account, and
is the party whose records identify the debtors § 51.002 notices must reach. In the **secondary
market**, the note is sold and pooled — Module 05's REMIC, § 860A's pass-through — while the
recorded lien names a book-entry mortgagee; the borrower's terms never move. If all goes well,
the loop closes twenty-odd years later with a release of lien. If it does not, the module's
machinery engages in order: cure notice (20 days), notice of sale (21), first Tuesday, trustee's
deed, waterfall, and — if the price fell short — § 51.003's offset arithmetic. Every unit of
this module is one station of that single circuit, which is why the vocabulary must be owned as
a system rather than memorised as a list.

**Personal liability, precisely placed.** A recurring client confusion deserves surgical
treatment: *the note binds people; the deed of trust binds property.* Whoever signs the note
owes the debt — including a co-signer who never appears on the deed — and keeps owing whatever
lawful deficiency survives a sale. Whoever signs the deed of trust subjects that property
interest to the lien — including an owner who signed the deed of trust but not the note,
pledging property without promising payment. The four combinations (both documents, note only,
deed of trust only, neither) produce four different exposures, and family transactions — the
parent "helping with the loan," the spouse "just signing at closing" — walk through all four
unaware. The licence holder's line: which paper each person signs decides what each person
risks, and that is a conversation with the lender and a lawyer *before* the signing table.

## Unit: FIRPTA Affidavit

**The problem the statute solves.** A foreign seller of U.S. real estate may owe U.S. tax on the
gain — and may be beyond the reach of collection once the proceeds leave the country. The Foreign
Investment in Real Property Tax Act answers by drafting the *buyer* as collection agent.

**The rule, from the text.** Under 26 U.S.C. § 1445(a), read for this course, on any disposition
of a *United States real property interest* by a **foreign person**, the **transferee** — the
buyer — must **deduct and withhold a tax equal to 15 percent of the amount realized** on the
disposition (in the section's current text as held). Withholding is the buyer's legal duty: a
buyer who fails to withhold when required is personally exposed for the amount that should have
been withheld.

**The affidavit, and why closings run on it.** The statute's own exemptions (§ 1445(b)) supply
the everyday escape: no withholding is required where the transferor furnishes the transferee a
**non-foreign affidavit** — a sworn statement, with the seller's taxpayer identification number,
that the seller is not a foreign person. This is the "FIRPTA affidavit" the PRINS1-1 form names
as a unit: a routine closing document in which the ordinary domestic seller swears away the
buyer's withholding duty, and the reason the issue is invisible in most transactions. Where the
seller *is* foreign, the machinery engages: the closing withholds from the seller's proceeds and
remits to the IRS, with the statute's further exemptions and adjustment procedures — reduced
amounts for certain lower-priced residence transactions and IRS-certified reductions among
them — applied by the professionals.

**The exemption catalogue, from the statute's own list.** Section 1445(b) organises the escapes,
and three beyond the everyday affidavit are worth naming from the read text. The **residence
exemption** (§ 1445(b)(5)): no withholding where the transferee acquires the property *for use
by him as a residence* and the **amount realized does not exceed $300,000** — the provision that
keeps modest owner-occupant purchases from foreign sellers out of the machinery entirely, and a
figure to verify against the current statute, never recited from memory in a live transaction.
The **qualifying statement** (§ 1445(b)(4)): the IRS itself certifies that the transferor's tax
has been arranged or excused and any withholding liability is satisfied or secured — the formal
route for negotiated reductions. And the **domestic-corporation affidavit** (§ 1445(b)(3)): a
non-publicly-traded domestic corporation may swear its shares are not United States real
property interests, the statute's acknowledgment that FIRPTA reaches entity interests as well as
deeds — which is exactly why the exercise below routes the foreign-member LLC to tax counsel
rather than to a guess.

**The computation, once and carefully.** The duty runs on the **amount realized** — in the
ordinary sale, the gross price — not on the seller's gain. *A foreign seller closes a Texas
rental at $400,000 with a $310,000 payoff and $28,000 of costs.* The withholding is 15% ×
$400,000 = **$60,000** — computed before the payoff and costs, which is why thin-equity foreign
sales can find the withholding exceeding the seller's net proceeds, and why the § 1445(b)(4)
adjustment route exists. At the table this is the closer's arithmetic; the licence holder's
value was upstream, in flagging the facts early enough for the seller's advisors to seek any
reduction before the closing date arrived with $60,000 frozen in escrow.

**FIRPTA at intake, as a habit.** Because the withholding lands at closing but the facts surface
at intake, build the early questions into routine: on the listing side, confirm how the seller
holds title and whether any owner's tax status needs the seller's advisor's attention *before*
the property is under contract; on the buying side, remember whose duty § 1445(a) creates — the
buyer's — so a buyer's agent who hears "the seller lives abroad" raises the issue the same day.
The affidavit is the ordinary ending; the professional failure mode is the extraordinary case
discovered with a week to closing, proceeds already promised elsewhere, and a 15-percent hold
nobody budgeted.

**The licence holder's role, bounded.** This is the second of the two federal tax rules this
course teaches (Module 05 drew the line), and it is taught because the *duty* sits on the
transaction itself. The agent's competence: know the issue exists on every sale; expect the
title company to obtain the affidavit or administer withholding; flag early any facts suggesting
foreign-seller status (and any buyer-side anxiety about it) to the closer and the parties'
advisors; and give no opinions on residency, treaty status, or amounts — tax advice, every word
of it. The exercise below rehearses exactly that posture.

**Late charges and grace, read the same way.** The companion clause prices tardiness: a stated
percentage of the overdue payment after a stated grace period. Two reading notes: the grace
period defers the *charge*, not the *due date* — interest accrual and credit reporting follow
their own rules — and habitual reliance on grace is how defaults begin, a pattern every
servicer's records make visible long before a cure letter is drafted.

**Prepayment, the quiet term worth reading first.** Because Texas buyers routinely plan to
refinance or sell before maturity, the note's prepayment clause is the business term most often
consulted after closing. Read for three things: whether prepayment is permitted in whole and in
part; whether any penalty applies, on what schedule, and to which prepayments; and how partial
prepayments are applied — to principal immediately, or held against future instalments. The
applied exercise's clause ("in whole or in part at any time without penalty") is the borrower's
best case; anything narrower changes the arithmetic of every "should we pay extra?"
conversation, which is why the accurate agent answer starts with "let's see what your note
says" rather than a general rule.

**Vocabulary drift the licence holder must survive.** Texas conversation uses "mortgage" for
everything — the loan, the lien, the payment, the lender — and precision costs nothing once the
statutory map is owned. When a client says *"my mortgage,"* sort silently: the *note* (what they
owe), the *deed of trust* (what encumbers the house), the *servicer* (whom they pay), the
*mortgagee* (who holds the lien — possibly a book-entry system fronting a pool of investors).
When a listing agent says a property "has two mortgages," the working translation is two
recorded deeds of trust with two priorities and two payoff statements to order. And when anyone
says a foreclosure "wiped out the loan," apply this module's precision: the *sale* extinguished
junior *liens* and produced proceeds; whether any *debt* survives is § 51.003's question, with
its two-year clock and its offset. Careers in this field are built from exactly this habit —
hearing loose words and thinking in instruments.

### Applied exercise: three closings, one statute

*Closing one: sellers are a retired couple, lifelong Texans. Closing two: seller is an investor
who mentions his residency is "complicated — mostly Monterrey these days." Closing three: seller
is an LLC with foreign members, selling a rental house.* **Task:** for each, state what § 1445
requires, who acts, and what the licence holder says and does not say. **Model answer:** (1) The
sellers execute the non-foreign affidavit at closing; no withholding; the agent's only job is
knowing the document belongs in the stack. (2) A flag: "complicated" residency is precisely what
the buyer's withholding duty turns on; the agent alerts the title company early and tells the
seller his own tax advisor should address status — the agent offers no opinion. (3) Entity
status under FIRPTA is a tax-counsel question (an entity's classification, not its members'
passports, drives the analysis); the agent routes it to the closer and the entity's advisors
now, because a 15-percent withholding surprise discovered at the table kills closings. In all
three: the buyer's duty, the affidavit's power, and the agent's silence on tax conclusions.

**One habit to start today.** Find a recent amortisation schedule — your own loan's, or one
generated from any lender's public calculator — and verify three lines of it by hand with this
unit's method: balance × periodic rate = interest; payment − interest = principal; balance −
principal = new balance. Ten minutes of checking will make the mechanics permanently yours, and
permanently checkable — which, in a profession that talks about other people's largest debts
every day, is the entire point.

**Self-check before the quiz.** Without looking back: What two instruments build a Texas home

are Tuesday-morning realities in Texas practice.

### Module recap

Texas finances real estate on two instruments: the note (the debt) and the deed of trust (the
lien), cast with § 51.0001's vocabulary — mortgagor, mortgagee, servicer, trustee — and armed
with a power of sale. Foreclosure under that power is § 51.002's machine: notice as prescribed,
then public auction, first Tuesday, 10 to 4, county courthouse; the trustee's deed passes title,
juniors are extinguished, seniors survive, and § 51.003 gives the deficiency suit two years and
the borrower a fair-market-value offset. The note's anatomy — principal, rate, term,
amortisation, acceleration, balloons — is arithmetic the agent must perform honestly, inside
Finance Code Chapter 343's consumer frame (home loan defined at § 343.001; scheduled negative
amortisation banned for high-cost loans at § 343.203), with origination left to its own licensed
profession. And FIRPTA (26 U.S.C. § 1445) makes the buyer withhold 15 percent of the amount
realized when a foreign person sells — unless the seller's non-foreign affidavit, the closing
stack's quiet fixture, turns the duty off.
