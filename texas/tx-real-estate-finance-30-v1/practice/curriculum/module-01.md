# Texas Real Estate Finance: calculations, loan comparisons, and contract protection

This collection supplies four lessons and 24 original practice questions. It is free practice on selected financing topics, not a complete 30-hour qualifying course or licensing credit. All dollar amounts and rates are hypothetical. Source review: September 6, 2026. No example promises loan approval or quotes a current market rate.

Use a calculator and write the equation before choosing an answer. Keep a [sample Loan Estimate](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/loan-estimate/), [Closing Disclosure](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/closing-disclosure/) and [TREC Third Party Financing Addendum 40-11](https://www.trec.texas.gov/sites/default/files/pdf-forms/40-11.pdf) available for the document questions.


## Loan math, value, and qualifying income

Loan-to-value compares the loan with the property value used for the calculation. If a $320,000 mortgage is divided by a $400,000 appraised value, the ratio is 0.80 or 80%. The unfinanced portion of that value is 20%. In a purchase, a lender may apply program rules about price and appraised value; state the denominator rather than silently assuming it. The [CFPB LTV explanation](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-loan-to-value-ratio-and-how-does-it-relate-to-my-costs-en-121/) explains how the ratio can affect terms and mortgage insurance.

If both the purchase price and appraised value are $500,000, a $50,000 down payment leaves a $450,000 loan, or 90% LTV. Increasing the down payment to $100,000 leaves a $400,000 loan, or 80% LTV, assuming no financed costs. The extra cash changes leverage. It does not guarantee a particular rate or eliminate every program-specific charge.

A discount point equals 1% of loan principal. Therefore 1.25 points on $360,000 is $360,000 × 0.0125 = $4,500. Do not calculate points on the sales price when the charge is quoted against the loan amount. A percentage-point change in the interest rate is a different concept. Paying one discount point does not guarantee a one-percentage-point rate reduction.

The basic debt-to-income calculation divides monthly debt payments by gross monthly income. With $3,000 monthly debt and $8,000 gross income, DTI is 37.5%. If gross income is $7,500 and debt $2,625, it is 35%, even if take-home pay is lower. Net income still matters for the buyer’s living budget. It is simply not the denominator in this stated ratio.

The [CFPB DTI guidance](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-debt-to-income-ratio-en-1791/) notes that product and lender limits differ. Do not convert a successful arithmetic answer into a universal approval claim. Underwriting also considers documentation, credit, assets, collateral and applicable loan requirements. An agent’s worksheet does not replace the lender’s decision.
