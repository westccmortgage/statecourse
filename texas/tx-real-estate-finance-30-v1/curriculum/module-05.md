# Module 05: Sources of Funds

REF-0 topic T05 suggests 110 minutes. A source of funds influences product availability, underwriting, loan duration, and exit expectations. Institutional labels help organize questions; they do not prove that any one source is best for a particular borrower.

## T05U1 — Commercial Banks

Commercial banks can fund mortgage lending through deposits, borrowing, capital, loan sales, and other permitted activities. A bank may hold a loan in portfolio or sell it. Portfolio status can permit institution-specific products, but it does not exempt a covered loan from consumer law or sound underwriting. A bank evaluates credit risk, liquidity, concentration, capital, and return along with the individual borrower's file.

Compare an amortizing permanent mortgage with a construction loan. The first pays principal down over a scheduled term. The second may advance funds as work progresses and rely on completion and a permanent loan or sale for repayment. A construction budget is not enough: the lender evaluates plans, permits, contractor capacity, inspections, contingency, title, and the source of repayment. A borrower should know whether conversion to permanent financing is automatic under a commitment or requires new approval.

### Applied exercise: A20 — Funding and exit

A hypothetical bank proposes a 12-month $800,000 construction facility. The borrower expects a permanent loan at completion but has no takeout commitment. Identify four evidence items needed before treating the exit as reliable, and explain the risk if construction is delayed six months.

Now assume the entire $800,000 is outstanding at the end of the original term, a hypothetical extension is available at 9% simple annual interest, and six months of delay adds $18,000 carrying costs besides interest. Compute the added funding need, then compare it with $42,000 available reserves. Prepare a lender question about extension authority and a separate contractor question about the revised completion schedule. Do not assume the extension exists merely because its cost can be modeled.

Feedback: completion budget and schedule, permits/contractor evidence, completed-value support, and a credible permanent financing or sale plan are material. Delay can increase interest and exceed maturity; expected future financing is not a present commitment.

Additional feedback: Six months of interest is $36,000; adding $18,000 carrying costs produces $54,000, which exceeds reserves by $12,000. An extension commitment, completed-value evidence, and a revised budget remain necessary; arithmetic does not create a lender obligation to extend.

## T05U2 — Life Insurance Companies

Life insurers invest premiums and other assets to support future policy obligations. Their investment horizons can suit longer-term commercial mortgage assets, subject to regulation and portfolio strategy. An insurer's loan is not a life-insurance benefit paid to the borrower. Commercial underwriting often focuses on property income, occupancy, lease quality, sponsor strength, debt-service coverage, and collateral.

A longer-term fixed-rate commitment may include significant restrictions or costs for early payoff. Yield maintenance seeks to compensate for specified economic effects of prepayment; defeasance substitutes qualifying collateral under contractual requirements. Their actual operation is document-specific. Never translate either into “you may prepay free whenever you want.” Compare flexibility as well as interest rate.

### Applied exercise: A21 — Read the exit term

An investor expects to sell in three years but receives a ten-year loan proposal with an early-payoff restriction. Draft questions about permitted transfers, assumption, prepayment cost, and release of guarantees. Explain why a lower coupon alone cannot establish the lower three-year cost.

Use a separate simplified comparison: the restricted loan saves $180 monthly for 36 months but imposes a stipulated $12,000 exit charge at the planned sale; the alternative has no such charge and otherwise identical balances and costs. Calculate the net three-year difference. Then remove the assumption of identical remaining balances and list the additional evidence needed. Distinguish the lender's payoff calculation from your illustrative comparison.

Feedback: the expected exit may trigger costs that exceed periodic interest savings. Obtain the actual clauses and a lender calculation for the expected date; do not estimate a legal payoff obligation from the product name.

Additional feedback: Payment savings total $6,480, less than the $12,000 exit charge by $5,520. Different amortization, financed fees, payoff dates, or guarantee terms could change the complete comparison. The actual legal obligation comes from the executed documents and a valid payoff calculation, not the simplified estimate.

## T05U3 — Pension & Retirement Programs

Retirement funds can invest in real estate directly or through mortgage loans, funds, securities, and other vehicles consistent with their governing rules. A large institutional investment by a pension plan is different from an individual borrowing against or withdrawing retirement savings for a home purchase. Each raises different tax, fiduciary, eligibility, and liquidity issues.

A borrower considering retirement funds must distinguish an allowable distribution, a plan loan, and assets merely shown as reserves. A statement balance does not mean the whole balance can be withdrawn without restrictions, taxes, penalties, or effects on underwriting. A plan loan can create a payment obligation and employment-related repayment risk. The lender and plan administrator need the actual terms; tax advice belongs with a qualified professional.

### Applied exercise: A22 — Available funds

A borrower lists $80,000 in a retirement plan as cash to close but has no distribution approval. Write an evidence request identifying vested balance, access terms, tax/penalty considerations, timing, and any repayment duty. Explain why the closing agent should not assume an $80,000 wire is available.

Add these stipulated facts: the plan permits a $50,000 gross distribution, $10,000 will be withheld, funds will be available two business days after the scheduled closing, and the household needs $38,000 at settlement. Build a gross-to-net ledger and a timing ledger. Explain why a positive dollar cushion does not solve the delivery problem. Draft questions about the timing, permitted distribution, tax review, and any lawful alternative funding.

Feedback: asset ownership, accessibility, net proceeds, timing, and debt treatment are separate issues. A reserve asset may support an application without being an acceptable immediate funding source.

Additional feedback: Net proceeds are $40,000, leaving a nominal $2,000 cushion, but they arrive too late for the stated settlement. The lender and settlement team need an actual acceptable funding arrangement or an agreed schedule change. The example supplies withholding; it does not compute the final tax liability or establish a legal distribution right.

## T05U4 — Credit Unions

Credit unions are member-owned financial cooperatives. Membership and product eligibility depend on the institution's field of membership and rules. They can make mortgage loans and may hold or sell them. Federally insured credit unions use NCUA share insurance; they should not be described as FDIC-insured simply because both systems protect qualifying deposits/shares.

Borrowers should compare Loan Estimates, service, underwriting, lock terms, fees, and total payment across institutions. “Member-owned” does not guarantee the lowest cost or automatic approval. A product advertised to members can still require income, collateral, credit, and other evidence. Membership eligibility and mortgage eligibility are two different determinations.

### Applied exercise: A23 — Comparison questions

Prepare five questions for a credit-union mortgage appointment: membership, product type, total costs, servicing, and conditions remaining before approval. Correct a statement that “NCUA insurance guarantees the borrower cannot lose the home.”

Compare two hypothetical offers for an otherwise identical loan. The credit union charges $2,400 net lender costs and the bank $1,200, while the credit-union payment is $35 lower. Compute simple cost recovery at 24 and 48 months, assuming no balance difference. Write a comparison separating membership, insured shares, credit approval, and loan economics. Name a missing fact that would make the simple calculation incomplete.

Feedback: share insurance protects eligible accounts against institution failure within its rules; it is not mortgage-payment or foreclosure protection for the borrower.

Additional feedback: The extra $1,200 costs are not recovered after 24 months: $840 savings leaves $360 unrecovered. After 48 months, $1,680 savings exceeds the difference by $480. Recovery is about 34.29 months. Remaining balances, prepayment terms, actual eligibility, and other costs must be checked before choosing.

## T05U5 — Mortgage Brokers & Bankers

A broker brings a borrower and lender together; a mortgage banker originates loans in its own name and may later sell them. A correspondent lender may fund loans intended for delivery to another investor. The funding chain affects workflow, but the consumer should know the creditor on the documents and who can answer questions about terms and approval.

Compensation arrangements must comply with applicable loan-originator and settlement rules. A consumer's payment to a broker, a lender-paid amount, discount points, and lender credits are not interchangeable labels. Regulation Z restricts compensation based on prohibited loan terms and certain steering practices. A broker's access to several lenders does not prove that every available loan was compared or that the recommendation is the cheapest. Ask what alternatives were considered and how the costs differ.

### Applied exercise: A24 — Identify authority

The real estate agent asks the broker to “guarantee approval” while the actual creditor's underwriter still awaits the appraisal. Write a status message identifying what is known, what is outstanding, and who can issue the credit decision.

Build a four-row responsibility log for a missing appraisal, unexplained deposit, signed rate-lock agreement, and scheduled signing appointment. For each, state the owner of the next decision, evidence needed, and whether the fact independently authorizes funding. Draft a second update after the appraisal arrives subject to repair. Explain why receiving a document and clearing its condition are separate events.

Feedback: identify conditional status and appraisal review; the broker's role does not remove creditor conditions. Avoid language implying that expected approval equals a funding commitment.

Additional feedback: The underwriter must accept the appraisal and any completion evidence; the processor/lender must resolve the deposit. A lock addresses specified pricing conditions, not every underwriting condition. Signing is a settlement event, not proof of release authority. The update should identify the repair condition and responsible reviewer without promising completion.

## T05U6 — Real Estate Trusts (REIT, REMT)

A real estate investment trust (REIT) is an investment structure subject to statutory qualification requirements. Equity REITs commonly own income-producing real estate; mortgage REITs invest in mortgages or mortgage-related assets. An investor buying shares obtains an interest in the entity, not ownership of a named home's mortgage note with direct control over the borrower. Publicly traded, publicly registered non-traded, and private vehicles can have very different liquidity and disclosure characteristics.

REF-0 prints “REMT” without a definition. The course retains the label and discusses mortgage-oriented real estate trusts without claiming that REMT is a separate standardized federal agency or guaranteed product. Review the actual organizational and offering documents. A distribution yield is not guaranteed principal protection, and a mortgage-oriented portfolio can use leverage and experience interest-rate, prepayment, credit, and liquidity risk.

### Applied exercise: A25 — Investment identity

An advertisement calls a non-traded mortgage trust “as safe as a savings account” and says investors can always withdraw immediately. Identify the documents and risk disclosures needed to evaluate those claims and explain why a real estate license alone does not authorize selling every securities product.

Analyze a hypothetical offering with a 7% stated distribution, a redemption window limited to quarterly requests, and a board power to suspend repurchases. Prepare a claim/evidence matrix for income, principal protection, and exit timing. Explain why a distribution rate does not establish total return and why an advertised window is not an unconditional liquidity commitment. Identify which questions belong with a qualified securities professional before a consumer acts.

Feedback: examine offering terms, redemption restrictions, leverage, fees, conflicts, and regulatory status. Neither the words “real estate” nor “trust” establishes deposit insurance or immediate liquidity. Securities-licensing questions require the appropriate professional/regulator.

Additional feedback: The investor must consider whether distributions include income, borrowing, or return of capital and whether asset value changes offset cash received. Redemption rights are subject to the actual offering conditions and suspension authority. No deposit-insurance conclusion follows from the distribution percentage or the underlying real estate.

## T05U7 — Real Estate Bonds

A bond is a debt security with payment obligations defined in its documents. Real estate can support repayment through a mortgage pledge, project revenues, public financing structure, or an issuer's general resources, depending on the bond. “Real estate bond” does not tell an investor exactly which claim has priority, whether recourse exists, or whether a government guarantees repayment.

Price and yield move in opposite directions for a fixed cash-flow bond, other factors equal. Credit deterioration can reduce price even without a general interest-rate change. A callable bond introduces reinvestment risk if redeemed when the investor would prefer to keep earning the coupon. Read maturity, call terms, collateral, covenants, seniority, and default remedies. Tax-exempt treatment, where available, depends on legal requirements and the investor's facts, not a universal feature of all property-related debt.

### Applied exercise: A26 — Coupon versus current yield

A hypothetical bond pays $600 annual interest on $10,000 face value but sells for $9,600. Calculate its coupon rate and simple current yield. Explain why current yield is not yield to maturity and why neither calculation alone establishes suitability.

Prepare a price-and-cash-flow worksheet rather than selecting a rate alone. First, calculate current yield if this same bond instead costs $10,300; the annual coupon and face amount do not change. Second, a hypothetical investor buys ten bonds at $9,600 each, receives one full year of coupons, and then sells all ten at $8,700 each. Assume no fees, accrued-interest adjustments, taxes, or default. Show initial investment, coupon cash, sale cash, capital gain or loss, net dollar result, and the simple one-year realized return on initial investment. Do not treat the lower sale price as a change in the contractual coupon.

Finally, correct a salesperson's statement that the original 6.25% current yield guarantees a positive return whenever the investor receives every coupon. Write a short file note distinguishing coupon rate, current yield, realized holding-period return, and yield to maturity. Identify the missing maturity/redemption information needed for a yield-to-maturity calculation, then request the actual bond's call terms, collateral priority, and default provisions. Explain one way an early call and one way a junior collateral claim could alter the investor's result. A higher quoted yield alone does not answer those document questions or justify a purchase recommendation.

Feedback: coupon rate is 6%; current yield at $9,600 is $600 ÷ $9,600 = 6.25%, while at $10,300 it is approximately 5.83%. The ten-bond initial investment is $96,000, coupons are $6,000, and sale proceeds are $87,000. The $9,000 capital loss exceeds the coupons, producing a $3,000 net loss and a simple realized return of −3.125%. Receiving all coupons did not guarantee a positive total return. Yield to maturity incorporates remaining payments, their timing, and redemption value under its assumptions; it is not interchangeable with the one-year sale result. A call can end expected future coupons and create reinvestment risk; junior collateral status can reduce recovery after higher-priority claims. Neither effect can be resolved by dividing the coupon by today's price. A complete worksheet separates the seven cash-flow fields, and the file note states the assumptions and unanswered document questions without promising a yield.

## T05U8 — Private Lenders (ie: sellers, individuals, gifts)

Private funds may come from a seller carrying a note, an individual making a loan, a private lending business, or a permitted gift. A gift is not a loan if there is genuinely no repayment obligation. Calling borrowed money a gift conceals debt and can produce mortgage fraud. Document the source, transfer, relationship, and absence of repayment according to the lender's program requirements.

Seller financing can bridge a transaction when conventional funding is unavailable, but it requires proper documentation and legal compliance. Consider existing liens, due-on-sale provisions, lien priority, servicing, taxes, insurance, balloon risk, licensing, and consumer-credit requirements. A private lender may charge different terms but does not operate outside law merely because it accepts more risk. Texas executory contracts, home-equity restrictions, and wrap financing can create additional requirements.

### Applied exercise: A27 — Gift or debt

A relative transfers $18,000 and signs a gift letter, but a private message requires the buyer to repay $300 monthly for five years. Identify the actual obligation, calculate total promised payments, and state how the file should be corrected.

The loan worksheet uses $7,500 monthly qualifying income, $2,300 proposed housing expense, and $625 other monthly debt. The exercise lender's stipulated maximum total DTI is 42%; this is an invented policy for analysis, not a universal approval threshold. Calculate DTI as originally submitted and with the family repayment included. State the percentage-point change and whether the corrected file satisfies this one supplied screen. Then reconcile closing liquidity: $20,000 of the buyer's own available funds plus the $18,000 transfer must cover $34,000 due at closing. Does describing the transfer accurately as debt change the cash physically present, the source's acceptability, both, or neither? Explain the distinction without approving use of the funds yourself.

Prepare a correction memo with four required parts: the conflict between the letter and message; the corrected liability and DTI calculation; the cash-to-close/reserve reconciliation; and the specific lender review needed for funds eligibility, documentation, and the changed underwriting result. Consider a later statement from the relative that repayment is now waived. Explain why the agent cannot simply erase the original evidence or assume the waiver establishes an acceptable gift. Ask the lender what reliable evidence is needed to establish the genuine current arrangement and whether this source satisfies the applicable program. The exercise does not establish that every family loan is permitted, that every waiver is sufficient, or that a real lender must deny the entire transaction.

Feedback: $300 × 60 = $18,000; the original agreement is debt under the stated facts despite the gift letter. Original DTI is $2,925 ÷ $7,500 = 39%; corrected DTI is $3,225 ÷ $7,500 = 43%, four percentage points higher and above the stipulated 42% screen. Available cash is $38,000, leaving $4,000 after the stated closing requirement, but physical availability does not make the source acceptable. The accurate debt classification affects obligations and source review, not the arithmetic existence of the deposit. Preserve and disclose the conflicting documents and obtain lender-directed correction and underwriting review. A later genuine change must be documented and evaluated; a replacement label cannot cure a concealed repayment agreement. The memo must keep qualification, actual liquidity, and evidence of gift status separate, and must not fabricate facts to obtain approval.

## T05U9 — Foreign Lenders

Cross-border capital can enter mortgage markets through direct lending, investment funds, banks, and securities. Currency, legal jurisdiction, sanctions, source-of-funds verification, tax, enforceability, and transfer logistics can complicate transactions. A foreign source of funds does not by itself prove wrongdoing; the correct response is lawful, consistent due diligence based on actual requirements.

Distinguish the lender's location from the borrower's citizenship and from the currency of the debt. A U.S.-dollar loan may be funded by a foreign investor. A borrower earning in another currency can face payment risk when exchange rates change. If a payment is $2,000 and the borrower's currency weakens so each dollar costs 10% more of that currency, the local-currency burden rises even though the dollar payment is unchanged. Legal eligibility and sanctions issues should be referred to the lender's compliance team, not decided through stereotypes about national origin.

### Applied exercise: A28 — Cross-border file review

List five verification questions for a proposed foreign-currency loan secured by Texas land: governing law, repayment currency, conversion costs, source/transfer of funds, and enforceability of security. Explain what happens to a fixed foreign-currency payment when the borrower's earning currency weakens.

A hypothetical payment is 2,000 foreign-currency units. The exchange cost changes from $1.10 to $1.25 per unit, and a fixed $35 transfer fee applies each month. Compute both dollar outflows, the monthly increase, and a six-month reserve for that increase. Prepare a separate evidence request concerning who bears conversion charges, transfer timing, and any lender stress assumption. Do not use these invented rates as market quotes.

Feedback: the earning-currency cost rises under the stated direction of exchange-rate movement. A fixed payment in one currency is not fixed in every currency. Legal and underwriting review must occur before promises are made to the consumer.

Additional feedback: Dollar outflow rises from $2,235 to $2,535, an increase of $300 monthly; six months of that increase is $1,800. The debt's foreign-currency amount remains unchanged. Transfer restrictions, fees, timing, legal enforceability, and qualification are independent questions that this arithmetic cannot answer.
