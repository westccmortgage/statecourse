# Wisconsin Salesperson Pre-License — Module WI-08

## Hours 45–51 · Real estate finance and basic math

**Board topic covered:** REEB 25.033(10) Real estate finance and basic math
**Clock hours:** 7 · **Hour allocation:** a StateCourse proposal for Board review.
**Course version:** 1.0 submission draft
**Regulatory status:** Development. Not approved. No Wisconsin pre-license credit is available.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module the student will be able to:

- Describe buyer qualification and compute the ratios a lender uses.
- Distinguish the note from the mortgage and name the parties to a Wisconsin mortgage.
- Explain the clauses that decide outcomes and the effect of each.
- Explain a Wisconsin **land contract** and what the vendor and vendee each hold.
- Describe the payment plans a buyer will meet and what risk each carries.
- Explain the secondary mortgage market and why guidelines exist.
- Compute LTV, points, interest, a payment from a factor, prorations and cash to close.

## Hour 45 — Buyer qualification — REEB 25.033(10)(a)

### 45.1 What underwriting examines

**Capacity** — income, its stability, and the ratios below. **Credit** — history and score, and the
explanation for anything derogatory. **Capital** — assets, reserves, and the **source** of funds; a
large undocumented deposit is a condition, and a gift needs a gift letter and a paper trail.
**Collateral** — the appraisal, and the property's condition and eligibility for the programme.

### 45.2 The ratios

**Front-end (housing) ratio** = monthly housing expense ÷ gross monthly income.
**Back-end (total debt) ratio** = total monthly obligations ÷ gross monthly income.

*Worked:* gross monthly income $7,800; PITI $2,180; other obligations $610.
Front-end 2,180 ÷ 7,800 = **27.9%**. Back-end 2,790 ÷ 7,800 = **35.8%**.

**PITI** is principal, interest, taxes and insurance, plus mortgage insurance and any association dues
the lender includes.

**The thresholds are the lender's and the programme's, and they change. This course states none.** A
licensee computes a ratio to help a buyer think; the lender decides.

### 45.3 Pre-qualification and pre-approval

A **pre-qualification** is an informal estimate from unverified information. A **pre-approval** rests on
a submitted application, verified documentation, and usually a conditional underwriting decision. A
seller evaluating offers is entitled to know which is in front of them, and a licensee who lets one be
presented as the other has misrepresented their buyer's position — a **§ 452.133(1)(a)** honesty problem
before it is anything else.

### 45.4 The consumer-protection frame

**TILA and Regulation Z** — disclosure of the cost of credit, principally the **annual percentage
rate**, and control of credit advertising through the **trigger terms**: the amount or percentage of a
down payment, the number of payments or the period of repayment, the amount of any payment, or the
amount of any finance charge. State one and the rest must follow. **"$1,850 a month!" in a property
advertisement is a credit advertisement.**

**RESPA and Regulation X** — the settlement process. **Section 8(a)**: no fee, kickback or thing of
value pursuant to an agreement or understanding for the **referral** of settlement service business.
**Section 8(b)**: no **unearned fees**. Affiliated business arrangements are permitted where disclosed
in the prescribed form, the referral is not required, and the only thing of value received is a return
on ownership interest.

Wisconsin has its own overlay: **§ 452.133(3)(a)** prohibits accepting any fee or compensation related
to the transaction from anyone other than the licensee's client or firm **without the prior written
consent of all parties**, and **§ 452.133(3)(c)** requires **written disclosure** of a referral interest.
A "marketing agreement" that is in substance payment for referrals engages both.

**TRID** — the **Loan Estimate** shortly after application and the **Closing Disclosure** received by
the borrower a defined period before consummation, with certain late changes **re-starting** that
period. **No period is stated here; the lender states it.**

**ECOA and Regulation B** — no discrimination in credit on the basis of race, colour, religion, national
origin, sex, marital status, age where the applicant has capacity to contract, receipt of public
assistance income, or the good faith exercise of consumer credit rights.

**HMDA**, **FCRA** and its adverse action notice, the **SAFE Act**, and **Dodd-Frank's ability-to-repay
and qualified mortgage** standards complete the frame. **REEB 25.033(19)(g)** separately requires
instruction on **mortgage banking under s. 224.71**, which Module WI-13 names.

### 45.5 What a licensee tells a buyer, and what they must not

**Tell them, in writing at binding acceptance:** do not change or leave a job; do not open credit
accounts or finance furniture, a vehicle or appliances; do not close accounts or pay off collections
without asking the loan officer; do not make undocumented large deposits; do not move money
unnecessarily; do not co-sign; do not miss a payment.

**Do not tell them:** that they will qualify, what rate they will get, or what a programme requires as
though it were settled. **§ 452.133(2)(am)** limits advice to matters within the scope of the knowledge,
skills and training required under ch. 452, and lending eligibility is outside it.

## Hour 46 — Mortgage instruments — REEB 25.033(10)(b)

### 46.1 Two documents, two jobs

**The promissory note** is the **debt** — the borrower's personal promise to repay, with the rate, the
payment, the term, late charges, prepayment terms, default and acceleration. It is a negotiable
instrument and can be sold. **It is not recorded.**

**The mortgage** is the **collateral** — it pledges the property as security and creates the lien. **It
is recorded**, in the county register of deeds.

Pay off the note and a **satisfaction of mortgage** must be recorded. A recorded but unsatisfied
mortgage is one of the most common clouds a Wisconsin title search reveals.

### 46.2 Wisconsin uses a mortgage, not a deed of trust

A **mortgage** has **two** parties: the **mortgagor**, the borrower who gives the mortgage, and the
**mortgagee**, the lender.

A **deed of trust** has three — borrower, trustee and lender — and the trustee conducts a sale under a
power of sale. **Wisconsin does not use it.** A student who learned "grantor, trustee, beneficiary" from
a national text, or from this project's Virginia course, must translate.

The consequence is procedural and large: **Wisconsin residential foreclosure is judicial.** Hour 47.

### 46.3 The clauses that decide outcomes

**Acceleration** — on default the lender may declare the entire balance immediately due. Without it a
lender could sue only for missed payments, and every foreclosure begins here.

**Alienation, or due-on-sale** — the lender may accelerate if the property is transferred without
consent. This makes most modern loans **non-assumable** and is why "taking over payments" is usually not
available.

**Prepayment** — whether the borrower may pay early and on what terms.

**Defeasance** — on full payment the mortgage is defeated and must be **satisfied** of record.

**Subordination** — an agreement to take a lower lien position than recording order would give.
Refinancing a first mortgage while a home equity line remains recorded requires one, or the equity line
would advance to first position.

**Escrow or impound** — the lender collects taxes and insurance monthly and pays them when due,
protecting its own collateral against a tax lien or an uninsured loss. In Wisconsin, where the tax bill
issues in December, the escrow analysis a buyer receives in their first year is frequently the first
time they see the real annual figure.

## Hour 47 — Default, assignment, release, subject to — REEB 25.033(10)(d)

### 47.1 Default and acceleration

**Default** is failure to perform: missed payments most often, but also failure to pay taxes, maintain
insurance, or maintain the property. Federal servicing rules impose requirements on the servicer before
it may proceed, including loss-mitigation obligations.

### 47.2 Wisconsin foreclosure is judicial

The lender brings an **action in circuit court**. A judgment of foreclosure is entered, a **redemption
period** runs, and the property is sold at a **sheriff's sale** which the **court must confirm** before
title passes.

Wisconsin's scheme has features that matter to brokerage: the redemption period's length varies with
the type of property and with **whether the lender waives a deficiency judgment**; and the **confirmation
hearing** is a further step at which the court considers whether the sale price was fair.

**This course states no Wisconsin redemption period, no deficiency rule, and no confirmation standard**,
because ch. 846 has not been read from primary text for this project. What a licensee must know is the
**shape** — court action, judgment, redemption, sheriff's sale, confirmation — and that a distressed
borrower goes to **counsel and to a HUD-approved housing counselling agency**, not to a licensee, for
timing.

### 47.3 Assignment, release, and subject to

**Assignment of the mortgage** transfers the lender's interest, which is what happens on the secondary
market, and it is recorded.

**Assumption** — the buyer takes over the existing loan and becomes **personally liable**. The seller is
**released only by a written release from the lender**; without one the seller remains liable.

**Subject to** — the buyer takes title with the loan in place but **assumes no personal liability**. The
seller stays liable, the loan stays in the seller's name, and the **due-on-sale clause is very likely
triggered.** It is a structure clients hear about and should not enter without counsel.

**Release** — a partial release frees part of the security, used where a parcel is being split off, and
it is recorded.

**Satisfaction** — the full discharge on payoff, recorded.

### 47.4 Alternatives to foreclosure

**Reinstatement**; **modification**; **forbearance**; **repayment plan**; **short sale**, which requires
the lender's agreement and in which deficiency treatment must be addressed **in writing**; and **deed in
lieu**, which junior liens generally survive.

Each has credit and tax consequences the licensee is not qualified to explain. The professional response
is to identify them as options and route the borrower to the servicer's loss-mitigation department, a
HUD-approved counselling agency, and counsel — and never to advise a client to stop paying in order to
qualify for something.

## Hour 48 — Land contracts — REEB 25.033(10)(e)

### 48.1 What a land contract is

A **land contract** — the rule gives it its own paragraph, and Wisconsin uses them substantially more
than most states — is a financing structure in which the **seller** finances the purchase directly.

**The vendor (seller) retains legal title.** **The vendee (buyer) takes possession and equitable
title**, pays in instalments over the agreed term, and receives a **deed** when the contract is
performed.

It is a **conveyance** for the purposes of ch. 706 and it is **recorded**.

### 48.2 Why Wisconsin uses them

Land contracts appear where conventional financing is unavailable or impractical: **farmland and rural
acreage**; properties a secondary-market lender will not accept; **family transfers**; buyers with
credit or documentation problems; and transactions where the seller wants the income stream.

### 48.3 What the vendee must understand

**Equitable title is not legal title.** The vendee cannot convey a clear fee until the contract is
performed, and a purported sale by the vendee is a sale of a vendee's interest.

**Existing liens.** If the vendor has a mortgage, the land contract sits behind it, and the vendor's
default can put the vendee's interest at risk. **A due-on-sale clause in the vendor's mortgage may be
triggered by the land contract itself.**

**Taxes and insurance.** Who pays, and how the vendee verifies that they were paid, must be in the
contract. A vendee who has paid faithfully and discovers the vendor did not pay the property tax has a
serious problem.

**Default and its consequence.** Wisconsin provides remedies for a vendor on a vendee's default, and
the vendee's exposure — including the possibility of losing what has been paid — is real. **This course
states no remedy, notice period or cure right**, because the provisions have not been read from primary
text for this project.

**Recording.** The contract is recorded so that the vendee's interest is of record and third parties are
on notice.

**And the deed at the end.** How and when the deed is delivered, and by what mechanism if the vendor has
died or become incapacitated, must be provided for.

### 48.4 What a licensee does

**Use the Board-approved land contract addendum or form** — REEB 25.033(8)(f)3 addenda, and ch. REEB 16.

**Get both parties to counsel before it is signed.** A land contract allocates risks that neither party
usually understands, and every one of the questions in 48.3 is legal.

**Disclose material adverse facts** as § 452.133(1)(c) requires, including anything the firm knows about
the vendor's existing financing.

**Do not advise on the structure.** § 452.133(2)(am) again: outside the scope of ch. 452.

## Hour 49 — Payment plans and programme categories — REEB 25.033(10)(c)

### 49.1 Payment plans

| Structure | Payment | Principal | Risk the borrower carries |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fully amortising fixed | Constant | Retired over the term | None from rate movement |
| Adjustable rate | Changes at each adjustment | Retired over the term | **Rate movement**, bounded by caps |
| Interest only | Constant during the period | **None** during the period | No equity from amortisation |
| Balloon | Constant, then the balance falls due | Partly retired | **Refinance or sale risk at maturity** |
| Graduated payment | Rises on a schedule | May negatively amortise early | Payment shock |
| Temporary buydown | Rises to the note rate | Normal | Increase at each step |
| Reverse | None required | Grows | Occupancy, tax, insurance and maintenance obligations continue |
| Land contract | As agreed | As agreed | **Legal title stays with the vendor** |

An **adjustable rate** moves with an **index** plus a fixed **margin**, bounded by an **initial cap**, a
**periodic cap**, and a **lifetime cap**. The question that matters is not the start rate but **the
payment at the caps**.

### 49.2 Programme categories

**The rule for this section:** programme guidelines change and eligibility depends on facts the licensee
does not have. **A licensee describes categories; a lender determines eligibility.**

**Conventional** — not insured or guaranteed by a government agency. **Conforming** loans meet the
purchase criteria of Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac including an annually set maximum that varies by county;
**jumbo** loans exceed it. **Private mortgage insurance** is generally required below a 20 percent down
payment and **protects the lender**, not the borrower.

**FHA** — HUD **insures** loans made by approved lenders; low minimum down payment, more flexible credit
criteria, **mortgage insurance premiums** upfront and annual, county loan limits, **minimum property
standards**, and owner-occupancy.

**VA** — the Department of Veterans Affairs **guarantees** part of a loan to an eligible veteran or
service member; frequently no down payment, no monthly mortgage insurance, a **funding fee** that varies
and is sometimes waived, entitlement established by a **certificate of eligibility**, and occupancy
requirements.

**USDA Rural Development** — no down payment, **geographic eligibility** limited to designated rural
areas on maps that change, and **household income limits**. **Much of Wisconsin is within designated
rural areas**, which makes the programme more relevant here than in most states.

**WHEDA** — the **Wisconsin Housing and Economic Development Authority** operates first-time homebuyer
and other programmes through participating lenders, with their own income and purchase-price limits.
**Name the programme; refer to a participating lender.**

**Local and portfolio lending.** Wisconsin community banks and credit unions frequently hold loans in
**portfolio**, set their own property standards, and will lend on property a secondary-market lender
will not — a farm, a large acreage parcel, a seasonal cottage, or a property with an unusual water
supply. Naming that category serves a client without advising on credit.

**No loan limit, mortgage insurance duration, funding fee, PMI cancellation threshold, income limit or
qualifying ratio is stated in this course.**

## Hour 50 — The secondary mortgage market — REEB 25.033(10)(f)

### 50.1 Primary and secondary

The **primary market** is where loans are **originated**: banks, savings institutions, credit unions,
mortgage bankers, and mortgage brokers.

The **secondary market** is where originated loans are **sold**. **Fannie Mae** and **Freddie Mac**
purchase conforming conventional loans; **Ginnie Mae** guarantees securities backed by government loans.

### 50.2 Why it exists and what it causes

Selling loans **returns capital** to the originator so it can lend again. Without it, a lender's capacity
would be limited to its deposits, and mortgage credit would be far scarcer and more local.

The consequence a licensee must understand: **underwriting guidelines exist because the loan must be
sellable.** A loan that does not meet the purchaser's criteria cannot be sold, so the criteria govern
origination — which is why a lender's answer to "can you make an exception?" is usually no, and why a
**portfolio** lender can say yes where a secondary-market lender cannot.

### 50.3 Servicing

The loan's **servicing** — collecting payments, administering escrow, handling default — is frequently
sold or transferred, and the servicer is often not the originator. **Borrowers must be notified**, and
**the terms of the note do not change.** A buyer who receives a servicing transfer notice a month after
closing has not been sold to a different loan.

## Hour 51 — Basic math

### 51.1 Loan-to-value and down payment

**LTV = loan amount ÷ the lesser of sale price or appraised value.**

*Purchase $312,000, loan $249,600.* LTV = **80%**; down payment **$62,400**.

*Purchase $312,000, appraisal $298,000, lender lends 80% LTV.* The lender lends against the **lower**
figure: 0.80 × 298,000 = **$238,400**. The buyer must bring 312,000 − 238,400 = **$73,600** — the
$62,400 planned plus a **$13,600 appraisal gap.** This is the whole reason the appraisal contingency
exists.

### 51.2 Points and interest

**One point = 1% of the loan amount.** *Loan $249,600, 1.5 points:* 249,600 × 0.015 = **$3,744**.

**Simple interest: I = P × R × T.** *$249,600 at 6.375% for one month:* 249,600 × 0.06375 = $15,912 per
year; ÷ 12 = **$1,326.00**.

**Per diem at closing:** annual interest ÷ 365 × days. *Closing 12 September, first payment 1 November:*
19 days, 12–30 September. 15,912 ÷ 365 = $43.5945 per day; × 19 = **$828.30**.

### 51.3 Payment and amortisation

*Factor 6.24 per $1,000, loan $249,600:* 249.6 × 6.24 = **$1,557.50** monthly principal and interest.
First month's interest $1,326.00, so principal = 1,557.50 − 1,326.00 = **$231.50**, and the balance
after one payment is **$249,368.50**.

### 51.4 Area, commission, and tax

*A lot 264 ft by 330 ft:* 87,120 sq ft ÷ 43,560 = **2 acres**.
*Sale $312,000 at a 5.5% fee, split evenly between firms, salesperson at 65% of the firm's half:*
total 312,000 × 0.055 = **$17,160**; firm's half **$8,580**; salesperson 8,580 × 0.65 = **$5,577**.
*Assessed $268,000 at a mill rate of $19.40 per $1,000:* 268,000 ÷ 1,000 = 268; × 19.40 = **$5,199.20**.

### 51.5 Proration, on the Wisconsin basis

**The convention comes from the offer**, not from habit, and Wisconsin's December billing makes the
question live — Module WI-06 Hour 38.3.

*Worked, on a calendar-year basis with the closing day to the buyer, 365-day year.* Annual tax
$5,199.20; closing 12 September.
Daily: 5,199.20 ÷ 365 = **$14.2444**. Seller's days 1 January–11 September = **254**.
254 × 14.2444 = **$3,618.08 — a credit to the buyer and a debit to the seller**, because the buyer will
pay the whole bill when it issues in December.

### 51.6 Cash to close

= down payment + closing costs + prepaid items + escrow reserves − loan amount already counted − earnest
money − seller and lender credits − prorated items credited to the buyer.

*Worked:* purchase $312,000; down payment $62,400; closing costs $4,900; prepaid interest $828.30;
first-year insurance $1,410; escrow reserves $1,180; earnest money $4,000; seller credit $2,500; tax
proration credit to buyer $3,618.08.
62,400 + 4,900 + 828.30 + 1,410 + 1,180 = 70,718.30; less 4,000, 2,500 and 3,618.08 = **$60,600.22**.

**This is an estimate.** The controlling figures are the lender's Closing Disclosure and the closing
agent's statement, and every estimate a licensee gives should say so in writing — which is what
**§ 452.133(1)(b)**, reasonable skill and care, requires.

### Module WI-08 engagement check

**Question.** A buyer wants a 60-acre parcel with a farmhouse, a private well, and an outbuilding. The
seller offers to sell on a **land contract** at a rate below what a bank would charge, and the buyer
asks whether that is a good idea and whether they should just do it instead of getting a mortgage.
Answer.

**Expected answer.**

**Whether it is a good idea is not mine to say**, and saying it would be advice outside
**§ 452.133(2)(am)**'s scope. What I can do is describe the structure and route it.

**In a land contract the vendor keeps legal title** and the vendee takes **possession and equitable
title**, receiving a deed only when the contract is performed. That is a materially different position
from owning subject to a mortgage, and the buyer must understand it before signing.

**The questions that must be answered before anything is signed**, all of them legal: is there an
existing mortgage on the property, and does the land contract **trigger its due-on-sale clause**; who
pays the **taxes and insurance** and how does the vendee **verify** they were paid; what happens on
**default**, and what does the vendee stand to lose; how and when is the **deed** delivered, and what
happens if the vendor dies or becomes incapacitated; and is the contract **recorded**.

**So: both parties to counsel, before signing**, and the transaction on the **Board-approved form**
under ch. REEB 16. I also mention the alternative category rather than the alternative product — **a
Wisconsin community bank or credit union lending in portfolio** may finance a 60-acre parcel with a
farmhouse and a private well where a secondary-market lender would not, and that is worth a
conversation with a lender. And I disclose in writing anything I know about the property or the
seller's financing that **§ 452.133(1)(c)** reaches.
