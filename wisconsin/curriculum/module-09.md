## Learning objectives

By the end of this module the student will be able to:

- State the principles of value and identify highest and best use.
- Adjust comparable sales in the correct direction and reconcile to a supported range.
- Compute value by the cost approach and classify depreciation by type.
- Compute NOI, value by direct capitalization, and value by gross rent multiplier.
- Reproduce the appraisal process in sequence.
- State the boundary between a licensee's market analysis and an appraisal, and the rule that draws it.

## Hour 52 — Principles of value — REEB 25.033(11)(a)

### 52.1 Value, price, and cost

**Value** is an opinion of what a property should bring under stated conditions. **Price** is what was
actually paid in a particular transaction, which may reflect unusual motivation or terms. **Cost** is
what it takes to create the improvements, which may be more or less than what they add to value.

**Market value** is the classic definition and its conditions do the work: the most probable price in a
competitive and open market, under conditions requisite to a fair sale, with buyer and seller each
acting prudently and knowledgeably, **neither under duress**, allowing a reasonable exposure time, with
payment in cash or its equivalent and terms typical of the market.

Strip out any one of those conditions — a distressed seller, a sale between family members, no market
exposure, a land contract at an off-market rate — and the observed price is not evidence of market value
without adjustment. That is the screen applied when comparables are selected.

Other values a Wisconsin licensee meets: **assessed value**, set by the municipal assessor for taxation
and drifting from the market between revaluations; **equalized value**, the Department of Revenue's
estimate used to apportion levies between municipalities; **insurable value**, a replacement-cost concept
that **excludes land**; **investment value**, value to a particular investor; and **liquidation value**,
which assumes an inadequate exposure period.

### 52.2 The principles

**Supply and demand** — value moves with the relationship between them, and real estate's supply is slow.

**Substitution** — a buyer will pay no more than the cost of acquiring an equally desirable substitute.
This is the foundation of **all three approaches**: comparison, cost and income each ask what a
substitute would cost.

**Anticipation** — value reflects expected future benefits, not past cost.

**Contribution** — a component adds value equal to what it contributes to the whole, measured in the
market, which is frequently far less than it cost. The principle a seller most often needs explained.

**Conformity, progression and regression** — value is maximised where a property conforms reasonably to
its surroundings; a lesser property gains from better neighbours and a superior property is dragged
toward the level of lesser ones.

**Increasing and diminishing returns** — additional investment adds value up to a point and then does
not.

**Competition** — excess profit invites competition, which erodes it.

**Change** — no condition is permanent, which is why every value opinion has an effective date.

**Plottage** is the increment from combining adjacent parcels under one ownership; **assemblage** is the
act of combining them.

**Externalities** — influences outside the property affect its value: the closed plant, the new bypass,
the neighbouring feedlot, the lake access that changed hands.

### 52.3 Highest and best use

The reasonably probable use that is **legally permissible, physically possible, financially feasible,
and maximally productive** — all four, in that order.

Land is valued at its highest and best use **as though vacant**, which is why an obsolete building on a
well-located lot may sell for land value.

In Wisconsin the **legally permissible** test carries unusual weight, because a parcel's use can be
constrained simultaneously by county or municipal zoning, **shoreland zoning**, **floodplain**
regulation, **farmland preservation**, subdivision status, wastewater capacity, a conservation easement,
and private covenants. A highest-and-best-use conclusion that has not checked those is a guess.

### 52.4 The four forces

**Physical and environmental** — location, topography, soils, water, climate, improvements.
**Economic** — employment, income, credit, rents, construction costs.
**Governmental** — zoning, codes, taxation, public services.
**Social** — population, household formation, tastes.

## Hour 53 — The direct market comparison approach — REEB 25.033(11)(b)

### 53.1 The rule

**Adjust the comparable, never the subject.** The subject is the unknown; the comparables are the
evidence. Every adjustment answers: *what would this comparable have sold for if it had been like the
subject?*

**Comparable superior to the subject → adjust the comparable DOWN.**
**Comparable inferior to the subject → adjust the comparable UP.**

The memory hook: **comparable better, subtract.**

### 53.2 Selecting comparables

**Closed sales are evidence. Active listings are not** — an active listing is an unaccepted offer.
Pending sales are better evidence than actives. **Expired and withdrawn** listings are useful in one
specific way: they show prices the market **refused**, which brackets the top of the range.

Screen for **arm's-length conditions**: unrelated parties, adequate exposure, no unusual pressure, market
terms.

Then compare on **real property rights conveyed**; **financing terms**, including seller concessions and
land contracts at off-market rates; **conditions of sale**; **expenditures made immediately after
purchase**; **market conditions**, which is the time adjustment; **location**; **physical
characteristics**; and **economic characteristics, use and non-realty components** — the boat, the lift,
the equipment included in the price.

In Wisconsin three variables frequently dominate: **water frontage**, measured in feet and in quality —
lake versus river, sand versus muck, depth at the shore, exposure, and whether frontage is on a
recreational or a navigable-but-quiet water; **acreage with or without development potential**; and
**season**, because a February sale and a July sale in a recreational market are not evidence of the
same market.

### 53.3 A worked grid

*Subject: 1,880 sq ft, 3 bed, 2 bath, attached 2-car garage, 120 feet of lake frontage.*

| | Comp A | Comp B | Comp C |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Sale price | $482,000 | $438,000 | $515,000 |
| Sold | 3 months ago | 8 months ago | 1 month ago |
| Market conditions +0.4%/month | +$5,784 | +$14,016 | +$2,060 |
| Frontage | 120 ft | 90 ft (−30) | 150 ft (+30) |
| Frontage adj. at $1,100/ft | $0 | +$33,000 | −$33,000 |
| Living area | 1,950 (+70) | 1,820 (−60) | 1,880 (same) |
| Area adj. at $85/sq ft | −$5,950 | +$5,100 | $0 |
| Garage | attached 2-car | detached 1-car | attached 2-car |
| Garage adj. | $0 | +$14,000 | $0 |
| **Net adjustment** | **−$166** | **+$66,116** | **−$30,940** |
| **Adjusted price** | **$481,834** | **$504,116** | **$484,060** |

Check the directions. Comp A is **larger** — superior — so the area adjustment is **negative**. Comp B
is **inferior** on frontage, area and garage, so all three adjustments are **positive**. Comp C has
**more frontage** — superior — so that adjustment is **negative**.

### 53.4 Reconciling

Indicated range **$481,834 to $504,116**, and reconciliation is **judgment, not averaging**. It weights
each comparable by reliability: how few adjustments it needed, how large the **gross adjustment** was,
how recent, how close.

Comp B required **$66,116** of gross adjustment on a $438,000 sale — about 15 percent of the price — and
is the weakest evidence however tidy its arithmetic looks. Comps A and C are close together and are the
stronger indications, and a defensible conclusion sits near **$482,000 to $485,000**.

**Gross adjustment measures comparability; net adjustment does not.** A comparable whose large
adjustments happen to offset is not a good comparable; it is a lucky one.

## Hour 54 — The cost approach and the income approach

### 54.1 The cost approach — REEB 25.033(11)(c)

**Value = land value + cost new of the improvements − accrued depreciation.**

**Land value** is estimated separately, normally by sales comparison of vacant sites — which in
Wisconsin means comparing frontage, acreage, soils and wastewater capacity, access, and development
status rather than acreage alone.

**Cost new** is either **reproduction cost** — an exact replica including its obsolete features — or
**replacement cost** — a substitute of equivalent utility using current materials and methods.
Replacement cost is used more often, because nobody would rebuild the obsolete features.

**Depreciation** is loss in value from any cause, and it has three sources:

**Physical deterioration** — wear, tear and age. **Curable** where the cost to fix is recovered in
value; **incurable** where it is not.

**Functional obsolescence** — a defect in the design or utility of the improvement itself: one bathroom
for four bedrooms, a bedroom reachable only through another, a farmhouse layout in a market that wants
open plan. Curable or incurable.

**External obsolescence** — loss caused by something **outside** the property: a closed employer, a new
highway, a changed neighbourhood, an adjoining use. **Always incurable**, because the owner cannot fix
it from inside the boundary.

*Worked:* land value $145,000; replacement cost new $296,000; physical deterioration $53,000; functional
obsolescence $11,000; external obsolescence $7,000.
145,000 + 296,000 − 53,000 − 11,000 − 7,000 = **$370,000**.

**Most reliable for new construction and special-purpose property**; least reliable for old improvements,
where the depreciation estimate carries most of the answer.

### 54.2 The income approach — REEB 25.033(11)(d)

**Potential Gross Income** — all income at full occupancy at market rent, plus other income.
**less Vacancy and Collection Loss**, supported by market evidence.
**= Effective Gross Income.**
**less Operating Expenses** — fixed, variable, and **replacement reserves**.
**= Net Operating Income.**

**Three items are excluded from operating expenses**, and the exclusion is the most examinable point:

**Debt service.** NOI measures the **property's** performance independent of financing. Including the
mortgage payment would give the same building a different NOI for every buyer.
**Depreciation.** An accounting and tax entry, not a cash expense.
**Capital expenditures.** A new roof is capital; the **replacement reserve** that spreads its
anticipated cost **is** an operating expense, but the outlay itself is not.

**Direct capitalization: Value = NOI ÷ capitalization rate**, written **V = I ÷ R**, with **I = V × R**
and **R = I ÷ V**.

*Worked:* 12 units at $1,150 per month plus $5,400 other annual income.
PGI = 12 × 1,150 × 12 + 5,400 = 165,600 + 5,400 = **$171,000**.
Vacancy and collection loss 7% = 11,970. **EGI = $159,030.**
Operating expenses $63,400, including a $6,000 replacement reserve.
**NOI = 159,030 − 63,400 = $95,630.**
At a market rate of 7.5%: **V = 95,630 ÷ 0.075 = $1,275,067**, say $1,275,000.

Two consequences. **The rate is derived from the market** — most defensibly by dividing comparable
sales' NOI by their prices — not chosen. And **a higher rate gives a lower value at the same NOI**: at
8.5% the same building indicates $1,125,059, about $150,000 less for one percentage point.

**Gross rent multiplier.** GRM = sale price ÷ **monthly** gross rent; value = GRM × the subject's monthly
gross rent. *Comparable GRMs of 11.8, 12.3 and 12.0; subject's monthly gross rent $14,250. At 12.0:*
**value = $171,000.**

**What the multiplier ignores is the point:** it uses **gross** rent, so it ignores vacancy and every
operating expense. Two buildings with identical gross rents and different expense structures look
identical to a GRM and are not.

### 54.3 The reserve line, and how a pro forma is dressed

The most common manipulation is **omitting the replacement reserve**. Remove the $6,000 above and NOI
appears to be $101,630; at 7.5% that indicates $1,355,067 rather than $1,275,067 — **$80,000 of apparent
value created by deleting one line.**

The second is presenting **market rent** as **contract rent**. A building with below-market leases is
worth what its leases produce until they roll.

## Hour 55 — The appraisal process and the licensee's boundary

### 55.1 The appraisal process — REEB 25.033(11)(e)

1. **Define the problem** — client, intended user, intended use, type of value, effective date, property
   rights appraised, and any assumptions or limiting conditions.
2. **Determine the scope of work.**
3. **Collect data** — general data about the market, specific data about the subject and comparables.
4. **Analyse highest and best use**, as vacant and as improved.
5. **Estimate land value** separately.
6. **Apply the approaches** — comparison, cost, income.
7. **Reconcile** into an opinion or a range. Reconciliation weights; it does not average.
8. **Report** in the form the assignment requires.

Change the **intended use** — a mortgage loan, a divorce, an estate, a tax appeal, a relocation — and the
scope of work and sometimes the type of value change with it. That is why an appraisal prepared for one
purpose is not evidence for another.

### 55.2 Which approach governs

**Residential owner-occupied** — the **direct market comparison approach** governs; cost supports; income
is largely irrelevant.
**Income property** — the **income approach** governs; comparison supports.
**New construction and special purpose** — the **cost approach** carries more weight.

### 55.3 The licensee's market analysis, and the boundary

A licensee's **comparative market analysis** uses these methods to present market evidence to a client
for the purpose of setting a listing price or an offer price. That is ordinary brokerage, and
**§ 452.133(1)(e)** makes it a duty: the firm owes every party **accurate information about market
conditions** that affect the transaction, on request.

**It is not an appraisal.** Wisconsin licenses and certifies appraisers separately, under a different
regulatory scheme, and a licensee must not represent a market analysis as an appraisal or prepare one
for a lender in place of an appraisal.

**And Wisconsin has an ethics rule directly on the point.** **REEB 24.09 — misleading market values** —
is a listed topic at REEB 25.033(13)(b)1.d, and Module WI-10 takes it. A price presented to win a listing
rather than to describe the evidence is the conduct the rule addresses.

**The assembled method**, which a Wisconsin salesperson uses weekly:

Define the market area by what actually changes buyer behaviour — the lake, the school district, the
municipal line that changes the mill rate. Pull **closed** sales over a defensible period, extended
further only where thin data requires it and the extension is stated. **Screen for arm's-length
conditions** and write down every exclusion. Adjust toward the subject in the direction Hour 53 gave, and
record each adjustment's basis. **Reconcile to a range**, weighted by gross adjustment, recency and
location. **Disclose the limits** — how many sales, how old, what could not be verified. And **describe
the work accurately**: market evidence presented in the course of brokerage, not an appraisal.

### 55.4 Common errors and their corrections

**Averaging the three approaches** — reconciliation weights.
**Using assessed or equalized value as evidence of market value** — assessed value is an administrative
figure for taxation, and equalized value apportions levies between municipalities.
**Adjusting the subject** — adjust the comparable, always.
**Treating net adjustment as a measure of comparability** — gross adjustment measures it.
**Using list prices as comparables** — they are unaccepted offers.
**Ignoring season in a Wisconsin recreational market.**
**Quoting a cap rate with no derivation.**
**Omitting the replacement reserve.**

### Module WI-09 engagement check

**Question.** A seller says: "The assessor has us at $412,000 and the neighbour listed at $469,000, so
list me at $469,000. And I put $60,000 into the kitchen last year, so add that." Respond, and name the
rules you are working under.

**Expected answer.**

**The assessment is not evidence of market value.** It is an administrative figure set by the municipal
assessor for taxation, on the municipality's revaluation cycle, and it drifts from the market between
revaluations — which is what the **assessment ratio** and **equalized value** measure. I would not use it
as a value indicator in either direction.

**The neighbour's listing is not evidence either.** An active listing is an **unaccepted offer** — it
tells us what one seller is asking, not what the market will pay. It is useful as **competitive supply**
and as a bracket, and if it later expires unsold it becomes evidence of a price the market **refused**.

**The kitchen is the principle of contribution.** A component adds value equal to what it **contributes
to the whole, measured in the market**, which is frequently far less than it cost — and **regression**
and **conformity** explain why an improvement that lifts the house above its neighbourhood standard may
contribute less still. Whether the kitchen contributes $60,000, $25,000 or $10,000 is answered by
**paired comparable sales**, not by the invoice.

**What I will do** is present closed arm's-length comparables, adjusted toward this property, reconciled
to a **range**, with the basis of every adjustment and the limits of the data stated — and the seller
decides the list price.

**The rules:** **§ 452.133(1)(e)** requires the firm to provide **accurate** information about market
conditions; **§ 452.133(1)(b)** requires reasonable skill and care; and **REEB 24.09** addresses
**misleading market values**, which is exactly what a price adopted to win a listing would be.

## Instructor source note for Module WI-09

Wisconsin statements rest on text read directly from `docs.legis.wisconsin.gov` on 2026-08-20 and
recorded in `wisconsin/regulatory/primary-text-readings.md`: **REEB 25.033(11)(a) through (e)**,
**(7)(c)**, and **(13)(b)1.d**; and **§ 452.133(1)(b) and (1)(e)**.

**Appraisal method is taught because REEB 25.033(11) requires it** and because a Wisconsin licensee uses
the method to present market evidence, which **§ 452.133(1)(e)** makes a duty. **It is not taught as
appraisal practice.** Wisconsin licenses and certifies appraisers under a separate regulatory scheme
which has **not been read for this project**, and this course states no appraiser qualification,
standard or requirement.

**REEB 24.09 is named and its subject stated — misleading market values — and its text is not quoted**,
because **ch. REEB 24 has not been read from primary text for this project.** Module WI-10 names each
cited REEB 24 section on the same basis.

All arithmetic is worked in full so a reviewer can verify each figure. **The prices, rates, adjustment
amounts, capitalization rates and multipliers are illustrative teaching figures and are not market data
for any Wisconsin market.** The $1,100 per foot of frontage in Hour 53.3 is a teaching number; real
frontage adjustments vary enormously by water body and shoreline character and are derived from paired
sales.
