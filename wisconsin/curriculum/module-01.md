# Wisconsin Salesperson Pre-License — Module WI-01

## Hours 1–5 · Real property and the real estate business

**Board topics covered:** REEB 25.033(1) Real property · REEB 25.033(2) The real estate business
**Clock hours:** 5. A Wisconsin clock hour is 60 minutes.
**Hour allocation:** a StateCourse proposal for Board review. **REEB 25.033 fixes the total at 72
hours and lists nineteen topics; it allocates no hours among them.**
**Course version:** 1.0 submission draft
**Regulatory status:** Development. Not approved. No Wisconsin pre-license credit is available.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module the student will be able to:

- Distinguish land, real estate and real property, and real from personal property.
- Classify a fixture and state which factor controls.
- Name the physical and economic characteristics of real estate and explain what each causes.
- Describe the forms of home ownership a Wisconsin buyer will meet, including condominium and
  cooperative, and state what a manufactured home's treatment turns on.
- List the recurring costs of ownership and the tax and insurance questions that go to a specialist.
- Explain supply, demand and the business cycle, and read the indicators honestly.
- Name the specialties within the real estate business and say where a salesperson sits under ch. 452.

## Hour 1 — Land, real estate, real property

### 1.1 The three words — REEB 25.033(1)(a) and (b)

The rule requires instruction on "land, real estate and real property" and on "the difference between
real property and personal property." They are three ideas and a boundary, and the whole of the first
week depends on keeping them apart.

**Land** is the surface of the earth, the space above it, and the ground beneath it, to the extent
the law recognises rights in each. Land has a vertical dimension: air rights above and subsurface
rights below, each of which can be owned and conveyed separately.

**Real estate** is land plus everything permanently attached to it, by nature or by people. A stand
of timber, a barn, a well, a fence, a driveway — attach them and they stop being separate objects.

**Real property** is real estate plus **the legal interests** a person may hold in it. This is what
is actually bought and sold. When a licensee lists "a house", what is being marketed is a set of
rights: to possess, to use, to exclude, to encumber, to transfer, and to do nothing at all.

The teaching image is the **bundle of rights**, and its value is that it makes visible the fact that
rights separate. A Wisconsin seller who conveys the surface and reserves the mineral or timber rights
has sold real property and kept real property.

### 1.2 Personal property, and why the line matters

**Personal property** — chattels, personalty — is everything that is not real property: movable
things, and intangible rights not attached to land.

The distinction decides the **instrument**. Real property is conveyed by **deed**. Personal property
transfers by **bill of sale** or by delivery. REEB 25.033(8)(f)13 requires instruction on **bills of
sale** precisely because a Wisconsin transaction routinely conveys both, and the offer to purchase is
where the parties say which is which.

Note also **§ 452.133(3)(d)**, which prohibits a licensee from negotiating the sale, exchange,
purchase or rental of **personal property unless related to the transaction** — with the licensee
permitted to use a Board-approved form under s. 452.05(1)(b) for the conveyance of the seller's
interest in personal property. So the line is not merely academic; it bounds what a licensee may do.

### 1.3 Water rights — REEB 25.033(5)(d), introduced here

Wisconsin has more shoreline and more inland water than most states, and **water rights** are a
listed topic in their own right at REEB 25.033(5)(d). Module WI-04 develops them. What belongs in
Hour 1 is the vocabulary:

**Riparian rights** attach to land abutting a flowing watercourse — a river or stream. **Littoral
rights** attach to land abutting a lake or other still water. Both are **appurtenant**: they run with
the land and are not separately owned by a person.

The **public trust doctrine** in Wisconsin holds navigable waters in trust for the public, and it is
the constitutional foundation of the state's shoreland and navigable-waters regulation. What a
riparian owner may do at the water's edge — a pier, a dredging, a bank alteration, a boathouse — is
regulated, and it is regulated by the Department of Natural Resources and the county rather than
answered by a licensee.

**Ordinary high water mark** is the boundary concept that decides where public rights begin and
private rights end on a navigable water. It is determined by evidence on the ground, not by a
map, and a licensee who tells a buyer where it is has answered a question belonging to the DNR and a
surveyor.

### 1.4 The licensee's boundary, stated at Hour 1

The licence permits a person to provide **real estate brokerage services**. It does not make them a
lawyer, an appraiser, an inspector, a surveyor, a lender, or an accountant.

**REEB 24.03** — competence in area of service — is a listed topic at REEB 25.033(13)(b)1.a, and it
is the rule that says so. Module WI-10 takes it in full. From Hour 1 the habit is: when a question
belongs to another profession, say so, name the profession, and put it in writing.

## Hour 2 — Fixtures and the characteristics of real estate

### 2.1 Classification of fixtures — REEB 25.033(1)(c)

A **fixture** is an item that was personal property and became real property by attachment. Once a
fixture, it passes with the deed unless the contract says otherwise.

The test has three principal factors and two supporting ones, commonly given as **MARIA**:

**Method of annexation.** How is it attached, and what happens on removal? Bolted, wired, plumbed or
built in points to a fixture, and so does damage or a non-functioning system on removal.

**Adaptation to the real estate.** Has it been fitted to this particular property? Custom storm
windows, a built-in cabinet cut to an alcove, and the key to the front door are adapted — and
adaptation makes a fixture even where the physical attachment is slight.

**Intention** of the party who attached it, judged **objectively** by what a reasonable person would
infer from the circumstances. Courts treat intention as controlling and the first two factors as
evidence of it.

**Relationship of the parties.** The same item is treated differently between landlord and tenant
than between seller and buyer, and doubt is generally resolved in favour of the weaker position.

**Agreement**, which is decisive where it exists.

**Trade fixtures** are installed by a *tenant* for the tenant's trade or business, remain the
tenant's personal property, and must be removed before the term ends — after which they generally
pass to the landlord by **accession**. **Severance** runs the other way: standing timber is real
estate, and cut timber is personal property.

### 2.2 How Wisconsin prevents the dispute

Most fixture disputes are contracts that failed to say. Wisconsin's answer is structural: the
**Board-approved offer to purchase** under **ch. REEB 16** carries defined lists of **included items**
and **excluded items**, and the licensee's job at listing is to walk the property and fill them in
correctly.

Items to resolve before the first photograph: light fittings and chandeliers; mounted televisions and
their brackets; mirrors; appliances; window treatments and their hardware; shelving; workshop
equipment; **fuel tanks** and whether they are owned or leased; **water softeners and iron filters**,
which on a private well are frequently rented; generators; playground equipment; above-ground pools;
sheds on skids as against sheds on foundations; and anything the seller intends to take.

A leased water softener is not the seller's to convey. Discovering that at the walk-through is a
failure of the listing conversation, not of the contract.

### 2.3 Characteristics of real estate — REEB 25.033(1)(d)

**Physical characteristics**, and what each causes:

**Immobility.** A parcel cannot be moved. Demand in Madison cannot be met by shipping in surplus
houses from Green Bay. This is why real estate markets are local, why location dominates value, and
why a national statistic is a poor description of any particular market.

**Indestructibility**, or more precisely durability. The land survives; improvements decay slowly and
are expensive to remove. So the housing stock at any moment is overwhelmingly made of buildings
built in the past, under past codes, for past tastes.

**Uniqueness**, also called heterogeneity or non-homogeneity. No two parcels occupy the same space.
This is why every valuation is an estimate rather than a lookup, and why **specific performance** is
available on a land contract in a way it is not for ordinary goods.

**Economic characteristics:**

**Scarcity** — not that land has run out, but that land in a particular useful location is finite.
**Improvements** — a change to one parcel affects the value of its neighbours.
**Permanence of investment** — capital sunk into land as sewers, roads and foundations is not
recoverable.
**Area preference**, or *situs* — the observed human preference for a particular location, which is
what people actually pay for.

## Hour 3 — Home ownership, property features, and the cost of owning

### 3.1 Types of home ownership — REEB 25.033(1)(e)

**Fee simple ownership of a lot and house.** The ordinary case: the owner holds the land and the
improvements.

**Condominium.** REEB 25.033(5)(j) requires instruction on condominiums **under ss. 703.08, 703.21
and 703.33**. A condominium divides a project into individually owned **units** and **common
elements** owned in undivided shares, with **limited common elements** serving fewer than all units.
The governing instruments are the declaration, the bylaws, the recorded plat and plans, and the
rules. Wisconsin's condominium law is **ch. 703**, and this course states no requirement from it,
because the chapter has not been read from primary text for this project. What a licensee must do is
obtain the documents, the budget, the reserve position, two years of minutes, the assessment history
and any discussed special assessment — before the offer.

**Cooperative.** REEB 25.033(5)(h). A corporation owns the real property; the resident owns **shares**
and holds a **proprietary lease**. The resident therefore owns **personal property**, not real
property — no deed, financing by share loan rather than mortgage, and a board that typically has
approval rights over a transfer, subject always to fair housing law.

**Time-share.** REEB 25.033(5)(i) requires instruction on time-shares under **ss. 70.095, 707.02,
707.05, 707.40, 707.47 and 707.49**. Wisconsin regulates time-share sales and disclosure in **ch.
707**, unread here, and a licensee working in time-share must read the chapter.

**Planned communities and homeowners associations**, governed by their recorded declarations.

**Manufactured and mobile homes.** REEB 25.033(1)(f) lists "mobile homes under s. 70.043, Stats." and
**the rule itself carries a Note that s. 70.043 was repealed by 2023 Wisconsin Act 12**. A course
must therefore teach the substance without citing a repealed section as operative.

The substance is a classification question with real consequences: a manufactured home may be
**personal property**, titled and taxed as such, or it may have become **real property** where it is
placed on a permanent foundation on land the owner holds and the appropriate steps have been taken.
Which it is decides whether it conveys by deed or by title transfer, whether it can be mortgaged as
real estate, and how it is taxed. **The current Wisconsin treatment must be read from the statutes as
they now stand**, and a transaction involving a manufactured home goes to the closing agent and to
counsel early.

### 3.2 Property features — REEB 25.033(1)(h)

What a buyer is actually evaluating: site characteristics — lot size, frontage, topography, drainage,
soils, exposure, trees; the structure — style, age, size, layout, condition, materials; systems —
heating, cooling, electrical service, plumbing, water supply and wastewater; **energy performance**,
which in a Wisconsin climate is a running cost rather than a preference; outbuildings; and location
attributes — schools, employment, services, and what is next door and might change.

A licensee describes these accurately and does not characterise them. Hour 2 of Module WI-10 takes
the rule that makes this a legal obligation rather than a habit.

### 3.3 Ownership expenses — REEB 25.033(1)(g)

The recurring costs a buyer must plan for, and which a licensee should be able to list without
prompting:

**Property taxes**, which in Wisconsin are levied by the municipality and are billed in December for
the year just assessed, payable in instalments — Module WI-05 takes ss. 74.15 and 74.47.
**Homeowner's insurance**, and separately **flood insurance** where required.
**Mortgage principal and interest**, and any **mortgage insurance**.
**Utilities**, with a Wisconsin heating season that makes fuel a material line.
**Maintenance**, conventionally estimated as a percentage of value annually, and **capital
replacement** — roof, furnace, water heater, windows, well pump, septic — which does not stop.
**Association assessments**, where applicable, and the special assessment risk.
**Snow removal, lawn care, and seasonal costs.**

The professional point: the payment a lender qualifies a buyer on is not the cost of ownership, and a
licensee who lets a buyer conflate the two has not served them.

## Hour 4 — Investment, tax, and insurance

### 4.1 Investment considerations — REEB 25.033(1)(i)

Four sources of return from owning: **cash flow** where the property produces income;
**appreciation**, which is expected rather than promised; **equity buildup** as amortisation retires
principal; and **tax treatment**, which depends entirely on the owner's circumstances.

Against them: **illiquidity** — selling takes months and costs a substantial share of the price;
**indivisibility**; **concentration** of household wealth in one undiversified asset; **management
burden**; **physical risk**; **market risk**; and **regulatory risk**.

**Leverage** is the use of borrowed money to control an asset larger than the buyer's own capital.
It **amplifies whatever is happening**, in both directions. Where the return on the asset exceeds the
cost of debt, borrowing raises the return on equity; where it does not, borrowing lowers it and
magnifies loss.

**Appreciation is not guaranteed**, and a licensee who tells a client a house is a sure investment
has made a representation about future value that nobody can support — which is a **misleading
statement of market value** problem under **REEB 24.09** and a reasonable-skill-and-care problem
under **§ 452.133(1)(b)**.

### 4.2 Tax benefits for home ownership — REEB 25.033(1)(j)

The rule requires the topic to be taught. It does not make a licensee a tax adviser, and the
professional treatment is to name the concepts so a client knows what to ask.

**Mortgage interest** and **property tax** deductions, both subject to federal limitations that
change and both dependent on whether the taxpayer itemises.
**Exclusion of gain on the sale of a principal residence**, subject to ownership and use requirements
and dollar limits set by federal law.
**Basis** — broadly purchase price plus capital improvements — and **gain** on sale.
**Depreciation** on income-producing property, on which **land is not depreciable**.
**Deferred exchanges** under section 1031, with their identification and completion periods.
**Wisconsin's own credits and relief programmes**, including the school levy and first dollar credits
that appear on a Wisconsin tax bill, and the **lottery and gaming credit**, which is claimed by an
owner who uses the property as a primary residence and which appears on the bill only if claimed.

**This course states no figure, limit, rate or threshold for any of them.** Every one depends on the
taxpayer's circumstances and on law that changes.

**§ 452.133(2)(am)** is the section that makes the boundary a statutory one: a firm owes a client, on
request, information and advice on matters material to the transaction **and within the scope of the
knowledge, skills and training required under ch. 452**. Tax advice is outside that scope. The
professional sentence is: *here is the concept and here is who answers it, and I am putting that in
writing.*

### 4.3 Homeowner's insurance — REEB 25.033(1)(k)

A homeowners policy is conventionally organised as **Coverage A** dwelling, **B** other structures,
**C** personal property, **D** loss of use, **E** personal liability, and **F** medical payments to
others.

**Named peril** forms cover only what they list; **open peril** or all-risk forms cover everything
except what they exclude — so reading the exclusions is how one reads an open-peril policy.

**Replacement cost** pays to replace without deduction for depreciation; **actual cash value**
deducts it, and the difference on a fifteen-year-old roof is large. **Extended** or **guaranteed
replacement cost** endorsements address rebuilding costs above the policy limit.

**Coinsurance** reduces a partial-loss recovery proportionally where the insured carries less than a
stated percentage of replacement value.

**Common exclusions**: **flood**, earth movement, ordinary wear and neglect, mould in many forms,
intentional acts, and business use. **Ordinance or law** coverage — the cost of rebuilding to current
code — is a common endorsement rather than a standard inclusion.

**Flood is excluded and requires a separate policy**, from the National Flood Insurance Program or
the private market. REEB 25.033(18)(g) lists **flood insurance** as a required topic in its own right
and REEB 25.033(18)(f) lists **floodplains under s. 87.30 Stats. and ss. NR 116.01 and 116.06**.
Module WI-12 takes them.

**Condominium unit owners** need a unit-owner policy whose scope depends entirely on where the
association's master policy stops — a boundary set by the declaration.

**Landlords** need a landlord or dwelling-fire policy, and **tenants** need renters coverage, because
the landlord's policy covers the building and not the tenant's belongings or liability.

A licensee identifies the questions and refers them to an insurance professional. Telling a buyer
what a policy will cover is not brokerage.

## Hour 5 — The real estate business

### 5.1 Real estate specialties — REEB 25.033(2)(a)

**Residential brokerage**; **commercial and industrial brokerage**; **farm and land brokerage**,
which in Wisconsin is a substantial specialty of its own and carries farmland preservation, drainage,
and current-use questions; **property management**; **appraisal**, separately licensed;
**mortgage banking**, which REEB 25.033(19)(g) reaches through **s. 224.71**; **title and closing
services**; **development and construction**; **counselling and consulting**; and **auction**.

A salesperson should be able to say which specialty a question belongs to, because most of what goes
wrong is a licensee answering a question from a specialty they do not practise.

### 5.2 The real estate market and what moves it — REEB 25.033(2)(b) and (c)

**Demand** is driven by population and household formation, employment and wages, the cost and
availability of credit, expectations, and the price of substitutes such as rentals. It changes in
weeks.

**Supply** is the existing stock plus what new construction can deliver — and new construction takes
land assembly, entitlement, financing, permitting, and building, which in Wisconsin is further
compressed by a construction season that closes in winter. It changes in years.

So the central prediction: **in the short run supply is close to fixed, and a demand increase shows
up almost entirely as price.** Days on market fall, inventory falls, and multiple offers appear. New
supply arrives later, sometimes after demand has moved again — and that mismatch is the structural
reason real estate cycles.

### 5.3 Business cycles — REEB 25.033(2)(d)

**Expansion** — demand growing faster than supply; vacancy below its long-run average; rents and
prices rising; construction beginning to respond.
**Oversupply or contraction** — construction started during expansion completes; supply catches up
and passes demand; vacancy rises; price growth decelerates; concessions appear.
**Recession** — demand below supply; vacancy above average; prices falling; construction stopped;
distressed sales entering.
**Recovery** — the surplus absorbing; vacancy falling toward average; prices lagging; construction
not yet resumed.

The most common student error is to equate the cycle with interest rates. Rates are one input to
demand. **Local employment is usually the stronger driver**, and in Wisconsin's manufacturing and
agricultural markets a single employer or a commodity price can move a local market on its own.

### 5.4 Reading the indicators honestly

**Months of inventory** — active listings divided by average monthly closed sales. **Absorption rate**
— the pace of take-up. **Days on market**, median rather than mean. **List-to-sale price ratio**.
**New listings against closed sales**, which turns before the stock does. **Vacancy rate** for rental
property.

Two constraints belong with them.

**The data is historical.** Closed sales report decisions made weeks earlier, and nothing in the set
describes the future. **§ 452.133(1)(e)** requires a firm to provide **accurate information about
market conditions** on request — accurate, which means what the evidence shows, not an extrapolation
presented as a forecast.

**Aggregates hide the property.** A change in a median can be entirely a change in what sold.

### 5.5 Real estate practice under ch. 452 — REEB 25.033(2)(e)

**Chapter 452** is the Real Estate Practice chapter, and it is where the licence, the duties, the
prohibitions and the discipline live. The sections a salesperson meets constantly, each of which a
later module develops:

**§ 452.133** — duties of licensees and prohibitions. Module WI-02.
**§ 452.135** — the requirement for an agency agreement and disclosure of agency. Module WI-02.
**§ 452.139** — revisions to common law duties and responsibilities. Module WI-02.
**§ 452.136** — advertising. Module WI-10.
**§ 452.13** — trust accounts, and the rules promulgated under § 452.13(5). Module WI-13.
**§ 452.23** — disclosures and inspections by brokers and salespersons, including particular disclosure limits, third-party-report conditions, contradictory facts, and the prudent-licensee standard of care. This is not the Department's investigation-power statute. Module WI-10, section 62.1.
**§ 452.05(1)(b)** — the Board's power to approve forms, which is why Wisconsin brokerage runs on
approved forms at all. Module WI-06.

And the structural fact a Wisconsin salesperson must absorb early: **the duties in § 452.133(1) are
owed to every party to a transaction, not only to clients.** Seven of them, including a **written**
material-adverse-facts disclosure duty and a confidentiality duty that **continues after the
transaction is complete**. Module WI-02 is built on it.

### Module WI-01 engagement check

**Question.** At a listing appointment the seller shows you a water softener and an iron filter in
the basement, a propane tank behind the garage, and a chandelier in the dining room that belonged to
her mother. She says "obviously the chandelier is coming with me". What do you do, and what rule are
you working under?

**Expected answer.** All four items are resolved **in writing, at listing, before any photograph is
taken**, and recorded in the included and excluded items of the **Board-approved offer to purchase
under ch. REEB 16**.

The **chandelier** is a fixture on the ordinary test — attached, adapted, and objectively intended to
be permanent — so the seller's wish is not the test and it will convey unless it is expressly
excluded. The professional answer is to have it removed and replaced **before marketing**, not after
a buyer has seen it.

The **water softener and iron filter** and the **propane tank** raise a different question:
**ownership**. Each is frequently **leased or rented** rather than owned, and a leased item is not
the seller's to convey. The lease must be produced and the arrangement — assignment, termination, or
exclusion — written into the offer.

The rule I am working under is **§ 452.133(1)(c)**: the firm owes **every party** the duty to timely
disclose **in writing** all material adverse facts the firm knows and the party does not know or
cannot discover through reasonably vigilant observation. A buyer cannot discover by looking that the
softener is rented. And **§ 452.133(1)(b)** — reasonable skill and care — is what makes doing this at
listing rather than at the walk-through part of the job.
