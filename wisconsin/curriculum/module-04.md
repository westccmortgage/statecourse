# Wisconsin Salesperson Pre-License — Module WI-04

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module the student will be able to:

- Name the four government powers and match a fact pattern to the correct one.
- Classify freehold and leasehold estates and diagram present and future interests.
- Identify an encumbrance by type and say who enforces it.
- Explain riparian and littoral rights, the public trust doctrine, and where a water question goes.
- Distinguish the forms of co-ownership Wisconsin recognises, including its marital property regime.
- Read a rectangular survey description and compute the acreage of an aliquot part.

## Hour 18 — Government powers and estates in land

### 18.1 The four powers — REEB 25.033(5)(a)

Every public limitation on land traces to one of four. The mnemonic is **PETE**.

**Police power** — regulation for public health, safety, morals and general welfare. Zoning, building
codes, subdivision control, shoreland and floodplain regulation, and environmental controls all sit
here. **No compensation is paid**, because nothing is taken; use is regulated. In Wisconsin the power
belongs to the state and is delegated to counties, cities, villages and towns.

**Eminent domain** — the power to take private property for public use with **just compensation**,
executed through a **condemnation** proceeding. Partial takings are common — a strip for a highway
widening — and compensation covers damage to the remainder.

**Taxation** — the levy against property to fund government, backed by a lien and ultimately by sale.
Module WI-05 takes Wisconsin's provisions.

**Escheat** — property passing to the state where an owner dies with no will and no heirs. Rare, and
the reason the list is four.

### 18.2 Fee simple — REEB 25.033(5)(b)

**Fee simple absolute** is the largest estate: potentially infinite, freely transferable during life
and at death, subject only to the four powers and to private restrictions a predecessor accepted. A
conveyance is presumed to convey a fee simple absolute unless the instrument says otherwise.

**Defeasible fees** may end on a stated event.

A **fee simple determinable** ends **automatically** when the event occurs, and the grantor retains a
**possibility of reverter**. The language is durational — "so long as", "while", "until".

A **fee simple subject to a condition subsequent** gives the grantor a **right of re-entry** that must
be exercised. The language is conditional — "but if", "provided that", "on condition that".

Both appear in the record and belong to the title examiner and to counsel.

### 18.3 Life estates

A **life estate** is a freehold measured by a life. The **life tenant** has full use and possession,
takes the income, and may convey what they hold — but may not convey more than they own and may not
commit **waste**: they must not damage the property or let it deteriorate, and ordinarily must pay
taxes and interest on existing debt.

When the measuring life ends, either a **reversion** returns the property to the grantor or the
grantor's heirs, or a **remainder** passes it to a named third party.

A **life estate pur autre vie** is measured by another's life and is the one life estate that can be
inherited while the measuring life continues.

**The listing consequence is practical and immediate:** a life tenant alone cannot convey a fee. Every
holder of an interest must sign. Establishing that at listing rather than at closing is the licensee's
job.

### 18.4 Leasehold estates, named

**Estate for years** — a fixed term with a definite beginning and end; it expires without notice, and
its length is irrelevant to the name. **Periodic estate** — renews automatically until notice.
**Estate at will** — no fixed period, terminable by either party subject to statutory notice.
**Estate at sufferance** — a holdover after a lawful entry, distinguished from trespass only by that
lawful entry.

Module WI-11 takes leases, including **residential rental practices under ATCP 134.02, 134.06 and
134.09**.

## Hour 19 — Encumbrances

### 19.1 What an encumbrance is — REEB 25.033(5)(c)

An **encumbrance** is a claim, right or interest held by someone other than the owner that affects
title or limits use. Encumbrances travel with the land and do not prevent transfer.

They fall into two families: those affecting **use** — easements, restrictive covenants,
encroachments — and those affecting **title as security for money** — liens, which are Module WI-05.

### 19.2 Easements

An **easement** is a non-possessory right to use another's land for a stated purpose, and it is an
interest in land.

An **easement appurtenant** benefits a parcel: the benefited parcel is the **dominant tenement**, the
burdened one the **servient tenement**, and it **runs with the land**, passing automatically to the
next owner whether or not anyone mentions it. Shared driveways, access to a back lot, and drainage are
the everyday cases.

An **easement in gross** benefits a person or entity rather than a parcel — utility easements being
the common example, and commercial easements in gross being assignable.

**Creation.** By **express grant or reservation** in a deed. By **implication**, where a use was
apparent, continuous and necessary when a parcel was divided. By **necessity**, the landlocked-parcel
case, requiring common ownership at severance. By **prescription**, from open, notorious, continuous
and adverse use for the statutory period. By **condemnation**.

**Termination.** By merger, release, abandonment shown by conduct rather than mere non-use, the end of
the necessity, or the easement's own terms.

**This course states no Wisconsin prescriptive-easement or adverse-possession period**, because those
provisions have not been read from primary text for this project. Both are legal determinations in any
event.

Two Wisconsin access questions recur and belong in every rural and waterfront intake: **access to
water**, which may be a deeded easement, a prescriptive right, an outlot, or mere tolerance; and
**private and town roads**, where whether the road is public, private, or a discontinued way — and
who plows it — decides whether a buyer can reach the house in February.

### 19.3 Licence, encroachment, party wall

A **licence** is revocable personal permission and is **not** an interest in land. The neighbour who
has "always" crossed the corner usually holds one — unless the use has ripened into a prescriptive
easement, which is a legal determination on facts nobody in the conversation has established.

An **encroachment** is an unauthorised physical intrusion across a boundary. It is found by
**survey**, not by looking, and long-standing encroachments can mature into rights.

A **party wall** is a shared wall on a boundary, each owner typically owning to the centre with an
easement of support in the other half.

### 19.4 Private land-use controls

**Restrictive covenants** are private limits on use, recorded so they bind successors. They regulate
setbacks, minimum sizes, materials, outbuildings, signage, and — increasingly — short-term rental.

Three points:

**They can be stricter than zoning**, and where zoning permits and a covenant forbids, the owner is
bound by both and must satisfy the stricter.

**They are enforced privately** — by an association or a neighbour — not by the municipality.

**Discriminatory covenants are void and must not be honoured.** Historic racial covenants remain
physically present in Wisconsin chains of title. Repeating one as though it operated is itself a fair
housing problem, and Module WI-10 takes **equal rights under s. 106.50**.

**REEB 25.033(17)(e)** lists private land-use controls as a required topic in its own right, and
Module WI-12 develops them alongside the public controls.

## Hour 20 — Water rights

### 20.1 Why Wisconsin gives this its own paragraph

**REEB 25.033(5)(d)** lists **water rights** as a required topic. Wisconsin has more than fifteen
thousand inland lakes, two Great Lakes shorelines, and a public trust doctrine of constitutional
standing, and waterfront property is a substantial part of the state's market.

### 20.2 The vocabulary

**Riparian rights** attach to land abutting a **flowing** watercourse — a river or stream.
**Littoral rights** attach to land abutting a **lake** or other still water. Both are **appurtenant**:
they belong to the land, run with it, and are not owned separately by a person.

**Accretion** is the gradual deposit of soil by water, which the riparian owner gains. **Reliction**
is the gradual withdrawal of water exposing land, which the owner also gains. **Erosion** is gradual
loss. **Avulsion** is sudden change, which generally does **not** move the boundary.

**The ordinary high water mark** is the concept that separates private riparian ownership from the
public's rights on a navigable water. It is determined by physical evidence on the ground, not by a
map or a plat, and it is not a licensee's to identify.

### 20.3 The public trust doctrine

Wisconsin holds its **navigable waters in trust for the public**. The doctrine is the foundation of
the state's regulation of piers, dredging, bank alteration, boathouses, filling, and shoreland
development.

Two consequences for brokerage:

**What a riparian owner may do at the water's edge is regulated.** A pier may need a permit or may sit
under an exemption; a boathouse may be a nonconforming structure that cannot be replaced; bank
alteration and dredging are permitted activities; and **shoreland zoning** administered by the county
governs setbacks, vegetation removal, impervious surface and nonconforming structures.

**Navigability is a legal determination**, and whether a water is navigable decides whether the
doctrine applies at all.

### 20.4 What a licensee does

Nothing but route. The questions and their offices:

**Is the water navigable, and where is the ordinary high water mark?** Department of Natural Resources,
and a surveyor.
**Is the existing pier, boathouse or shore structure permitted, and can it be replaced?** DNR and the
county zoning office, **in writing**.
**What does county shoreland zoning permit here — setback, vegetation, impervious surface?** County
zoning, in writing.
**Is any part of the parcel in a floodplain?** REEB 25.033(18)(f) — **s. 87.30 Stats. and ss. NR
116.01 and 116.06** — the municipality and the mapping.
**Are there wetlands?** REEB 25.033(18)(h) — **s. 23.32** — the DNR.
**Is there a private well, and what is the water quality?** A certified laboratory.

A licensee who tells a buyer they can build a pier, remove trees for a view, or replace the boathouse
has answered a question belonging to the DNR and the county — and has done it in the area where
Wisconsin enforcement is most active.

## Hour 21 — Forms of ownership

### 21.1 Severalty and co-ownership — REEB 25.033(5)(e)

**Severalty** is ownership by one person or one entity alone.

**Tenancy in common** — each co-owner holds an **undivided fractional interest**, meaning each may
possess the whole rather than a marked-off part. Shares need not be equal. There is **no
survivorship**: on death an owner's share passes to their heirs or devisees. Any co-tenant may
transfer their share without the others' consent, and any may seek **partition**.

**Joint tenancy** adds the **right of survivorship**: on a joint tenant's death the interest passes to
the survivors outside probate. The common-law unities are **time, title, interest and possession**. A
joint tenant who conveys their interest severs the joint tenancy as to that share, and the transferee
holds as a tenant in common.

### 21.2 Wisconsin is a marital property state

This is the single most distinctive ownership fact in Wisconsin, and a licensee arriving from another
state must not carry that state's assumptions here.

**REEB 25.033(19)(f)** requires instruction on **property rights of married persons under ss. 766.31,
766.51, 766.60 and 766.63**, and **REEB 25.033(5)(k)** requires instruction on **homestead under ss.
706.01(7) and 766.605**.

**Wisconsin's Marital Property Act, ch. 766**, is a community-property-style regime. Its general
effect is that property acquired by spouses during marriage is presumptively **marital property** in
which each spouse holds an undivided one-half interest, subject to classification rules,
**individual property** categories, and any **marital property agreement** the spouses have made. It
also recognises **survivorship marital property**.

**Ch. 766 has not been read from primary text for this project, and this course states no rule from
it.** What it states is the **consequence for practice**, which is unavoidable and must be taught:

**A spouse may hold an interest, and a spouse's signature may be required to convey or to encumber,
even where that spouse does not appear on the deed.** The **homestead** provisions make this
particularly acute for the residence.

**So the question is asked at listing, every time**: is the seller married; is the property the
homestead; is there a marital property agreement; and who must sign? And it is answered by the
**closing agent, the title company and counsel** — never by the licensee.

A licensee who takes a listing signed by one spouse alone, on a Wisconsin homestead, has taken a
listing that may not be performable.

### 21.3 Trusts and business entities — REEB 25.033(5)(f) and (g)

In a **trust**, the trustee holds legal title and manages; the beneficiary takes the benefit. **The
trustee signs.** A **revocable living trust** is a common way to hold a residence.

Property may also be owned by a **corporation**, a **partnership**, a **limited liability company**,
or another entity. Two consequences: the entity's **authority to sign** is a legal question evidenced
by organisational documents and resolutions and verified by the closing agent and counsel; and the
entity's involvement does not change the firm's § 452.133 duties.

Note **§ 452.133(3)(b)**: a licensee may not act in a transaction on behalf of **any organisation or
business entity in which the licensee has an interest** without the **prior written consent of all
parties, obtained in the transaction contract.**

## Hour 22 — Cooperatives, condominiums, and time-shares

### 22.1 Cooperatives — REEB 25.033(5)(h)

A corporation owns the real property. The resident owns **shares** and holds a **proprietary lease**.
The resident therefore owns **personal property**, not real property: no deed, financing by share loan
rather than mortgage, a different title and closing mechanic entirely, and a board that typically has
approval rights over a transfer — subject always to fair housing law.

### 22.2 Condominiums — REEB 25.033(5)(j)

Instruction is required **under ss. 703.08, 703.21 and 703.33**.

A condominium divides a project into individually owned **units** and **common elements** owned in
undivided shares by all unit owners, with **limited common elements** — a balcony, an assigned parking
stall, a storage locker — serving fewer than all units. The unit is a **volume of space**, described by
reference to a recorded condominium plat and by elevation.

The instruments are the **declaration**, the **bylaws**, the recorded **plat and plans**, and the
**rules**. The declaration fixes each unit's percentage interest, which usually determines both voting
power and assessment share.

**This course states no requirement from ch. 703**, which has not been read from primary text for this
project — including anything about the disclosure materials a Wisconsin condominium seller must
furnish or any cancellation right attached to them. **Read ch. 703 before stating one.**

What a licensee must do: obtain the declaration, bylaws and rules; the **budget** and current
assessments; the **reserve position**; **two years of minutes**, where a special assessment is
discussed long before it is levied; the assessment and litigation history; the **insurance summary**
and where the master policy stops; and the restrictions on **leasing**, pets, vehicles and
alterations — before the offer.

### 22.3 Time-shares — REEB 25.033(5)(i)

Instruction is required **under ss. 70.095, 707.02, 707.05, 707.40, 707.47 and 707.49**.

A time-share divides the right to occupy a property among many purchasers by period. Wisconsin
regulates time-share sales, disclosure and cancellation in **ch. 707**, and taxation of time-share
property is reached at **s. 70.095**.

**Ch. 707 has not been read from primary text for this project and no requirement from it is stated
here.** A licensee practising in time-share must read the chapter, and a licensee who meets a
time-share in a general residential practice should treat it as a specialty and route it.

## Hour 23 — Legal descriptions

### 23.1 Why an address will not do — REEB 25.033(6)(a)

A street address identifies a mailbox. A **legal description** identifies a unique parcel in a way a
surveyor could reproduce on the ground. Deeds, mortgages, plats and judgments use legal descriptions;
offers should.

### 23.2 The three methods

**Metes and bounds.** Describes a boundary by walking it: a **point of beginning**, then **calls** of
bearing and distance, closing back at the point of beginning. A call reads *N 42° 15′ 00″ E, 318.6
feet to an iron pin*. **Monuments control over courses and distances**, because a monument is what a
person on the ground can find.

**Rectangular or government survey.** **Wisconsin is a public land survey state**, and this system
governs most of its land. A student must be able to work it.

**Lot and block**, by reference to a **recorded plat**: *Lot 7, Block 3, Sunset Ridge, in the City of
___, ___ County, Wisconsin*, with the plat recorded in the register of deeds. The plat carries the
metes and bounds of each lot, the streets, the easements, and the surveyor's certification. **A lot
does not exist until the plat is recorded.**

Wisconsin also uses **certified survey maps** — CSMs — for smaller divisions, recorded and referenced
in the same way. Module WI-12 takes them with subdivision regulation.

### 23.3 The rectangular survey system, worked — REEB 25.033(6)(b)

**Principal meridians** run north–south; **base lines** run east–west. Wisconsin's land is surveyed
from the **Fourth Principal Meridian**.

**Township lines** run east–west, six miles apart, creating **tiers** numbered north or south of the
base line. **Range lines** run north–south, six miles apart, numbered east or west of the principal
meridian.

A **township** is the six-mile square where a tier and a range intersect — **36 square miles**.

A township contains **36 sections**, each **one mile square** and **640 acres**, numbered **1 through
36 in a boustrophedonic pattern**: section 1 in the north-east corner, running west to section 6,
then dropping south and running east to section 12, and so on, ending with section 36 in the
south-east corner.

**Aliquot parts.** A section divides into halves and quarters, and each of those divides again. The
arithmetic:

**A section** = 640 acres.
**A half section** = 320 acres.
**A quarter section** = 160 acres.
**A quarter of a quarter** = 40 acres.
**A half of a quarter of a quarter** = 20 acres.

**Read a description from right to left**, taking a fraction of what precedes.

*Worked:* **"The NE¼ of the SW¼ of Section 14"** — start with the section, 640 acres; take the SW
quarter, 160; take the NE quarter of that, **40 acres**.

*Worked:* **"The S½ of the NW¼ of the NE¼"** — 640 × ¼ (NE) = 160; × ¼ (NW) = 40; × ½ (S) = **20
acres**.

*Worked:* **"The W½ of the SE¼ and the NE¼ of the SE¼"** — SE quarter is 160; W half of it is 80; NE
quarter of the SE quarter is 40; total **120 acres**.

**Government lots** are the irregular parcels created where a section is cut by a lake, a river, or a
state boundary and cannot be divided into regular aliquot parts. **They are extremely common on
Wisconsin waterfront**, and their acreage is stated on the plat rather than computed — which is
exactly why a waterfront parcel's acreage cannot be derived from its description.

### 23.4 Land units and measurements

**1 acre = 43,560 square feet.** **1 mile = 5,280 feet.** **1 square mile = 640 acres.**
**1 rod = 16.5 feet.** **1 chain = 66 feet = 4 rods.** **80 chains = 1 mile.**

*Worked:* a lot measuring 264 feet by 330 feet. 264 × 330 = 87,120 square feet; ÷ 43,560 = **2 acres**.
*Worked:* 40 rods = 40 × 16.5 = **660 feet**, which is an eighth of a mile.

### 23.5 Survey and the licensee

A **survey** is a licensed surveyor's determination of where the boundaries are on the ground, and it
is the only thing that answers a boundary question. It finds what a title search cannot:
encroachments, structures over setback lines, fences that do not follow the line, and a septic field
on the wrong parcel.

When to recommend one: irregular parcels; older metes-and-bounds descriptions; government lots;
visible fences or structures near a line; planned construction; unimproved land; **waterfront**; and
any mention of a boundary dispute.

Reading a plat and telling a buyer where the line is falls outside the licence.

### Module WI-04 engagement check

**Question.** A seller wants to list forty acres described as "the SE¼ of the NW¼ of Section 9", with
a cabin near a lake at the north edge of the parcel and a driveway that crosses a neighbour's land.
The seller is married; his wife is not on the deed and "has nothing to do with it". What do you
establish before you take the listing?

**Expected answer.**

**The acreage checks out on its face**: 640 × ¼ (NW) × ¼ (SE) = **40 acres**. But if any part of the
parcel is cut by the lake it may in fact be a **government lot** rather than a regular aliquot part,
in which case the acreage is what the plat states and cannot be computed from the description. That
goes to the plat and to a surveyor.

**The wife's interest is not the seller's to characterise.** **Wisconsin is a marital property state**
under **ch. 766**, and **REEB 25.033(5)(k) and (19)(f)** require the homestead and marital property
provisions to be taught for exactly this reason: a spouse may hold an interest and a spouse's
signature may be required to convey or encumber **even where that spouse is not on the deed**. I do
not decide it — I raise it at listing and route it to the **title company, the closing agent and
counsel**, because a listing signed by one spouse alone may not be performable.

**The driveway** is a title and boundary question: it may be a recorded easement appurtenant running
with the land, a prescriptive easement, a revocable licence, or an encroachment — and which it is
decides whether the buyer has access. Register of deeds, a **survey**, and counsel.

**The lake frontage** raises the **ordinary high water mark**, the **public trust doctrine**, **county
shoreland zoning**, and whether the cabin is a **nonconforming structure** that may or may not be
expanded or replaced. Those go to the **DNR** and the **county zoning office in writing**, before the
buyer relies on any plan for the property.
