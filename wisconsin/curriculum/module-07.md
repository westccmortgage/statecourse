# Wisconsin Salesperson Pre-License — Module WI-07

## Hours 40–44 · Title records and transfers of title

**Board topic covered:** REEB 25.033(9) Title records and transfers of title

## Learning objectives

By the end of this module the student will be able to:

- State what ch. 706 requires of a valid conveyance and where each requirement comes from.
- Distinguish the Wisconsin deed forms by what each warrants.
- Distinguish voluntary from involuntary alienation and name the Wisconsin routes of each.
- Explain probate's effect on a listing and on a closing.
- Explain recording, notice, and the difference between recording and transferring title.
- Read a title commitment well enough to separate requirements from exceptions.

## Hour 40 — What a conveyance is and what makes one valid

### 40.1 Conveyance defined — REEB 25.033(9)(h)

**§ 706.01** defines "conveyance" for the purposes of Wisconsin's conveyancing chapter, and the
definition matters because **ch. 706** is what governs the transaction's paper. The chapter reaches
deeds, land contracts, mortgages, leases of stated length, easements, and other instruments affecting
an interest in land.

**Ch. 706 has not been read from primary text for this project and this course states no definition,
exception or formal requirement from it.** What it states is the structure a licensee must recognise
and the routing.

### 40.2 Requirements of a valid conveyance — REEB 25.033(9)(a)

The rule requires instruction on the requirements **under ss. 240.10, 706.02 and 706.03**.

**§ 240.10** is Wisconsin's statute of frauds provision governing **real estate broker contracts** — it
is why a Wisconsin listing must be in writing and signed, and REEB 25.033(8)(f)1 cites it for listing
agreements.

**§ 706.02** sets out the formal requisites of a conveyance: broadly, a writing identifying the parties,
the land and the interest conveyed, signed by or on behalf of each of the grantors, with the formalities
the section prescribes — and Wisconsin's **marital property and homestead** provisions mean that
identifying "each of the grantors" is not always obvious from the deed.

**§ 706.03** addresses **agency and authority** — who may sign for whom, and the authority a signer must
have.

**Because the sections have not been read**, this course teaches the **consequences a licensee acts on**
rather than the elements:

**Everyone whose signature is required must sign**, and who that is includes co-owners, a life tenant
and the remaindermen, a trustee, a personal representative, an entity's authorised signatory, an
attorney-in-fact under a proper power, and — on a Wisconsin **homestead** — **a spouse who may not appear
on the deed at all**.

**Authority must be evidenced**, and it is verified by the **closing agent and the title company**.

**Both questions are established at listing**, because a signature problem discovered at closing is a
closing that does not happen.

### 40.3 Delivery and acceptance

A deed takes effect on **delivery** with the grantor's present intent to pass title, and on the
grantee's **acceptance**, which is presumed where the conveyance benefits them.

The consequence students most often get wrong: **recording does not transfer title.** A signed,
notarised deed locked in a drawer transfers nothing. Title passes on delivery; **recording gives notice
to the world**, and Hour 43 is why that matters enough to do immediately.

### 40.4 The licensee's boundary

**In Wisconsin the deed is prepared by an attorney or by the closing agent**, not by the licensee.
Selecting between deed forms is legal advice. A licensee asked "which deed should we use?" has one
correct answer: *that is a question for your attorney or the closing agent, and here is how to reach
them* — which is the sentence **§ 452.135**'s own printed disclosure supplies.

## Hour 41 — Types of deeds

### 41.1 The Wisconsin forms — REEB 25.033(9)(b)

Wisconsin deed forms are distinguished by **what the grantor warrants**.

**Warranty deed.** The grantor warrants title against all defects, whenever arising — including before
the grantor owned the property. The strongest deed and the norm in Wisconsin residential resale.

**Special warranty deed.** The grantor warrants only against defects arising **during the grantor's own
ownership**. Anything earlier is not warranted. Used by fiduciaries, entities, relocation companies, and
commonly by builders on new construction.

**Quit claim deed.** The grantor conveys whatever interest the grantor has — **which may be nothing** —
and warrants nothing at all. It is a **curative** instrument: clearing a possible interest, correcting a
name, releasing a spouse's or a former spouse's interest, resolving a boundary, or conveying between
family members. **A quit claim is not a lesser way to buy a house**, and a buyer accepting one is
accepting the risk that the grantor owned nothing.

**Trustee's deed**, **personal representative's deed**, **guardian's deed**, and **sheriff's deed** —
fiduciary and judicial deeds, each carrying the authority under which the signer acts, and each raising
the question whether that authority was properly obtained and exercised.

**Deed in lieu of foreclosure**, by which a borrower conveys to the lender voluntarily. Junior liens
generally survive it, which is why lenders often refuse it.

**Land contract** — REEB 25.033(10)(e) — is not a deed but is a Wisconsin conveyancing structure in
which legal title stays with the **vendor** until payment is complete while the **vendee** takes
possession and equitable title. Module WI-08 takes it, and a **deed** is delivered at the end.

### 41.2 The transfer return

Every Wisconsin conveyance recorded carries an **electronic real estate transfer return** and, unless
exempt, the **real estate transfer fee** under **ss. 77.22 and 77.27** — Module WI-05 Hour 27.3. The
closing agent prepares the return and computes the fee.

## Hour 42 — Involuntary alienation, probate, and transfer by will

### 42.1 Involuntary alienation — REEB 25.033(9)(c)

Title passes without the owner's agreement in several ways, and each appears in a chain of title as
something other than an ordinary deed:

**Foreclosure**, which in Wisconsin is **judicial** for residential mortgages — a court action, a
judgment, a redemption period, and a sheriff's sale confirmed by the court.

**Tax delinquency**, ending in the county taking title.

**Eminent domain**, through condemnation.

**Adverse possession**, where possession that is open, notorious, continuous, exclusive and hostile for
the statutory period ripens into title. **This course states no Wisconsin period**, which has not been
read from primary text for this project, and adverse possession is a legal determination in any event.

**Partition**, where co-owners cannot agree and a court divides or orders a sale.

**Escheat**, where an owner dies with no will and no heirs.

**Bankruptcy**, where a trustee administers and may sell.

Each carries its own question about whether the process was completed correctly — which is why title
insurers examine such chains closely and why a transaction with one in it takes longer.

### 42.2 Probate — REEB 25.033(9)(d)

**Probate** is the court-supervised administration of a deceased person's estate. Its brokerage
consequences are practical and recurring.

**Who may sign.** The **personal representative** appointed by the court, acting under **letters** whose
scope must be checked. A listing signed by a family member who has not been appointed is a listing that
cannot close.

**Whether court approval is required** for the sale, which depends on the form of administration and on
the will's terms.

**How long it takes.** Weeks at best, months commonly, and a purchase contract with a closing date that
ignores the estate's timetable is a contract that will need amendment.

**What deed the estate gives** — typically a **personal representative's deed** rather than a warranty
deed, which changes what the buyer is getting.

**Whether the estate is even in probate.** Wisconsin has mechanisms by which some property passes
without full administration — **transfer on death** designations for real property, marital property
agreements with survivorship features, and survivorship marital property — and which applies is a
**legal** question for the title company and counsel.

**This course states no Wisconsin probate procedure, threshold or period**, because ch. 851 to 879 have
not been read from primary text for this project. What a licensee does is **ask, at listing, whether an
owner has died**, and route the answer.

### 42.3 Transfer by will — REEB 25.033(9)(e)

A **testator** disposes of real property by **devise** to a **devisee**. A will is effective only on
death and only if admitted to probate, and until then the intended devisee owns nothing.

Where there is no will, **intestate succession** distributes the estate by statute, and Wisconsin's
**marital property** regime affects the result.

The recurring problem in a Wisconsin chain of title is an **unprobated estate** — a deceased owner in
the chain whose estate was never administered. Title cannot be conveyed until it is, and the time that
takes is measured in months. It is discovered when the title commitment arrives, which is why title is
ordered early.

## Hour 43 — Public records, recording, and notice

### 43.1 The register of deeds — REEB 25.033(9)(f)

Wisconsin records land instruments in the **county register of deeds**. What is recorded there includes
deeds, mortgages and satisfactions, land contracts, easements, restrictive covenants, plats and
certified survey maps, judgments and liens, lis pendens, and probate documents affecting title.

**Recording is constructive notice to the world.** That is the point of the whole system: it allows a
stranger to a transaction to discover, by searching a public office, what interests exist in a parcel,
without asking anyone.

### 43.2 The three kinds of notice

**Actual notice** is what a person in fact knows.

**Constructive notice** is what the law charges a person with knowing whether they know it or not,
because it is recorded. Everyone is charged with notice of every properly recorded instrument in the
chain of title.

**Inquiry notice** is what a person would have discovered by making the investigation the visible facts
demanded. **Possession by someone other than the owner is the classic trigger.** A buyer who sees a
tenant in the house is on notice to ask, and is charged with what a reasonable inquiry would have
revealed — even if nothing is recorded.

The practical lesson: **possession is notice**, and a property occupied by anyone other than the seller
raises a question that must be answered in writing before closing.

### 43.3 Chain of title, gaps, and clouds

A **chain of title** is the succession of recorded owners. A searcher works backward from the current
owner and forward again, checking each ownership period for liens and adverse instruments.

A **gap** is a break — a conveyance out from someone who never appears as a grantee. In Wisconsin the
usual causes are an **unprobated estate**, an unrecorded deed, or a name change.

A **cloud** is a recorded instrument or claim casting doubt on title without necessarily defeating it —
an unreleased mortgage, an old judgment, an ambiguous easement, a defective acknowledgment, a deed from
one of several heirs.

**Marketable title** is title a reasonable, well-informed purchaser would accept: not perfect title, but
title free from reasonable doubt and the threat of litigation. It is what the offer's title provisions
require the seller to convey.

**Quiet title** is the judicial proceeding used where nothing else will clear a cloud.

### 43.4 Record immediately

Wisconsin's recording act determines priority between competing claimants, and **this course does not
state which rule Wisconsin applies or where it sits**, because the provision has not been read from
primary text for this project.

What follows under any of the three models — race, notice, or race-notice — is the operating
instruction: **record immediately, and treat any delay as risk.** The closing agent records; the
licensee's contribution is to make sure nothing is holding it up.

## Hour 44 — Evidence of title

### 44.1 What "evidence of title" means — REEB 25.033(9)(g)

The seller must show the buyer that title is what the contract requires it to be. Historically that was
an **abstract of title** — a condensed history of everything recorded affecting the parcel — with an
attorney's **title opinion** on it. Wisconsin practice today uses **title insurance** in most
transactions, and the offer specifies what evidence the seller must furnish.

An opinion compensates only if the attorney was negligent. **A policy pays on the risk**, and it
includes a duty to **defend** — which is frequently worth more than the indemnity.

### 44.2 Title insurance

**Backward-looking indemnity** against defects in title **existing at the policy date** and not
excepted. One premium, paid at closing, no renewals.

An **owner's policy** insures the buyer's interest for the purchase price, for as long as the insured or
their heirs hold an interest. A **loan policy** insures the lender's security interest for the loan
amount, declining as it is repaid.

**A loan policy does not protect the buyer.** This is the most important consumer point in the hour: a
buyer who pays for the lender's policy and declines an owner's policy has purchased protection for
someone else.

### 44.3 Reading the commitment

**Schedule A** — the facts: the proposed insured, the amount, the estate insured, the current record
owner, and the legal description.

**Schedule B-I, Requirements** — what must be **done** before the policy issues. Pay off and satisfy the
existing mortgage. Obtain a death certificate. Complete a probate. Obtain a spouse's signature. Record a
corrective deed. Obtain a lien waiver from a contractor. **These are action items with owners and
deadlines, and they are the part of the commitment most likely to derail a closing if nobody reads them
early.**

**Schedule B-II, Exceptions** — what the policy will **not** cover: recorded easements, restrictive
covenants, mineral reservations, **matters an accurate survey would show**, **rights of parties in
possession**, and taxes not yet due. Exceptions are not defects; they are risks the insurer declines and
the buyer keeps.

**Exclusions** are different: printed in the policy form, removing whole categories — governmental
police power and zoning, eminent domain, defects the insured created or agreed to, and matters first
attaching after the policy date.

**Extended coverage** removes some standard exceptions, typically requiring a **survey** and an
affidavit, and costs more. Recommending that a buyer **ask the closing agent about** extended coverage
is proper; telling a buyer they do not need it is not.

### 44.4 What title insurance does not cover

Anything **excepted**. Anything **excluded**. And **every problem with the physical property** — a
leaking roof, a failed septic system, radon, a boundary a survey would have shown, the neighbour's
noise. Buyers conflate title and condition constantly, and a licensee who does not correct the confusion
leaves a buyer believing they are insured against something they are not.

### Module WI-07 engagement check

**Question.** Your seller's title commitment arrives with Schedule B-I requiring: (1) satisfaction of a
1998 mortgage; (2) completion of probate for a co-owner who died in 2019; and (3) the signature of the
seller's spouse, who is not on the deed. Schedule B-II excepts "rights of parties in possession" and
"matters an accurate survey would disclose". The seller says the spouse "has nothing to do with it" and
that the brother-in-law living in the basement "is just staying for a while". What do you do?

**Expected answer.**

**All three requirements are action items with owners and deadlines, and I escalate them the day the
commitment arrives.** The **1998 mortgage** needs a recorded **satisfaction**, and locating a lender
acquired twice since takes time. The **2019 death** means an **unprobated estate** in the chain — title
cannot be conveyed until it is administered, that takes **months**, and the closing date in the contract
almost certainly needs amending.

**The spouse's signature is not the seller's to waive.** Wisconsin is a **marital property state**, and
**§ 706.02** requires the signature of each grantor while the **homestead** provisions may require a
spouse's signature **even where the spouse is not on the deed**. The title company has required it for a
reason. I route it to the **closing agent and counsel** and I do not accept the seller's
characterisation.

**The brother-in-law in the basement is inquiry notice**, and it lines up exactly with the Schedule B-II
exception for **rights of parties in possession**. A buyer who sees an occupant is charged with what a
reasonable inquiry would have revealed, and the exception means the policy will not cover whatever that
turns out to be. The occupancy is put in writing to the seller and the closing agent, and it is also a
fact I must consider under **§ 452.133(1)(c)** — a material adverse fact the firm knows that a buyer may
not be able to discover through reasonably vigilant observation.

**The survey exception** means the buyer should consider a **survey**, or ask the closing agent about
**extended coverage**. I recommend they ask; I do not tell them what they need.

## Extended treatment — title worked through

### E7.1 The deeds compared

| Deed | Warrants | Typical use | Buyer's risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warranty deed | Against **all** defects, whenever arising | Residential resale | Lowest |
| Special warranty | Only defects arising **during the grantor's ownership** | Fiduciaries, entities, builders | Pre-ownership defects uncovered |
| Quit claim | **Nothing** — conveys whatever the grantor has | Curative; family; releasing an interest | The grantor may have owned nothing |
| Personal representative's | The authority under which the signer acts | Estate sales | Whether the authority was properly obtained |
| Sheriff's | The judicial process behind it | Foreclosure sales | Whether the process was completed correctly |
| Deed in lieu | Voluntary conveyance to a lender | Avoiding foreclosure | **Junior liens generally survive** |

### E7.2 A chain of title, read

*The commitment shows: 1971 warranty deed to Harold and Ruth; 1998 quit claim from Ruth to Harold; 2004
warranty deed from Harold to the current seller; a 1999 mortgage marked unsatisfied; a 2016 judgment
against "H. Anderson"; and a 2021 easement to a utility.*

**The 1998 quit claim** is ordinary — a spouse releasing an interest, most likely on a divorce or an
estate plan — but a quit claim in a chain always raises the question of **what interest existed to
release**, and in Wisconsin the **marital property** question sits behind it.

**The 1999 mortgage marked unsatisfied** is a Schedule B-I requirement and probably the longest lead
item: locating a lender acquired twice since and obtaining a recorded **satisfaction** takes weeks.

**The 2016 judgment against "H. Anderson"** is a **common-name** problem. It may be the seller or it may
be a different person entirely, and clearing it requires an affidavit or a search the title company
directs. It is routine and it takes time.

**The 2021 utility easement** is a Schedule B-II **exception** — a risk the insurer declines and the
buyer keeps — and its location matters if the buyer intends to build.

**The licensee's part in all four:** read Schedule B-I the day it arrives, identify the owner and the
deadline for each requirement, escalate to the closing agent, and tell the client in writing what may
move the closing date.

### E7.3 Recording, notice, and the possession problem

**Recording gives constructive notice** and does not transfer title. **Title passes on delivery** with
present intent, and on acceptance.

**Possession is inquiry notice**, and it is the notice a licensee is most likely to encounter and least
likely to act on. A tenant, a relative, a former owner, a person storing equipment in an outbuilding —
each raises a question a reasonable purchaser must investigate, and the buyer is charged with what a
proper inquiry would have revealed even where nothing is recorded.

**And it lines up with the Schedule B-II exception for "rights of parties in possession"**, so the
policy will not cover whatever the inquiry would have found.

**What a licensee does:** disclose the occupancy in writing as a fact the firm knows —
**§ 452.133(1)(c)** — and insist the question go in writing to the seller and to the closing agent
before closing. **Never accept the seller's characterisation as settling it.**

### E7.4 Probate's effect on a timetable

| Step | Who | Typical effect on a closing date |
|---|---|---|
| Determining whether administration is required | Title company, counsel | Days |
| Appointment of a personal representative | Court | **Weeks** |
| Confirming the scope of the letters | Title company | Days |
| Court approval of a sale, where required | Court | **Weeks** |
| Executing a personal representative's deed | Personal representative | At closing |

**A purchase contract written on an estate property with an ordinary closing date is a contract that will
need amendment.** The licensee's contribution is to ask at listing whether an owner has died, route the
answer, and set a realistic date — not to discover it when the commitment arrives.
