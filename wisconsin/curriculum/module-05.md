## Learning objectives

By the end of this module the student will be able to:

- Classify a lien as voluntary or involuntary and as general or specific.
- Rank competing liens from stated recording and statutory facts and say which survive a foreclosure.
- Explain Wisconsin's property tax cycle from assessment through equalization to the tax bill.
- Identify a construction lien risk at listing and route it.
- Say where the real estate transfer fee comes from and who computes it.

## Hour 24 — Liens

### 24.1 What a lien is — REEB 25.033(7)(a)

A **lien** is a charge against property securing payment of a debt or performance of an obligation. It
is an encumbrance on **title**, and it gives the holder the right to force a sale if the obligation is
not met.

Liens do not affect **possession**. An owner with three liens still lives in the house. What they
affect is transferability, which is why they surface at closing rather than in daily life.

### 24.2 Two classifications, used together

**Voluntary or involuntary.** A **voluntary** lien is created deliberately by the owner — in
Wisconsin, most commonly a **mortgage**. An **involuntary** lien arises by operation of law: tax
liens, judgment liens, construction liens.

**General or specific.** A **general** lien attaches to **all** of the debtor's property within the
jurisdiction — judgment liens, federal and state tax liens, estate tax liens. A **specific** lien
attaches to **one identified parcel** — the mortgage, the property tax lien, the construction lien,
the special assessment.

Every lien is describable in both dimensions. A judgment lien is involuntary and general; a mortgage
is voluntary and specific.

**Statutory or equitable** is a third axis: a statutory lien is created by statute — the tax lien, the
construction lien; an equitable lien arises from a court's application of fairness or from the parties'
conduct.

### 24.3 Priority

**Priority determines who is paid first from a forced sale, and whose lien survives it.** Liens junior
to the foreclosing lien are generally extinguished as to the property; senior liens survive and the
purchaser takes subject to them.

The general rule is **first to record, first in right**. Three sets of exceptions:

**Statutory superpriority.** Property tax liens come first, whenever recorded, because the statute
puts them there rather than because of their date.

**Relation back.** A **construction lien**'s priority is measured from a date tied to the commencement
of the work rather than from filing, so a lien filed after closing can outrank a mortgage recorded
before it. Hour 26.

**Subordination.** A lienholder may agree in a recorded **subordination agreement** to take a lower
position than recording order would give — which is why refinancing a first mortgage while a home
equity line remains recorded requires one.

### 24.4 Release

A lien ends when the obligation is satisfied **and a release is recorded**. For a mortgage, that is a
**satisfaction of mortgage**. Payment alone does not clear the record, and an unreleased paid-off
mortgage is among the most common clouds a Wisconsin title search reveals.

The licensee's contribution is timing and disclosure: order title early, read the commitment's
requirements the day it arrives, escalate anything involving recent construction or a judgment
immediately, and disclose what § 452.133(1)(c) requires — in writing, to every party.

## Hour 25 — Tax liens and the Wisconsin property tax cycle

### 25.1 Tax liens — REEB 25.033(7)(b)

**Real property tax liens** secure the general property tax. They are **specific**, **involuntary**,
and hold **statutory priority** over other liens regardless of when those liens were recorded.

Unpaid taxes are enforced by the county through a statutory process on its own timetable, ending in
the county taking title. **This course states no delinquency period, interest rate, or redemption
period**, because those provisions have not been read from primary text for this project. They are
answered by the county treasurer.

### 25.2 Assessment, equalization, and the tax bill — REEB 25.033(7)(c)

The rule requires instruction on "general tax assessment, equalization, and tax bills", and Wisconsin's
cycle has features a licensee must be able to explain.

**Assessment** is performed by the **municipal assessor** — the city, village or town — who determines
the assessed value of each parcel. Wisconsin assessors are certified by the Department of Revenue.

**Assessed value is not market value.** Municipalities revalue on their own cycles, and between
revaluations assessed values drift away from the market. The **assessment ratio** — the relationship
between a municipality's total assessed value and its **equalized value** — is what measures the drift.

**Equalized value** is the Department of Revenue's estimate of the full market value of all taxable
property in a municipality. It exists because tax levies are shared across overlapping jurisdictions —
county, school district, technical college — whose boundaries do not match municipal boundaries. Without
equalization, a municipality that assessed at half of market would shoulder half its fair share of the
county levy. **Equalization apportions the levy fairly between municipalities; it does not change any
individual parcel's assessment.**

**The mill rate** is the levy divided by the assessed value base, conventionally expressed as dollars
per thousand of assessed value.

**The Wisconsin tax bill** is issued in **December** for the year just assessed, and it shows the
levies of each taxing jurisdiction, the state credits applied, and the net amount. Payment is made to
the **municipal treasurer** in the first instance, on the municipality's instalment schedule, with
later instalments and delinquent taxes going to the **county treasurer**.

Two credits appear on a Wisconsin bill and a licensee should recognise them: the **school levy credit**
and **first dollar credit**, applied automatically; and the **lottery and gaming credit**, which
applies only to a property the owner used as a **primary residence** as of a stated date and **only if
claimed**. A buyer of a property that was not the seller's primary residence may not have the credit in
the first year.

### 25.3 What a licensee may and may not say

**May:** state the current annual tax from the municipality's or county's own record; explain the
assessment and equalization concepts; explain that the bill arrives in December; and direct a client to
the assessor, the treasurer, and the Department of Revenue.

**May not:** present assessed value as evidence of market value — that is a **misleading market values**
problem under **REEB 24.09** and inconsistent with **§ 452.133(1)(e)**'s duty to provide **accurate**
information about market conditions; or tell a buyer their taxes will stay at the seller's level, since
a sale frequently prompts a reassessment and credits may change.

**Proration** is Module WI-07's arithmetic, but the Wisconsin trap belongs here: **the tax year, the
billing date and the instalment schedule must be read from the actual bill and the municipality's own
schedule**, not assumed from another state's calendar.

## Hour 26 — Mortgage liens, construction liens, and judgments

### 26.1 Mortgage liens — REEB 25.033(7)(d)

Wisconsin uses a **mortgage**, not a deed of trust. Two parties: the **mortgagor**, who is the borrower
and gives the mortgage, and the **mortgagee**, the lender. The **note** is the debt and is not recorded;
the **mortgage** is the security and is recorded.

Priority ordinarily runs from the date and time of recording, subject to the exceptions in 24.3.
Wisconsin residential foreclosure is **judicial**, and Module WI-08 develops the instrument and the
process.

**Land contracts** — REEB 25.033(10)(e) — are a Wisconsin financing structure in their own right, and
Module WI-08 takes them. Their lien and title consequences are real: legal title remains with the
vendor while the vendee holds equitable title and possession.

### 26.2 Construction liens — REEB 25.033(7)(e)

**Wisconsin calls them construction liens**, not mechanics' liens, and they are governed by **ch. 779**.

They secure payment for labour, services, materials or plans furnished to improve real property, and
they are the most dangerous lien for a buyer of recently improved property because of **relation
back**: priority is measured from a date tied to the commencement of the work, so a lien **filed after
closing** can outrank a mortgage recorded before it.

Wisconsin's scheme also carries **notice requirements** — preliminary notices given by contractors and
subcontractors — with deadlines, and strict filing and enforcement periods.

**This course states none of ch. 779's notices, deadlines or periods**, because the chapter has not
been read from primary text for this project.

What a licensee must carry is the **warning sign and the routing**: **recent or ongoing construction,
a recent remodel, a new roof, a new well or septic, or an unpaid contractor dispute on a property
being sold is a question for the closing agent and counsel, every time.** A seller's assurance that
"everyone was paid" is not evidence, and a lien waiver is a document the closing agent obtains.

### 26.3 Judgments — REEB 25.033(7)(f)

A **judgment lien** arises when a court judgment is docketed and attaches to the debtor's real
property in that county. It is **general** and **involuntary**, and — importantly — it attaches to
property the debtor acquires **later** as well as property owned at the time.

A judgment against a seller shows up in the title search and must be satisfied at closing. A judgment
against a **buyer** matters too: it attaches to the property the moment the buyer takes title, which
can defeat the lender's expected first position.

**Common-name searches** produce false positives, and clearing a judgment against a different person
with the same name is a routine title requirement that takes time — which is why title is ordered
early.

**Lis pendens** is not a lien but is recorded and gives notice of pending litigation affecting the
property, and it stops a transaction as effectively as a lien would.

## Hour 27 — The remaining liens, and the statutes the rule names

### 27.1 Estate and inheritance tax liens — REEB 25.033(7)(g)

Liens securing death taxes attach on death and are **general**. Whether any is in play depends on
federal law and on Wisconsin's own treatment, both of which change, and neither of which this course
states. Where a seller's title comes through an estate, the question belongs to the **title company and
counsel**, and it is one of several reasons an estate sale takes longer than a conventional one.

**Probate** is a listed topic at REEB 25.033(9)(d), and Module WI-07 takes it.

### 27.2 Other liens — REEB 25.033(7)(h)

**Special assessments** for public improvements benefiting particular properties — sewer, water,
sidewalks, street lighting. Specific, involuntary, frequently payable in instalments, and frequently
carrying a high priority. **A special assessment levied but not yet payable is a fact a buyer needs at
the offer**, and it is obtained from the municipality.

**Municipal utility charges** — water, sewer and refuse — which in Wisconsin can be placed on the tax
roll and become a lien if unpaid.

**Association assessment liens** in condominiums and planned communities, created by the governing
documents and by statute.

**Federal tax liens** from unpaid federal taxes — general and involuntary.

**State tax liens** — delinquent income, sales or withholding tax.

**Vendor's and vendee's liens** — equitable liens securing, respectively, an unpaid seller and a
buyer's deposit.

**Child support liens** and other statutory liens that attach by operation of law.

### 27.3 The statutes REEB 25.033(7) names

**Taxes under ss. 74.15 and 74.47(1) and (2)** — REEB 25.033(7)(i). **Ch. 74** governs property tax
collection, and the paragraphs named reach the mechanics of payment and of delinquency charges. **This
course states no figure from them**, because ch. 74 has not been read from primary text for this
project, and the county treasurer answers the question in any event.

**Taxation and transfer fees under ss. 77.22 and 77.27** — REEB 25.033(7)(j). Wisconsin imposes a
**real estate transfer fee** on conveyances, submitted with an **electronic real estate transfer
return** at recording. The fee is conventionally paid by the **seller** unless the parties agree
otherwise, and exemptions exist.

**This course states no transfer fee rate and no exemption**, because ch. 77 has not been read from
primary text. The **closing agent computes the fee and prepares the return**, and a licensee who
quotes a rate from memory has answered a question they did not verify.

What a licensee should know: **the transfer return is filed at recording and the fee is a real closing
cost**; **exemptions exist** and a transaction that may qualify — a transfer between spouses, to or
from a trust, or on a divorce — goes to the closing agent early; and the return itself records the
conveyance's value, which is one reason Wisconsin closing figures are visible in the public record.

### 27.4 The licensee's part in all of it

Liens are found by **title search**, cleared by the **closing agent**, and interpreted by **counsel**.

The licensee's contribution is four things: **order title early**; **read the commitment's
requirements the day it arrives**; **escalate immediately** anything involving recent construction, a
judgment, an estate, a special assessment, or an unreleased mortgage; and **disclose in writing** what
**§ 452.133(1)(c)** requires — material adverse facts the firm knows that the party does not know and
cannot discover through reasonably vigilant observation.

### Module WI-05 engagement check

**Question.** A property sells at a Wisconsin foreclosure sale under a mortgage recorded in March 2019.
Also of record: unpaid current-year property taxes; a judgment docketed in 2021; a construction lien
filed in 2023 for a roof installed in **September 2018**; and a second mortgage recorded in 2022. Rank
them, and say which survive.

**Expected answer.**

**Property taxes are paid first** by statutory priority, regardless of when the other liens were
recorded.

**The construction lien is the trap.** It was **filed** in 2023 but the work was performed in
**September 2018** — before the March 2019 mortgage was recorded. Wisconsin construction liens under
**ch. 779** **relate back** to a date tied to the commencement of the work, so on these facts the lien
may well be **senior** to the 2019 mortgage rather than junior to it. **Whether it does, and from what
date, is a ch. 779 question this course does not answer** — it goes to the closing agent and counsel,
and it is exactly why recent construction on a property being sold is escalated every time.

**Then the 2019 first mortgage**, the foreclosing lien. **Then, from any surplus, the 2021 judgment and
the 2022 second mortgage in recording order.**

**What survives:** the tax lien and anything senior to the foreclosing mortgage — which may include the
construction lien. The **2021 judgment and the 2022 second mortgage are junior** and are extinguished
as to the property, whether or not any surplus reaches them; the underlying debts may survive against
the debtor personally.

None of this is advice a licensee gives. It is why a buyer at a foreclosure sale needs a title search
and counsel before bidding.

**Deliberately not stated, for want of a primary reading:** every provision of **ch. 779**
(construction liens), including its **notice requirements, filing deadlines, enforcement periods and
the date from which priority relates back**; every figure in **ch. 74**, including delinquency periods,
interest, and redemption; the **real estate transfer fee rate and every exemption** in **ch. 77**; and
the treatment of **estate and inheritance tax liens** under federal and Wisconsin law.

**The construction lien relation-back rule is taught as a warning sign rather than as a rule with a
date.** Hour 26.2 states that priority is measured from a date tied to the commencement of the work
rather than from filing, which is what makes the lien dangerous, and routes the determination to the
closing agent and counsel. The engagement check is written so that the correct student answer
identifies the risk and the referral — not a date.

**Wisconsin's equalization concept in Hour 25.2 is explained because it is a listed topic** at REEB
25.033(7)(c) and because licensees routinely misdescribe it. Equalization apportions a shared levy
between municipalities; it does not change an individual parcel's assessment. The **lottery and gaming
credit** is described as claimed by an owner using the property as a primary residence, without stating
any date or amount.
